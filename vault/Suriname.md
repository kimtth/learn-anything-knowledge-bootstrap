# Suriname

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> Suriname is a resource-rich, ethnically diverse South American country whose population and plantation history are concentrated along the northern coast while rainforest covers most of its interior. The source follows Dutch colonialism, independence, military rule, civilian restoration, river-and-forest geography, and the cultural legacies of Indigenous, African, South Asian, Javanese, and migrant communities.

## Introduction

![[attachments/suriname_001.png|420]]

*Relief map of Suriname*

Suriname is a small country on the northern coast of South America. It has one of the most ethnically diverse populations in the region and an economy based heavily on natural resources, especially bauxite, gold, oil, forests, and agriculture.

Formerly known as Dutch Guiana, Suriname was a Dutch plantation colony until independence on November 25, 1975. It was governed by military regimes from 1980 to 1987, returned to civilian rule under a new constitution in 1987, experienced another coup in 1990, and returned again to civilian rule in 1991.

The capital, Paramaribo, lies on the Suriname River about 9 miles from the Atlantic. The southern four-fifths of the country is mostly pristine tropical rainforest, while population and agriculture are concentrated in the north.

## Land

Suriname borders the Atlantic Ocean, French Guiana, Brazil, and Guyana. It has territorial disputes with Guyana over the New River Triangle and the Courantyne River boundary, and with French Guiana over land between the Itany and Marouini rivers. In 2007 a UN tribunal settled a maritime boundary dispute with Guyana, awarding Suriname one-third of the disputed Caribbean Sea area.

## Relief

The northern coastal zone has sandbanks and mudbanks deposited by currents carrying material from the Amazon region. South of this lies the New Coastal Plain, a swampy region of clay and peat crossed by sandy ridges parallel to the coast.

The Old Coastal Plain lies farther south and includes old ridges, clay flats, and swamps. South of it is the Zanderij formation, a belt of rolling hills on quartz-rich bleached sands. Farther south, rainforest-covered highlands and central mountain ranges border Brazil. Juliana Top in the Wilhelmina Mountains is the highest summit.

## Drainage, Soils, and Climate

Suriname's main [[Rivers|rivers]] flow north to the Atlantic, including the Courantyne, Coppename, Suriname, and Maroni. The Courantyne borders Guyana, and the Maroni borders French Guiana. The most fertile soils occur in reclaimed polder lands of the New Coastal Plain.

Suriname has a tropical climate with four northern seasons: minor rainy, minor dry, major rainy, and major dry. Paramaribo daytime temperatures usually range from 70 to 90 F. Rainfall is highest in the central and southeastern interior and averages about 95 inches in Paramaribo.

## Plant and Animal Life

![[attachments/suriname_003.webp|420]]

*Rainforest landscape in Suriname*

About nine-tenths of Suriname is covered with forest, including more than 1,000 tree species. Coastal flora includes ferns, seed plants, mosses, weeds, and mildews. Important trees include baboen, used for plywood, and kapok, which can grow more than 150 feet high.

The Central Suriname Nature Reserve, created in 1998 and made a UNESCO World Heritage site in 2000, is one of the world's largest protected rainforest areas. Wildlife includes monkeys, wild pigs, deer, manatees, jaguars, ocelots, armadillos, sloths, anteaters, tapirs, caimans, iguanas, boas, marine turtles, parrots, vultures, hummingbirds, and hundreds of fish species.

## People

Suriname's population is highly diverse. South Asians, descendants of Indian contract labourers, are the largest group. Creoles of mainly African descent form another major group, while Javanese descendants of Indonesian contract labourers and Maroons descended from escaped enslaved Africans are also important.

Indigenous peoples such as Carib, Arawak, Trio, Wayana-Aparai, Warao, Wayarekule, Tucayana, and Akurio form a small share of the population. Other groups include descendants of Chinese, Lebanese, Portuguese, Dutch, West Indian, Brazilian, and newer Chinese immigrants.

Dutch is the official language, though many people learn it as a second language. Other languages include Sranan, English, Sarnami, Javanese, Maroon languages, and Indigenous languages.

Religion is also diverse. [[Christianity]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christianity)) is the largest religious tradition, especially Roman Catholicism and Moravian Protestantism. Hindus are mainly South Asian, Muslims are mainly Javanese and South Asian, Judaism has been present since the early colonial period, and African, Indian, Confucian, and other traditions remain visible.

## Settlement and Demography

![[attachments/suriname_002.webp|420]]

*Historic inner city of Paramaribo*

Nearly three-fourths of Suriname's people live in urban areas, and about one-fifth are concentrated in Paramaribo and nearby areas. Paramaribo preserves many Dutch colonial buildings and its historic centre became a UNESCO World Heritage site in 2002.

Smaller centres include Nieuw Nickerie, Albina, Moengo, and Paranam. Maroon and Indigenous settlements dominate much of the interior. After independence was announced, many people emigrated to the Netherlands; by 1980, perhaps one-third of the population had left, including many skilled workers.

## Economy

Suriname's economy ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economy)) depends on natural resources, especially bauxite, gold, oil, forests, and agriculture. Remittances from the Netherlands, French Guiana, and the United States are also important. Suriname is a member of the Caribbean Community.

Less than 1 percent of land is arable, and most farmland lies on the New Coastal Plain. Drainage is needed during wet periods and irrigation during dry periods. Rice is the staple crop and covers more than half of cultivated land, with two harvests each year. Bananas, citrus, coconuts, palm oil, sugar, coffee, and cocoa also matter.

Suriname's forests provide major timber resources, though they are not fully exploited. Plywood and timber are exported. Fishing is small but centred on Paramaribo and includes shrimp exports to North America.

Bauxite is the leading mineral, with mines near Paranam and Overdacht. Gold mining has grown, and Suriname also has chromium, clay, copper, diamonds, iron ore, manganese, nickel, platinum, and tin. Staatsolie produces oil from Tambaredjo and Calcutta fields.

## Manufacturing, Finance, and Trade

The main industry is bauxite mining and processing, with an alumina refinery and aluminum smelter at Paranam. Other manufacturing is small and import-substituting, including processed foods, clothing, cigarettes, and construction materials.

The Central Bank of Suriname, established in 1957, controls monetary policy with the finance minister. The Suriname dollar replaced the guilder in 2004. Banks and insurers often cooperate with or are subsidiaries of foreign companies, mainly Dutch and American.

Bauxite, alumina, and gold account for most exports. Imports include machinery, transport equipment, fuels, foods, and chemicals. Important trading partners include the United States, Canada, the Netherlands, and Trinidad and Tobago. Tourism began developing in the early 21st century around environmental attractions.

## Transportation and Government

Transport is concentrated in the coastal zone. The East-West Highway links Paramaribo with Albina and Nieuw Nickerie, and another road reaches Afobaka near Brokopondo Dam. Rivers and canals remain important, and Paramaribo is the chief port. The international airport is at Zanderij.

Under the 1987 constitution, legislative power belongs to a 51-member National Assembly elected by popular vote. The assembly elects a president and vice president for five-year terms. The president chairs a military-influenced Council of State that can review government actions and annul laws.

Local government is divided into districts and subdistricts. District councils and subdistrict councils are elected every five years. Universal suffrage began in 1948, and citizens age 18 and older may vote.

## Education and Cultural Life

About nine-tenths of Surinamese age 15 and older are literate. [[Education]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Education)) follows Dutch models, uses Dutch as the language of instruction, is compulsory up to age 12, and is free at all levels. Anton de Kom University of Suriname was founded in 1968 and includes faculties in law, medicine, social science and economics, engineering, and natural resources.

Suriname's culture ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Culture)) is marked by relatively harmonious contact among ethnic groups. Cuisine blends South Asian, Javanese, Creole, West African, Chinese, and European elements. Javanese gamelan troupes, Maroon crafts, Indigenous arts, textiles, baskets, wood carving, and painting all contribute to national culture.

Writers include Albert Helman, Shrinivasi, Astrid Roemer, and Cynthia McLeod. Music, crafts, literature, and festivals reflect the country's multilingual and multiethnic character.

## History

Indigenous peoples inhabited Suriname for millennia. Arawak, Carib, and Surinen peoples were among early inhabitants. Europeans learned of the coast from Christopher Columbus in 1498, and Spanish explorers Amerigo Vespucci, Alonso de Ojeda, and Vicente Yanez Pinzon sailed along or visited the coast around 1499-1500.

Early European settlement attempts failed because of Indigenous resistance. The first permanent European settlement was established in 1651 by British planters and enslaved Africans. In 1667 the Dutch seized Suriname and exchanged New Amsterdam, now New York City, for control of the colony.

Suriname became a plantation colony producing sugar, coffee, cacao, cotton, indigo, wood, and later bauxite-related wealth. Enslaved Africans formed the majority until the mid-19th century. Jewish settlers, Europeans, Chinese, Madeiran, Indian, and Javanese labourers contributed to a plural society.

After 1954, Dutch and European aid supported development. Political parties often formed around ethnic constituencies: the NPS, PSV, VHP, KTPI, PNR, and others. Despite South Asian fears of Creole domination, Suriname became independent on November 25, 1975.

In 1980, noncommissioned officers overthrew the government. Military rule under the National Military Council increased spending, suspended aid, weakened the economy, and strained relations among military, civilian politicians, and society.

Elections in 1991 brought the New Front coalition and President Ronald Venetiaan. The military was formally deprived of political power, and agreements addressed Maroon refugees. Yet inflation, bureaucracy, cocaine trafficking, and the influence of military leader Desi Bouterse remained serious issues.

Bouterse's National Democratic Party remained influential. He was convicted in the Netherlands in 1999 on drug charges but was not extradited. In 2007 he was tried in Suriname over the 1982 murders of 15 opponents. In 2010 the National Assembly elected him president, and in 2012 parliament granted immunity for human rights violations from his military period.
