# Faraday

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> Michael Faraday was a nineteenth-century experimental scientist whose work transformed electricity, magnetism, and chemistry. His demonstration of electromagnetic induction established principles that made electric generation and many modern technologies possible.

## Introduction

Michael Faraday was born on 22 September 1791 in Newington, Surrey, and died on 25 August 1867 at Hampton Court. He became one of the most important scientists of the 19th century through experiments that transformed understanding of [[Electricity and Magnetism (Electricity)|electricity]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electricity)), [[Electricity and Magnetism (Magnetism)|magnetism]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnetism)), and chemistry. Faraday began as a chemist, discovered new organic compounds, and was the first to liquefy a permanent gas. His greatest achievement, however, was to show how magnetism could produce electric current, which opened the path to the electric motor and dynamo.
Faraday is both an experimental genius and a thinker shaped by his religious beliefs. His membership in the Sandemanian sect gave him a strong sense of purpose, and his work often reflected a deep conviction that nature was governed by unified forces.

Faraday grew up in poverty in a family of four children. His father was a blacksmith, and the family often struggled to have enough food. Faraday received only basic schooling, but he learned to read and write in a church Sunday school. As a boy he worked delivering newspapers for a book dealer and bookbinder. This job gave him access to books, and he especially admired an article on electricity in the Encyclopaedia Britannica. He built simple experimental devices from bottles and wood, which showed his early interest in science.

His big chance came when he attended lectures by Sir Humphry Davy at the Royal Institution in London. Faraday took detailed notes, bound them, and sent them to Davy with a request for work. Davy later hired him as a laboratory assistant after another assistant was dismissed. This appointment began Faraday’s career in science.

## Chemical research

At the Royal Institution, Faraday learned chemistry under Davy, who was then transforming the field. Faraday helped with experiments on new elements and compounds, including chlorine, and worked on substitution reactions. He later isolated and described benzene in 1825, and his work on steel alloys and optical glass helped lay foundations for metallurgy and glass science.
Faraday’s early discoveries were not only important in themselves, but also proof of his talent as an experimenter. He could move from practical laboratory work to broad theoretical insight.

## Electricity and magnetism

Faraday’s major breakthrough came when he understood the relationship between electric current and magnetism. After Hans Christian Oersted showed that current in a wire creates a magnetic field, and André-Marie Ampère clarified the circular nature of that field, Faraday realized that a magnetic pole should move around a wire carrying current. He built a device that confirmed this idea, and this invention became the first electric motor.

Faraday then explored the production of current from magnetism. He found that moving a permanent magnet in and out of a coil of wire induced a current. This led to the first dynamo, in which a rotating copper disk between magnetic poles produced a continuous current. He also proposed that electricity should be understood as a state of strain or tension in matter, not simply as a flowing fluid.

## Electrochemistry and induction

Faraday carried out careful experiments on electrolysis and concluded that the amount of material deposited at electrodes depends on the quantity of electricity passed through the cell. These results became his two laws of electrochemistry. He also extended the idea to static electricity and argued that all materials have a specific inductive capacity. This helped him develop a broad theory that electrical action involves strains in matter and the transmission of force.

## Later life

In 1839, Faraday’s health broke down after years of intense work. He did little creative science for six years. When he returned in 1845, he resumed his search for evidence of the invisible forces that he believed linked all natural phenomena. He tested the effect of magnetism on light and found that a magnetic field could rotate the plane of polarization in glass. This was a major discovery and gave support to his belief in the unity of natural forces.

He also found that some substances are attracted to magnetic fields while others are repelled. This led to the discovery of diamagnetism, the behavior of certain substances in strong magnetic fields. Faraday’s work thus connected electricity, magnetism, light, and matter in a new way.

## Legacy

Faraday’s experimental work provided the foundation for James Clerk Maxwell, whose classical electromagnetic theory built on Faraday’s ideas. Faraday is remembered not only for specific inventions, but also for a new conception of physical reality in which forces and fields are central. His life and work became a model of scientific discovery through careful experiment, imagination, and persistence.

## Core Equations

Physical models often begin by relating force, energy, waves, and conserved quantities:

$$\vec{F}=m\vec{a}$$

This is Newton's second law: force is the product of mass and acceleration, so a larger mass or a faster change in motion requires a larger net force.

$$E=h\nu$$

This relation says that photon energy is proportional to frequency; higher-frequency radiation carries more energy per photon.

$$E=mc^2$$

This expresses mass-energy equivalence: mass is a form of energy, with the speed of light squared as the conversion factor.

## Key Events & Developments

- 1791: Born in Newington, Surrey.
- 1812: Began work as Sir Humphry Davy’s laboratory assistant.
- 1825: Isolated and described benzene.
- 1821: Married Sarah Barnard and settled at the Royal Institution.
- 1831: Discovered electromagnetic induction and built the first dynamo.
- 1845: Resumed research and discovered the magnetic effect on light and diamagnetism.

## Key People

- Michael Faraday ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michael_Faraday)): experimental physicist and chemist whose work shaped electromagnetism.
- Sir Humphry Davy ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sir_Humphry_Davy)): Faraday’s mentor and a leading chemist of the time.
- Hans Christian Oersted ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hans_Christian_Oersted)): showed that electric current creates a magnetic field.
- André-Marie Ampère ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andr%C3%A9-Marie_Amp%C3%A8re)): helped explain the circular nature of magnetic force.
- James Clerk Maxwell ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Clerk_Maxwell)): later built classical field theory on Faraday’s foundations.

## Interesting Facts

- Faraday was first trained as a bookbinder and learned science by reading books that came into the shop.
- He was the first to liquefy a permanent gas.
- His experiments on magnetism led directly to the electric motor and dynamo.
- He believed that all natural forces might be manifestations of one universal force.
