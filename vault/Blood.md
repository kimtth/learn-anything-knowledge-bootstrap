# Blood

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> Blood is the circulating tissue that integrates the body by transporting oxygen, nutrients, hormones, and wastes while maintaining fluid, chemical, and thermal balance. Plasma, red cells, white cells, antibodies, platelets, and clotting systems connect circulation with respiration, immunity, metabolism, diagnosis, and the prevention of blood loss.

## Introduction

Blood ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blood)) is the transport tissue and fluid that carries oxygen, nutrients, hormones, and wastes through the body. Blood is essential to life because it keeps the internal environment stable and connects the organs that exchange and regulate substances. It is both a tissue and a fluid: a collection of specialized cells suspended in plasma.

Blood only functions properly because it circulates. Through the lungs, kidneys, digestive tract, and endocrine glands, it links the body's major regulatory systems. Blood is therefore not a simple red liquid but the medium through which the whole body maintains balance.

Blood composition is tightly controlled. Falling glucose, for example, can trigger feedback responses that restore normal levels. That pattern of regulation is a central theme, because the blood must stay within narrow limits for life to continue.

## Plasma

The liquid part of blood is plasma, which is mostly water and contains proteins, nutrients, salts, and dissolved wastes. Plasma proteins help maintain fluid balance, transport substances, and support the constancy of the blood's composition. Water is the largest constituent, and the plasma water is freely exchangeable with body cells and other extracellular fluids.

Plasma proteins, especially serum albumin ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Serum_albumin)), are particularly important. These proteins help hold water in the bloodstream by osmotic effect and also carry substances such as bilirubin. The result is that plasma is not just a solvent; it is an active transport medium that helps maintain circulation, hydration, and chemical balance.
Many nutrients enter the plasma after digestion and are distributed from there to tissues. Amino acids, for example, are carried in the blood after food proteins are broken down. Plasma therefore serves as the highway for both raw materials and regulated body fluids.

## Red Cells and Hemoglobin

![[attachments/blood_001.webp|420]]

*Diagram of oxyhemoglobin, carbaminohemoglobin, and carboxyhemoglobin structures*

Red blood cells contain hemoglobin, the molecule that binds oxygen in the lungs and releases it in the tissues. Its structure includes heme and iron, and oxygen binding is affected by pH, 2,3-DPG, and carbon monoxide. Hemoglobin is central because almost all oxygen transport depends on it.

Hemoglobin carries oxygen with very high affinity in the lungs and releases it more readily in tissues where oxygen tension is lower. This makes oxygen delivery efficient and responsive to need. Carbon monoxide is dangerous because it binds hemoglobin much more strongly than oxygen does.

The red cell is therefore a specialized oxygen carrier. Its structure, pigment, and chemical behavior are all adapted to one crucial task: moving oxygen from the lungs to the rest of the body and releasing it where metabolism requires it.

## White Blood Cells

The major leukocytes are granulocytes, monocytes, and lymphocytes. Each group has a distinct role. Neutrophils are fast-moving phagocytes that respond quickly to infection, while eosinophils help defend against parasites and moderate hypersensitivity. Basophils release histamine and other substances that help trigger inflammatory reactions.

Monocytes become macrophages after entering tissues, and those macrophages ingest debris and foreign material. Lymphocytes are the major cells of acquired immunity, and they move through lymphoid organs such as the spleen, lymph nodes, thymus, and tonsils. The white-cell section therefore links circulation with defense.
White blood cells do not work in isolation. They move toward damaged tissue by chemotaxis, respond to infection, and help coordinate inflammatory and immune responses. Blood is thus a battlefield as well as a transport system.

## Immunity

Blood is central to immune defense. Antibodies, complement, macrophages, and lymphocytes work together to recognize foreign material, destroy microbes, and support transplant-related tissue typing and immunologic competence. Immunity is a coordinated system rather than a single response.

Antibodies can mark microbes for phagocytosis, while complement proteins can injure target cells by helping lyse their membranes. T lymphocytes and B lymphocytes work differently but cooperatively, with B cells producing antibodies and T cells helping to regulate or destroy infected cells. That division of labor is one of the most important biological themes.
Immunity also connects to organ transplantation. Matching donor and recipient tissue types improves the chance that a graft will be accepted, and the study of immune tolerance has practical medical value. The immune system is therefore described both as a defense against disease and as a major challenge in surgery.

## Respiration, Nutrition, and Excretion

Blood ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blood)) transports oxygen from the lungs, nutrients from the digestive tract, and wastes to the kidneys and liver. It links respiration, metabolism, and excretion in a single regulatory system. It keeps the body supplied and also carries away the products of use.

Blood is responsible for maintaining glucose, ions, water balance, and pH through hormonal control and organ exchange. Insulin helps regulate glucose after carbohydrate intake, while the kidneys remove excess water, salts, and nitrogenous wastes. Blood is therefore the medium through which chemical balance is preserved.

The liver and kidneys are major organs of control. They clear waste products, regulate fluid volume, and help keep the blood composition constant. Respiration, nutrition, and excretion are shown as one connected cycle rather than separate functions.

## Temperature and Hemostasis

![[attachments/blood_002.webp|420]]

*Diagram of platelet activation and fibrin mesh formation during blood clotting*

Blood distributes heat and helps regulate body temperature through circulation. Warm blood moving through the skin can release heat, while the brain's temperature-control center responds to blood temperature as if it were a thermostat. Body temperature depends on circulation as much as on metabolism.

At the same time, blood prevents dangerous bleeding through platelets, clotting factors, and clot breakdown mechanisms such as plasmin. Platelets aggregate at damaged vessel walls, while coagulation proteins form a clot that seals the opening. Later, plasmin can dissolve clots after they are no longer needed.

This balance between sealing and removal is central to hemostasis. Blood must remain liquid inside vessels, but it must clot quickly when vessels are injured. That dual task makes the hemostatic system one of the most delicate in physiology.

## Laboratory Study

Blood is routinely examined in medicine to measure hemoglobin, cells, clotting, and many other properties. These tests make blood one of the most important diagnostic fluids in human biology. A physician can learn a great deal from blood because it reflects oxygen capacity, immune activity, and chemical balance.

Laboratory study also shows why blood is so clinically important. Changes in its composition reveal disease, dehydration, anemia, infection, clotting disorders, and many other problems. Laboratory analysis is the practical extension of blood physiology.

## Conclusion

Blood ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blood)) is the body's internal transport and defense medium. Its composition, circulation, and regulation are essential to respiration, nutrition, immunity, temperature control, and survival. Blood is the body's central integrating system, because many different organs depend on it to function together.

Without blood, the body cannot maintain the steady internal conditions that life requires. That is why the subject is not only a matter of anatomy but also of physiology, immunity, and medicine.

## Key Events & Developments

- Blood carries oxygen from the lungs and releases carbon dioxide from the tissues.
- Plasma proteins maintain fluid balance and transport nutrients, hormones, and waste products.
- Red cells use hemoglobin to move oxygen efficiently through the body.
- White cells move from the bloodstream into tissues to fight infection and coordinate immunity.
- Platelets and clotting factors prevent blood loss, while plasmin later dissolves clots.
