# Religious Experience

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> Religious experience names encounters with, or interpretations of reality through, the divine, sacred, or ultimate. The source surveys psychological, philosophical, theological, naturalist, personalist, existential, process, and analytic accounts while asking whether such experience has cognitive as well as transformative value.

## Introduction

![[attachments/religious_experience_001.webp|420]]

*Portrait of William James*

Religious experience ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mystical_or_religious_experience)) can mean a special encounter with the divine or ultimate, or a way of seeing ordinary experience as pointing toward the divine or ultimate. It includes wonder before the cosmos, awe before the sacred, dependence on divine power, guilt before judgment, peace after forgiveness, and concern with life's purpose and personal destiny.

In one sense, religious experience is like meeting a person or object, except that the encounter is with God, the holy, or an unseen order. In another sense, it is not a separate event but a quality of experience: reality is felt as holy, meaningful, or grounded in something ultimate. These two meanings often overlap in religious life.

## Study and Evaluation

![[attachments/religious_experience_002.webp|420]]

*Portrait of Rudolf Otto*

The term religious experience became prominent after William James published The Varieties of Religious Experience ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Varieties_of_Religious_Experience)) in 1902. The idea itself is older, reaching back to Spanish mystics of the 16th century and Protestant reformers who treated personal faith, conversion, sin, salvation, and worship as lived realities.

Thinkers such as Jonathan Edwards, Friedrich Schleiermacher, and Rudolf Otto stressed experience as a way to understand religion as an actual force in life, not merely church membership or obedience to doctrine. This opened religion to study by psychologists, historians, anthropologists, sociologists, theologians, philosophers, phenomenologists, and existentialists.

Four major questions guide the study of religious experience. Scholars ask whether it is a special experience of the divine or any experience interpreted religiously; how it differs from morality, art, and secular spirituality; whether it can be explained by psychological and social origins or is sui generis; and whether it has cognitive value or is only subjective feeling.

## William James and Experiential Religion

William James judged religious experience partly by its consequences for the individual. For him, valid experience showed philosophical reasonableness and moral helpfulness. This pragmatic approach did not try to prove every doctrine directly, but it treated changed life, strengthened purpose, and moral transformation as evidence that experience matters.

The experiential approach made concepts such as God, faith, conversion, sin, salvation, and worship available for personal and comparative study. It also raised difficult problems: a powerful experience may transform a person, but scholars still ask whether it reveals a real divine object or only a human psychological state.

## Idealist, Naturalist, and Personalist Interpretations

Josiah Royce, William E. Hocking, and Wilbur M. Urban represented an idealist tradition that linked religion to purpose, value, and meaning. They treated experience as pointing beyond isolated sensations toward a larger intelligible order.

Naturalist philosophers such as John Dewey saw the religious as a quality of experience and an attitude toward life rather than proof of supernatural reality. For Dewey, religious value could arise when ideals creatively transform ordinary life.

Personalist philosophers such as Edgar S. Brightman and Peter Bertocci treated personhood as the basic category for understanding reality. They interpreted religious experience as the medium through which God is apprehended as the cosmic person.

## Existential, Process, Empiricist, and Analytic Views

Existential thinkers such as Soren Kierkegaard, Gabriel Marcel, and Paul Tillich understood God as a power encountered in experience that overcomes estrangement and helps persons become integrated. This view emphasizes anxiety, decision, trust, and fulfillment.

Process philosophers such as Alfred North Whitehead and Charles Hartshorne held that the idea of God emerges in religious experience, but that God's nature and reality require logical and metaphysical interpretation. They stressed the relation between God and the world as a temporal process.

Logical empiricists such as A.J. Ayer denied literal cognitive meaning to religious and theological claims because they could not be verified or falsified by sense experience. Analytic philosophers influenced by Ludwig Wittgenstein instead studied the structure of religious language and asked how religious expressions function within forms of life.

## Conceptions of Experience

Classical empiricism traced ideas and beliefs back to sense impressions. On that view, religious ideas need grounding in sensory origin if they are to be justified. Critics argued that experience is richer than passive reception of sense data.

Immanuel Kant claimed that experience combines sensory material with understanding shaped by necessary categories. German idealists such as Fichte, Schelling, and [[Hegel and Hegelianism (Hegel)|Hegel]] then treated experience as the many-sided reflection of encounters with the world, other persons, and the self.

American pragmatists such as Charles Sanders Peirce, William James, and John Dewey understood experience as active, social, interpreted, and related to purposes and interests. Experience includes moral, aesthetic, political, and religious dimensions through which what is encountered becomes meaningful.

## Marks of Specifically Religious Experience

Specifically religious experience has been identified as awareness of the holy, awe, reverence, absolute dependence, unity with the divine, perception of unseen order, direct perception of God, encounter with a wholly other reality, or the sense of transforming power. Hebrew prophetic experience could also appear as judgment on humanity and separation from the holy.

Those who treat religion as a dimension of experience emphasize ultimate concern, total reaction to life, an overarching ideal, or a power that integrates personality. Such attitudes are called religious because they are directed toward an ultimate object that transcends ordinary human limits.

Religion and morality are closely related but not identical. Religion concerns what a person is and what is acknowledged as worshipful reality; morality concerns what a person does and the principles governing relations with others. Religion normally affects conduct, but it cannot be reduced to duty alone.

## Evaluation and Preparation

John Dewey described a religious quality in experience that points to God as an ideal in creative tension with actual events. Whitehead connected divine presence with a permanent rightness in things and argued that cosmology needs God as a principle selecting toward the good. James evaluated religious experience by its fruits in personal life.

Mystics, prophets, and religious thinkers stress preparation for insight. Ordinary habits of seeing can block religious truth, so traditions prescribe rational discipline, moral purification, and contemplative or ascetic practice. These disciplines train mind and body to receive or recognize what ordinary life hides.

## Key Events & Developments

- 1902: William James published The Varieties of Religious Experience ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Varieties_of_Religious_Experience)), making the term central in modern study.
- 1793: Immanuel Kant's work on religion encouraged debate over whether religion is essentially morality.
- The 20th century: Phenomenology, existentialism, pragmatism, analytic philosophy, and social science all reshaped the study of religious experience.

## Key People

- William James ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_James)): Interpreted religious experience through personal transformation and practical consequences.
- Rudolf Otto ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rudolf_Otto)): Emphasized the holy, awe, and nonrational elements of religion.
- John Dewey ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Dewey)): Treated the religious as a quality of experience rather than proof of supernatural reality.
- Paul Tillich ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_Tillich)): Interpreted religion through ultimate concern, estrangement, and integration.
- Alfred North Whitehead ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alfred_North_Whitehead)) and Charles Hartshorne ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_Hartshorne)): Developed process views of God and experience.

## Interesting Facts

- Religious experience ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mystical_or_religious_experience)) can mean either a special encounter or a religious interpretation of all experience.
- Logical positivists denied cognitive value to religious claims, while pragmatists judged them partly by their effects.
- Preparation for religious insight often combines rational discipline, moral purification, and contemplative practice.
