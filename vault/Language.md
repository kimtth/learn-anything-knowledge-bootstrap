## Introduction

Language ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Language)) is a system of conventional spoken or written symbols through which human beings, as members of social groups and cultures, express themselves. Its functions include communication, identity, play, imagination, ritual, persuasion, emotional release, and social cooperation.

Definitions of language differ. Henry Sweet stressed speech sounds joined into words and sentences to express ideas. Bernard Bloch and George L. Trager called language a system of arbitrary vocal symbols by which a social group cooperates. Both definitions raise questions about thought, arbitrariness, and social use.

Human language ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Language)) is normally acquired in childhood as a vocal communication system produced by organs in the throat and mouth. Different languages are systems that require learning for mutual understanding, while dialects are different speech forms that do not wholly block comprehension. An idiolect is one person's speech pattern.

The line between a language and a dialect is therefore practical rather than absolute. Mutual intelligibility lies on a scale, and social agreement helps decide where the boundary is drawn. Voices, accents, and personal habits can identify individuals without making their speech separate languages.

First language ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_language)) acquisition differs from later language learning. People usually acquire one mother tongue first, while second languages are learned with different degrees of success. Bilingualism means complete mastery of two languages, though many multilingual communities produce bilingual speakers naturally.

Child speech develops from exposure before formal teaching, whereas later learning begins after one linguistic system has already shaped expectations. This helps explain why first-language patterns can interfere with new grammar, pronunciation, and idiom even when learners are intelligent and motivated.

Species-specific language separates humans from animals because it has open-ended productivity. People can talk about anything, including things not present in time or space. Bee dances communicate food locations, and parrots can mimic speech, but neither equals human language's flexible creativity.

[[Linguistics]] studies language descriptively and historically. Its major areas include phonetics, phonology, grammar, syntax, morphology, semantics, pragmatics, dialects, language change, and the relation between language and society.

Language analysis must therefore treat each language as both a present working system and a historical product. A language carries inherited sound patterns, grammar, and meanings while also serving future change through new speakers, new uses, and new social needs.

Language functions also extend beyond neutral information transfer. Speech can cooperate, command, persuade, identify group membership, mark distance, create art, release emotion, and sustain ritual authority.

## Historical Attitudes Toward Language

![[attachments/language_003.png|420]]

*Language tree diagram for Altaic language groups*

Naming has long been treated as powerful. Many cultures connect names with control, possession, taboo, or ritual danger. Euphemisms for death, disease, obscenity, and blasphemy show that modern societies also avoid direct naming in sensitive areas.

Myths of language origin often give speech or writing a divine source. Biblical tradition describes Adam naming animals under God's guidance and explains linguistic diversity through the Tower of Babel. Norse, Indian, Greek, Arab, and other traditions also connect language or writing with gods or culture heroes.

These traditions also recognize the social power of speech. Naming can claim knowledge or authority, a shared language can enable collective action, and linguistic diversity can mark the boundary between neighbouring communities that otherwise live close together.

The origin of language cannot be reconstructed directly. Written records are only about 4,000 to 5,000 years old, while speech is probably as old as Homo sapiens. Theories deriving language from animal cries or emotional ejaculations were criticized as inadequate, and no existing language can be treated as humanity's original tongue.

Language and thought have been linked since ancient Indian, Greek, medieval, and rationalist theories. Earlier views often treated language as the expression of already formed thought. Later thinkers such as Etienne Bonnot de Condillac and Johann Gottfried von Herder made it more plausible that thought and speech developed together.

Linguistic relativity grows from this connection between language and thought. People share enough reality to translate, but languages divide and organize experience differently. Translation difficulties in science, law, morality, and social life show that languages are not just different labels for identical inventories.

## Phonetics And Phonology

[[Speech]] is the universal material of human language, even though language can also be written or signed. Phonetics studies the anatomy, physiology, neurology, and acoustics of speech. Articulatory phonetics concerns production, while acoustic phonetics concerns sound waves.

Phonology studies how each language organizes speech sounds. Languages use different subsets of possible human sounds, and sounds that are meaningful in one language may not be meaningful in another. English t sounds vary phonetically but count as one sound category for English speakers.

Tone and syllable structure show phonological difference. In northern Chinese, different tones on ma distinguish meanings such as mother, hemp, and curse. English allows complex consonant clusters such as strengths, while languages such as Japanese and Swahili use simpler consonant-vowel patterns.

## Grammar

Grammar concerns relations among words in sentences and the internal structure of words. Syntax deals with word relations in sentence structure; morphology deals with forms inside words. The distinction is useful but not always clear.

The word itself is a grammatical unit rather than a simple stretch of sound, since ordinary speech does not place pauses between every word. Speakers still recognize recurring units because they occupy patterned positions in sentences and carry contrasts such as singular and plural.

Grammatical categories such as plural, tense, case, and gender are expressed differently in different languages. Classical Chinese used little word-form variation, English uses some and relies heavily on word order, while Latin and Greek used more inflection. Grammatical form does not always match external meaning directly.

## Semantics

Semantics is the study of meaning. It includes meanings carried by intonation, grammar, and individual words. A language's lexicon is very large; English has hundreds of thousands of dictionary words and more when dialectal, technical, and global varieties are counted.

Lexical meaning is studied through relations such as synonymy, antonymy, semantic features, and semantic fields. No living language is primitive in the sense of having only a few hundred words. Semantics remains closely connected with grammar and phonology, and many questions remain debated.

## Dialects

Dialects are recognizable varieties within a speech community that do not prevent communication. Because mutual intelligibility forms a scale, the line between dialect and language is partly arbitrary. English has many dialects, while Welsh and English are separate languages even when spoken in nearby communities.

## Jargon

Jargon is specialized language used by groups, professions, games, religions, sciences, and trades. Criminal argots may conceal meaning, but professional jargons often increase precision and efficiency. Legal, medical, scientific, philosophical, and symbolic-logical language develop special vocabulary to avoid ambiguity.

Ritual language shows the old belief that exact wording has power. Magic, religious worship, Sanskrit ritual, the Authorized Version of the Bible, and The Book of Common Prayer all show communities preserving special forms because correctness itself is valued.

## Other Symbol Systems And Speech

Language ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Language)) is the most flexible symbol system, but cultures also use maps, blueprints, painting conventions, musical notation, dance notation, and computer programming languages. These systems are called languages metaphorically or by extension, yet none has the same productivity and precision as human language.

Speech primacy matters because spoken language is learned naturally before literacy and has shaped language history. Writing, machines, and programming extend language, but ordinary human languages are first social systems of speaking and hearing.

## Arbitrariness, Meaning, And Vocabulary Change

Arbitrariness means most words have no natural connection between sound and meaning. Onomatopoeic words such as cuckoo and symbolic sound patterns exist, but they form only a small part of vocabulary. Horse, Pferd, cheval, equus, and hippos name the same animal without sharing sound form.

Semantic flexibility lets language adapt. Words can gain new meanings, lose old meanings, or become obsolete. Technology after 1945 produced words and meanings such as transistor, cosmonaut, Internet, nuclear disarmament, computer, and jet.

Neologisms arise through invention, borrowing, compounding, metaphor, and changed use. Kodak was deliberately invented; chortle was a playful creation that entered common use. Vocabulary changes much faster than grammar or phonology.

## Style And Social Function

Style lets speakers express attitudes toward listeners and subjects, conceal feelings, persuade, threaten, plead, joke, flatter, or create social distance. Intonation, voice quality, grammar, and vocabulary all contribute, though exact phonetic analysis of these effects remains difficult.

Social language variation marks class, education, occupation, region, and aspiration. People may deliberately cultivate new dialect features for advancement, as in George Bernard Shaw's Pygmalion. Immigrants may adopt a new language for social membership, while later generations may seek ancestral languages again.

## Translation And Secrecy

Translation arises whenever speakers of mutually unintelligible languages need to communicate. It may be oral interpreting or written conversion, and it must balance literal word correspondence with a whole sentence or text that carries the original meaning within a different culture.

Translation problems arise because languages are embedded in cultures and do not match word for word. Law, science, social structure, religion, and emotion often require adjustment rather than simple replacement.

Secret communication restricts rather than extends access. Codes, ciphers, telephone scrambling, and cryptography protect diplomatic, military, and commercial messages. Code-breaking methods have also helped decipher unknown scripts, including Linear B as Mycenaean Greek.

## Language Learning, Bilingualism, And Literacy

![[attachments/language_002.webp|420]]

*Map of human language families*

First-language learning is normally unconscious and complete in basic structure by childhood. Second-language learning is different because it begins after the first language is already established. Motivation strongly affects success, as seen in differences between compulsory study and practical need.

Bilingualism can arise naturally in multilingual homes or communities, but many people never master another language fully. Knowing one language may actually make later learning harder because first-language patterns interfere with new structures.

Literacy is deliberately taught and consciously learned, unlike the spontaneous acquisition of speech. It is valuable but expensive in social resources and is not necessary for maintaining a language's grammar or vocabulary. Most human languages existed for most of history without writing.

Because literacy depends on teaching, print, trained readers, and a wide community of use, it is not equally practical for every language. Very small speech communities may maintain rich spoken structures even when large-scale schooling and publication are not economically possible.

## Spelling And Language Change

Spelling never represents speech perfectly. Letters and sounds differ, historical pronunciation changes while writing remains conservative, and printing preserves old spellings. English examples such as th, silent e, and ough show how spelling and pronunciation diverge.

Language change is cumulative across generations. Children's speech differs slightly from parents' speech, peer groups influence urban speech, and separated communities may diverge into distinct languages. Romance languages developed from spoken Latin in this way, and Old English became largely unintelligible to modern English speakers.

Language universals are general features found across languages, though their exact nature remains debated. Languages also differ structurally: analytic languages rely heavily on word order and particles, agglutinative languages string grammatical elements onto words, and other languages combine several functions in inflectional endings.

## Key Events & Developments

- 4,000-5,000 years ago: Written records began, giving the oldest linguistic evidence.
- 18th century: Condillac and Herder linked language development with thought.
- 19th century: F. Max Muller criticized simple origin theories such as animal-cry explanations.
- 1945 onward: Modern technology rapidly expanded vocabularies.
