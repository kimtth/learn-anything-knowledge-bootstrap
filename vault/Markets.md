# Markets

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> Markets are systems of exchange in which buyers and sellers meet directly or through intermediaries and prices adjust through communication and competition. From physical marketplaces to networks of brokers, retailers, and exchanges, they organize supply, demand, information, and allocation.

## Introduction

A market is a means of exchange in which buyers and sellers meet directly or through agents. In simple form it is a place of buying and selling, but in modern industry it can be a region where sellers compete and prices move together.

Alfred Marshall, whose Principles of Economics first appeared in 1890, built on A. Cournot. Buyers and sellers must be close enough in communication and competition for prices of the same goods to approach equality.

Cournot treated a Market ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Market_%28economics%29)) as any region where buyers and sellers are in free enough intercourse that prices of the same goods become equal easily and quickly. The definition shifts attention from a physical marketplace to a network of exchange and price adjustment.

Marshall added that the nearer a market is to perfection, the stronger the tendency for the same thing to have the same price at the same time across the market. The idea fits standardized goods such as wool or automobiles, but it also extends more loosely to real estate, art, and labour, where exchange differs from the sale of ordinary goods.

Most markets depend on supply and demand ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supply_and_demand)) working through intermediaries. Brokers, dealers, grocers, wholesalers, and exchanges move products from scattered producers toward scattered buyers, sometimes adding storage, grading, or packaging.

Two broad product-market types operate differently. In one, the producer accepts the price goods bring. In the other, the producer sets a price and sells as much as buyers will take. Trade also spread into securities exchanges, money markets, and other financial fields.

Modern theory connects strongly with [[Adam Smith]], who argued in the 18th century that the division of labour depends on market extent. Wider markets encouraged factories, power use, applied science, and modern industrial scale.

Late 19th-century theory focused on allocating existing resources. Leon Walras built a general equilibrium system, but its mechanical model ignored time, expectations, and unequal purchasing power. The belief that free markets naturally brought full employment remained influential even during the Great Depression.

The Keynesian Revolution challenged that view. Investment depends on expected future profit, so bad expectations can reduce investment, employment, income, purchases, and demand. High demand can create the opposite pressure, with labour scarcity raising wages, costs, and prices.

The theory of perfect competition was also weakened. Traditional theory imagined many small producers selling at a market price until marginal cost equaled price. Business practice showed that firms often work below capacity, add margins to direct costs, and sell actively to fill capacity.

Economic history shows that markets were not universal. Gift exchange encouraged specialization but aimed at honour and obligation, not gain. Tribute systems redistributed goods through political administration; Herodotus even said the Persians had no marketplaces.

Commerce differs because goods are offered to gain purchasing power. A money-commodity helps exchange and can become more than an ordinary useful object. Commercial markets arose from rural fairs, landlord rent systems that fed towns, and especially international trade by merchant adventurers such as the Phoenicians and Arabs.

International trade advanced the market system because merchants often stood outside local duties. Medieval just-price ideas restrained local dealings, but long-distance trade let commercial principles act more freely. English wool exports to Italian weavers helped weaken feudal relations.

The 19th century was the high point of the import-export merchant, who knew foreign needs and linked many small producers and customers. Later, giant corporations moved many merchant functions into sales departments, and large retailers dealt directly with manufacturers, although specialized exchanges stayed important for primary commodities.

Under Socialism ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Socialism)), markets were reshaped. Soviet industry was state-owned and centrally planned, wages were decreed, and materials moved through planning offices. Consumer goods passed through retail shops, but sellers acted as agents of the central plan.

Soviet collective farms used work points and politically set prices. Agricultural production did not grow fast enough for the industrial labour force, so prices were kept low for industry. Yet farm members could sell produce from household plots on a free market, where peasants and buyers acted as principals.

[[China]], after 1949, organized socialist trade through contracts between cities and farms that fixed prices, varieties, quantities, and delivery dates. City authorities directed goods to retail outlets and used demand to guide contracts with manufacturers.

Early Israeli kibbutzim showed that a small community with a shared ideal could reduce formal market relations. Members shared work proceeds without individual incomes, though prosperity later softened their austerity.

Commodity markets often involve scattered small producers, distant buyers, and powerful dealers. Short-run supply and demand are inelastic, while weather, pests, industrial activity, tastes, and technical needs cause shifts. Dealers may stabilize prices with stocks, but speculation can worsen fluctuations.

Because whole communities may depend on one primary commodity, governments often protect agriculture with regulations, price supports, or tariffs. Attempts to control world commodity markets have usually been weak, and developing countries often complain that the world market system favours industrialized nations.

## Key Events & Developments

![[attachments/markets_002.webp|420]]

*Stock market trading floor*

- The 18th century: [[Adam Smith]] linked market extent with the division of labour and industrial growth.
- 1890: Alfred Marshall used Cournot's definition to frame the modern regional meaning of market.
- The 1930s: The [[Great Depression]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Depression)) exposed weaknesses in the belief that markets naturally create full employment.
- After 1949: [[China]] used socialist contracts to connect farms, cities, retailers, and manufacturers.

## Key People

- A. Cournot: Defined a market as a region of free contact between buyers and sellers.
- Alfred Marshall ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alfred_Marshall)): Stressed the movement toward one price in a more perfect market.
- [[Adam Smith]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adam_Smith)): Connected market size with division of labour and industrial production.
- Leon Walras ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leon_Walras)): Built an abstract general equilibrium model.
- John Maynard Keynes ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Maynard_Keynes)): Challenged equilibrium thinking through demand, investment, and unemployment.

## Interesting Facts

- Herodotus said the Persians had no marketplaces, showing that complex economies could exist without commerce as the main organizing principle.
- A money-commodity can become the embodiment of value once it stores purchasing power.
- Early Israeli kibbutzim sometimes rejected private property in clothing.
- Commodity dealers can both stabilize and destabilize prices through storage and speculation.
