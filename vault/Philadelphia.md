## Introduction

![[attachments/philadelphia_001.webp|420]]

*Philadelphia skyline*

Philadelphia ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philadelphia)) is a city and port in southeastern Pennsylvania, coextensive with Philadelphia County and located at the confluence of the Delaware and Schuylkill rivers. It is Pennsylvania's largest city and a major U.S. metropolitan centre. Despite national and international stature, Philadelphia retains small-town qualities through trees, parks, quiet streets, Quaker heritage, and many reminders of the colonial and Revolutionary city.

Philadelphia's national significance rests on its role in the Declaration of Independence ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Declaration_of_Independence)), the Constitutional Convention of 1787, and service as the second U.S. national capital. Benjamin Franklin, a Philadelphian by adoption, left a deep institutional, cultural, and commercial imprint. The Port of Philadelphia and Camden is one of the world's largest freshwater ports and part of a major Delaware River shipping complex.

## Character and Economy

![[attachments/philadelphia_002.webp|420]]

*Independence Hall*

Philadelphia combines historic calm with modern industry, commerce, finance, research, education, and culture. Its industrial production continues a long role in the Industrial Revolution and American commerce. The city sits within the large urban corridor of the eastern seaboard, connecting American memory with contemporary economic life.

## Industry

Philadelphia's economy ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economy)) changed from 18th-century businesses and once-dominant textile mills to modern service, high-technology, printing, publishing, food processing, chemicals, machinery, fabricated metals, electronics, transportation equipment, scientific instruments, apparel, paper, rubber, plastics, and metals. World War II boosted industry, but manufacturing later declined, and employment shifted toward nonmanufacturing services.

The Philadelphia Naval Shipyard, opened after a 1799 act of Congress and formally begun in 1801, was a long-standing economic mainstay. Shipbuilding began earlier, with the Continental Congress commissioning five ships from Philadelphia during the Revolutionary War. During World War II the yard built about 50 ships and repaired hundreds more.

## Research and Food Distribution

Philadelphia has a strong tradition of research and invention. [[George Washington]] approved the first U.S. patent in 1790 for Philadelphian Samuel Hopkins. West Philadelphia's Science Center links academic and scientific institutions. The region is a major health education, biotechnology, pharmaceutical, helicopter, and computer research centre; ENIAC was launched at the University of Pennsylvania in 1945. The Food Distribution Center unites public and private food marketing, warehousing, and processing.

## Finance

Philadelphia was the financial capital of the United States until the 1850s, when New York surpassed it. It was the birthplace of American banking and the first building and loan association. The city developed commercial, maritime, stock, and exchange institutions, including the oldest U.S. stock exchange, founded in 1790, and it contains a U.S. Mint.

## Transportation

Philadelphia opened one of the first U.S. subway systems in 1907. SEPTA, created in 1963, coordinates regional transit, including subway, trolley, bus, and rail services. Bridges, ferries, the PATCO high-speed line, the Delaware River Port Authority, the Port of Philadelphia and Camden, and Philadelphia International Airport connect the city to New Jersey, the Delaware Valley, national freight systems, and global travel.

## Government

City-county consolidation in 1854 responded to inadequate public services, disorder, and outdated committee government. Corruption later plagued ward-based politics. Reform beginning in 1939, joined by business leaders in 1948, produced a new city charter that strengthened the mayor, professionalized civil service, and limited council's administrative role. Joseph S. Clark and Richardson Dilworth were early reform mayors under the charter.

## Education

Philadelphia has a major educational network. Public schools operate as an independent governmental unit, while private education includes Roman Catholic and Quaker schools. The University of Pennsylvania, founded in 1740, opened the first U.S. medical school in 1765 and became the first designated U.S. university in 1779. Temple, Drexel, LaSalle, St. Joseph's, Haverford, Swarthmore, Bryn Mawr, Villanova, Curtis Institute, and the Pennsylvania Academy of Fine Arts add depth.

## Cultural Life

Philadelphia's cultural identity rests on its small-town atmosphere, parks, squares, memorials, riverside, factories, business institutions, colonial memory, and diverse communities. It was central to centennial and bicentennial celebrations in 1876 and 1976. The city has important museums, libraries, music institutions, sacred architecture, social histories, and long traditions of working-class, ethnic, African American, Quaker, and elite communities.

## Founding and Early Settlement

William Penn acquired Pennsylvania from Charles II in 1681 as a haven for Quakers and a place for self-government and religious toleration. He named the city Philadelphia, or city of brotherly love, and wanted a green country town with houses on their own plots. By 1699, Philadelphia already had shops, trading houses, hundreds of dwellings, and about 10,000 people.

## Colonial and Revolutionary Growth

Penn's policies of religious toleration, popular participation, and prosperity attracted English, German, and Scotch-Irish settlers. By the 1770s, Philadelphia had at least 30,000 residents and ranked among the British Empire's most important business centres. Its wealth, institutions, and political leadership made it central to the American Revolution, independence, and constitution-making.
