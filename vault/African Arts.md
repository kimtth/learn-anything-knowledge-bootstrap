# African Arts

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> African arts encompass the diverse visual traditions of Africa, especially sub-Saharan sculpture, masks, textiles, pottery, and performance-related objects. Their meaning commonly joins artistic form with ritual, political authority, social institutions, and practical use.

## Introduction

African art ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/African_art)) refers to the visual arts of native [[Africa]], especially sub-Saharan Africa ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sub-Saharan_Africa)), and includes sculpture, painting, pottery, rock art, textiles, masks, personal decoration, and jewelry. These visual arts are considered apart from architecture, which is treated separately. It is difficult to summarize the main characteristics of sub-Saharan African art because the diversity of forms and practices is so great. Some works have entertainment value, some have political or ideological meaning, some serve ritual purposes, and some have aesthetic value in themselves. More often, a single work combines several of these roles. There are also both full-time and part-time artists, and artists may be central to political authority or socially marginal. Because of this variety, claims that there is one simple pan-African aesthetic are highly controversial.

A central point is that precolonial African traditions did not always separate art from craft in the same way that many modern Western traditions do. In many African languages, a concept of art as something distinct from skill would be unusual. This is not because African culture lacked artistic thought, but because the social and intellectual history that produced the modern Western separation of fine art from useful craft did not occur in the same way before colonial times. Therefore, precolonial African works must often be understood as artifacts made with technical mastery, ritual power, practical use, and aesthetic purpose all at once.

## Sculpture and Associated Arts

![[attachments/african_arts_001.webp|420]]

*Benin bronze plaques at the British Museum*

Although [[Forestry and Wood Production (Wood)|wood]] is the best-known medium of African sculpture, many other materials have also been used, including copper alloys, iron, ivory, pottery, unfired clay, and occasionally stone. Unfired clay was probably the most widely used sculptural medium on the continent, but because it is fragile and hard to preserve, it has often been neglected in scholarship. Fired clay figurines excavated at Daima near Lake Chad from levels dating to the 5th century BCE or earlier, and similar discoveries in Zimbabwe, suggest even earlier traditions of unfired clay modeling. Around the same time, farther west, the fully Iron Age Nok culture ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nok_culture)) was already producing large hollow sculptures in well-fired pottery.

Early and highly sophisticated work also appears in copper-alloy casting using the lost-wax technique. By the 9th century CE, smiths at Igbo Ukwu in what is now Nigeria were casting leaded bronze and working copper. A few centuries later, smiths of Ife produced masterpieces in apparently unalloyed copper, including celebrated royal and ritual images. Zinc brass was used more commonly, especially in Benin, where a large body of cast works survives. Together with pottery sculpture, these castings form the main archaeological evidence for the early history of sculpture in sub-Saharan [[Africa]]. Sculpture is therefore not one narrow genre but a wide field of technical and symbolic experimentation.

## West Africa

For analytical purposes, the visual arts of West Africa fall into three broad areas: the western Sudan, the Guinea Coast ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guinea_%28region%29)), and Nigeria. This division helps reveal both regional variety and broader continuities. The western Sudan is the savanna region of West Africa, dominated historically by Islamic states at the southern ends of the trans-Saharan trade routes. Sculpture there is often characterized by schematic styles of representation. Some observers have linked this to Islamic influence, but that is too simple, because [[Muhammad and the Religion of Islam (Islam)|Islam]] in West Africa sometimes tolerated such traditions and sometimes destroyed them while also affecting them in other ways.

In this region, peoples such as the Dogon, Bambara, Senufo, and others stand out. Their art often connects closely with ancestor worship, initiation, agriculture, and social order. The forms may look abstract or schematic, but they are deeply tied to myths, social institutions, and ritual obligations. In other words, style cannot be separated from function, and representation cannot be read only as visual appearance.

## Dogon and Tellem

The Dogon, who live along the Bandiagara escarpment in Mali, produce sculpture closely linked to beliefs about ancestors and mythic spirits called Nommo. Figures are made to house the spirits of deceased family members and are kept in shrines. Masks are used at the end of mourning periods to drive away the spirit of the deceased so it may pass properly to the realm of the ancestors. The Dogon developed about 80 mask types, used by the men's masking association called Awa. One type, the sirige, is marked by a tall flat projection above the face; the Great Mask, made anew every 60 years and never worn, represents a primordial ancestor in serpent form.

Other important masks include the kanaga, whose architectonic structure refers to a whole range of concepts, animals, and the authority of God, and the satimbe, surmounted by a powerful mythical woman. The Dogon sculptural system is not simply decorative but part of a rich cosmological and social order. Even formal features such as height, projection, and geometric arrangement carry religious and symbolic weight.

## Bambara (Bamana)

The Bambara of the region around Bamako, Mali, have artistic traditions associated with six male societies, each with its own mask type. The Ntomo society, for boys before circumcision, uses masks with vertical projections above the face signifying beliefs related to human creation. The Tyiwara society, which prepares young men for adult agricultural and family roles, uses headdresses in antelope form representing the mythical being that taught humans agriculture. The Komo, guardian of tradition and community life, uses elongated and very powerful masks decorated with real horns, quills, skulls, and other materials. The Kono, which enforces civic morality, uses similarly elongated forms covered with sacrificial material.

The Kore society once used masks representing animals such as hyena, lion, monkey, antelope, and horse, though it is now represented more through puppetry. Bambara ancestor figures belong to the same broad artistic tradition as many Dogon figures. Blacksmiths produce them, and because blacksmiths are believed to transform earth into powerful form, they are associated with nyama, a force or power that inheres in matter and action. Also notable is bokolanfini, a textile painted with earth-based designs, used in initiation, marriage, and burial and understood as carrying and absorbing spiritual power.

## Djenne-Mopti and Senufo

The towns of Djenne and Mopti in Mali are noted as centres of cloth trade and architecture, but the surrounding area is also known for many pottery sculptures of uncertain age. These works may be linked to the empires of Ghana and Mali. Despite extensive contact across the Sahara, these medieval empires did not fundamentally transform the underlying social structure of the western Sudan. Their art therefore reflects both long-distance contact and deep local continuity.

The Senufo of northern Côte d'Ivoire produce a rich range of sculpture associated mainly with Poro, a men's society guided by a female ancestral spirit called the Ancient Mother. During initiation, young men are instructed through sculptural figures. Some with massive bases are carried in procession and struck against the earth to summon ancestral spirits. Statues of the Ancient Mother are kept in sacred groves. Several mask types are notable, including kponyugu, whose composite animal imagery refers to creation, legend, and obligation to spirits, and kpelie, small refined human-faced masks linked to female spirits and to Poro knowledge.

Women have a parallel initiation society called Sandogo, whose shrines include small sculptures, python imagery, and divination objects. Spirits may instruct clients to commission brass jewelry and amulets to communicate with the unseen world and reinforce moral values. In Senufo art, sculpture, divination, initiation, and moral formation are inseparable.

## Guinea Coast

The Guinea Coast ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guinea_%28region%29)) is the forested region of West Africa where [[Muhammad and the Religion of Islam (Islam)|Islam]] was historically less dominant until relatively recent times. Political organization in the past was often on a smaller scale, sometimes governed by chiefs, sometimes by assemblies, and sometimes by secret associations that used masquerade to manifest power. Larger states developed especially toward the eastern end of the region, among peoples such as the Asante, the Fon, the Yoruba of Oyo, and the Edo of Benin. These states profited from trade with both the savanna and, after the late 15th century, with Europeans.

Compared with the western Sudan, sculpture of the Guinea Coast often shows a stronger tendency toward naturalistic representation. Yet naturalism is only one tendency among many. Even where human and animal forms are rendered more directly, they still operate within systems of ritual, kingship, spirit mediation, and public performance.

## Bidyogo, Baga, and Mende

![[attachments/african_arts_002.webp|420]]

*Mende Sande society mask*

The Bidyogo of the Bijagos Islands in Guinea-Bissau are known for impressive masquerade costumes and large headdresses representing animals such as bulls, swordfish, sharks, hippopotamuses, and crocodiles. They also carve hollow cylinders covered in red cloth to house guardian spirits called [[Iran|iran]]. These objects join sacred function and dramatic appearance. Modern carnival in Bissau also incorporates papier-mâché representations of modern ideas and objects, showing that visual traditions can absorb new influences.

The Baga carve sacred objects called tshol, with cylindrical bases and birdlike projections. Different tshol forms symbolize authority, elegance, fertility, water, and wealth. They also produce famous masquerade forms such as the nimba, a massive female mask with large cantilevered head, worn with the body hidden under raffia. Muslim missionary activity led to the suppression of some of these forms. The Mende of Sierra Leone are especially noted for black helmet masks of the women's Sande society, one of the rare women's masking institutions in [[Africa]]. These masks symbolize the transformation of girls into beautiful and socially powerful women. Men of the corresponding Poro society use different masks tied to male ritual and social order.

## Dan-We, Asante, Fante, and Baule

The Dan, De, and Diomande on one side and the We on the other form a stylistic complex marked by a range from smooth and restrained to grotesque and forceful carving. Portable miniature masks may protect owners away from home, and large anthropomorphic rice ladles designate the most hospitable woman during harvest festivals. Here again, art is tied to social reputation, ritual protection, and communal judgment.

In southern Ghana, Asante art is deeply connected with royal power. The Golden Stool, believed to house the spirit of the Asante people, stands at the centre of the system. Gold is a key material, appearing in swords, staffs, containers, jewelry, and the stool itself. Gold dust once served as currency, and small cast-brass weights used to measure it often represented proverbs and ideas in visual form. Asante weavers also created kente cloth, famed for technical mastery and complex design. Funerary vessels, portrait heads, and akuaba fertility figures also appear. Nearby, the Fante became known for distinctive flags inspired by colonial military banners, while the Baule developed standing figures and masks that differ from other Akan traditions.

## Fon

The Fon kingdom of Dahomey, with capital at Abomey, was founded in the early 17th century. Its artists were organized into guilds, much like artists among the Asante. They produced pavilions, umbrellas, banners with appliqué, metal images of deities, and powerful sculptural objects called bocio. Palace walls were decorated with painted clay reliefs celebrating royal achievements. Royal bocio combined human and animal features and served to protect and empower kingship.

A major example is the image of Gu, the god of iron and war, made from sheets of metal. The thrones of Fon kings resemble Asante stools but are taller and function as ancestral focuses after the king's death. Small brass figures decorated royal tombs, and brightly coloured appliqué cloth decorated state objects and later tourist goods. Fon art is strongly tied to kingship, power, protection, and political memory.

## Nigeria: Nok, Ife, Yoruba, and Ijo

Because of its especially long and well-documented artistic history, Nigeria is treated separately. The earliest known large sculpture in the Sudan is the fired clay work of the Nok culture ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nok_culture)), which flourished in northern Nigeria from the 5th century BCE into the early centuries CE. These people were also among the earliest known iron manufacturers in western [[Africa]]. Nok human figures are marked by cylindrical, spherical, or conical heads and by subtractive carving methods suggesting earlier wood-carving traditions.

The Yoruba of southwestern Nigeria have one of the most important sculptural traditions in [[Africa]]. Excavations at Ife show that highly naturalistic sculpture in brass and pottery was being made between 1100 and 1450 CE. These sculptures may represent rulers and attendants, and life-size portrait heads may have served in funerary contexts. Ife appears to have had wide influence, and the naturalism of its art helped shape later Yoruba style. Throughout Yorubaland, human figures are generally represented naturalistically, though with stylized bulging eyes, protruding lips, and distinctive ears. Many local styles can be distinguished within the broader Yoruba canon.

In the Niger delta, the Ijo use masks connected with water-spirit cults, often in forms taken from aquatic animals such as hippopotamus and crocodile. Western Ijo ejiri figures place schematic human forms on quadrupeds representing guardian spirits. Eastern Ijo shrine figures are often large but kept hidden, and some communities create rectangular ancestor screens recalling Benin compositional models. Ijo sculpture is strongly schematic and contrasts with the relative naturalism of neighbouring styles.

## Igbo, Ibibio, Ekoi, Hausa, and Nupe

Among the Igbo, who traditionally lived in decentralized forest settlements, artistic variety is especially rich because no single political system unified all communities. The extraordinary finds at Igbo Ukwu include graves and ritual stores dating to the 9th century CE that contained some of the earliest artistic castings known from sub-Saharan [[Africa]]. These ritual vessels and ceremonial objects display extremely intricate surface decoration. Igbo masking traditions are also highly diverse, used in social satire, sacred ritual, initiation, second burial, festivals, and modern public holidays. The towering ijele masks of northern Igbo communities, rising to about 12 feet, are especially striking and honour the dead while ensuring communal continuity.

The Ibibio are known for ancestor figures called ekpu, some thought to date to the late 18th century, and for the Ekpo society, whose masks maintain order and propitiate ancestors. The Ekoi create remarkable skin-covered masks with two or three faces and smaller headpieces with metal teeth and inlaid eyes. In northern Ekoi territory, carved stone circles called akwanshi also appear. Farther north, the Hausa are known less for sculpture and more for decorated architecture and technical crafts such as leatherwork, jewelry, ironwork, weaving, and embroidery. The Nupe, Muslim for centuries, are especially known for weaving, embroidery, beadmaking, carving, and sheet metalwork, but they also make masks for secular and protective performances.

## Central Africa

In the Cameroon grasslands, several stylistic zones can be identified. In the Bamileke area, figures are highly expressive combinations of rounded and angular forms. In the Bamum kingdom, roundness of form is pushed toward exaggeration, producing inflated cheeks and fuller volumes. Among the Tikar, Bekom, and Babanki, forms remain rounded but less exaggerated. Exchanges of art objects and metal-casting techniques across the region have made stylistic boundaries more difficult to define.

In Gabon, three broad groups are highlighted: the Fang, the Ogowe peoples, and the Kota. Fang art includes bieri, reliquary boxes containing ancestral bones and topped by figures representing protective ancestral force. Fang masks painted white with black outlines were used by troubadours, hunters, and those who punished sorcerers. The Ogowe, especially the Mpongwe, connect white-painted masks to death rituals and female ancestral spirits. The Kota create highly distinctive brass- or copper-covered reliquary figures called mbulu-ngulu, mounted over baskets containing ancestral remains.

## Congo Regions and Kuba

The art of Congo (Kinshasa) and Congo (Brazzaville) combines symbolism and realism. Human and animal forms are often recognizable but rendered in an exaggerated rather than strictly imitative way. Wood carving is the most common medium, but ivory, copper, brass, iron, and occasionally stone are also used. Painted surfaces are important, especially in masks and ritual contexts. Basketry, textiles, pottery, and architecture all interact with carving, and decorative forms often move from one medium into another.

Among the most highly developed traditions is that of the Kuba. Kuba cultural achievements are linked to remembered kings such as Mucu Mushanga and Shamba Bolongongo, who were credited with introducing major cultural practices. Kuba royal portrait figures established a strong tradition of court sculpture, usually showing kings facing front with crossed legs, a dagger, and identifying symbols of their reign. Alongside this court style, a more schematic popular style also developed in which geometric emphasis became stronger. Kuba masks made of raffia, shells, beads, feathers, and bells dramatized dynastic origins and matrilineal descent.

## Northeast, Northwest, and Sudan Regions

In the northeast of central [[Africa]], Mangbetu sculpture often reflects the elongated skull forms produced by head binding, while Zande sculpture appears closely related to Mangbetu traditions. In the northwest, the Ngbaka and Ngbandi produce figures, hunting fetishes, circumcision masks, and funeral clay images, though style is not uniform. In [[Sudan]] and South Sudan, where cattle are central to life among peoples such as the Nuer and Dinka, artistic feeling often appears not in elaborate carving but in the value and beauty attached to oxen themselves, almost as if the prize ox were sculpture.

Grave figures, divinatory images, life-size royal representations, decorated houses, mural painting, and fine pottery nonetheless appear, including the wall painting of peoples such as the Burun and the pottery and body painting of the Nuba. Here, visual culture often lies as much in architecture, ornament, weapon design, and the decorated body as in carved statues.

## Horn of Africa, Coastal East Africa, and Makonde

In the Horn of Africa, the Amhara of Ethiopia inherited a Christian artistic tradition rooted in Coptic and Byzantine forms, while the Somali developed rich decorative traditions in an Islamic framework. Southern Ethiopian peoples such as the Konso carve wooden tomb posts surmounted by carved heads and shoulders for nobles and warriors. On the Swahili coast, long trade with [[Arabia]] and [[India]] influenced decorative arts such as carved wooden doors, silversmithing, and plaited polychrome mats.

Farther inland, Arab influence is less direct. Peoples such as the Kamba developed carving styles that later became widely sold in commercial settings, while clay figures, stools, shields, gourds, and body ornaments remained important in local ritual and social life. The Makonde, living across the Tanzania-Mozambique border, are especially important as prolific woodcarvers. Their masks may cover only the face or the whole head, and they also developed new spirit-figure carving styles in ebony. In nearby regions such as Zambia, Malawi, and Zimbabwe, carved bowls, stools, headrests, musical instruments, pipes, snuff bottles, and intricate metal wire ornaments show that the visual arts extend well beyond monumental sculpture.

## Pottery

Pottery ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pottery)) is one of the most widespread arts of sub-Saharan [[Africa]]. Many peoples make pottery themselves, though urban life and industrial wares have reduced some traditions. Preindustrial pottery generally used coarse clay formed by hand, often with ring building or hammer-and-anvil techniques, and fired in open bonfires at low temperatures. The variety of form and design is almost endless. Pottery also served as the basis for certain sculptural forms, including grave memorials and major ancient sculptural traditions such as those of Nok, Ife, Djenne, and Mopti. In most modern settings, pottery making is primarily women's work.

## Textiles

Traditional cloth in both East and West Africa was usually woven from locally grown and hand-spun cotton, though factory-spun cotton later became common in parts of West Africa. In East Africa, weaving traditions largely declined under competition from ready-made fabrics. Wool was woven in rural Amazigh areas of [[North Africa]] and by Fulani weavers near the inland Niger delta. Silk was also used in West Africa, sometimes from wild local silk and sometimes from imported material. Raffia-palm fibres were woven into cloth especially in central [[Africa]].

Across most of the continent, men were the principal weavers, though in some areas women also wove, often on different types of looms. Textiles could be designed by color arrangement in weaving itself or later by dyeing, embroidery, appliqué, and beadwork. The Asante and Ewe are especially accomplished in woven design, with Fulani and middle Niger weavers close behind. Other traditions include bead-stitched cloth, leatherwork, bark cloth, matting, and basketry, all of which broaden the idea of textile art beyond woven cotton alone.

## Personal Decoration

The adornment of the human body involves nearly all the arts practiced in [[Africa]]. The body may be altered permanently through scarification, which can indicate lineage, life stage, or aesthetic preference. Examples include Yoruba lineage marks, scar patterns among Nuba women indicating physiological and social stages, and decorative scarification among the Tiv. The body may also be changed through clothing, hairstyles, jewelry, paint, and semipermanent effects that become socially necessary in public appearance.

This reinforces a central claim: in many African traditions, art is not confined to isolated objects. It exists in the body, the house, the shrine, the field, the public performance, and the political court. A mask is not just a carved form, and a textile is not just fabric. Both become part of larger systems of value, power, identity, ritual, memory, and beauty.

## Key Events & Developments

- 5th century BCE: Early clay sculpture appears at Daima and related sites.
- 9th century CE: Igbo Ukwu shows major advances in lost-wax casting.
- 1100 to 1450 CE: Ife becomes a major centre of naturalistic brass and pottery sculpture.
- 17th century: Asante and Fon court arts develop around royal power and gold.
- Late 15th century onward: Coastal trade and European contact reshape many Guinea Coast traditions.
- 20th century: Urban, tourist, and commercial markets expand alongside traditional ritual arts.

## Key People

- Osei Tutu ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Osei_Kofi_Tutu_I)): First Asantehene linked to the Golden Stool tradition.
- Shamba Bolongongo: Kuba ruler associated with royal portrait sculpture and cultural reform.
- Mucu Mushanga: Kuba king credited in tradition with major cultural innovations.
- Gu: Fon god of iron and war central to royal metal sculpture.
- Amma: Dogon creator figure associated with ancestral cosmology.

## Interesting Facts

- African art often combines ritual, political, and aesthetic functions in a single object.
- Pottery ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pottery)) remains one of the most widespread art forms in sub-Saharan [[Africa]].
