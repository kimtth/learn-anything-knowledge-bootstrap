## Introduction

![[attachments/virgil_001.webp|420]]

*Mosaic portrait of Virgil*

Virgil, also spelled Vergil, was the Roman poet Publius Vergilius Maro. He died on September 21, 19 BC, at Brundisium. His greatest work is the Aeneid ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aeneid)), begun about 30 BC and left unfinished at his death. Romans regarded him as their greatest poet, and later generations kept that judgment.

His fame rests mainly on the Aeneid ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aeneid)), which tells the story of Rome's legendary founder and presents Rome's mission as divinely guided. His poetry endures because of its musical language, careful structure, and attention to duty, suffering, loss, and public responsibility.

Virgil was born from peasant stock, and the Italian countryside and rural workers shaped all his poetry. He studied at Cremona, [[Milan]], and [[Rome]], learning Greek and Roman literature, rhetoric, and philosophy. His teacher Siro influenced his early Epicurean interests, though his later work moved closer to Stoicism.

The political background of his life was civil war. As the Roman Republic collapsed, conflict moved from Marius and Sulla to Pompey and Julius Caesar, and then to the wars that ended with [[Augustus]]' victory at Actium in 31 BC. Fear of civil war is central to Virgil and his contemporary Horace.

Virgil lived quietly and gave himself to poetry and study. Poor health, shyness, and a retiring nature kept him out of public affairs. As his poetry became famous, he gained powerful Roman friends and became both a provincial from Cisalpine Gaul and a Roman poet of the capital.

## Literary Career

![[attachments/virgil_002.webp|420]]

*Aeneid manuscript*

![[attachments/virgil_003.webp|420]]

*Painting of Virgil reading the Aeneid*

Some early poems attributed to Virgil survive in the Appendix Vergiliana, but few are likely genuine. His first certain work is the Eclogues, ten pastoral poems composed between 42 and 37 BC. They use the Greek model of Theocritus to create an idealized shepherd world of song, love, and grief.

The Eclogues also connect pastoral poetry with real political pain. The first and ninth poems lament farmers driven from their land, probably reflecting the post-Philippi settlement of veterans after 42 BC. The fifth is linked with the death of Julius Caesar, and the tenth brings the poet-statesman Gallus into a pastoral setting. The fourth eclogue, later read by Christians as messianic, prophesies the birth of a child and a restored Golden Age of peace.

The Georgics, written between 37 and 30 BC, respond to the ruin of rural Italy during civil war. They are didactic poems about plowing, trees, cattle, and bees, but they were meant to delight readers, not simply instruct farmers. Dedicated to Maecenas, they connect Italian agriculture with Augustan efforts to restore land and social order.

After Octavian, later [[Augustus]], defeated Antony and Cleopatra at Actium in 31 BC, Virgil began the Aeneid ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aeneid)). The poem uses the Trojan hero Aeneas to link mythic origins with Augustan Rome. It borrows from [[Homer]]'s Iliad and Odyssey ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Odyssey)), but it also uses prophecies, visions, and symbolic scenes to foreshadow Roman history and present Aeneas as a model of Roman duty.

The Aeneid ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aeneid)) praises Rome's destiny, but it is not simple propaganda. Jupiter's prophecy and Anchises' underworld vision present Rome as appointed to rule, spare the conquered, and defeat the proud. Yet the poem also gives sympathy to people harmed by Rome's mission, especially Dido of Carthage and Turnus. This tension between public greatness and private suffering gives the epic much of its power.

Virgil worked on the Aeneid ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aeneid)) for 11 years. In 19 BC, he traveled toward [[Greece]] to revise parts set in Greek waters, became ill, returned to Italy, and died at Brundisium. Tradition says he wanted the unfinished poem burned, but [[Augustus]] prevented this. The surviving epic became both a monument to Augustan Rome and the work of a poet who understood “the tears in things” as well as imperial glory.

## Influence and Reputation

Virgil's poetry became famous immediately in [[Rome]]. Romans admired him as a national poet and as a master of structure, diction, and metre. His poems soon became school texts, and Quintilian recommended that education be based on Virgil's works.

Later writers treated Virgil as a source of wisdom as well as poetry. Ovid imitated and echoed him, and his influence continued through the Silver Age. By the 4th century AD, scholars such as Macrobius and Servius read his poems as stores of knowledge. Allegorical readings grew, especially in the Christian Middle Ages.

Christian readers valued the fourth Eclogue as a supposed prophecy of Christ and found near-Christian moral values in the Aeneid ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aeneid)). This tradition reached its most famous form in [[Dante]]'s Divine Comedy, where Virgil guides the poet through Hell and Purgatory to the gates of Paradise.

Virgil's influence on [[English Literature|English literature]] was enormous. Edmund Spenser drew inspiration from him, and John Milton's Paradise Lost ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paradise_Lost)) used the Aeneid as an epic model. John Dryden and the English Augustans praised his form and moral content. Victorians such as Matthew Arnold and Alfred, Lord Tennyson later valued his sensitivity and pathos.

## Key Events & Developments

- 42-37 BC: Virgil composed the Eclogues.
- 42 BC: Veteran settlements after Philippi shaped poems about dispossessed farmers.
- 37-30 BC: Virgil wrote the Georgics.
- 31 BC: [[Augustus]]' victory at Actium ended the civil wars and shaped the Augustan world.
- c. 30 BC: Virgil began the Aeneid ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aeneid)).
- 19 BC: Virgil died at Brundisium before finishing final revisions of the Aeneid.

## Key People

- Virgil ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Virgil)): Roman poet of the Eclogues, Georgics, and Aeneid, later regarded as Rome's greatest poet.
- [[Augustus]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Augustus)): Roman ruler whose peace and national program shaped Virgil's mature poetry.
- Maecenas ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maecenas)): Augustan minister and arts patron to whom the Georgics were dedicated.
- Aeneas ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aeneas)): Trojan hero of the Aeneid and model of Roman duty and destiny.
- Dido ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dido)): Queen of Carthage, whose suffering complicates the epic's praise of Rome.
- [[Dante]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dante_Alighieri)): Medieval poet who made Virgil his guide in the Divine Comedy.

## Interesting Facts

- Virgil came from rural northern Italy, and country life colours all his poetry.
- The fourth Eclogue was later interpreted by Christians as a prophecy of Christ.
- The Georgics teach farming topics but were written to delight readers with poetry.
- The Aeneid ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aeneid)) works on two time scales: heroic Trojan myth and Augustan Roman history.
- Tradition says Virgil wanted the unfinished Aeneid burned, but [[Augustus]] saved it.
- [[Dante]] placed Virgil in a position of honour as guide through Hell and Purgatory.
