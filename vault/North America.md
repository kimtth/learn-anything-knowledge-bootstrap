# North America

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> North America is a continent of striking physical contrasts, from Arctic coasts and high mountains to interior plains and below-sea-level deserts. Its geologic history, shields, basins, cordilleras, drainage, and varying cultural definitions explain why continental boundaries and regional identities are understood in several ways.

## Introduction

![[attachments/north_america_002.webp|420]]

![[attachments/north_america_001.webp|420]]

*Satellite view of North America*

North America ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_America)) is the world's third largest continent, lying mostly between the Arctic Circle and the Tropic of Cancer. It stretches more than 5,000 miles north-south and east-west and covers about 9,355,000 square miles. It includes mainland North America, Greenland, the Arctic Archipelago, the West Indies, Haida Gwaii, and the Aleutian Islands. It is bounded by the Arctic, North Atlantic, Caribbean, and North Pacific waters and connects to South America at the Isthmus of Panama.

The continent's physical contrasts are large. Denali in Alaska is the highest point at 20,320 feet, while Death Valley is 282 feet below sea level. The coastline is about 37,000 miles long and deeply indented, especially in the north. The name America comes from Amerigo Vespucci. Physical, cultural, and regional definitions differ: Mexico and Central America may be grouped as Middle America or Latin America, while the United States and Canada are often called Anglo-America.

## Death Valley

Death Valley ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Death_Valley)) in California marks North America's lowest land elevation and illustrates the continent's extremes of relief, climate, and aridity. Its contrast with Denali, the Arctic Archipelago, Greenland, the Great Lakes, and the tropical lowlands shows why North America cannot be understood as one uniform physical region.

## Geologic History

North America contains some of Earth's oldest rocks. Its geology reflects repeated continental breakup, seafloor spreading, subduction, collision, mountain building, erosion, and glaciation. Lithospheric plates move from oceanic ridges to subduction zones, where slabs sink into the asthenosphere. These processes created the Canadian Shield, interior basins, Appalachians, Cordilleras, Arctic structures, Atlantic margins, and Pacific mountain belts.

## The Canadian Shield

The Canadian Shield ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canadian_Shield)) is the main surface exposure of Precambrian rocks older than about 540 million years. It was rifted from Greenland by spreading in the Labrador Sea and Baffin Bay between about 90 and 40 million years ago, after which spreading shifted east of Greenland. The shield also served as a centre for repeated Pleistocene ice-sheet growth.

The shield is an aggregate of ancient cratons, including Slave, Nain, Superior, and the Churchill province, fused about 2.0 to 1.8 billion years ago. Its granitic intrusions, greenstone belts, and deformed volcanic and sedimentary rocks record crustal thickening and plate convergence. Greenstone belts contain gold, silver, copper, zinc, and lead.

These ancient structures also explain why northern North America combines mineral wealth with thin soils and countless lakes. Ice stripped much of the shield to bedrock, but the same resistant rocks preserve iron, nickel, copper, gold, uranium, and other ores. The southern shield and adjoining lowlands benefited from glacial drift, while the far north gained more fresh water than farmland.

## Interior Platform and Basins

Beyond the shield, sedimentary cover lies on Precambrian crust. It is thin across broad platforms but thickens in basins such as Hudson Bay, Michigan, Williston, and Illinois and in troughs near mountain belts. These basins preserve long histories of seas, erosion, sedimentation, and later resource formation.

## Precambrian Bedrock and Continental Breakup

After early continental assembly, continental breakup began about 2.45 billion years ago along the Superior province, producing dikes, rift valleys, lavas, and new continental fragments. The later Atlantic separation moved northward: Grand Banks, Labrador Sea, Baffin Bay, Greenland, and Arctic Alaska all record rifting, rotation, folding, faulting, volcanism, and changing plate boundaries.

## Atlantic and Arctic Plate Movements

Atlantic spreading opened parts of the northern ocean between about 120 and 70 million years ago. Greenland rotated counterclockwise as Labrador Sea and Baffin Bay opened, causing Eurekan deformation in Arctic Canada. Arctic Alaska separated from Canada and helped create the Amerasia basin. Meanwhile, western North America continued overriding Pacific plates, producing accretion, volcanic arcs, and Cordilleran mountain building.

The continent therefore records two different margins at once. The Atlantic side became a broad passive margin with coastal plains, continental shelves, and older eroded mountains, while the Pacific side remained tectonically active, adding terranes, volcanoes, earthquakes, high relief, and complex island arcs from Central America through Alaska.

## Glaciation and the Younger Dryas

Repeated Pleistocene glaciations reshaped the continent, scouring the Canadian Shield and depositing drift southward. The last ice retreat occurred from about 13,000 to 6,000 years ago but was interrupted by the Younger Dryas between about 11,000 and 10,000 years ago. Glacial meltwater may have shifted from the Mississippi system to the St. Lawrence, disrupting North Atlantic circulation. The Little Ice Age from about 1450 to 1850 remains less well explained.

## Relief

North America's relief is dominated by the central shield, marginal mountains, interior lowlands, and western Cordilleras. The shield is a low plateau tilted at its margins and depressed around Hudson Bay. Its old mountains, domes, basins, eskers, drumlins, ice-plucked hollows, countless lakes, and raised beaches preserve glacial erosion and postglacial rebound.

## The Appalachians

The Appalachians are ancient marginal mountains deeply lowered by erosion. Glaciation affected northern sections, deepened valleys, and left offshore banks and islands. South of the Susquehanna, unglaciated ridges, valleys, flat-topped summits, the Blue Ridge, Piedmont, and fall line shape drainage and settlement. The Hudson-Mohawk gap provides a natural route into the continental interior.

## The Cordilleras

![[attachments/north_america_003.webp|420]]

*Rocky Mountains landscape*

The Cordilleras occupy about one-third of North America and dominate Alaska, western Canada, the western United States, and Central America. They include the Rocky Mountains, Rocky Mountain Trench, interior uplands, Coast Mountains, Inside Passage, and outer island arcs. In the United States, the Rockies, intermontane plateaus, Columbia-Snake lava fields, Great Basin, and Pacific ranges record folding, faulting, volcanism, uplift, and glaciation.

## Drainage

North America's drainage reflects climate and topography. The Great Lakes-St. Lawrence system has immense water capacity, while the Mississippi-Missouri-Ohio system drains the heartland. The Mackenzie, Nelson, Saskatchewan, Red River, Colorado, Columbia, Fraser, and Yukon systems connect shield, plains, mountains, and oceans. The main continental divide lies far west, so the longest rivers flow eastward.

This east-west asymmetry made eastern gulfs and lowland corridors especially important. Hudson Bay, the Gulf of St. Lawrence, and the Gulf of Mexico receive many of the great river systems, while western rivers are shorter, steeper, and valuable for irrigation, hydropower, and arid-region settlement. Drainage therefore joined physical geography to transportation and economic development.

## The Great Lakes

![[attachments/north_america_004.webp|420]]

*Great Lakes satellite image*

The Great Lakes formed from glacially deepened basins, older synclines, blocked outlets, and changing postglacial drainage. Early Great Lakes drained south while ice blocked the St. Lawrence outlet; later they shifted to the lower St. Lawrence route. Raised beaches and strandlines around the Great Lakes, Lake Champlain, Lake Winnipeg, Athabasca, Great Slave, and Great Bear lakes record former larger glacial lakes.

## Rivers

North American river regimes vary strongly. Northward-flowing rivers freeze and flood when upper courses thaw before lower ones. The St. Lawrence and Mississippi run high in spring and early summer because snowmelt joins rainfall. Southwestern rivers flood mainly in winter and shrink in summer, while northern Pacific rivers receive year-round precipitation with winter maxima. Tropical southern rivers are more regular except in rain shadows.

## Soils

North America contains all 12 major soil orders. Soil taxonomy now classifies soils by horizons, temperature, colour, drainage, chemistry, and mineralogy rather than climate alone. Broadly, soils reflect forest, grassland, desert, tundra, volcanic ash, youth, wetness, and glacial or alluvial histories.

## Forest Soils

Spodosols form in cool sandy coniferous regions and are acidic and leached. Andisols form in volcanic ash along the Cascades and are fertile but erodible. Alfisols are moderately fertile soils of the Laurentian mixed forest, supporting dairy and Ohio Valley agriculture. Ultisols are older red southeastern soils needing fertilization. Oxisols in tropical Mexico and Central America are deeply weathered, acidic, and often poor after cultivation.

## Grassland, Desert, Tundra, and Young Soils

Mollisols formed under grasslands and support the Wheat Belt, Palouse, and Corn Belt. Vertisols crack deeply in dry seasons and can be productive when irrigated. Aridisols are salty, lime-rich desert soils. Gelisols contain permafrost. Entisols, Inceptisols, and Histosols are young, weakly developed, or waterlogged organic soils found across many environments.

## Climate

North America's [[Climate and Weather (Climate)|climate]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Climate)) is shaped by great width in higher latitudes, southern tapering, the jet stream, mountain barriers, maritime influences, and continental air masses. Much of the continent is temperate, but Greenland, the Canadian Shield, Mackenzie lowlands, and northern Cordilleras have long cold winters and permafrost. The Pacific coast is warmed by ocean currents, while the east is cooled by Greenland and Arctic influences.

Air masses sharpen these contrasts. Arctic and polar continental air can push cold far south across the open interior, while maritime Pacific air brings moisture to windward slopes but loses much of it beyond the Cordilleras. Maritime tropical air from the Gulf of Mexico feeds summer humidity, storms, and river floods in central and eastern regions.

## Temperature and Precipitation

Temperature patterns show strong maritime-continental contrasts. Warmth extends north along the Pacific coast but is blocked inland by the Cordilleras; cold extends south through the Mackenzie plains and shield. Precipitation is heavy on Pacific windward slopes, adequate across much central and eastern North America, very low in Arctic and Greenland regions, and low in southwestern rain shadows and high-pressure zones.

## Forest and Scrub Communities

The Laurentian mixed forest contains maple, beech, oak, hickory, elm, ash, birch, pine, and hemlock. The Eastern Upland or Acadian forest contains spruce, fir, aspen, alder, and birch and has suffered acid-rain damage. The Eastern mesophytic forest mixes hardwoods and softwoods, while southeastern pineries supply timber and pulpwood. California and the Southwest support fire-adapted sclerophyllous scrub.

## Tropical Rainforest, Deserts, and Tundra

Tropical rainforests cover windward slopes in southern Mexico and Central America, with mahogany, palms, lianas, epiphytes, monkeys, parrots, reptiles, insects, and disease environments. Deserts in the western United States and northern Mexico contain sagebrush, creosote bush, mesquite, yucca, cacti, saguaro, Joshua tree, reptiles, and nocturnal animals. Arctic tundra supports sedges, mosses, lichens, cotton grass, caribou, polar bears, insects, and many summer birds.

## Human Imprint on the Landscape

Before Europeans, human impact was relatively limited except for prairie burning by Native Americans. European settlers cleared forests, killed wildlife, extracted coal, oil, iron, metals, and timber, and polluted air, land, and water through agriculture, industry, and urbanization. The idea of limitless resources shaped environmental damage across the continent.

## The People

North America remained relatively lightly populated and economically undeveloped until after 1500, when migrants from Europe and Africa transformed it. Indigenous histories are complex and regionally varied, but the nonindigenous history includes settlement, immigration, slavery, industrialization, urbanization, and modern continental integration.

## Population and Cities

North American population is concentrated unevenly, with dense belts around coasts, river corridors, the Great Lakes-St. Lawrence system, the northeastern United States, central Mexico, California, and major metropolitan regions. Settlement followed ports, waterways, rail lines, mineral districts, farmland, and later highways and airports. This created a continent of contrasts: sparse Arctic and desert regions, intensive agricultural plains, old industrial belts, rapidly growing Sun Belt and Pacific cities, and borderlands tied to both national and continental economies.

Urbanization turned North America into a network of metropolitan regions. Cities such as [[New York City]], [[Chicago]], [[Los Angeles]], [[Mexico City]], [[Toronto]], [[Montreal]], Vancouver, and Houston organize finance, manufacturing, transport, culture, migration, and political power. Their growth shows how continental geography became social geography: resources and farms feed cities, cities direct capital and technology, and transportation binds distant regions into shared markets.

## The North American Indian Heritage

The first Indigenous peoples probably came from northeastern Siberia through or near Beringia, though exact timing remains debated. Some dates are earlier, but at least by 20,000 years ago people may have entered during glacial recession. They dispersed widely, hunted and foraged, and developed diverse cultures. Mesoamerica later produced cities, agriculture, science, and architecture, while northern societies remained highly varied in language, economy, and social form.

## Resources, Agriculture, and Hydroelectricity

North America has vast resources: farmland, forests, coal, oil, gas, metals, water, fisheries, and hydroelectric potential. Agriculture ranges from grain belts and corn belts to ranching, irrigated agriculture, cotton, fruit, and tropical crops. Hydroelectricity is important in Canada, the Pacific Northwest, and other mountain or river systems, though it accounts for only a modest share of the continent's total energy budget.

Resource geography differs sharply by region. Shield ores support mining in Canada and the northern United States; coastal plains and interior basins contain fossil fuels; tropical and subtropical zones specialize in crops such as sugarcane, coffee, bananas, citrus, cotton, and winter vegetables; and prairie mollisols make the Wheat Belt, Corn Belt, and Palouse among the world's productive agricultural districts.

## Industry, Trade, and Transportation

North American industry developed in major belts and cities such as Pittsburgh-Detroit-Chicago, San Francisco-Los Angeles, and other manufacturing regions. Space-age industries, finance, services, and high technology reshaped economic geography. Trade moves through waterways, railways, roads, pipelines, air routes, and ports. The Great Lakes-St. Lawrence, Mississippi, coastal ports, interstate highways, and continental rail networks bind the region together.

The continent's cultural geography reflects Indigenous foundations, European colonization, African enslavement, immigration, language regions, and modern urban growth. Anglo-America, Latin America, the Caribbean, Arctic Indigenous regions, and borderlands overlap rather than form clean divisions. Cities, farms, reserves, suburbs, industrial corridors, mining zones, and tourist regions all show different layers of settlement and economic change.

Environmental questions remain central. Resource use brought wealth through forests, soils, minerals, fossil fuels, water, and fisheries, but also erosion, species loss, air pollution, water contamination, dammed rivers, urban sprawl, and climate-related stress. Conservation, national parks, Indigenous land claims, environmental law, and cross-border water agreements show attempts to manage a heavily transformed continent.

North America's continental scale also makes transportation decisive. Railways, highways, pipelines, inland waterways, air routes, and seaports connect Arctic resource zones, prairie farms, manufacturing belts, Pacific trade, Atlantic ports, Gulf energy centres, and Caribbean routes into one interdependent economic space.

Waterways opened the first interior corridors, but railways, roads, and air routes reorganized distance. The Erie Canal, Great Lakes-St. Lawrence route, Mississippi-Ohio system, transcontinental railways, interstate highways, Trans-Canada Highway, Pan-American Highway, and major airports shifted trade toward metropolitan hubs while extending settlement and industry across plains, mountains, and coasts.

North American trade also shows continental contrasts. Canada has relied heavily on exports of raw and semiprocessed resources, the United States has combined a huge internal market with major foreign trade, and Mexico and Central America have mixed subsistence regions with exports of oil, metals, and tropical crops. Those patterns tie resource location to population density and industrial demand.

## Key Events & Developments

- 2.6 billion years ago: Major shield crustal fragments were already forming.
- 2.0-1.8 billion years ago: Shield cratons fused.
- 120-70 million years ago: Northern Atlantic separation advanced.
- 13,000-6,000 years ago: Last glacial recession shaped modern landscapes.
- 1450-1850: The Little Ice Age cooled parts of the continent.
- 1500 onward: European and African arrivals transformed population and economy.
