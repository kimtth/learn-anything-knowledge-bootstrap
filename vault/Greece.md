## Introduction

Greece ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greece)) is a country shaped by its geography, its islands, and its long history. The sea and mountains have always influenced settlement, travel, and political development. Greece lies at the meeting point of Europe, Asia, and Africa, and it is heir to the legacies of ancient Greek civilization, the Byzantine Empire, and the Ottoman period. This combination of physical diversity and historical depth helps explain why Greece has long been both a crossroads and a distinct cultural world.

The physical landscape of Greece is strikingly varied. The country’s soils, rivers, and climate. Greece has a Mediterranean climate with regional variation, and its natural vegetation ranges from forests and scrubland to dry uplands and coastal plains. Most rivers are short, erratic, and unsuitable for navigation, and in limestone districts they vanish into sinkholes known as katavothra. Arable land ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arable_land)) is limited, making valleys, coastal plains, and irrigated areas especially important. Olives, grapes, wheat, vegetables, sheep, goats, and fishing have long reflected the interaction between Mediterranean climate and rugged terrain.

The Aegean and Ionian seas are not peripheral in Greek geography. They link islands, ports, trade routes, migration, tourism, and naval history. The sea helped compensate for difficult land communications and made maritime life central to Greek identity.

Relief and geology divide Greece into six major regions: central, northeastern, eastern, southern, and western mainland Greece, together with the islands. The central core is the Pindos (Pindus) Mountains, which sweep southeast from the Albanian and Macedonian frontiers and form a powerful barrier; their highest point, Mount Smolikas, reaches 8,652 feet (2,637 metres). The passes of Metsovon and Mount Timfristos divide the range into northern, central, and almost uncrossable southern units.

Northeastern Greece, the regions of Makedonia and Thraki, is drained by the five great rivers of the north: the Maritsa (Evros), Nestos, Strymonas (Struma), Vardarais (Vardar or Axios), and Aliakmonos. On the easternmost prong of the three-pronged Chalkidiki Peninsula rises Mount Athos, the Holy Mountain and famous site of Greek Orthodox monastic communities. In eastern Greece stands Mount Olympos (Olympus), the mythical seat of the gods and, at 9,570 feet (2,917 metres), the highest point in the country, while nearby Mount Parnassos (8,061 feet) was held to be the home of the Muses, and the pass of Thermopyles recalls the famous battle of antiquity.

Southern Greece forms the Peloponnisos, also called the Morea, which became technically an island once the 3.9-mile (6.3-km) Korinthiakos Canal was cut across its narrow neck. Its arid limestone heart, the plateaus of Arkadia and the Taygetos Mountains, is fringed by fertile coastal plains and four southward-pointing peninsulas. Western Greece, made up of Ipeiros (Epirus) and Akarnania, is wetter and more isolated, and is often taken to include the Ionian Islands such as Corfu (Kerkyra), Kefalonia, and Zakynthos.

The Aegean islands lie in distinct clusters: the Kyklades (Cyclades) around Dilos (Delos), the Dodekanisa (Dodecanese) whose largest island is Rodos (Rhodes), the Northern Sporades, and the eastern islands of Lesbos, Chios, and Samos near the Turkish coast. Thira (Thera, or Santorini) was virtually destroyed by a major eruption in the 2nd millennium BCE, and the vents of the Kameni islands in its flooded crater remain active.

Climate varies with this relief. The average January temperature is in the low 50s F (about 10 C) in Athens but in the low 40s F (about 6 C) in Thessaloniki, where the icy boreas wind can blow from the Eurasian interior. Rainfall is uneven: Corfu averages about 52 inches (1,320 mm) a year, Crete about 25 inches (640 mm), and Athens only about 16 inches (400 mm). Summers are hot and dry, eased on the coast by sea breezes but intensified on Crete by the parching meltemi, or etesian, winds. Greece is home to about 6,000 species of wildflowers, some 600 of them endemic, while forests shelter brown bears, wildcats, wild boars, and, more rarely, wolves and lynx.

## History and National Identity

A major theme is the long process by which Greek identity developed. The historical narrative moves from the Byzantine and Ottoman periods to the struggle for independence in the 19th century, the Balkan Wars, and the difficult twentieth century. The Greek state emerged in a landscape of regional fragmentation, external intervention, and repeated conflict. The legacy of this history remains visible in modern Greek politics, where regional differences, patronage, and strong personalities have long influenced public life.

The Megali Idea, or aspiration to unite Greek-speaking and historically Greek lands, shaped foreign policy in the 19th and early 20th centuries. It helped drive expansion but also ended in disaster after the Greco-Turkish war and population exchange.
