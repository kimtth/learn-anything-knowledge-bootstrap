# Bach

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> Johann Sebastian Bach was a German Baroque composer whose sacred, orchestral, chamber, organ, and keyboard music united contrapuntal rigor with expressive and theological depth. His careers in Weimar, Kothen, and Leipzig produced works from the Brandenburg Concertos to the passions and Mass in B Minor, and his later revival made him a central figure in Western music.

## Introduction

Johann Sebastian Bach ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Johann_Sebastian_Bach)) was the greatest member of a large family of northern German musicians and one of the supreme composers of all time. Though admired in his own lifetime especially as an organist, harpsichordist, and expert on organs, he is now known above all for the extraordinary range and depth of his compositions.
Bach appeared at a decisive historical moment. He inherited the principal forms, styles, and national traditions of earlier European music and fused them into a synthesis of unusual richness. Works such as the Brandenburg Concertos ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brandenburg_Concertos)), The Well-Tempered Clavier ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Well-Tempered_Clavier)), and the Mass in B Minor ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mass_in_B_minor)) exemplify that achievement.

## Family and Early Years

Bach was born in Eisenach in 1685, the youngest child of Johann Ambrosius Bach and Elisabeth Lammerhirt. He belonged to a remarkable musical family whose pride in its lineage was strong enough that Bach later drafted a genealogy of the clan.

After the early deaths of both parents, he was cared for by his elder brother Johann Christoph, an organist who gave him important early keyboard instruction. Bach's education and musical development continued at Luneburg, where his voice won him a place in a choir school and where he broadened his knowledge through libraries, church music, and contact with major northern musicians.

## First Positions and Early Style

By the early 1700s, Bach was already a capable organist. After a brief association with the court at Weimar, he took up the organist post at Arnstadt in 1703. These years were formative, as he absorbed both the southern German keyboard tradition and the freer northern style associated especially with Buxtehude.

By about 1708 Bach had already created an initial synthesis of these traditions. A number of early organ works and keyboard pieces are linked to this phase, though exact dating is often uncertain.

## Muhlhausen

In 1707, Bach moved to Muhlhausen, where he married Maria Barbara Bach. There he composed church cantatas in a relatively conservative mould and continued writing important organ works.
Muhlhausen is a short but productive stage. Bach tried to improve musical standards, enlarged the choir library, and took part in rebuilding the organ. Yet he soon left, citing obstacles to the kind of well-regulated church music he hoped to create.

## Weimar

At Weimar, Bach served first as court organist and later as concertmaster. This was a crucial period in his stylistic development. He concentrated heavily on organ music at first, then increasingly absorbed the influence of modern Italian forms, especially the concerto style associated with Antonio Vivaldi.

Weimar was the setting in which Bach's style deepened and changed profoundly, though the details are hard to trace because of the scarcity of securely dated works. The cantatas of 1714–16 show the importance of this shift toward newer dramatic and structural models.

## Kothen

After conflict in Weimar, including a brief imprisonment when he insisted on leaving, Bach moved to Kothen. These years were probably the happiest period of his life. Under a musically sympathetic prince, Bach focused mainly on chamber and orchestral music.

Many major instrumental works either took shape or reached their present form there, including the Brandenburg Concertos ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brandenburg_Concertos)), the sonatas and partitas for solo violin, the cello suites, and much keyboard music. The first book of The Well-Tempered Clavier ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Well-Tempered_Clavier)) belongs especially to this period and is treated as a remarkable exploration of both all keys and the possibilities of fugue and tonal organization.

## Leipzig

In 1723, Bach became cantor at Leipzig, a position that dominated the rest of his life. There he was responsible for music in several churches and had to supply a huge amount of sacred music, especially cantatas.

This role was demanding and often frustrating. There was repeated friction with civic and school authorities, who did not always provide good musicians or fully respect his priorities. Even so, Leipzig became the setting for his greatest sustained achievements in church music.

## Sacred Music and Major Works

During his Leipzig years Bach composed or revised a vast number of cantatas and other liturgical works. The St. John Passion, St. Matthew Passion, the Christmas Oratorio, and eventually the Mass in B Minor ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mass_in_B_minor)) stand as central monuments of his sacred output.

His church music joined theological seriousness with immense formal power. Chorales, biblical texts, counterpoint, symbolism, and dramatic expression all worked together in ways that were both traditional and innovative.

## Instrumental and Keyboard Innovation

Alongside sacred duties, Bach remained deeply active in instrumental music. At Leipzig he directed the Collegium Musicum, adapting earlier concerti into harpsichord concertos and helping establish the keyboard concerto as a major form.
Bach, often considered conservative, was also an innovator. His use of the harpsichord as a true melodic solo instrument and his later works such as the Goldberg Variations, The Musical Offering, and The Art of the Fugue show both experimental rigor and mastery.

## Symbolism and Method

Bach worked within a rich musical culture of rhetorical figures, pictorial symbolism, and possibly numerical symbolism. In vocal music, rising and falling figures could vividly mirror words and emotions.

At the same time, exaggerated readings of symbolic numbers and patterns should be resisted. Bach clearly worked within a tradition where musical figures carried expressive meaning, but not every numerical occurrence should be treated as intentional symbolism.

## Last Years and Death

In his final years Bach continued to compose, revise, teach, and travel. His visit to [[Frederick the Great]] at Potsdam led to The Musical Offering. He also joined Mizler's scholarly musical society and continued work on abstract contrapuntal pieces.

His last illness prevented him from finishing The Art of the Fugue. He died in 1750 after unsuccessful eye operations by the itinerant surgeon John Taylor. His employers were relieved to replace him, an ironic contrast to his later fame.

## Reputation and Revival

For about half a century after his death, Bach's music was relatively neglected, partly because tastes had shifted and much of his church music seemed tied to an older religious world. Yet important musicians such as Haydn, [[Mozart]], and [[Beethoven]] were not untouched by him.

The great revival began after 1800, gathering force through biography, scholarship, editions, and performance. This revival was the first major modern rediscovery of older music and the beginning of Bach's posthumous elevation to near-unrivaled status.

## Conclusion

Bach ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Johann_Sebastian_Bach)) is a composer who gathered the traditions of his age into an unmatched synthesis of contrapuntal logic, expressive power, and formal invention. His work spans church music, chamber music, orchestral music, organ literature, and keyboard art on a scale few others approached.

Though underappreciated for a time after his death, his reputation eventually came to rest on the depth and permanence of the music itself. He stands as both the summit of the Baroque and one of the central figures of all Western music.
