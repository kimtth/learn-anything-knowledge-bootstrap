# Kyoto

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> Kyoto was Japan's imperial capital from 794 to 1868 and remains a major center of temples, gardens, Buddhism, textiles, crafts, and traditional culture. Its historic urban fabric preserves imperial and religious institutions while the city also participates in Japan's modern industrial economy.

## Introduction

Kyoto is a city in west-central Honshu, Japan, and the seat of Kyoto urban prefecture. It lies near Osaka and Nara and was Japan's capital from 794 to 1868. Its name means Capital City.

Kyoto's identity rests on traditional Japanese culture, Buddhism, textiles, crafts, temples, gardens, and imperial memory. It is also part of the Keihanshin Industrial Zone. Several historic temples and gardens became a UNESCO World Heritage site in 1994.

## Landscape

The city site was chosen by Emperor Kammu and laid out in 794 on the model of Tang China's Chang'an. The rectangular grid placed the Imperial Palace in the north-central section, while Mount Hiei and Mount Atago were treated as protective natural guardians.

Kyoto's rivers, especially the Kamo and Katsura, shaped early boundaries and later growth. The city slopes gently north to south and expanded toward the eastern hills beyond the original plan.

## Climate

Kyoto's climate is admired in spring and fall. Summers are hot and humid, the June-July rainy season lasts several weeks, and winter brings light snow and a penetrating chill. The street grid remains visible, with Shijo-dori as the busiest avenue and Karasuma-dori dividing the city.

Historic Kyoto has few large inner-city factories. Shops, workshops, residences, and offices stand side by side, while building-height controls protect the older visual character. Major landmarks include Higashi and Nishi Hongan-ji, Ryoan-ji, Tenryu-ji, Kiyomizu-dera, Kinkaku-ji, Ginkaku-ji, Heian Shrine, Nijo Castle, Katsura Detached Palace, and Shugakuin Detached Palace.

The population includes foreign communities, especially Koreans, Chinese, and Americans. A continuing social issue is the position of burakumin, the historical outcaste group, despite the removal of discriminatory legal barriers in 1969.

## Industry

Kyoto's industry is based on many small and medium family firms. Traditional workshops make fans, dolls, Buddhist altar fittings, lacquer ware, pottery, silk, fine textiles, and embroidery. Fushimi produces sake, and newer industries include ceramics, garments, medical instruments, electronics, robotics, and computers.

## Commerce

Commerce is mostly retail and consumer-oriented. Kyoto is a national centre of silk and fine textile wholesaling, while tourism supports ryokan, hotels, restaurants, weddings, conventions, and entertainment districts such as Gion and Pontocho.

[[Transportation]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transport)) follows Kyoto's long east-west role. It was the western terminus of the Tokaido road to Edo in the Tokugawa period. Today Shinkansen trains, local railways, and the Meishin Expressway connect it with Osaka, Kobe, Nara, Nagoya, Tokyo, and other cities.

## Government

Kyoto prefecture extends to the Sea of Japan and is led by an elected governor. The city is governed by an elected mayor and city council.

## Education

[[Education]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Education)) is central to Kyoto's identity. More than 40 colleges and universities enroll over 100,000 students. Kyoto University, founded in 1897, is a leading national university, while Doshisha University was founded in 1875 by Niijima Jo. Buddhist universities include Ryukoku, Otani, and Hanazano.

Cultural life ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Culture)) preserves tea ceremony, flower arranging, Noh, Kabuki, traditional dance, calligraphy, painting, sculpture, architecture, national treasures, and living national treasures. Kyoto's calm setting, religious institutions, and heritage help sustain its educational and cultural reputation.

## History

Kyoto's capital history began in 794, though Korean immigrants had earlier brought sericulture and silk-weaving skills. The government moved from Nara partly because Buddhist influence there had become too strong.

Heian Kyoto was dominated by the Fujiwara family. Later, shogunal power moved to Kamakura, but Kyoto remained culturally central. Kamakura and Muromachi periods brought temples, Zen influence, Noh, tea ceremony, flower arranging, and pottery.

War and revival shaped Kyoto in the 16th century. The city was devastated by conflict, then revived under Oda Nobunaga and Toyotomi Hideyoshi. Nobunaga burned the Mount Hiei Tendai complex, while Hideyoshi patronized the arts.

The Meiji shift ended Kyoto's capital role. In 1867, the last Tokugawa shogun returned authority to the imperial court at Nijo Castle. After the Meiji Restoration, the emperor moved to Tokyo. During World War II, Henry L. Stimson helped keep Kyoto off bombing target lists, preserving its treasures.

## Key Events & Developments

- 794: Emperor Kammu established Kyoto as capital.
- 1867: Nijo Castle witnessed the Tokugawa return of authority.
- 1868: The Meiji Restoration moved the imperial household to Tokyo.
- 1895: Electric streetcars began in Kyoto.
- 1994: Historic Kyoto temples and gardens became a UNESCO World Heritage site.
