# Endocrine Systems (Endocrine System, Human)

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> The human endocrine system consists of ductless glands and hormone-secreting cells that regulate distant target tissues through the bloodstream. Its rhythms, feedback systems, synthesis pathways, and disorders connect molecular signaling with growth, metabolism, and reproduction.

## Introduction

The endocrine system ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Endocrine_system)) is a group of ductless glands that regulate body processes by secreting chemical messengers called hormones. These hormones act on nearby tissues or are carried in the bloodstream to reach specific target organs and distant tissues. A key distinction separates an endocrine gland ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Endocrine_gland)), which discharges hormones directly into the blood, from an exocrine gland ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exocrine_gland)), which releases its products through a duct onto an external or internal surface. Salivary glands and sweat glands are exocrine, acting only on nearby tissue, whereas endocrine secretions travel through the circulation to act far from where they are made. Disorders arise from oversecretion, undersecretion, or the failure of target organs to respond.

Knowledge of these glands is ancient. As far back as 3000 BCE, Chinese physicians treated some endocrine disorders, prescribing iodine-rich seaweed for goitre, the enlargement of the thyroid gland. The castration of men created reliable harem guards and, during the Middle Ages and into the 19th century, preserved the treble voices of prepubertal boys. Such practices identified the testes as the source of the substances responsible for maleness and sparked an enduring interest in male sexual function, pursued in the 18th century by the London-based Scottish surgeon and physiologist John Hunter.

## Hormone Synthesis and the Endocrine Cell

Endocrine cells are fairly uniform and usually cuboidal in shape, and under the electron microscope their internal structures, the organelles, reveal the machinery of hormone production. In protein hormone synthesis, a target cell is activated when a hormone binds to a receptor on the cell surface. For example, growth hormone-releasing hormone binds receptors on anterior pituitary cells to stimulate the synthesis and secretion of growth hormone. In some cases a metabolite itself triggers synthesis, as when glucose enters the insulin-producing beta cells of the islets of Langerhans in the pancreas.

Other signals inhibit cellular activity rather than promote it. Dopamine, released from neurons, binds receptors on lactotrophs in the anterior pituitary to block the secretion of prolactin. Once a surface receptor is stimulated, a complex series of events within the cell membrane activates specific genes in the nucleus. These genes carry the DNA sequences that code for protein hormones or for the enzymes that build other hormones, and their transcription produces messenger RNA, which carries the code needed to assemble the target hormone.

## Biorhythms

![[attachments/endocrine_systems_endocrine_system_human_002.webp|420]]

*Negative feedback loop - Endocrine system*

Hormones are not released at a steady rate but often in bursts. Pulsatile secretion is well shown by insulin, which is released in short pulses every few minutes, the interval reflecting the lag time the cell needs to sense changing blood glucose. Pituitary hormones may pulse at one- to two-hour intervals, and this rhythm is essential: when gonadotrophs receive gonadotropin-releasing hormone at 90- to 120-minute intervals, gonadotropin secretion rises and is sustained, but a continuous infusion of the same hormone instead suppresses it.

Many hormones also vary across the day in circadian rhythms. Cortisol, the major steroid of the adrenal cortex, rises sharply in the early morning, falls through the day with small bumps after meals, and stays low at night; this pattern depends on the night-day cycle and is disrupted after travel across time zones, producing jet lag. Growth hormone, thyrotropin, and the gonadotropins peak shortly after sleep begins, and this sleep-related rise in gonadotropins is the first biochemical sign of puberty. Women add to these patterns a monthly rhythm tied to the reproductive cycle.

## Disorders of Hormone Secretion

![[attachments/endocrine_systems_endocrine_system_human_001.webp|420]]

*Endocrine system glands*

Several endocrine disorders have genetic origins linked to abnormal sex chromosomes, including Klinefelter syndrome (such as 47,XXY), Turner syndrome (such as 45,X), and forms of hermaphroditism (46,XX). A separate group of syndromes results from the unregulated overproduction of hormones, usually by tumours. In ectopic hormone production, benign or malignant tumours of tissues that do not normally make a given hormone begin to secrete it.

The hormone most often produced ectopically is adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH), causing ectopic Cushing syndrome. This occurs most often with small-cell carcinoma of the lung, but also with carcinoid tumours, islet-cell tumours of the pancreas, and other cancers. Affected patients show the features of Cushing syndrome along with intense pigmentation from excess ACTH and severe potassium depletion, or hypokalemia, caused by the mineralocorticoid action of high cortisol. Treatment usually means removing or destroying the tumour, but when that fails, removal of the adrenal glands or a drug such as ketoconazole, which blocks adrenal steroid synthesis, may help. Tumour-driven secretion can also cause hypercalcemia, hyponatremia, hypoglycemia, and acromegaly.

## Aging and the Endocrine System

The most striking age-related endocrine change is menopause. Estrogens are made by the granulosa and interstitial cells that line the egg-containing ovarian follicles, so the depletion of these follicles with age inevitably lowers estrogen output. After menopause, serum estrogen falls by at least 80 percent, which drives a rise in follicle-stimulating hormone and luteinizing hormone, showing that the pituitary remains functional even as the ovaries decline.

Male change is far more gradual. Serum testosterone begins a slow decline around age 30, and men aged 70 or older may have substantially reduced levels. About 2 percent of men develop late-onset hypogonadism, sometimes called andropause, which starts near age 40 and brings reduced libido, fatigue, depression, and erectile dysfunction; the cause is a falling number of testosterone-secreting Leydig cells, accompanied by a gradual decline in sperm production though many men stay fertile for years. Thyroid function, by contrast, changes little with age, because the slower clearance of thyroxine and triiodothyronine is matched by a comparable drop in their production, keeping serum concentrations stable.
