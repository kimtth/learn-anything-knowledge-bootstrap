# Minerals and Rocks (Rock)

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> Rocks are coherent natural aggregates of minerals classified by origin as igneous, sedimentary, or metamorphic. Their texture, porosity, density, deformation, thermal behavior, and magnetism are connected by the rock cycle of weathering, deposition, burial, metamorphism, melting, and crystallization.

## Introduction

![[attachments/rock_001.png|420]]

*Rock cycle diagram*

A rock is a naturally occurring coherent aggregate of one or more minerals. Rocks form recognizable and mappable bodies and make up the solid [[Earth - Its Properties, Composition, and Structure (Earth)|Earth]].

Rocks are grouped into three major classes by origin. Igneous rocks ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Igneous_rock)) solidify from magma or lava; sedimentary rocks ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sedimentary_rock)) form from fragments of older rocks or chemical precipitates; and metamorphic rocks ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metamorphic_rock)) form when older rocks change under heat, pressure, fluids, and deformation.

Igneous rocks ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Igneous_rock)) form at high temperature, commonly deep in the crust or upper mantle, and may be intrusive within the crust or extrusive at the surface. Sedimentary rocks are deposited and lithified by compaction, cementation, or crystallization from solution, often showing layers. Metamorphic rocks recrystallize mostly in the solid state and may develop banding as minerals segregate.

The rock cycle links these classes. Weathering and erosion break rocks down; transport and deposition create sediments; diagenesis and lithification form sedimentary rock; heat, pressure, and fluids make metamorphic rock; and melting and crystallization create igneous rock again.

## Texture

![[attachments/rock_002.webp|420]]

*Rock types display*

Texture is the size, shape, and arrangement of grains or crystals in a rock. It also includes homogeneity and isotropy, or whether the rock is uniform and similar in all directions.

Texture records origin and history. In sedimentary rocks it can reveal source material, transport, sorting, deposition, compaction, and cementation. In igneous and metamorphic rocks it reflects crystallization, recrystallization, deformation, and later change.

Grain-size terms differ by rock type. Igneous and metamorphic rocks may be aphanitic, phaneritic, or pegmatitic. Sediments are divided into coarse, medium, and fine sizes, with silt and clay too small to distinguish by the eye. Pyroclastic rocks contain volcanic fragments such as blocks and bombs.

## Porosity

![[attachments/rock_003.png|420]]

*Stratigraphy and rock layers*

Porosity ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Porosity)) is the proportion of bulk rock volume made of voids, including pores and cracks. It depends on compaction, grain shape, packing, cementation, and sorting.

Well-sorted sediments with similar grain sizes may retain substantial pore space, while poorly sorted sediments have smaller grains filling gaps and lower porosity. Engineering terminology reverses this language: a geologically poorly sorted sediment is called well graded.

Total porosity includes all voids, while apparent or effective porosity counts only connected pores accessible to fluids. Effective porosity is crucial for groundwater, petroleum, and contaminant movement.

Rock physical properties matter to geology, geophysics, engineering, petrology, seismology, materials science, and petroleum work. They depend on mineral composition, grain size, packing, pore space, cement, temperature, pressure, and fluid content.

## Density

Density varies with mineralogy and porosity. It helps interpret underground rock type and structure. Bulk density includes grains, crystals, cement, and voids, and may be dry or saturated depending on pore fluids.

Specific gravity is a related dimensionless comparison with water. Sedimentary rocks often have wide density ranges because porosity varies. Granite has a narrow density range, basalt can vary because vesicles create pores, and sandstone varies with sorting, compaction, packing, and cementation.

Burial generally increases sedimentary rock density because overburden pressure compacts grains and cement fills pores. Saturated bulk density is higher than dry density because pore fluid adds weight.

## Elastic Constants

Elastic constants describe how rocks deform under stress. Young's modulus relates stress to length change, shear modulus relates stress to distortion, bulk modulus relates pressure to volume reduction, and Poisson's ratio relates lateral strain to longitudinal strain.

## Stress and Strain

Stress is force per unit area, and strain is deformation. Stress may be axial, shear, or hydrostatic. Common units include bars, kilobars, pascals, and gigapascals; 10 kilobars equals 1 gigapascal.

Rock mechanics studies how rocks deform under stress. Laboratory experiments simulate confining pressure, pore-fluid pressure, directed stress, temperature, time, loading rate, and chemically active fluids.

At depth, confining pressure and temperature rise. Hydrostatic pressure increases about 320 bars per kilometre, and temperature commonly rises about 20 to 40 C per kilometre. Tectonic stress and earthquake stress release add further deformation forces.

## Brittle and Ductile Deformation

Figure 7 represents the transition from brittle fracture through faulting to ductile flow as confining pressure increases. Surface rocks often break, but deeply buried rocks may fold and flow because pressure and temperature increase ductility.

Stress-strain relations describe elastic, viscous, plastic, elasticoviscous, firmoviscous, and plasticoviscous behaviour. Elastic strain is recoverable; plastic strain is permanent after yield; viscous flow depends on internal resistance; creep is slow permanent deformation under constant load.

## Stress-Strain Curves

Typical stress-strain curves show brittle materials remaining elastic until fracture, while ductile materials yield and deform plastically before breaking. Ultimate strength is the highest stress reached before failure.

Environmental conditions strongly affect rock strength. Higher confining pressure increases elasticity, strength, and ductility; pore-fluid pressure reduces effective stress; higher temperature lowers strength and increases ductility; fluids, time, loading rate, and compaction also change behaviour.

Thermal properties include heat flow, thermal conductivity, thermal expansion, and radioactive heat generation. Conductivity depends on mineral content, fluid saturation, pressure, temperature, isotropy, and homogeneity. Quartz-rich rocks conduct heat differently from mafic rocks rich in magnesium and iron.

## Thermal Expansion

Thermal expansion measures how a rock changes length or volume with temperature. Most rocks have volume-expansion coefficients around 15 to 33 x 10^-6 per degree Celsius, and quartz-rich rocks tend to expand more.

Radioactive decay of uranium, thorium, and potassium isotopes produces heat. This heat helped shape Earth's thermal evolution, plastic deformation, plate tectonics, seafloor spreading, continental drift, subduction, earthquakes, and volcanism.

Electrical properties include resistivity, conductivity, dielectric constant, and their changes with temperature and frequency. Dry rock is usually highly resistive, while seawater conducts well because of dissolved salts. In the subsurface, pore fluids often dominate electrical conductivity.

Magnetic properties depend on small amounts of magnetic minerals, especially iron oxides and sulfides. Rocks of the same lithology may have different magnetization because minor magnetic grains vary with chemistry, grain size, temperature, pressure, and geologic history.

Rock magnetization is important for prospecting, geology, and materials science. Magnetic surveys help locate buried structures and resources; paleomagnetism records past magnetic fields and supports plate tectonics; materials work uses magnetic principles for memory cores, tapes, and permanent magnets.

Six basic types of magnetization are diamagnetism, paramagnetism, ferromagnetism, antiferromagnetism, ferrimagnetism, and superparamagnetism. Magnetite is ferrimagnetic and is the most magnetic common mineral.

Rocks can retain remanent magnetization. Chemical, depositional, isothermal, natural, pressure, thermoremanent, partial thermoremanent, and viscous remanent magnetizations record different formation or alteration processes. Thermoremanent magnetization is especially important in igneous and sedimentary rocks.

## Magnetic Hysteresis

A magnetic hysteresis curve shows how magnetization changes with an applied field and how some magnetization remains after the field is removed. Susceptibility is useful in both laboratory study and magnetic mapping of buried rocks.

Major magnetic minerals include magnetite, titanomagnetite, hematite, maghemite, limonite, and pyrrhotite. Mafic igneous rocks often have higher susceptibility because they contain more magnetite, while fine-grained seafloor basalts may have strong stable remanence.

## Core Equations

Chemical and materials descriptions connect composition, reaction balance, and material response:

$$\mathrm{aA+bB \rightarrow cC+dD}$$

Read the left side as the reactants or starting materials and the right side as the products; the coefficients show the proportions needed to conserve atoms or charge.

$$\sigma=E\varepsilon$$

For an elastic material, stress is proportional to strain, with Young's modulus E measuring stiffness.

$$\Delta G=\Delta H-T\Delta S$$

The delta symbol marks a change in a quantity, so the equation tracks how a system shifts between states rather than only describing a static value.

## Key Events & Developments

- 1968: Akira Sawaoka, Naoto Kawai, and Robert Carmichael designed high-pressure apparatus for rock deformation experiments.
- 20th century: Magnetic surveys of oceanic crust gave quantitative evidence for seafloor spreading and plate tectonics.
- modern geology: Density, porosity, stress-strain, thermal, electrical, and magnetic measurements became central to subsurface interpretation.

## Interesting Facts

- Porosity ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Porosity)) controls how groundwater, petroleum, and contaminants move through rock.
- Rocks that break near Earth's surface may flow and fold when deeply buried.
- Dry rock is usually very resistive, but briny pore water can make it conduct electricity.
- Magnetic stripes in oceanic crust helped prove large-scale seafloor spreading.
- Radioactive decay inside rocks contributes to Earth's internal heat and tectonic activity.
