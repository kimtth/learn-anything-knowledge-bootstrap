# Minerals and Rocks (Mineral)

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> A mineral is a naturally formed homogeneous solid with a definite composition and ordered atomic structure. This account explains mineral definition, crystal structure, symmetry, morphology, classification, and the limited suite of rock-forming minerals that makes up most of the solid Earth.

## Introduction

A mineral is a naturally occurring homogeneous solid with a definite chemical composition and a highly ordered atomic arrangement. Minerals usually form by inorganic processes, and about 100 rock-forming minerals make up most rocks.

## Definition

A mineral differs from a laboratory-made equivalent because it forms through natural processes. Synthetic emeralds, sapphires, diamonds, and other gemstones may be nearly identical to natural minerals, but they are not minerals under the strict definition.

A mineral is also a homogeneous solid. It consists of one solid substance with uniform composition, though homogeneity depends on scale: a specimen that looks uniform to the eye may reveal several components under a microscope or by X-ray diffraction. Granite, for example, contains feldspar, quartz, mica, and amphibole.

Liquids and gases are excluded. [[Ice and Ice Formations (Ice)|Ice]] is a mineral because it is solid water, but liquid water is not. Liquid mercury and natural glasslike substances are treated as mineraloids when they resemble minerals but do not fully satisfy the definition.

A definite chemical composition can often be written as a formula. Quartz is SiO2, but many minerals vary within limits because ions substitute for one another. Siderite, for example, may include iron, manganese, magnesium, or calcium while keeping the same carbonate structure.

Minerals are crystalline solids because their atoms form ordered geometric arrangements. Under favourable growth conditions, this internal order appears as external crystal form. Amorphous natural solids lack this order and are classed as mineraloids.

Modern mineralogy may include organically produced compounds if they meet other mineral requirements. Aragonite can form inorganically, but oyster shells and pearls are also largely aragonite. Human bones and teeth contain hydroxylapatite.

## Nomenclature

![[attachments/mineral_003.png|420]]

*Mohs hardness scale*

Minerals are classified by major anionic chemical groups, such as silicates, oxides, nitrates, and sulfides, but their names are less systematic. Names may come from colour, composition, locality, public figures, or mineralogists.

Examples include albite, named from the Latin for white; goethite, honouring Johann Wolfgang von Goethe; franklinite, named for Franklin, New Jersey; and sillimanite, honouring Benjamin Silliman. Since 1960, an international nomenclature committee has reviewed new mineral names and type material.

Minerals form in four main ways. Igneous minerals crystallize from melts; sedimentary minerals form from deposited and weathered materials; metamorphic minerals grow in solid rock under changed temperature or pressure; and hydrothermal minerals precipitate from hot solutions moving through fractures.

Open spaces in rocks allow well-shaped crystals to grow. Geodes, common in limestones, form when groundwater deposits minerals in rounded cavities, often lining them with crystals.

## Occurrence

The repeated pyrite figure links mineral occurrence to visible crystal growth. Well-formed pyrite crystals show how free space, chemical supply, and internal symmetry combine.

## Morphology

Morphology is the external form of a crystal. Smooth faces and regular shapes appear when a mineral grows freely, but a crystal remains crystalline even without visible faces because crystallinity depends on internal order.

## Crystal Symmetry

Crystal form expresses symmetry elements: rotation axes, rotoinversion axes, centres of symmetry, and mirror planes. These operations describe how a crystal repeats visually under rotation, reflection, or inversion.

## Crystal Form

Pyrite is used repeatedly because its faces, striations, and cubic-related forms make symmetry easy to visualize. External form is a clue to the deeper internal arrangement.

## The 32 Crystal Classes

Translation-free symmetry describes operations visible in crystal morphology without considering repeated atomic translations. The 32 possible crystal classes are based on these combinations.

## Crystal Systems

The crystal classes ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crystallographic_point_group)) are grouped into six crystal systems: isometric, hexagonal, tetragonal, orthorhombic, monoclinic, and triclinic. They differ in crystallographic axes, axis lengths, and angles.

## Hermann-Mauguin Notation

The Hermann-Mauguin notation symbolically records crystal symmetry. It provides a concise way to identify the symmetry content of the 32 crystal classes.

## Symmetry Examples

Different crystals may show monoclinic, tetragonal, or isometric symmetry. The isometric system has the highest overall symmetry, while triclinic crystals have the least.

## Crystal Faces

Well-shaped crystals are classified by visible symmetry, but their external perfection is not required for mineral identity. Growth environment controls whether faces develop clearly.

## Crystal Classes

Crystals with different symmetry contents belong to different crystal classes ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crystallographic_point_group)). This classification links external morphology to internal structure.

## Growth Conditions

A well-shaped crystal needs open space and steady growth. Crowded rock-forming minerals may lack attractive crystal faces even though their atomic order is complete.

## Mineral Identification

Crystal habit can help identify minerals, but it must be combined with hardness, cleavage, colour, density, and chemical tests because shape alone can be misleading.

## Twinning

Twinning occurs when two or more crystals form a symmetrical intergrowth. A twin plane, twin axis, or twin centre relates the parts, and three or more joined crystals form a repeated twin.

## Twin Laws

A twin law specifies the plane, axis, or centre that relates twinned crystals. Twinning is important because it adds symmetry not present in a single untwinned crystal.

Internal crystal structure is revealed by X-ray, neutron, and electron diffraction, along with spectroscopic methods. These techniques locate atoms, bond types, and internal symmetry on the scale of angstroms and nanometres.

Space groups combine translation-free symmetry with translations, screw axes, and glide planes. The 32 crystal classes expand into 230 possible space groups, describing how motifs repeat through three-dimensional space.

## Structural Models

Crystal structures are often illustrated by projected models. Tridymite, a form of SiO2, can be shown as sheets of SiO4 tetrahedrons, and enlarged physical models help visualize atomic arrangements.

## Polymorphism

Polymorphism is the ability of one chemical composition to crystallize in more than one structure. Carbon occurs as graphite and diamond; FeS2 occurs as pyrite and marcasite; SiO2 occurs as quartz, tridymite, cristobalite, coesite, and stishovite.

Pressure and temperature control polymorph stability. High-temperature forms of SiO2 occur in lava flows, while high-pressure forms such as coesite and stishovite occur in meteorite craters, the upper mantle, or subduction zones.

A mineral's properties depend on both chemical composition and internal geometry. Analyses may use wet chemistry, optical emission, X-ray fluorescence, atomic absorption, or electron microprobe methods. Clean samples matter because impurities distort formulas.

Mineral formulas express element proportions. Some minerals are pure substances, such as quartz and kyanite, but many allow solid solution. Substitutional, interstitial, and omission solid solutions explain how ions replace, enter, or leave structural sites while maintaining overall charge balance.

Chemical bonds control hardness, melting point, cleavage, conductivity, and expansion. Ionic, covalent, metallic, van der Waals, and hydrogen bonds are ideal types, though real minerals often mix bond characters.

## Ionic and Covalent Bonds

Ionic bonding joins oppositely charged ions, as in halite, and tends to produce moderate hardness, high symmetry, high melting points, and poor solid-state conductivity. Covalent bonding shares electrons and can create very strong, directional structures such as diamond.

## Metallic Bonds

Metallic bonding holds positive ions in a mobile cloud of electrons. This explains ductility, malleability, heat and electrical conductivity, metallic lustre, and the photoelectric effect in some metals.

## Weak Bonds

Van der Waals bonds are weak attractions between neutral molecules or layers, as in graphite between carbon sheets. Hydrogen bonding links hydrogen attached to electronegative atoms with neighbouring negative regions, common in hydroxides, micas, and clays.

## Habits and Aggregates

Minerals appear as habits and aggregates: granular, lamellar, bladed, fibrous, acicular, radiating, globular, dendritic, mammillary, botryoidal, stalactitic, concentric, geodic, and oolitic forms. These terms describe how crystals grow together.

## Common Aggregations

Common crystal aggregations help field identification. Geodes, botryoidal masses, fibres, needles, and plantlike dendrites reflect growth conditions and mineral deposition patterns.

Cleavage is breakage along planes of structural weakness, while fracture is breakage without preferred planes. Mica has perfect cleavage; quartz commonly has conchoidal fracture; other minerals may be splintery or hackly.

## Lustre

Lustre describes how a mineral surface reflects light. Metallic lustre resembles fresh metal and is typical of opaque minerals such as pyrite, chalcopyrite, and galena.

Nonmetallic lustres include vitreous, resinous, pearly, greasy, silky, and adamantine. Quartz is commonly vitreous, sphalerite may be resinous, talc may be pearly, satin spar is silky, and diamondlike high-refractive minerals are adamantine.

## Colour

[[Colour]] can help identify minerals but is not always reliable. Metallic minerals often have diagnostic fresh colours but tarnish with time, as galena dulls or bornite becomes purple-blue peacock ore.

Some nonmetallic minerals have stable colours, such as green malachite, blue azurite, pink rhodonite, and yellow sulfur. Others, including tourmaline, corundum, beryl, and quartz, occur in many colours and receive varietal gemstone names.

## Hardness

Hardness is resistance to scratching and is measured relative to the Mohs scale of 10 minerals. Fingernails, copper coins, steel knives, glass, needles, and streak plates provide practical comparison points.

Hydrous minerals, halides, carbonates, sulfates, phosphates, and most sulfides are generally softer, while anhydrous oxides and silicates are usually harder. Hardness is a major sorting property in mineral identification.

## Hardness Trends

General hardness trends follow chemistry and bonding: water-bearing minerals are soft, many saltlike minerals are soft, pyrite and marcasite are harder sulfide exceptions, and many oxides and silicates are hard.

## Tenacity

Tenacity describes how minerals respond to stress. Gold, silver, and copper may be malleable, sectile, and ductile; talc is flexible; mica is elastic; many silicates are brittle.

Specific gravity compares a mineral's weight with an equal volume of water. It depends on atomic weights and packing. Diamond is denser than graphite despite identical composition, and metallic minerals usually feel heavier than nonmetallic ones.

## Magnetism

[[Electricity and Magnetism (Magnetism)|Magnetism]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnetism)) is easily observed in magnetite and more weakly in pyrrhotite. Ferromagnetic, paramagnetic, and diamagnetic responses allow mineral separation with electromagnets.

## Fluorescence

Fluorescence occurs when minerals emit visible light under ultraviolet radiation. Fluorite, scheelite, calcite, scapolite, willemite, and autunite may fluoresce, though response varies by specimen and wavelength.

Carbonates can be identified by hydrochloric acid effervescence. Calcite and aragonite fizz readily, while dolomite and related carbonates react slowly, especially when powdered or heated.

## Radioactivity

Minerals containing uranium or thorium emit radiation through decay. Geiger or scintillation counters help identify uraninite, pitchblende, thorianite, autunite, and minerals used in isotopic dating.

Mineral classification combines chemistry and structure. Since the 19th century, minerals have been grouped by dominant anions, but X-ray studies showed that internal structure is also essential. W. Lawrence Bragg and Victor Moritz Goldschmidt helped apply crystallochemical principles.

## Metals

Native elements occur uncombined or nearly uncombined. Native metals include gold, silver, copper, lead, platinum-group minerals, iron, nickel-iron, mercury, tantalum, tin, and zinc. Gold, silver, and copper have cubic closest-packed structures and are soft, ductile, malleable, sectile, conductive, and metallic.

## Native Element Structures

Native-element structures include close-packed metals, semimetals, and carbon polymorphs. Iron-nickel alloys such as kamacite and taenite are common in meteorites, and Earth's core is thought to be largely iron-nickel alloy.

## Close-Packed Metals

Close-packed metal structures explain high density and shared physical properties in native metals. Semimetals such as arsenic, antimony, and bismuth have more directional bonding and are more brittle.

## Bonding and Properties

The repeated native-element figures contrast metallic, semimetallic, and nonmetallic structures. Their differences show how bonding and packing control hardness, cleavage, density, and conductivity.

## Iron Group

The iron group includes native iron and nickel-iron minerals. Their similar atomic radii allow frequent substitution of nickel for iron.

## Nonmetals

Native nonmetals include diamond, fullerene, graphite, and sulfur. Diamond has tetrahedral covalent bonding and extreme hardness; graphite has covalently bonded sheets held by weak van der Waals forces, giving perfect basal cleavage.

## Carbon Polymorphs

Carbon polymorphs show how identical composition can produce different properties. Diamond, graphite, and fullerenes differ because their atoms are arranged and bonded differently.

## Nonmetal Comparison

Nonmetal structures are not simple metal close packing. Their varied bonding explains why sulfur, graphite, diamond, and fullerene behave so differently.

## Layered Carbon Structure

Graphite's layered structure allows sheets to slide because weak forces connect the layers. This contrasts sharply with diamond's rigid three-dimensional network.

## Fullerene Occurrences

Fullerenes occur in some meta-anthracite, fulgurites, Cretaceous-Tertiary boundary clays, and organic-rich layers near the Sudbury nickel mine.

## Sulfides

Sulfides include many ore minerals and consist of metals with sulfur; sulfarsenides contain arsenic replacing some sulfur. Galena, pyrite, sphalerite, chalcopyrite, and arsenopyrite illustrate varied sulfide structures and bonding.

## Sulfide Structures

Pyrite, FeS2, models the close relation between sulfide chemistry, symmetry, and crystal form. Its structure resembles sodium chloride with disulfide groups coordinated around iron.

## Sulfide Example

Pyrite's high symmetry produces recognizable external forms. The mineral is used as a recurring example for morphology, sulfide structure, and crystal symmetry.

## Sulfosalts

Sulfosalts differ from sulfides and sulfarsenides because semimetals such as arsenic and antimony occupy metal positions rather than sulfur positions. Enargite, Cu3AsS4, may also be viewed as a double sulfide.

Oxides combine oxygen with metals, while hydroxides contain hydroxyl groups. Oxides are generally hard, dense, refractory, and often economically important as ores of iron, chromium, manganese, tin, and uranium. Hydroxides form at low temperatures and often as weathering products.

## The Spinel Structure

The spinel structure contains oxygen in approximate cubic closest packing, with cations in octahedral and tetrahedral sites. Spinel-group minerals are important multiple oxides.

## Halides

Halides contain chlorine, bromine, iodine, or fluorine. They often have large, weakly charged ions, high symmetry, low hardness, moderate to high melting points, and good electrical conductivity when molten.

Common halides include halite, sylvite, chlorargyrite, cryolite, fluorite, and atacamite. Halite has the sodium chloride structure, while fluorite has a different cubic structure also found in uraninite and thorianite.

## The Halite Structure

The halite structure places each sodium and chlorine ion in octahedral coordination with six neighbours. No separate molecules exist in the crystal.

## The Fluorite Structure

The fluorite structure, CaF2, gives calcium eightfold coordination and fluorine tetrahedral coordination. Similar structures occur in some oxides.

## Halide Structures

Halide structure diagrams compare NaCl-type and fluorite-type arrangements. These structures explain coordination, symmetry, and the properties of saltlike minerals.

## Fluorite-Type Minerals

The fluorite type also appears in uraninite and thorianite. This connection links halide structural principles with oxide minerals important in nuclear chemistry.

## Carbonates

Carbonates contain triangular CO3 groups that do not share oxygen atoms. They are commonly identified by acid effervescence. Main anhydrous carbonate groups include calcite, aragonite, and dolomite, while malachite and azurite are notable hydrous copper carbonates.

## Calcium Carbonate Polymorphs

Calcium carbonate occurs chiefly as calcite and aragonite. Calcite is rhombohedral with calcium coordinated to six oxygens; aragonite is orthorhombic with calcium coordinated to nine oxygens.

## The Aragonite Group

The aragonite group includes carbonates with large divalent cations such as barium, strontium, and lead. Specific gravity generally rises with the atomic weight of the metal ion.

## Nitrates

Nitrates contain triangular NO3 groups similar to carbonate groups, but nitrogen-oxygen bonds are stronger, so nitrates decompose less readily in acid. Nitratite corresponds structurally to calcite, and nitre corresponds to aragonite.

## Borates

Borates contain boron-oxygen triangles and tetrahedrons that polymerize into rings, sheets, chains, and complex groups. Colemanite and borax show how boron groups combine with hydroxyls and water.

## Borate Linkages

Borate structures illustrate polymerization, a principle also central to silicates. Oxygen sharing links triangular and tetrahedral boron groups.

## Borax and Colemanite

Borax and colemanite contain complex boron-oxygen groups. Their structures show how borates form multiple linked units rather than only simple isolated groups.

## Sulfates

Sulfates contain isolated SO4 tetrahedrons. Barite-group minerals are common anhydrous sulfates, while gypsum is the most important hydrous sulfate.

## Phosphates

Phosphates contain PO4 tetrahedrons similar to sulfate groups. Most phosphate minerals are rare, but apatite is abundant and important. Arsenates and vanadates are structurally related.

## Silicates

Silicates ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silicate)) are the most important mineral class because they dominate Earth's crust. Their basic unit is the SiO4 tetrahedron, with silicon at the centre and oxygen at four corners.

Silicate tetrahedrons polymerize by sharing oxygen atoms. Different sharing patterns create isolated, paired, ring, chain, sheet, and framework structures. Aluminum can substitute for silicon in tetrahedral sites or occupy octahedral sites with magnesium, iron, manganese, titanium, and other cations.

## The Silicon-Oxygen Tetrahedron

The silicon-oxygen tetrahedron is the foundation of silicate structures. Its strong half-ionic, half-covalent bonds help hold the terrestrial crust together.

## Nesosilicates

Nesosilicates contain isolated SiO4 tetrahedrons linked by interstitial cations. They tend to be equidimensional, dense, hard, and poor in prominent cleavage.

## Sorosilicates

Sorosilicates contain paired SiO4 tetrahedrons forming Si2O7 groups. Most are rare, but the epidote group and vesuvianite are common examples.

## Cyclosilicates

Cyclosilicates contain tetrahedrons linked into rings. Important examples include beryl and tourmaline, both based on six-membered Si6O18 rings.

## Inosilicates

Inosilicates contain single or double chains. Single-chain pyroxenes and double-chain amphiboles share many cations but differ in hydroxyl content, crystal habit, cleavage angles, density, and typical formation temperature.

## Phyllosilicates

Phyllosilicates contain infinite sheets of tetrahedrons. Their structures produce platy habit, strong cleavage, softness, low density, flexibility, and important soil properties such as water retention and nutrient exchange.

## Tectosilicates

Tectosilicates have three-dimensional frameworks in which every oxygen is shared. They make up nearly 75 percent of Earth's crust, and include quartz, feldspars, feldspathoids, and zeolites.

Minerals in rocks form assemblages. Petrology identifies minerals, abundance, grain size, and texture to reconstruct rock history. A phase is a homogeneous substance with fixed composition, and phase equilibrium describes stable coexistence of phases over time.

Reaction rims show disequilibrium between touching minerals, while minerals touching without reaction products may have formed in equilibrium. Phase diagrams show stability fields controlled by pressure, temperature, Eh, pH, or composition.

Igneous petrology uses liquidus diagrams to study melting and crystallization. Metamorphic petrology uses pressure-temperature diagrams to infer formation conditions, such as those involving kyanite, sillimanite, muscovite, quartz, and feldspar. Sedimentary petrology uses Eh-pH diagrams to interpret oxidizing, reducing, acidic, and basic environments.

## Core Equations

Chemical and materials descriptions connect composition, reaction balance, and material response:

$$\mathrm{aA+bB \rightarrow cC+dD}$$

Read the left side as the reactants or starting materials and the right side as the products; the coefficients show the proportions needed to conserve atoms or charge.

$$\sigma=E\varepsilon$$

For an elastic material, stress is proportional to strain, with Young's modulus E measuring stiffness.

$$\Delta G=\Delta H-T\Delta S$$

The delta symbol marks a change in a quantity, so the equation tracks how a system shifts between states rather than only describing a static value.

## Key Events & Developments

- 19th century: Mineral classification became organized mainly around chemical composition and dominant anions.
- 1947: Faster instrumental analysis methods began replacing many older wet-chemical procedures.
- 1960: An international committee began reviewing new mineral names and type materials.
- 20th century: X-ray diffraction and crystallochemical studies made internal structure central to mineral classification.

## Interesting Facts

- [[Ice and Ice Formations (Ice)|Ice]] is a mineral, but liquid water is not.
- Diamond and graphite are both carbon, but their different structures give them radically different properties.
- Halite contains no separate NaCl molecules; it is a continuous crystal lattice of ions.
- The fizz test with dilute hydrochloric acid is a practical way to identify many carbonates.
- Nearly 75 percent of Earth's crust is made of tectosilicate framework minerals.
