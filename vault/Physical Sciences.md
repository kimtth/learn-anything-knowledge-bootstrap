# Physical Sciences

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> The physical sciences unite the study of matter, energy, force, mechanics, optics, electricity, magnetism, heat, atoms, and chemical behavior. This historical survey follows the move from ancient natural explanation through the scientific revolution and Newtonian synthesis to twentieth-century physics, astronomy, and quantum chemistry.

## Introduction

![[attachments/physical_sciences_001.png|420]]

Physical science ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Outline_of_physical_science)) is the systematic study of the inorganic world. It differs from biological science, which studies living things. It is usually divided into astronomy, physics, chemistry, and the Earth sciences, but this account concentrates mainly on astronomy, physics, and chemistry. These fields did not begin as separate modern sciences. They grew from older attempts to explain motion, matter, light, heat, celestial bodies, and chemical change in an ordered way.

Modern physics was formed in the mid-19th century by joining several older sciences: mechanics, optics, acoustics, electricity, magnetism, heat, and the physical properties of matter. This union depended on the idea that the forces of nature are connected and can be changed into one another because they are forms of energy. The modern field therefore became less a collection of separate studies and more a search for general principles that apply to all matter and force.

The boundary between physics and chemistry is partly artificial. In the 20th century, physics focused strongly on atoms and their smaller components, while chemistry focused on molecules, properties, and reactions. Yet chemical reactions depend on atoms and energy, especially heat, so the two sciences overlap deeply. Chemists usually ask why particular elements and compounds behave as they do, while physicists often look for general properties shared by matter as a whole.

Astronomy ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astronomy)) studies the universe beyond Earth. It includes Earth only when its mass, rotation, and motion affect or are affected by other bodies in the solar system. Before the 18th century, astronomy mainly concerned the Sun, Moon, planets, and comets. Later it expanded to stars, galaxies, nebulas, and the interstellar medium. Celestial mechanics then linked astronomy to physics by treating heavenly motion through mathematical laws of force and motion.

The older physical sciences ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Outline_of_physical_science)) began in Greek efforts to replace myth with natural explanation. Thinkers such as Thales and later Greek philosophers asked what the world is made of, how order arises, how change is possible, and whether nature can be understood through reason. Egyptian calendar astronomy and Babylonian prediction of celestial events supplied practical knowledge, while Greek geometry gave astronomy and mechanics a more abstract mathematical form.

Ancient and medieval traditions gave later science both problems and tools. [[Aristotle and Aristotelianism|Aristotle]] supplied a powerful system of natural place, natural motion, and elements. [[Archimedes]] showed how mathematics could analyze statics and simple machines. Islamic scholars preserved and extended Greek learning, and medieval European thinkers developed impetus theory and Oxford kinematics. These traditions did not create modern science by themselves, but they prepared the questions that later astronomy, mechanics, optics, and chemistry transformed.

## Ancient Astronomy

Ancient astronomy tried to explain observed heavenly motions with geometrical models. The theory of homocentric spheres could account for some regular motions, but it failed before two important observations. Planets changed in brightness, which suggested that they were not always the same distance from Earth. Mercury and Venus also showed bounded elongations, meaning that Venus was never seen more than about 48 degrees from the Sun and Mercury never more than about 24 degrees from it.

Heracleides of Pontus tried to solve these problems in the 4th century BCE. He proposed that Mercury and Venus revolve around the Sun, while the Sun and other planets revolve around Earth. He also held that Earth rotates on its axis to explain the daily motion of the heavens. This idea did not reshape ancient astronomy, but it showed that alternatives to a wholly Earth-centred arrangement were already being imagined.

Aristarchus of Samos later advanced a heliocentric hypothesis in the 3rd century BCE. His idea resembled the system that [[Copernicus]] would propose many centuries later, but it gained little support in antiquity. Ancient astronomy remained dominated by geocentric models because they seemed compatible with common experience, Greek physics, and the demand for circular heavenly motion.

Hipparchus, who flourished around 130 BCE, made major advances in observational and theoretical astronomy. He built theories of the Sun and Moon from a large body of observations and used the eccentric circle to explain irregularities in their motion. In this device, Earth is not at the exact centre of the circle. Hipparchus also showed that the eccentric circle is mathematically equivalent to the epicycle-deferent system, a geometrical tool associated with Apollonius of Perga.

One of Hipparchus's most important discoveries was the precession of the equinoxes. He observed a gradual change in the apparent longitude between fixed stars and the equinoctial points. This meant that the north celestial pole moves slowly relative to the stars. The discovery showed that careful comparison of observations across time could reveal hidden long-term motions in the heavens.

## Scientific Revolution in Astronomy

The scientific revolution began in astronomy because the structure of the cosmos became the first major field where inherited authority was overthrown by a new mathematical and physical system. Nicolaus Copernicus proposed a comprehensive heliocentric theory in 1543. He placed the Sun at the centre and set Earth in motion around it, turning the older world picture inside out.

[[Copernicus]] was motivated by the wish to restore unity and harmony to astronomy and to satisfy the demand for uniform circular motion. His system used much of the same observational data available to Ptolemy, and it was not always mathematically simpler when detailed predictions were required. Its deeper simplicity was cosmological: the Sun became the central body, and the motions of planets could be understood within one ordered arrangement.

The most radical feature of Copernican astronomy was its claim to describe the real physical system of the world. Earlier mathematical astronomy often treated models as instruments for prediction. Copernicus insisted that a satisfactory astronomy should describe how the heavens actually are. This changed astronomy from a calculating art into a challenge to the whole older philosophy of nature.

The acceptance of heliocentrism happened gradually. Large public opposition came later, but many professional astronomers found Copernican mathematical methods useful before they accepted the central cosmological claim. Erasmus Reinhold used Copernican methods to produce the Prutenic Tables in 1551. These tables were more accurate and current than older tables and became important to astronomers and astrologers.

Tycho Brahe rejected both the Ptolemaic and Copernican systems, but his work strongly helped the new astronomy. He used larger, steadier, and better calibrated instruments and made regular observations over long periods. His planetary measurements reached an accuracy of about one minute of arc, far better than earlier observations. This precision gave later astronomers data strong enough to test competing models.

Several observations by Tycho Brahe weakened Aristotelian cosmology. The nova of 1572 showed no parallax, so it had to be very distant and not part of the sublunary region. Comets also seemed to move freely through regions that Aristotelian theory treated as solid or unchanging. These observations challenged the belief that the heavens were immutable and sharply different from the earthly world.

## Galileo, Kepler, and Mechanics

Galileo Galilei defended Copernicanism by changing mechanics. The old Ptolemaic-Aristotelian system treated Earth as fixed at the centre of the cosmos and explained motion through natural places. If Earth moved, this whole physical system seemed to collapse. Galileo therefore had to show that motion on Earth could be understood in a way that made Earth's motion possible.

Galileo wanted to make [[Mechanics - Energy, Forces, and Their Effects|mechanics]] mathematical in the style of [[Archimedes]]. He studied falling bodies and derived the law of free fall, according to which distance varies as the square of time. He also used an early form of the principle of inertia. By joining these ideas, he explained projectile motion as a parabolic path rather than as a motion that must follow Aristotelian natural tendencies.

The principle of inertia answered a major objection to Earth's motion. If bodies in motion tend to remain in motion, then objects on Earth share Earth's motion. Projectiles, clouds, and people do not get left behind because they already participate in the motion of Earth. This made Copernican astronomy physically more believable, not just mathematically possible.

Johannes Kepler transformed Tycho Brahe's observations into laws of planetary motion. His work replaced older circular assumptions with a more exact mathematical account of planetary paths. Together, Kepler and Galileo weakened the older system from two sides: Kepler made the planetary theory fit precise data, while Galileo rebuilt terrestrial mechanics so a moving Earth could make sense.

The mechanical philosophy ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mechanism_%28philosophy%29)) of the 17th century tried to explain all natural phenomena through matter and motion. Rene Descartes was less focused on solving technical mechanical problems than on creating foundations for science. He rejected action through empty space and pictured the universe as filled with ether-like matter moving in vortices. In this view, even celestial motion had to be explained by contact and pressure rather than by invisible attraction.

Christiaan Huygens helped make mechanics more quantitative. He developed laws of impact and contributed to the ideas of momentum and kinetic energy. His work showed how the mechanical philosophy could become a mathematical program rather than only a broad worldview. By the late 17th century, the key problem was to unite mathematical mechanics with the observed motions of celestial bodies.

## Newtonian Synthesis

![[attachments/physical_sciences_002.webp|420]]

*Newton Principia title page*

Isaac Newton's Philosophiae Naturalis Principia Mathematica, published in 1687, solved the central problems of the scientific revolution in mechanics and cosmology. It gave a physical basis for [[Kepler]]'s laws, unified terrestrial and celestial physics, and established the methods that dominated astronomy and physics for more than a century. Newton joined the mechanical philosophy to the mathematization of nature through the concept of force.

Newton's first law stated that a body continues at rest or in straight-line motion unless a force changes that state. His second law connected change of motion to impressed force, and Leonhard Euler later put it into the familiar form F = ma in 1750. His third law stated that every action has an equal and opposite reaction. These laws made motion calculable and gave mechanics a general structure.

To apply his laws to astronomy, [[Newton]] went beyond [[Descartes and Cartesianism (Descartes)|Descartes]]'s limits. He postulated universal gravitation, an attractive force between any two objects in the universe. He could not explain how gravity was transmitted, but the law worked mathematically. With an inverse-square relation between gravitational force and distance, Newton derived Kepler's planetary laws and showed that Galileo's free-fall law belonged to the same system.

The same gravitational force that makes bodies fall near Earth also keeps the Moon and planets in orbit. This was the great unification of celestial and terrestrial physics. Tides, lunar motion, planetary paths, and falling objects became different expressions of one mathematical order. The heavens no longer required a separate kind of physics.

Newtonian physics also predicted that [[Earth - Its Properties, Composition, and Structure (Earth)|Earth]] is not a perfect sphere but bulges at the Equator. French expeditions in the mid-18th century confirmed this prediction, helping persuade European scientists to move from Cartesian to Newtonian physics. Newton also used Earth's nonspherical shape to explain the precession of the equinoxes through the action of the Sun and Moon on Earth's equatorial bulge.

## Optics

[[Principles of Optics (Optics)|Optics]] joined experiment, geometry, and mechanical explanation. Its roots went back to Greek work, especially Euclid, who described results in geometrical optics, including the law of reflection. Medieval scholars such as Roger Bacon, Robert Grosseteste, and John Pecham built on the work of the Arab scholar Alhazen, including studies of vision and the rainbow.

[[Kepler]] reshaped optics in the 17th century by tracing rays point by point from an object to an image. He broke the problem of vision into geometrical parts, much as mechanical philosophers broke nature into matter and motion. His lens theory gave the first mathematical account of [[Galileo]]'s telescope and made optical instruments part of exact science.

[[Descartes and Cartesianism (Descartes)|Descartes]] tried to include light within the mechanical philosophy ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mechanism_%28philosophy%29)). He used mechanical analogies to explain reflection and refraction and derived several known optical laws mathematically. His work showed the broad ambition of the period: even light should be explained by matter, motion, and geometry.

[[Newton]] made some of the most important 17th-century contributions to colour theory. Traditional views treated colours as modifications of white light. Newton's experiments showed instead that white light contains different colours and that refraction separates them. This overturned older theories and linked colour to the composition of light itself.

Newton's optical views dominated much of the 18th century. He believed that dispersion necessarily accompanies refraction and that chromatic aberration in telescopes could be avoided by using reflectors instead of refractors. Euler challenged this view and pointed to the human eye as evidence that an achromatic lens system could exist. Euler also rejected Newton's corpuscular theory of light and explained optical phenomena as vibrations in a fluid ether.

The wave theory of light revived strongly around the turn of the 19th century. Thomas Young, while studying the eye's focusing power, investigated interference, including cases where two rays of light cancel and produce darkness. Augustin-Jean Fresnel developed the wave theory from 1815 onward through mathematical and experimental memoirs. Corpuscular supporters resisted, but interference and diffraction gave wave theory increasing power.

## Chemistry and Alchemy

Chemistry had many origins: philosophy, alchemy, metallurgy, medicine, and practical work with substances. It became a separate science only gradually, especially with the rise of mechanical philosophy in the 17th century. Earlier views were not simply discarded; they supplied vocabulary, laboratory practices, and problems that later chemists reworked.

[[Aristotle and Aristotelianism|Aristotle]] treated earth, water, air, and fire as the ultimate constituents of things. These four elements were thought to be transmutable and present in substances through qualities. This theory gave early chemistry a philosophical structure, but it did not provide a shared experimental method for cumulative chemical knowledge.

Alchemy, which arose in Egypt and the Middle East, had both practical and cosmological sides. Practically, it sought transformations such as making gold from baser substances. Cosmologically, it connected human beings and the universe through correspondences. Even when its theories later lost authority, alchemy left chemistry a long tradition of experience with many materials and operations.

Paracelsus, a 16th-century Swiss natural philosopher, became a major figure in chemical medicine. He combined Aristotelian matter theory, alchemical correspondences, mystical forms of knowledge, and chemical therapy. His influence helped keep chemistry tied to medicine and to broad systems of nature rather than only to laboratory analysis.

In the early 17th century, chemists had few shared doctrines. Many accepted the four elements, the Paracelsian principles of salt, sulfur, and mercury, or both. They often built large chemical philosophies that tried to explain the whole universe in chemical terms, and many retained occult tendencies. This made chemical knowledge less cumulative than mechanics or astronomy.

The mechanical philosophy ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mechanism_%28philosophy%29)) changed chemistry by giving chemists a language that seemed respectable, experimental, and compatible with the new science. Robert Boyle is the strongest example of this change. He studied the chemical properties of matter, gave experimental support to mechanical philosophy, and tried to show that chemical properties could be explained mechanically. He was also a skilled laboratory chemist whose work helped make experiment central to chemistry.

## Eighteenth-Century Physics and Chemistry

Newtonian mechanics deepened during the 18th century as mathematicians applied and extended its methods. Leonhard Euler, Jean Le Rond d'Alembert, Joseph-Louis Lagrange, and Pierre-Simon Laplace used the calculus of variations and related mathematical tools to solve problems in mechanics and astronomy. Their work showed that Newtonian theory could become a powerful system for precise calculation.

The lunar theory was especially important because it was connected to the navigational problem of determining longitude at sea. Alexis Clairaut derived equations that were difficult to integrate and at first could not calculate the observed progression of the lunar apogee from Newton's inverse-square law. In 1747, he and Euler considered whether the inverse-square law might be only an approximation. Two years later, more detailed calculations showed that the simple inverse-square relation was enough.

The success of celestial mechanics grew as approximation methods improved. Difficult three-body problems, including the secular acceleration of the Moon, gradually yielded to mathematical treatment. Laplace's five-volume Traite de mecanique celeste, published between 1798 and 1827, presented the solar system as a dynamically stable Newtonian gravitational system. This was a major triumph of mathematical physics.

Eighteenth-century [[Principles of Optics (Optics)|optics]] continued to debate Newton's ideas. Newton's corpuscular view remained influential because it fit his broader theory of matter and because Newtonian gravitational physics was becoming so successful. Yet Euler argued for vibrations in an ether, and later Young and Fresnel made wave theory increasingly persuasive through interference, diffraction, and mathematical explanation.

Eighteenth-century chemistry drew problems from mechanics, light, heat, medicine, and the study of reactions. Newton's Opticks, especially its Queries, encouraged the idea that particles form clusters through specific attractions. This led to tables of affinities, including those of Herman Boerhaave and later Torbern Bergman, who gave quantitative values for affinities in dry reactions and in solution.

The theory of phlogiston, developed from work by Johann Joachim Becher and Georg Ernst Stahl, became influential after the middle of the 18th century. It held that a fiery principle called phlogiston was released during combustion, calcination, and respiration. Air was treated as a receptacle for phlogiston, and combustible or calcinable substances were thought to contain it.

Gas studies helped prepare the chemical revolution. Joseph Black quantitatively studied fixed air, now identified as carbon dioxide. Joseph Priestley discovered dephlogisticated air, later understood as oxygen. These discoveries made the old phlogiston framework harder to maintain and opened the way to a new chemistry based on gases, weight, and reactions.

## Twentieth-Century Astronomy

Modern astronomy made some of its most dramatic advances through research on the large-scale structure and development of the universe. The problem went back to William Herschel's observations of nebulas at the end of the 18th century. Some astronomers interpreted nebulas as island universes, huge stellar systems outside and comparable to the Milky Way. Others treated them as gaseous clouds inside the Milky Way, perhaps forming stars and planetary systems.

Vesto Melvin Slipher began measuring the velocities of nebulas at Lowell Observatory in 1912. He used the Doppler shift of spectral lines, where motion along the line of sight changes the observed wavelength of radiation. By 1925, he had studied about 40 nebulas, most of which showed redshifts and therefore seemed to be moving away from Earth.

Measuring the distances of these nebulas required an indirect method because they were too far for ordinary stellar parallax. Henrietta Swan Leavitt had discovered in 1908 a relation between the period and brightness of Cepheid variable stars. If the same relation held for all such stars, astronomers could use observed periods and apparent magnitudes to estimate distance.

The Cepheid relation made it possible to recognize galaxies beyond the Milky Way and to study cosmic expansion. It connected careful stellar observation to the structure of the universe as a whole. Combined with redshift measurements, it helped turn nebular astronomy into modern extragalactic astronomy and made the large-scale universe a central subject of physical science.

## Twentieth-Century Physics

Between 1896 and 1932, physics underwent a revolution comparable in depth to the scientific revolution of the 16th and 17th centuries. Ideas about space, time, mass, energy, atoms, light, force, determinism, and causality changed radically. Newtonian physics remained powerful, but it no longer looked like the final foundation of nature.

The new physics did not arise simply because a few experiments contradicted older theories. Historians have shown that famous anomalies, such as the failure to detect Earth's absolute motion in the Michelson-Morley experiment, did not directly cause the theories that later explained them. As with Copernicus, deeper motives included the search for coherent, beautiful, and structurally satisfying theories.

The discovery of radioactivity by Henri Becquerel in 1896 is often treated as the beginning of 20th-century physics. The isolation of radium and the study of radioactive change showed that atoms were not permanent, indivisible units. Radioactivity also raised the possibility of transmutation, a change from one element into another, which older chemistry and physics had not accepted in modern form.

J.J. Thomson's discovery of the electron in 1897 further undermined older atomic ideas. Atoms had internal parts. This discovery encouraged new theories of matter and chemical bonding and connected electricity directly to the structure of atoms. The electron became a bridge between physics and chemistry.

Quantum theory, relativity, and nuclear physics transformed the foundations of physical science. They challenged mechanical explanation, altered the meaning of causality and determinism, and made observation itself more important in some interpretations. The revolution did not erase Newtonian physics for ordinary scales, but it changed the deeper picture of what matter, energy, and law could mean.

## Quantum Chemistry

Quantum mechanics ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_mechanics)) finally solved the long-standing problem of the force that holds atoms together in molecules. It is often said that chemistry was reduced to physics through this development, but the relationship was more complex. One of the key postulates of quantum mechanics was introduced mainly to explain chemical facts, not because it already had independent physical support.

Wolfgang Pauli's exclusion principle forbids more than one electron from occupying a given quantum state in an atom. The state of an electron includes its spin, a property introduced by George E. Uhlenbeck and Samuel A. Goudsmit. Together, these ideas made it possible to explain electron shells and the periodic behaviour of elements.

The theory of electron shells explained chemical valence through the loss, gain, or sharing of electrons in the outer shell. This connected the structure of atoms to the formation of molecules. Chemical bonding could now be understood through quantum states rather than through vague attractions or purely mechanical hooks between atoms.

Quantum chemistry ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_chemistry)) addressed several old problems. It explained why chemical forces are saturated, so atoms do not combine without limit. It helped explain stereochemistry, including the three-dimensional direction of bonds such as those around tetrahedral carbon. It also clarified bond length, the stable distance between atoms in a molecule, and why some atoms such as helium normally form no bonds while others form one or more.

Early theories of chemical bonding after J.J. Thomson's electron discovery tried to use electrons directly, but quantum mechanics gave the successful framework. Chemistry therefore became deeply linked to physics without losing its own problems. Valence, bond saturation, molecular shape, and inert elements remained chemical facts that helped guide the development of physical theory.

## Core Equations

Physical models often begin by relating force, energy, waves, and conserved quantities:

$$\vec{F}=m\vec{a}$$

This is Newton's second law: force is the product of mass and acceleration, so a larger mass or a faster change in motion requires a larger net force.

$$E=h\nu$$

This relation says that photon energy is proportional to frequency; higher-frequency radiation carries more energy per photon.

$$E=mc^2$$

This expresses mass-energy equivalence: mass is a form of energy, with the speed of light squared as the conversion factor.
