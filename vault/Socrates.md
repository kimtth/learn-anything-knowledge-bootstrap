# Socrates

> [!abstract] Reference navigation

## Introduction

![[attachments/socrates_001.webp|420]]

*Bust of Socrates in the Louvre*

Socrates, born about 470 BCE and executed in 399 BCE, was an Athenian philosopher whose life, character, and questioning changed ancient and modern philosophy. He wrote nothing himself, but he appears in works by admirers such as Plato and Xenophon. These works portray him as insightful, self-controlled, morally serious, and highly skilled in argument.

He was also controversial in [[Athens]]. Comic dramatists mocked him, especially [[Aristophanes]] in The Clouds ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cloud)) of 423 BCE. At age 70, Socrates was tried for impiety and corrupting the young, sentenced by an Athenian jury, and killed by poison, probably hemlock.

[[Plato and Platonism (Plato)|Plato]]'s Apology of Socrates ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apology_%28Plato%29)) presents the speech Socrates gave in his own defense. Its defense of the examined life and its criticism of Athenian democracy made it one of the central texts of Western thought.

## Philosophical and Literary Sources

![[attachments/socrates_003.webp|420]]

*Detail from The Death of Socrates painting*

Knowledge of Socrates depends on difficult sources. Comic plays show that he was famous, but comedy was not trying to give a balanced portrait. Aristophanes used Socrates to represent intellectual trends in Athens, including the study of language and nature, and implied atheism or moral danger. Plato's Apology says Socrates rejected that image as false.

After Socrates' death, members of his circle wrote Socratic discourses, placing him in conversation with followers, politicians, and thinkers. Most are lost. The surviving full sources are mainly Plato and Xenophon, with later evidence from Aristotle. None can be treated as a simple word-for-word record.

## Xenophon

Xenophon's Memorabilia gives his fullest portrait of Socrates. Its opening chapters directly answer the charges from the trial and therefore supplement Xenophon's own Apology. Some later parts may depend on Plato's dialogues, which lowers their independent evidential value.

Xenophon's Symposium shows Socrates in conversation at a drinking party, and some scholars value it as a recreation of his way of life. The Oeconomicus applies Socratic qualities to household management and farming, but it was probably not meant as a report of an actual conversation.

## Plato

[[Plato and Platonism (Plato)|Plato]] is both the deepest source and the hardest to use. His philosophical genius may have helped him understand Socrates better than Xenophon. Yet that same originality means he often uses Socrates as a literary speaker for ideas that go beyond the historical Socrates.

In some late dialogues, such as Parmenides, Sophist, Statesman, and Timaeus, Socrates has a smaller role; in Laws he is absent. Scholars generally reject the simple idea that dialogues with Socrates as main speaker always present Socrates' own philosophy, while dialogues with less Socrates present Plato's philosophy.

The most historically useful Platonic dialogues are those where Socrates searches without final success for definitions of virtues, such as Laches, Euthyphro, and Charmides. They fit the Apology's picture of Socrates as a questioner concerned with how to become a good human being, not with natural science.

## Aristotle

[[Aristotle and Aristotelianism|Aristotle]] provides important confirmation. He came to Plato's Academy in 367 BCE, about 30 years after Socrates' death, and studied there for 20 years. His information likely came from Plato and others who had known Socrates.

Aristotle says that Socrates asked questions but gave no answers because he lacked knowledge; sought definitions of virtues; and focused on ethics rather than nature. This supports the cautious portrait drawn from Plato's early dialogues.

## Life and Personality

Only limited biographical facts survive, but the portrait is vivid. Socrates' father was Sophroniscus, probably a stonemason; his mother was Phaenarete; his wife was Xanthippe; and he had three sons. Plato compares Socrates' questioning to midwifery because he helped others give birth to ideas.

By conventional standards, Socrates was physically unattractive, with a snub nose and bulging eyes. He served bravely as a hoplite in the Athenian army. Unlike many thinkers, he did not travel widely for intellectual purposes, and he did not seek high office, but he knew many politically important Athenians.

His life showed extreme self-mastery. In Plato's Apology, he says he does not fear death because he does not know whether it is bad. This reflects his confidence that reason should control emotion. He believed fear of death rests on pretending to know what one does not know.

## Method, Virtue, and Irony

The Socratic method is a pattern of cross-examination. Socrates presents himself not as a teacher with answers but as an ignorant inquirer. He asks a question such as "What is piety?" and then tests the answer until contradictions appear. The goal is to expose false confidence and search for moral knowledge.

Socrates held several firm convictions. Human wisdom begins with recognizing one's ignorance. The unexamined life is not worth living. Ethical virtue is the most important good. A good person cannot truly be harmed, because even poverty, injury, or death cannot destroy virtue.

He also treated virtue as knowledge. If a person truly knows what is good, that person will act well. Wrongdoing comes from mistaking something else for the good. This view leads to the unity of virtues: courage, piety, justice, and other virtues cannot be completely separated.

Socrates was famous for eironeia, a Greek term related to irony but closer to dissembling. He often seemed to hide what he knew or meant. This made him fascinating and frustrating, and it helped explain why some Athenians distrusted him. Even in court, readers must ask when he is speaking plainly and when he is using irony.

## Background of the Trial

The trial of Socrates in 399 BCE followed Athens's defeat by Sparta in the Peloponnesian War. Athens was democratic and allowed wide freedom for male citizens, while Sparta was more controlled and aristocratic. Some elite Athenians admired Spartan discipline, and Socrates shared some Spartan-like habits of toughness and simplicity.

[[Athens]]'s political trauma mattered. Events such as religious scandals, oligarchic coups, and the rule of anti-democratic factions made Athenians suspicious of intellectuals and elite critics of democracy. Some of Socrates' associates had links to anti-democratic politics, though Socrates himself did not leave Athens during political crisis.

## Plato's Apology

![[attachments/socrates_002.webp|420]]

*Painting of the Death of Socrates*

[[Plato and Platonism (Plato)|Plato]] presents himself as present at the trial, making his Apology especially important. Xenophon was not present and says he relied on Hermogenes. The two accounts differ, but they agree on many essentials: the charges, the divine sign, the cross-examination of Meletus, the Delphic oracle, Socrates' refusal to propose an acceptable penalty, and his final lack of regret.

The formal charges were that Socrates did not acknowledge the gods recognized by the city, introduced new divinities, and corrupted the young. Meletus used Socrates' claim of a personal divine voice or sign against him. Socrates defended his way of life as obedience to the god connected with Delphi.

The oracle had told Chaerephon that no one was wiser than Socrates. Socrates interpreted this as a divine puzzle and tested people who claimed knowledge. He concluded that he was wiser only because he knew he did not know. His questioning embarrassed politicians, poets, craftsmen, and others, creating resentment.

After conviction, Socrates refused to offer a penalty that would satisfy the jury. He would not admit wrongdoing or promise to stop philosophizing. After the death sentence, he expressed no regret for his life or defense. This ending turned his trial into a lasting symbol of philosophical integrity and conflict between questioning and civic authority.

## Key Events & Developments

- c. 470 BCE: Socrates was born in Athens.
- 423 BCE: [[Aristophanes]]' The Clouds ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cloud)) mocked Socrates on the comic stage.
- 431-404 BCE: The Peloponnesian War created the political background for later suspicion in Athens.
- 399 BCE: Socrates was tried, convicted, and executed by poison.
- 367 BCE: [[Aristotle and Aristotelianism|Aristotle]] entered Plato's Academy, helping preserve later testimony about Socrates.

## Key People

- Socrates ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Socrates)): Athenian philosopher known for ethical questioning, self-mastery, irony, and martyr-like death.
- [[Plato and Platonism (Plato)|Plato]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plato)): Student and literary-philosophical source for Socrates, especially through the Apology and dialogues.
- Xenophon ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Xenophon)): Wrote Memorabilia, Symposium, Oeconomicus, and an Apology defending Socrates.
- [[Aristotle and Aristotelianism|Aristotle]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aristotle)): Later source confirming Socrates' focus on ethics and definitions.
- [[Aristophanes]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aristophanes)): Comic playwright who mocked Socrates in The Clouds.
- Meletus ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meletus)): Accuser who charged Socrates with impiety and corrupting the young.
- Chaerephon ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chaerephon)): Friend who asked the Delphic oracle whether anyone was wiser than Socrates.
- Xanthippe ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Xanthippe)): Socrates' wife.

## Interesting Facts

- Socrates wrote nothing, so his philosophy is known through other writers.
- Apologia means defense, not apology in the modern sense of saying sorry.
- Socratic irony could make Socrates seem both humble and evasive.
- The examined life became one of the most famous ideals in Western philosophy.
- The divine sign was a personal warning voice that Socrates said guided him.
