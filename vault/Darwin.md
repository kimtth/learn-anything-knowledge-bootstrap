## Introduction

Charles Robert Darwin was an English naturalist whose theory of evolution by natural selection changed modern biology. In 1859, he gave full public form to his ideas in On the Origin of Species, but the thinking behind the book had grown over many years. His work first shocked many religious Victorians because it suggested that humans and animals had a shared ancestry, yet it later became central to science, literature, and politics. Darwin was also an agnostic, and his burial in Westminster Abbey showed how deeply his status had changed by the end of his life.

## Early Life and Family

![[attachments/darwin_002.webp|420]]

*Charles Darwin 01*

Darwin was born into a respectable and intellectually rich family. He was the second son of Robert Waring Darwin and Susannah Wedgwood, and his family links tied him to both medicine and industrial nonconformity. His grandfather Erasmus Darwin was a freethinking physician and poet, so Darwin grew up with examples of scientific curiosity and religious independence. His mother died when he was eight, and he was raised mainly by his older sisters, while his father remained a strong and sometimes intimidating presence in his life.

## The Beagle Voyage

![[attachments/darwin_001.webp|420]]

*Charles Darwin by Julia Margaret Cameron, c. 1868*

Darwin's career changed when he joined the HMS Beagle as a gentleman naturalist. The voyage became the great turning point of his life because it gave him a vast range of real-world evidence. At first he suffered badly from seasickness, but he soon began to observe the natural world with unusual intensity. On calm days he studied plankton and wondered why the sea held so much life beyond human use. In 1832, in places such as the Cape Verde Islands and [[Brazil]], he saw signs that land and sea had changed over time, and this helped him accept Charles Lyell's gradual geology.

## Geology and Deep Time

The most important scientific lesson of the voyage was that the Earth was ancient and always changing. When Darwin saw raised mussel beds above the tide line in [[Chile]], and later fossil trees high above the sea, he understood that land itself could rise slowly over immense periods of time. This gave him a sense of deep time ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deep_time)) and helped him think in terms of long, cumulative change instead of sudden creation. By the time the Beagle reached the Galapagos Islands, his thinking was already moving in that direction, even though the later legend of a sudden island revelation is not accurate.

## Fossils, Birds, and Common Descent

As Darwin studied fossils, birds, and broader patterns of life, he saw more evidence that species were not fixed. Richard Owen identified some of his fossil skull material as belonging to extinct South American forms, while John Gould showed that the Galapagos birds were not a random mix but a group of distinct finches and mockingbirds. These findings suggested that animals on one continent or one island could be replaced by closely related forms adapted to local conditions. Darwin now began to think in terms of succession, divergence, and common descent.

## Malthus and Natural Selection

Darwin's thinking changed decisively when he read Thomas Malthus in 1838. Malthus had argued that populations grow faster than food supply, which creates competition and hardship. Darwin applied this idea to nature and saw that if more young were born than could survive, then a constant struggle would follow. In that struggle, small differences mattered. Variants that helped survival would be preserved, while less useful forms would be lost. Darwin called this mechanism natural selection.

## A Theory Kept Secret

By 1839, Darwin had largely formed his theory, but he did not publish it. He knew the idea was dangerous both socially and intellectually. When he showed it in private, even to Emma Darwin, he saw that he needed to present it carefully. He still believed in a lawgiving God, but not in a world shaped by repeated miracles. In 1842 and 1844, he wrote sketches of his theory, yet he kept them secret and even asked Emma to publish them only if he died first.

## The 1850s and Renewed Work

The public climate improved in the 1850s. Mid-Victorian Britain was calmer than the earlier decades, and scientific culture was changing. Darwin had already lost much of his Christian belief after the death of his daughter Annie Darwin in 1851. He then focused on solving the remaining problems in his theory, especially how species could branch and how variation could lead to new forms over time.

## On the Origin of Species and Its Reception

The reaction to On the Origin of Species was mixed but powerful. Many readers were disturbed by the book's refusal to rely on miracles, and some saw it as an attack on religion. Darwin did not appear in public to defend himself; instead, Thomas Henry Huxley became the most active champion of his ideas. Huxley argued for human evolution, defended Darwin at the famous Oxford meeting in 1860, and later wrote his own work on human ancestry.

## Botanical Studies after the Origin

After the Origin, Darwin kept working on specific problems and showed that his theory could explain many different living forms. In The Various Contrivances by which British and Foreign Orchids are Fertilised by Insects in 1862, he showed that orchid structures were shaped by insect pollination rather than by decorative design. He used this kind of detailed case study to show that natural selection could account for beauty, function, and complex arrangement in plants.

## The Descent of Man and Sexual Selection

Darwin also turned to human beings directly in The Descent of Man, and Selection in Relation to Sex in 1871. Here he argued that humans belonged inside the same evolutionary story as other animals. The first volume treated human origins and civilization, while the second examined birds, beauty, and the selection of mates. He used the idea of sexual selection ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sexual_selection)) to explain why animals develop striking features, such as bright plumage, that may help them attract partners.

## Emotion and Expression

Darwin's later work also included studies of emotion and expression. In 1872, he published The Expression of the Emotions in Man and Animals, which extended his earlier interest in orangutans, facial movement, and the meaning of expression. He tried to show that human emotions were not unique gifts placed in the body for spiritual reasons, but part of a broader animal history. This fit his larger claim that humans should be studied as natural beings.

## Ill Health

Darwin's health never became strong. He suffered repeated illness, especially stomach trouble, palpitations, and later angina. He worked under stress for many years and often had to retreat from public life. His domestic routine at Down House helped him keep working, but it did not cure him. He continued to suffer physically while producing his major books.

## Changing Reception

Darwin's reception changed over time. At first, his theory seemed threatening because it challenged religious ideas about creation and human uniqueness. Even after Origin of Species appeared, many readers were more ready to accept evolution in general than the full force of natural selection. Some critics thought his ideas were too materialist, too secular, or too harsh. Others feared that his work would deny human dignity or immortality.

## Legacy

Darwin's legacy rests first on evolution by natural selection, but his influence was wider than one mechanism. He helped establish a new style of science based on close observation, comparison, and long argument across many fields. He also opened the study of human beings to biological explanation, including emotion, sexuality, and ancestry.

## Key Events & Developments

- 1831-1836: Charles Darwin's voyage on the Beagle provides the observations that support his later theory.
- 1859: On the Origin of Species gives evolution by natural selection its classic form.
- later 19th century: Darwin's ideas reshape biology, geology, and the study of human origins.
