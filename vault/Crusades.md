# Crusades

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> The Crusades were medieval military expeditions and wider movements associated with Latin Christian campaigns in the eastern Mediterranean. They transformed relations among European, Byzantine, and Islamic societies.

## Introduction

The Crusades ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crusades)) were military expeditions organized by Western Christians from the late 11th century onward in response to Muslim expansion and other religious-political goals. Crusading aimed to defend or recover the Holy Land, fight pagans, and sometimes attack heresy, while also becoming a major force in the expansion of medieval Europe.

By the end of the 11th century, roughly two-thirds of the old Christian world, including [[Palestine]], [[Syria]], [[Egypt]], and [[Turkey and Ancient Anatolia (Anatolia)|Anatolia]], had been conquered by Muslims, and participants saw crusading as a path to redemption and the expiation of sins. The main expeditions ran from 1095 to 1291, when the Latin Christians were finally expelled from Syria, though campaigns continued for centuries afterward. The movement declined rapidly in the 16th century with the Protestant Reformation and the weakening of papal authority.

## The Council of Clermont

At the Council of Clermont in 1095, Pope Urban II called on Christians to aid the East and promised indulgence to those who took part. The response was immediate, revealing a combination of popular religious fervor, papal leadership, and enough institutional capacity to organize a large expedition.

Urban spoke at a great outdoor assembly on November 18, 1095, stressing the plight of Eastern Christians and the desecration of the holy places, and a council canon renewed the Peace of God and granted a plenary indulgence to those who aided the East. Cries of Deus le volt ("God wills it") rang out, and it was decided that volunteers should wear a cross. To Urban's surprise, not only warrior knights but a large popular element came forward as well.

## From Constantinople to Antioch

The First Crusade began with difficult cooperation between the Western armies and the Byzantines. After victories such as Dorylaeum, the Crusaders advanced through Anatolia and besieged Antioch ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antioch)), where starvation, desertion, and relief armies made the campaign desperate before the city was finally taken.

The Turkish capital of Nicaea surrendered to the Byzantines on June 19, 1097, and at Dorylaeum on July 1 the Crusaders routed a Turkish attack through cooperation between the Western contingents and the Greeks. Along the way Baldwin, Godfrey's brother, broke off to become ruler of Edessa, the first Crusader state and a buffer against Turkish attacks. The siege of Antioch, ringed by walls with more than 400 towers, was long and deadly, and the threat of a relief army under Kerbogha of Mosul drove some, including Peter the Hermit and Stephen of Blois, to attempt desertion.

## The Second Crusade

The loss of Edessa led to the Second Crusade, preached by St. Bernard of Clairvaux and led by major rulers such as Louis VII and Conrad III. The enterprise expanded beyond one front to Spain and the Baltic, yet still failed in the East.

After Edessa fell to Zangi in 1144, Pope Eugenius III issued the bull Quantum praedecessores in 1145, the first of its kind, with detailed provisions protecting Crusaders' families and property. St. Bernard of Clairvaux preached it in France and Germany and reframed the Crusade as a means of redemption, while the campaign opened three fronts: the East, Spain against the Muslims, and the Baltic against the pagan Wends. The Byzantine emperor Manuel Comnenus, alarmed by another army marching toward Constantinople, made a truce with the Turkish sultan of Rum in 1146.

## The Third Crusade

The fall of Jerusalem to Saladin produced the Third Crusade, led by Richard I, Philip II, and Frederick Barbarossa. Richard's military brilliance, Frederick's death on the way east, and the uneasy mix of chivalry, rivalry, and limited success all marked the campaign.

Conrad of Montferrat held Tyre against Saladin, and King Guy, released at the end of 1188, gambled by besieging Acre. The German emperor Frederick Barbarossa defeated a Seljuq army but drowned crossing a stream on June 10, 1190, breaking his army's morale. Richard I, the favourite son of Eleanor of Aquitaine, epitomized the chivalrous Crusader, honouring noble Muslim opponents yet ruthless toward lowborn captives, while his rivalry with Philip II over Angevin lands in France strained the common enterprise.

## The Latin East after the Third Crusade

![[attachments/crusades_003.webp|420]]

*Europe and the Nearer East in the time of the Crusades (Smith, 1915)*

After Saladin's death, the Latin states survived through shifting truces, trade, and dynastic rearrangements. Cyprus, Acre, Tripoli, Antioch, and Armenia all adjusted to a changed political balance in which the Crusader presence remained alive but weaker and more fragmented.

Saladin died on March 3, 1193, and his Ayyubid successors kept a truce with the Crusaders that was advantageous to both sides, letting Italian merchants build profitable trade ties in Egypt. The Franks reorganized a titular kingdom of Jerusalem, the so-called Second Kingdom, centred on Acre, and Amalric of Lusignan united Cyprus and Jerusalem-Acre before they were divided again on his death in 1205. The Livre au Roi, part of the Assizes of Jerusalem, dates from his able reign.

## Constantinople

The Fourth Crusade took a disastrous turn when Boniface of Montferrat and the Crusaders diverted toward Byzantium. This is one of the great reversals of crusading history, because it turned a holy war toward the conquest and sack of Constantinople instead of the recovery of Jerusalem.

Pope Innocent III called the Crusade in 1198 and in 1199 levied the first systematic tax on clerical income, setting a precedent for later papal taxation. Transport was arranged with [[Venice]], and after Thibaut of Champagne died the barons turned to Boniface of Montferrat, whose family ties to the Byzantine throne drew the expedition toward a scheme to depose Alexius III. Innocent III explicitly forbade the diversion, but the Crusaders went ahead, sacking Constantinople instead of marching on Jerusalem.

## The Albigensian Crusade

![[attachments/crusades_001.webp|420]]

*Combat deuxième croisade*

Crusading also moved into western Europe, where heresy was treated as a threat to Christian society. The Albigensian Crusade against the Cathars in southern France showed how religious warfare could be redirected against fellow Christians, with severe violence and long-term political consequences.

Catharism was a dualist faith that saw the universe as a battle between spirit (good) and matter (evil), led by austere "perfects," and it flourished in southern France under the protection of Raymond VI of Toulouse. St. Dominic founded his Order of Preachers to combat it by debate, but after a papal legate was murdered, Innocent III called a Crusade in March 1208. It was popular in northern France because warriors could win the indulgence near home in only 40 days, and at Beziers the Crusaders massacred almost the entire population.

## The Children's Crusade

The Children's Crusade of 1212 was not a true papally sanctioned crusade but a popular movement driven by piety, frustration, and rumor. It is a striking example of how crusading ideals could inspire mass hopes that had little connection to military reality.

No pope called the movement; instead a young man named Nicholas of Cologne claimed divine instruction and believed God would dry up the Mediterranean so he could walk to Palestine. Thousands of children, women, the elderly, and the poor joined his march south, hailed as heroes in every town but ridiculed by the educated clergy. A parallel movement arose in France around Stephen, a 12-year-old shepherd of Cloyes who claimed a vision of Jesus.

## The Teutonic Knights and the Baltic Crusades

The Teutonic Knights redirected crusading energy toward eastern Europe, where they fought pagans in Transylvania and then in Prussia and the Baltic. Their victories produced a durable military-religious state and extended crusading beyond the Mediterranean world.

## The Fifth Crusade

The Fifth Crusade shifted strategy toward Egypt in the hope of weakening Muslim power before attacking Jerusalem. Despite church supervision and strong planning, the campaign failed to achieve its aim.

Innocent III drafted the blueprints at the Fourth Lateran Council in 1215, determined to keep the new effort under church control, but he died before it set out and Honorius III oversaw its progress. The first contingents under King Andrew of Hungary reached Acre in 1217, and in 1218 the combined army moved against [[Egypt]] under John of Brienne, following a strategy Richard the Lion-Heart had once endorsed.

## The Crusade of Frederick II

Frederick II negotiated while delaying his expedition and then entered the East under papal censure. He was a deeply political Crusader whose ambitions in Sicily, Italy, Cyprus, and Jerusalem were bound together in a larger imperial design.

Frederick II claimed the kingship of Jerusalem through marriage to Isabella (Yolande), daughter of John of Brienne, and suzerainty over [[Cyprus]] from his father Henry VI. While he delayed through illness, he received peace envoys from al-Malik al-Kamil of Egypt, but the impatient Pope Gregory IX excommunicated him, so when he finally sailed in 1228 he did so as a Crusader under the church's ban, reaching Cyprus on July 21.

## The Crusades of St. Louis

Louis IX represented the most devout royal ideal of crusading, yet his campaigns also showed the limits of even exemplary leadership. His expedition to Egypt led to heavy losses, despite his personal piety and prestige.

Pope Innocent IV backed the Crusade at the Council of Lyons in 1245, and Louis IX (canonized in 1297) embarked in August 1248 with a formidable army of perhaps 15,000, accompanied by chronicler Jean de Joinville. Leaving France in the hands of his mother Blanche of Castile, he attacked [[Egypt]] and retook Damietta by June 1249, but the campaign soon turned to heavy losses despite his piety and prestige.

## Crusade as Metaphor

![[attachments/crusades_002.webp|420]]

*Crusade, vers 1250*

The word crusade eventually came to mean any zealous common effort for a worthy cause. Medieval holy war was transformed into a lasting cultural metaphor for idealistic struggle against evil.

The Latin word for crusade first appeared in the late 12th or early 13th century, and over several centuries the religio-military campaigns were reinvented as metaphors for zealous struggle. Pierre Dubois in the early 14th century imagined a French-led Crusade seizing control of Christendom, and Christopher Columbus dreamed of a Spanish reconquest of Constantinople and Jerusalem. Proposals against the Ottoman Turks recurred from the Reformation to the age of Louis XIV, keeping the idea alive in public consciousness.

## Key Events & Developments

- 1095: Pope Urban II calls for the first crusade, linking holy war with the recovery of [[Jerusalem]].
- 1099: Crusaders capture Jerusalem and establish Latin rule in the eastern Mediterranean.
- later centuries: Crusading continues in altered forms, but the movement gradually loses its original force.
