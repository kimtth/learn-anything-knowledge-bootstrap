# Venezuela

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> Venezuela is a northern South American state whose Caribbean coast, mountains, plains, forested uplands, and petroleum reserves transformed its society. The source explains how oil-driven urbanization, industrial growth, migration, inequality, public debt, corruption, territorial disputes, and environmental hazards shaped modern Venezuela.

## Introduction

![[attachments/venezuela_001.webp|420]]

*Relief map of Venezuela*

Venezuela is a northern South American country facing the Caribbean Sea and the [[Atlantic Ocean]]. It borders [[Guyana]], [[Brazil]], and [[Colombia]], and its capital, [[Caracas]], is the leading centre of industry, commerce, education, and tourism. The country also administers Caribbean islands such as Margarita Island, La Blanquilla, La Tortuga, Los Roques, and Los Monjes.

Venezuela has long-standing territorial disputes. Since the early 19th century it has claimed land west of the Essequibo River, about 53,000 square miles. It has also disputed maritime boundaries with [[Colombia]].

Petroleum reshaped modern Venezuela. During the 20th century, a poor agrarian society became a fast-growing urban society because of huge oil reserves. That growth also produced deep imbalances: rural migrants and immigrants crowded into cities, urban poverty expanded, public debt grew, and government patronage and corruption became widespread. Natural disasters, especially the floods and mud slides of 1999, added to these social strains.

## Relief

![[attachments/venezuela_002.webp|420]]

*Angel Falls in Venezuela*

Venezuela has three broad elevation zones: lowland plains rising from sea level to about 1,650 feet, mountains reaching about 16,400 feet, and interior forested uplands with peaks above 6,550 feet. Within these zones are seven major regions: islands and coastal plains, the Lake Maracaibo Lowlands, the Merida and Perija ranges, the coastal mountain system, the northwestern valleys and Segovia Highlands, the Llanos, and the Guiana Highlands.

The northern islands and coastal plains include Margarita, La Tortuga, and peninsulas such as Paraguana, Araya, and Paria. Farther east, the Orinoco delta opens to the Atlantic through many swampy channels.

The Venezuelan Andes extend from the Colombian Cordillera Oriental. The Perija Mountains follow the Colombian border, while the Cordillera de Merida divides the Lake Maracaibo and Orinoco River basins.

The Llanos are broad plains in the Orinoco basin, while the Guiana Highlands contain ancient tablelands called tepuis, many rivers, and Angel Falls.

## Drainage

![[attachments/venezuela_003.webp|420]]

*Historic map of the Orinoco River delta*

Venezuela drains mainly through two systems: the larger [[Atlantic Ocean]] watershed and the smaller Caribbean Sea watershed. The Orinoco River and its main tributary, the Caroni, carry about four-fifths of the country’s surface runoff and occupy a basin of about 366,000 square miles.

The Orinoco begins in the southern Guiana Highlands, flows northwest, then north, and finally east to a delta with about 275 miles of Atlantic coastline. In its middle course it receives rivers from the Llanos, including the Apure and Meta, and rivers from the highlands, especially the Caroni.

This striking hydrologic link occurs near the Orinoco source. Because the land gradient is almost level, one branch flows southwest through the Casiquiare River to the Negro River, a tributary of the Amazon, while the other branch continues north through Venezuela. This connects two of South America’s great river systems.

Lake Maracaibo is the largest lake in [[South America]], covering about 5,130 square miles. It connects to the Gulf of Venezuela through a northern channel, so its northern water is brackish while its southern water is fresh. Rivers such as the Catatumbo, Chama, Escalante, and Santa Ana feed the lake, and thousands of oil derricks and pumps rise from its surface.

## Soils

Latosols, often reddish and relatively infertile, are common in the Llanos and the Guiana Highlands. Heavy moisture leaches many soils, leaving resistant minerals such as iron and aluminum compounds. These processes help explain why some large regions are not naturally easy to farm.

The best agricultural soils are river alluvium and recent volcanic deposits, found in the southern Maracaibo Lowlands, near the Llanos, in northern valleys, and in the Orinoco delta. Poor drainage still limits farming in some low areas.

## Climate

Venezuela lies in the tropics, so temperatures change little by season. Elevation matters more than month of the year. More than nine-tenths of the country has a mean annual temperature above 75 F, but [[Caracas]], in a high valley, is cooler than nearby La Guaira, and highland Merida is cooler than sea-level Maracaibo.

The country has a wet season from about May to October and a dry season from December to March. Rainfall varies sharply: the northwest coast is arid, while windward slopes and the southern interior receive much more.

The Llanos and southern interior have enough rainfall for savanna, rainforest, crops, and pasture, but they also face strong flood-and-drought cycles. Heavy tropical downpours can be deadly, as shown by the northern coastal floods and mud slides of 1999.

## Flora

Most vegetation ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vegetation)) in Venezuela is tropical and evergreen or semideciduous. Forests cover about two-fifths of the land, while savanna grasses cover about half. Elevation and rainfall shape the pattern from coastal scrub to rainforest and high mountain plants.

True tropical species usually do not grow well above about 1,500 feet. In interior transition zones, rainforest mixes with tall savanna grasses, while mangrove swamps occupy the Orinoco delta. Higher slopes support semitropical vegetation, tree ferns, and epiphytes such as orchids.

In the Andes, vegetation changes upward from fern forests to mountain vegetation and then to paramo above about 9,800 feet, where trees are scarce and small shrubs and lichens dominate. The southern Maracaibo basin has dense rainforest, while drier areas near the Caribbean support xerophytic scrub and grasses, much of which has been cleared or overused since the mid-20th century.

## Fauna

Venezuela has varied wildlife, including jaguars, ocelots, pumas, monkeys, bears, deer, tapirs, manatees, caimans, alligators, turtles, venomous snakes, boas, anacondas, many birds, reef fish, electric eels, piranhas, and catfishes.

## Conservation

Venezuela has many national parks. Canaima National Park, created in 1962 and named a UNESCO World Heritage site in 1994, protects southeastern rivers and tepuis. Other parks include Aguaro-Guariquito, Cinaruco y Capanaparo, Los Roques, and El Avila near [[Caracas]].

## Urbanization

Settlement patterns in Venezuela range from dense metropolitan centres and mountain valleys to riverbank Indigenous houses and sparsely settled plains. Colonial agriculture helped create towns as market and transport centres, while short gold rushes produced temporary boomtowns. Disease, limited arable land, and difficult lowland conditions kept many rural areas thinly settled.

A northern urban corridor formed during 1920-40. The oil boom in the Maracaibo Lowlands drew people from the Andes, while commercial and agricultural centres such as Barquisimeto, San Cristobal, and [[Barcelona]] also grew. Greater Caracas became the main destination for internal migrants and foreign immigrants.

Between 1940 and 1970, Venezuela urbanized faster than any other Latin American country. Oil jobs, public spending, and the hope of city prosperity pulled people out of rural highlands. Rural hardship was tied to unequal landholding: large latifundios were underused by wealthy owners, while small minifundios were overworked and barely supported families.

## Decentralization

Since the 1950s, roads, pipelines, airports, bridges, tunnels, and power projects have changed Venezuela’s population map, extending the main northern axis from San Cristobal and [[Caracas]] toward Lake Maracaibo, Valencia, and the lower Orinoco River valley.

In the 1960s, the government tried to reduce the dominance of the Caracas-Valencia core by building an industrial growth pole at Ciudad Guayana, where steel and aluminum mills stood near mining resources and the Orinoco-Caroni river junction.

Decentralization added pipelines, power links, airports, parks, reserves, and ecotourism, but terrain kept inequalities in place.

## Languages

Venezuela is mainly Spanish-speaking, but Indigenous groups speak more than 25 languages. Most of those languages belong to the Cariban, Arawak, and Chibcha families.

## Religion

Venezuela guarantees freedom of religion, and religious tolerance is generally observed. Most people are at least nominally Roman Catholic. Protestant groups form the largest minority, with smaller Jewish and Muslim communities.

Some Indigenous religions continue, although many Indigenous people have converted to Catholicism. The Roman Catholic church is officially apolitical, but priests and bishops have entered debates over liberation theology, reform, and radical government policies.

## Minerals

The Venezuelan economy ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economy)) is built chiefly on petroleum. From the late 1940s to 1970, the country was the world’s largest petroleum exporter, and it remains a major oil supplier to the [[United States of America (United States)|United States]]. Oil earnings funded modernization, diversification, infrastructure, public works, and the national slogan sembrando el petroleo, or “sowing the oil.”

Venezuela also has iron ore, nickel, coal, bauxite, hydroelectric power, and large natural gas deposits. Petroleum and gas mining produce about one-fifth of GDP but employ less than one percent of workers. Major oil areas include the Orinoco region, the Llanos, and the Lake Maracaibo Lowlands.

Before nationalization, multinational companies produced most oil and refined much of it offshore in Aruba, Curacao, and other Caribbean centres. After nationalization, Petroleos de Venezuela, SA, or PDVSA, controlled production but still relied on foreign firms for refining, transport, marketing, and technical help.

Economic trouble in the late 1980s and 1990s pushed the state to reopen parts of the petroleum sector to foreign investment, especially for heavy crude in the Orinoco basin. Hugo Chavez reversed that trend in 2006-07, when the oil industry became a central target of renewed nationalization and the state seized operational control of the last privately run Orinoco basin projects.

## Hydroelectric

Hydroelectricity supplies about half of Venezuela’s power. The main generating centre is the Guri Dam on the Caroni River, which provides electricity for Ciudad Guayana and nearby mining complexes. The Santo Domingo River and other short Andean rivers add more power resources.

Oil-, gas-, and coal-fired thermal generators supply the rest of the country’s electricity. More than nine-tenths of Venezuelans have household access to electricity, one of the stronger rates in Latin America, but the national grid needs costly repairs and power outages are frequent.

## Agriculture, Fishing, and Forestry

Before the 1950s, agriculture ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agriculture)), fishing, and forestry produced more than half of Venezuela’s GDP. Their share fell as oil and petrochemicals expanded, and by the petrochemical growth period of the 1970s and 1980s, the agricultural share of the workforce dropped from about one-fifth to about one-tenth.

The government has used subsidies and low-interest loans to support farming since the 1990s, but agriculture, fishing, and forestry contribute less to GDP than before. Coffee is the main cash crop, while corn and rice are staple food crops. Most cropland lies in the northern mountains and foothills.

Cattle grazing is extensive in the Llanos and more limited in the Maracaibo Lowlands. South of the Orinoco, interior forests support shifting cultivation and small river plots. Less than one-fourth of national land is used for crops or grazing.

## Industry

Before the 1950s, Venezuela had little industry beyond food processing and petroleum extraction. Oil revenue, cheap energy, raw materials, available capital, and protective tariffs helped manufacturing grow, especially after state efforts to diversify the economy in the 1960s.

The oil price rise of 1973-74 greatly increased state revenue and encouraged large resource-based projects, including iron and steel, aluminum, transport equipment, and petrochemicals. Later oil price declines slowed this growth. Manufacturing now provides about one-sixth of GDP and employment.

Venezuelan industry has three main groups. The most important is oil storage, transport, refining, and petrochemicals. The northwest includes the oldest petrochemical region and the Paraguana Peninsula refining centre, which handles more than two-thirds of domestic refining. El Tablazo, Moron, and eastern Orinoco facilities form other important centres.

Other industries include basic metals and consumer goods. The Ciudad Guayana complex uses nearby iron ore, bauxite, and hydroelectric power for steel and aluminum, while factories also make foods, textiles, chemicals, machinery, vehicles, and construction materials.

## Services

The service sector ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tertiary_sector)) accounts for about half of GDP and more than half of employment in Venezuela. Finance and trade are especially important, each producing about one-sixth of GDP.

## Finance

Since 1958, the state has strongly shaped the financial system. The Central Bank of Venezuela sets interest rates, regulates the money supply, issues the bolivar fuerte, and lends to commercial banks. Other state banks include the Industrial Bank, Workers’ Bank, and regional banks, while private banks, investment banks, insurance firms, and the stock exchange are concentrated in [[Caracas]].

Venezuela helped found OPEC in 1960. When oil prices rose more than 400 percent in 1973-74, it gained windfall profits, influence, and favourable energy agreements.

The financial system suffered after the Latin American debt crisis of 1982 and the oil price collapse of 1986. Foreign exchange problems, falling reserves, slow growth, and inflation led to agreements with the International Monetary Fund and the World Bank in 1989. Problems returned during the 1990s because of oil-price swings, political instability, a banking crisis, and overborrowing.

## Trade

Oil dominates Venezuela’s foreign trade and provides more than three-fourths of export earnings. The country has usually maintained a positive trade balance because of petroleum exports.

## Transportation

Venezuela has a developed transportation system in the north and northwest. Travel depends mainly on roads, while freight uses shipping, waterways, and pipelines.

The country has about 22,400 miles of paved roads, including the Pan-American Highway, northwestern routes toward Lake Maracaibo, and the Llanos Highway. Railways are secondary, while sea trade uses ports such as Maracaibo, Puerto Cabello, and La Guaira. Dredged channels connect Lake Maracaibo and the Orinoco River to oceangoing traffic.

## Government

The 1999 constitution defines Venezuela as republican, democratic, and federalist. Universal suffrage began in 1946, and the state has executive, legislative, and judicial branches.

From 1961 to 1999, Venezuela had a directly elected president serving one five-year term, a bicameral legislature, and a judicial system headed by the Supreme Court. Economic trouble and anger over corruption in the 1980s and 1990s helped Hugo Chavez Frias win power and push for a new constituent assembly.

The 1999 constitution, modeled partly on the Fifth French Republic, expanded presidential power, replaced the bicameral legislature with a unicameral National Assembly, reformed the judiciary, promised more personal liberties, recognized Indigenous rights, and renamed the country the Bolivarian Republic of Venezuela. The president serves a six-year term and is commander in chief of the armed forces.

The old party system centred on Democratic Action and COPEI, but both collapsed in the 1998 election. Chavez led the Movement of the Fifth Republic, later replaced by the United Socialist Party of Venezuela. The country has 23 states and the federal district of [[Caracas]].

## Education

Basic education in Venezuela is free and compulsory from ages 6 to 15. Secondary education lasts two years and is free but not required. More than nine-tenths of people aged 15 and older are literate, and most children attend school, though many adults lack secondary or formal education.

## Health and Welfare

The Venezuelan welfare state expanded strongly during the 1970s, especially in cities. Public medical assistance is free, while private care is also available. The Ministry of Health manages many services, and social security programs cover illness, maternity, disability, old age, and unemployment.

## The Arts

Since the 1920s, the Venezuelan government has promoted artistic expression as a way to protect cultural autonomy from foreign influence. Greater freedom in writing and publishing helped create a national literature.

Romulo Gallegos, author of Dona Barbara, became the best-known Venezuelan writer. Painters such as Armando Reveron and Manuel Cabre, architect Carlos Raul Villanueva, the Venezuelan Symphony Orchestra, and El Sistema also expressed national cultural pride.

## Cultural Institutions

Most major cultural institutions are in [[Caracas]]. The Museum of Fine Arts, founded in 1938, holds Venezuelan and foreign paintings and sculpture. Other museums include the Museum of Colonial Art, housed in an 18th-century mansion, and the Science Museum Foundation, founded in 1875 as the National Museum.

## History

The earliest inhabitants of Venezuela were food-gathering Indigenous peoples who arrived in the Upper Paleolithic Period. Later groups included Arawak and Carib peoples. Nomadic hunters and fishers lived around Lake Maracaibo, the Llanos, and the coast, while the most technologically advanced Indigenous communities farmed in the Andes.

Christopher Columbus reached the region in 1498, and Europeans later called it Venezuela, or “Little Venice,” after seeing houses on stilts. Early contact centred on slave hunting and pearl fishing, and Cumana was founded in 1523.

Spanish settlement expanded through encomiendas, which granted land and Indigenous labour. [[Caracas]] was founded in 1567, and by 1600 more than 20 settlements existed in the Venezuelan Andes and Caribbean coast. Missionary orders later entered the Llanos and Maracaibo regions, while agriculture and stock raising became the basis of colonial life.

Colonial Venezuela exported sugar, cacao, tobacco, and hides and relied on corn, beans, and beef at home. Spanish officials and clergy held top offices, but Creole landowners controlled wealth, while mestizos, mulattoes, Indigenous peoples, and enslaved Africans had little power.

Creole movements for independence ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Independence)) began before the wider Latin American revolutions. A failed republican proclamation came in 1797, and Francisco de Miranda tried unsuccessfully to land on the coast in 1806. He later led a junta that drafted a constitution, but after an armistice with Spain, other revolutionaries handed him to the Spanish, and he died in prison in 1816.

Simon Bolivar, born in [[Caracas]] in 1783, became commander in 1813. His struggle against royalists was difficult, but royalist abuses, Haitian aid, British and Irish volunteers, and the defection of llaneros and Black fighters strengthened the republican side.

The Republic of Gran Colombia was proclaimed in 1819, with Bolivar as president. His forces, reinforced by Jose Antonio Paez, won at Carabobo in 1821; the last royalists surrendered at Puerto Cabello in 1823.

After independence, regional rivalries broke Gran Colombia apart. Venezuela separated in 1829, [[Ecuador]] followed, and Bolivar died in Santa Marta in 1830. Venezuela then entered more than a century of caudillo rule, in which military leaders used personal armies and state power for factional and personal advantage.

Jose Antonio Paez dominated politics from 1830 to 1848. He helped separate Venezuela from Gran Colombia, subdued caudillos, and ruled with landholders and merchants. The 1830 constitution created a centralist state, property-based voting, free trade protections, and continued slavery.

Opposition grew in the 1840s under Antonio Leocadio Guzman and the Liberal Party, which demanded abolition of slavery, wider voting rights, and protection for debtors. Falling world demand for agricultural exports deepened economic hardship and weakened Conservative control.

Jose Tadeo Monagas, elected in 1846, moved toward the Liberals, defeated Paez, and began a decade of Monagas dominance. Liberal laws abolished slavery and widened suffrage, but weak implementation and corruption damaged the state.

The Federalist Wars followed the fall of the Monagas dynasty in 1858. Liberals fought for federalism, democracy, and social reform; Conservatives defended centralism and the old order. The wars were extremely bloody, and power changed hands several times before Liberal generals Juan Falcon and Antonio Guzman Blanco won in 1863. A new constitution in 1864 adopted federal principles, but local freedoms soon fell under provincial caudillos.

Antonio Guzman Blanco seized power in 1870 and combined military control with reform. He restored credit, encouraged foreign investment, built public education, proclaimed religious liberty, legalized civil marriage, and attacked church privileges, while ruling autocratically.

After Guzman Blanco, political disorder returned until Joaquin Crespo took power in 1892. Crespo’s rule faced economic trouble and a major boundary dispute with Great Britain over the eastern frontier with British Guiana. U.S. pressure under Grover Cleveland forced arbitration, but the 1899 decision did not satisfy Venezuela’s demands.

## The Andinos

In 1899, Cipriano Castro, a caudillo from Tachira, marched on [[Caracas]] and took the presidency. Five military strongmen from Tachira, known as Andinos, then controlled Venezuela for most of the next 59 years, except for the 1945-48 interlude.

Castro ruled from 1899 to 1909 through tyranny, financial irresponsibility, domestic revolt, and foreign conflict. His refusal to compensate foreigners for property damaged in uprisings led to a British-German-Italian blockade in 1902-03 and a Dutch attack on the navy in 1908.

Juan Vicente Gomez took power when Castro left for medical treatment in 1908 and ruled until 1935. He manipulated elections, abolished organized politics, silenced the press, and maintained order through police and military force.

That harsh political order attracted foreign petroleum investors. By 1928, Venezuela was the world’s leading oil exporter and second only to the [[United States of America (United States)|United States]] in oil production. Oil brought jobs, revenue, roads, ports, and debt repayment, but wealth stayed unequal.

After Gomez, Eleazar Lopez Contreras restored civil liberties and allowed political activity and labour organization in 1936, but he tightened control again when opposition grew. He also began a development plan for schools, hospitals, agriculture, and private industry.

Isaias Medina Angarita governed from 1941 to 1945, continued development, restored political liberties, and used a 1943 oil law to raise the state share of petroleum profits. In October 1945, military officers and civilian leaders overthrew him, allowing Democratic Action to become the first mass party to hold national power.

Romulo Betancourt led the 1945-48 civilian-military junta. A 1947 constitution reflected labour-left ideas, and Romulo Gallegos won the presidency, but a military coup in November 1948 ended the reform experiment.

Marcos Perez Jimenez controlled Venezuela from 1951 to 1957. He banned politics, crushed labour, closed universities, silenced the press, abandoned Democratic Action reforms, modernized [[Caracas]], and enriched himself and military allies. Popular opposition, with the navy and air force, overthrew him in January 1958.

The second Betancourt administration, from 1959 to 1964, was more moderate than the first. Democratic Action cooperated with the Christian Democrats, modernized agriculture, developed industry, improved health, attacked illiteracy, passed agrarian reform in 1960, and began a national steel industry in 1962. It also faced guerrilla insurgency, depression, and diplomatic breaks with the Dominican Republic and Cuba.

Raul Leoni won the tense 1963 election, and oil, iron ore, and petrochemicals expanded. In 1968, Rafael Caldera Rodriguez won, marking Venezuela’s first peaceful transfer from an incumbent government to an opposition electoral victor.

Carlos Andres Perez, elected in 1973, nationalized iron ore in 1975 and petroleum in 1976. The oil boom brought revenue but also spending, imports, corruption, and elite privilege. Later oil decline caused stagnation, inflation, unemployment, capital flight, and debt problems.

Perez returned to power in 1988, but austerity measures, including higher bus fares, caused nationwide riots in 1989. Troops killed hundreds while suppressing looting. Protests, strikes, market reforms, and political anger followed. In 1992, junior officers led by Hugo Chavez Frias attempted a coup, and another coup attempt followed later that year. Perez survived but was removed in 1993 over misuse of public funds.

Rafael Caldera returned as an independent in 1993, faced a banking crisis, negotiated with the International Monetary Fund, and released Chavez from prison before trial.

## The Hugo Chavez Presidency

By the 1998 election, more than half of Venezuelans lived below the poverty line, inflation exceeded 30 percent, and oil prices were falling. Voters rejected Democratic Action and COPEI and elected Hugo Chavez, whose platform promised to fight corruption, help the poor, reduce elite power, write a new constitution, and remake democracy.

In 1999, Venezuelans elected a pro-Chavez constituent assembly and approved a new constitution. That same year, severe rains caused mud slides and floods along the northern coast and parts of [[Caracas]], killing thousands and making reconstruction urgent.

Chavez used petroleum income for social programs, including education, food coupons, and services for the working class, but his economic reforms provoked strong opposition. Protests and strikes grew in 2001. A coup briefly removed him in April 2002, but supporters and loyal troops restored him within two days.

Opponents organized a general strike in late 2002, badly damaging the economy and especially the oil sector. The strike weakened by February 2003. In 2004, the opposition forced a recall referendum, but Chavez survived with nearly three-fifths of the vote. He continued supporting Fidel Castro, supplied discounted petroleum to Cuba and other developing countries, opposed U.S. influence, and promoted Mercosur.

Chavez won a new six-year term in 2006 and expanded nationalization. In 2007, the state took operational control of foreign-owned oil projects in the Orinoco basin and sought at least 60 percent national ownership. Venezuela also set its clocks back half an hour. Later that year voters narrowly rejected a referendum that would have allowed indefinite presidential reelection.

The defeat did not stop Chavez. In 2008, he nationalized telecommunications, electricity, steel, and cement companies. In 2009, voters approved a referendum removing term limits for all elected officials, allowing him to run again. In 2010, his United Socialist Party of Venezuela won more than half the National Assembly seats but lost the two-thirds majority needed to block opposition influence over some laws and appointments.

In 2010, Chavez broke relations with [[Colombia]] after accusations that Venezuela sheltered FARC rebels, but ties resumed after a meeting with Colombian president Juan Manuel Santos. In 2011, Chavez revealed that he had cancer and repeatedly traveled to Cuba for treatment.

Despite illness, Chavez defeated Henrique Capriles Radonski in the October 2012 election with more than 54 percent of the vote. He returned to Cuba for surgery in December 2012, missed his scheduled inauguration, came back to Venezuela in February 2013, and died on March 5, 2013. Vice president Nicolas Maduro became acting president.

A special election on April 14, 2013 placed Maduro against Capriles. Maduro won narrowly, while Capriles demanded a fuller recount and alleged voting irregularities. The electoral council ordered an audit of unaudited precincts, but Capriles rejected the process because signatures and fingerprints would not be examined. Maduro was sworn in on April 19.

By late 2013 and early 2014, Venezuela’s economy had slowed sharply, inflation exceeded 50 percent, basic goods such as toilet paper, milk, and flour became scarce, and crime added to public anger. Student protests that began in San Cristobal spread to [[Caracas]] and other cities. Security forces and armed pro-government civilian groups responded violently, and by late February 2014 about a dozen people had died.

## Key Events & Developments

- 1498-1567: [[Columbus]] arrived, Cumana became the first permanent Spanish settlement, and [[Caracas]] was founded.
- 1819-23: Bolivar led independence through Gran Colombia, Carabobo, and the final royalist surrender.
- 1829-63: Venezuela separated from Gran Colombia and passed through caudillo rule, Monagas dominance, and the Federalist Wars.
- 1870-1935: Guzman Blanco reformed the state, then Castro and Gomez began Andino dominance as oil exports rose.
- 1945-58: Democratic Action reform, military dictatorship, and the fall of Perez Jimenez opened modern party democracy.
- 1975-89: Oil nationalization and austerity showed the boom-and-bust pattern of the petroleum economy.
- 1992-99: Chavez rose from failed coup leader to president and created the Bolivarian Republic of Venezuela.
- 2002-14: Chavez survived a coup and recall, deepened nationalization, died, and was succeeded by Maduro amid crisis.
