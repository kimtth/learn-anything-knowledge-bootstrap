# Philosophy of Mind

> [!abstract] Reference navigation

## Introduction

Philosophy of mind ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philosophy_of_mind)) reflects on mental phenomena and especially on the relation between mind, body, and the physical world. It asks general questions about thought, feeling, perception, consciousness, sensory experience, rationality, and mental content. Unlike empirical psychology, it is concerned not only with what happens in actual humans but also with what is possible, necessary, or essential for having mental states.

Questions about whether [[Computers|computers]], extraterrestrials, ghosts, or beings with different bodies could have minds clarify what philosophers mean by mental phenomena. Empirical findings from neuroscience, psychology, medicine, and artificial intelligence matter, but philosophy asks whether particular physical structures are essential to fear, pain, thought, consciousness, and understanding.

This is why philosophy of mind treats possible cases as seriously as actual ones: a theory of mind should explain not only human brains but also whether differently built systems could think, feel, or understand.

## Understanding and Explanation

The philosophy of mind also addresses whether human action requires forms of understanding different from natural-scientific explanation. Max Weber distinguished empathic understanding, or Verstehen, from causal explanation. Later philosophers such as Donald Davidson and Daniel Dennett stressed rational interpretation: explaining action often requires reasons, meanings, beliefs, and desires rather than only physical correlations.

## Metaphysical Categories

Mental phenomena can be described through basic metaphysical categories. Substances are basic stuffs or things; objects include ordinary things, unobservable scientific entities, and puzzling mental objects such as pains or images. Philosophers distinguish concrete objects located in space and time from abstract objects such as numbers, propositions, meanings, and thought types.

Phantom limb pain makes the category problem vivid: a pain can seem located in a limb that no longer exists, so the object, location, and experience cannot be treated as ordinary physical objects.

## Thoughts, Types, and Tokens

[[Thought and Thought Processes (Thought)|Thoughts]] can be shareable propositions or individual mental events. The thought that snow is white may be expressed in English, German, or French, suggesting that propositions differ from sentence tokens. Philosophers distinguish types from tokens: word tokens or thought tokens occur at particular times, while word types and thought types can be instantiated repeatedly.

## Concepts and Propositional Attitudes

Concepts are constituents of thoughts much as words are constituents of sentences. They may refer to real things, fictional things, abstract things, or nothing. Beliefs, hopes, fears, expectations, and desires are often called propositional attitudes because agents stand in relations to propositions, as when someone believes that the stock market will fall or wants to be a doctor.

Concepts can also differ between people even when they use the same word, because each person's token concept may connect with different memories, beliefs, and expectations.

## Sensations and Consciousness

Sensations such as pains, itches, images, and qualitative experiences do not always fit propositional-attitude models. Consciousness can mean mental activity, wakefulness, or first-person acquaintance with experience. Cartesian views emphasize incorrigible access to one's own states, but introspection has proved difficult and unreliable. Bertrand Russell, Ludwig Wittgenstein, Ray Jackendoff, Richard Nisbett, and Timothy Wilson all shape debates about self-knowledge.

Experiments reviewed by Nisbett and Wilson suggest that people sometimes confabulate explanations of their own problem-solving, so first-person reports cannot always be treated as direct access to mental processes.

## Rationality

Philosophers distinguish four forms of rationality. Deduction guarantees truth if premises are true and is formalized in symbolic logic. Induction makes conclusions probable through representative samples. Abduction, or inference to the best explanation, explains data by positing unobserved causes, as in detective reasoning or scientific theory. Practical reason moves from belief and desire to action.

These forms of reason all involve propositional attitudes, especially beliefs and desires, which is why rational explanation and intentional content are closely connected.

## Intentionality

Intentionality ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intentionality)), in Franz Brentano's sense, is the aboutness of mental states. Beliefs, thoughts, feelings, and perceptions represent or concern something, unlike stones or stars. Words, paintings, and gestures have derived intentionality from minds, while mental states are often said to have original intentionality. Whether computers can possess original intentionality is a major controversy.

## Reductionism and Identity Theory

Identity theory explains mental states as physical states. J.J.C. Smart compared mental-physical identity to lightning being electrical discharge or water being H2O. The view promises causal unity and ontological economy, but type-identity theories face the problem that different organisms, brains, or possible extraterrestrials might share the same mental state without sharing the same physical state type.

## Dualism

*Statue of Rene Descartes at the Louvre*

Dualism holds that mental phenomena cannot be reduced to physical phenomena. Cartesian substance dualism treats mind as a distinct substance, sometimes an immortal soul. Property dualism is more modest, claiming that mental properties are not physical properties. Interactionism faces the problem that bodily processes appear physically continuous, while epiphenomenalism risks making mental states causally idle.

## Wittgenstein and Ordinary Language

Ludwig Wittgenstein resisted treating inner life as a set of hidden objects. He argued that meaning depends on use within language games and that mental terms need not refer to mysterious inner entities. Talk of thought, sensation, or understanding should be examined through ordinary criteria, forms of life, and outward practices, not forced into object-like models.

## Functionalism

Functionalism ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Functionalism_%28philosophy_of_mind%29)) defines mental states by their roles in systems: relations to inputs, outputs, and other states. Influenced by Hilary Putnam, David Lewis, and Frank Ramsey, it compares mental states to computer states or machine parts. Functionalism allows multiple physical realizations, avoiding strict type-identity reduction while preserving a systematic account of belief, desire, thought, and intention.

Its appeal is that a mental state can be identified by what it does inside a system, rather than by the particular biological material that realizes it.

## Computational Theory of Thought

The computational-representational theory of thought, or CRTT, was developed by thinkers such as Allen Newell, Herbert Simon, Hilary Putnam, Gilbert Harman, and especially Jerry Fodor. It holds that thinking consists in manipulating representational tokens in a language of thought. Alan Turing's model of computation made it plausible that formal reasoning could be mechanized by simple rule-governed steps.

## Objections and Artificial Intelligence

Critics argue that computational theories do not match introspection, risk positing hidden homunculi, or fail to capture consciousness. CRTT replies that it explains underlying processes, not introspective appearance, and Turing-machine steps require no intelligence. Artificial intelligence raises questions about programming, innate structure, machine problem-solving, and whether human cognition is itself physically programmed.

The Turing machine is important because it shows how formal symbol manipulation can be broken into simple mechanical operations that require no inner interpreter.

## Phenomenal Consciousness and Qualia

Ned Block distinguished access consciousness from phenomenal consciousness. Thomas Nagel's bat argument and Frank Jackson's Mary case suggest that physical knowledge may not capture what experience feels like. Qualia debates ask whether subjective feels can be explained by functional, computational, physical, or representational theories.

## Representationalism

Representationalism treats qualitative experience as representational content rather than as private mental objects. J.J.C. Smart suggested that talk of pains or itches can classify experiences without positing inner objects. Gilbert Harman, William Lycan, and Michael Tye defend wide content tied to worldly properties, while Peter Carruthers and Georges Rey defend narrower views. Explanatory gaps remain controversial.

## Key Events & Developments

- 1918: Max Weber exemplified Verstehen debates in social explanation.
- 1953: Wittgenstein's Philosophical Investigations reshaped mental-language analysis.
- 1963: J.J.C. Smart defended materialist identity theory.
- 1974: Thomas Nagel asked what it is like to be a bat.
- 1975: Jerry Fodor published The Language of Thought.
- 1982: Frank Jackson formulated the Mary argument.
