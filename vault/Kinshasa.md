# Kinshasa

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> Kinshasa is the capital and largest city of the Democratic Republic of the Congo, located on the Congo River. Its political power, rapid growth, migration, inequality, shortages, and cultural creativity make it a central expression of modern Congolese and African urban life.

## Introduction

![[attachments/kinshasa_001.webp|420]]

*View of La Gombe in Kinshasa*

Kinshasa, called Leopoldville until 1966, is the capital and largest city of the Democratic Republic of the Congo. It lies on the south bank of the Congo River, about 320 miles from the Atlantic Ocean, and is a special political unit with its own governor.

Kinshasa's character mixes national power, wealth, poverty, shortages, migration, and cultural invention. Its people, the Kinois, live with difficult supplies, yet strongly influence African intellectual and popular culture.

## Landscape

The city site spreads south from Malebo Pool, a widening of the Congo River. Nearby countryside is farmed savanna and gallery forest producing cassava, sugarcane, oil palms, plantains, corn, peanuts, and beans. The climate is hot all year, with a dry season from May to September and a rainy season from October to May.

The city layout includes industrial, residential, commercial, port, elite, and squatter zones. Gombe contains government offices, embassies, and wealthy housing. Boulevard du 30-Juin is the main commercial artery, while poorer districts and informal settlements spread southward.

## People

Population growth changed Kinshasa from 5,000 people in 1889 to nearly five million by the early 21st century. Growth came from migration, boundary expansion, government expansion, rural poverty, and the attraction of city opportunities.

The population is very young, with more than half under age 22. Since independence, most migrants have come from within Congo, especially Bandundu and Bas-Congo, and many recent arrivals live in squatting zones.

## Economy

Kinshasa's economy dominates Congolese consumer, industrial, financial, and commercial life. It houses major public corporations, private firms, banks, food processing, beer, textile, footwear, construction, and service industries.

Food supply is a constant pressure. Bas-Congo provides much of the capital's food, but shortages remain politically sensitive. Poor residents rely on markets, vendors, gardens, and firewood, while overcultivation and tree cutting cause erosion.

## Transportation

![[attachments/kinshasa_003.webp|420]]

*Boulevard du 30 Juin in Kinshasa*

[[Transportation]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transport)) links Kinshasa by road to Matadi and Kikwit, by rail to Matadi, by river toward Kisangani, and by air through Ndjili International Airport. A ferry connects Kinshasa with Brazzaville across Malebo Pool.

Urban transport is overcrowded and inadequate. Buses, minibuses, taxis, and fula-fula trucks move passengers, but congestion, poor maintenance, and shortages of spare parts weaken service.

## Administration And Society

Political power is concentrated in Kinshasa because national decisions are made there. Since 1982, the city administration has included a governor, vice-governors, and zone commissioners.

Public services lag behind growth. Water, electricity, sanitation, planning, roads, and housing are uneven, especially in poorer and informal districts. The built environment contrasts elite villas with rough shelters and unpaved streets.

## History

Kinshasa's site developed from Nshasa and Ntamo, later Kintamo, villages dominated by the Bahumbu and visited by Bateke fishermen and traders. Henry Morton Stanley visited in 1877, returned in 1881, and founded Leopoldville for Leopold II.

Colonial growth depended on transport. The Matadi railway was completed in 1898, an oil pipeline in 1914, air service in 1920, and the Belgian Congo's administrative headquarters moved from Boma to Leopoldville in 1923.

Independence ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Independence)) made Leopoldville the capital in 1960, and the city became Kinshasa in 1966. It grew rapidly, then became central to the rebellion against Mobutu Sese Seko, who fell in 1997. Continued insurgency and hardship persisted into the 21st century.
