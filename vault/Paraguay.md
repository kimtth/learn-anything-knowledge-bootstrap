## Introduction

Paraguay ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paraguay)) is a landlocked country in south-central South America, with its capital at Asuncion. Its modern history has been shaped by war, authoritarianism, civil conflict, dictatorship, and difficult democratization. The War of the Triple Alliance, the Chaco War, the 1947 civil war, and the dictatorship of Alfredo Stroessner left deep social and political legacies.

Paraguay has a relatively homogeneous population, mostly of European and Guarani ancestry. Guarani culture is strongly represented in folk art, festivals, identity, and language. Guarani became an official language under the 1992 constitution, and many Paraguayans are proud to speak it. Rivers are central to the economy, providing access toward the Atlantic and sites for major hydroelectric exports.

## Land

![[attachments/paraguay_001.webp|420]]

*Relief location map of Paraguay*

Paraguay is bordered by [[Bolivia]], [[Brazil]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brazil)), and [[Argentina]]. The Paraguay River divides the country into the Eastern Region and the Western Region, or Chaco Boreal. The Eastern Region is an extension of the Brazilian Plateau, with hills, river valleys, the Parana Plateau, and favourable settlement lands. The Chaco Boreal covers about two-thirds of the country and forms part of the flat tropical Gran Chaco.

## Relief and Drainage

The Eastern Region includes the Amambay, Mbaracayu, and San Rafael ranges, the broad Paraguay River valley, and the Parana Plateau. The Chaco Boreal is flat and largely featureless. Four-fifths of Paraguay's perimeter follows the Paraguay, Apa, Parana, and Pilcomayo rivers. The Parana and Paraguay rivers meet at the southwestern corner, while the Pilcomayo forms much of the southern Chaco boundary.

## Soils, Climate, and Ecology

Eastern Paraguay has deep red sandy [[Soils|soils]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soil)), sandstone-derived soils near Asuncion, fertile basaltic soils on the Parana Plateau, and transported soils along the Paraguay River. The Chaco has alluvial mud, clay, and sand from the Bolivian highlands. The climate is subtropical in the east and tropical in the Chaco, with hot summers, cooler winters, floods, droughts, and most rain from October to April.

Paraguay's plant life includes Chaco hardwoods, red quebracho for tannin, bottle-trunk samuu, cedars, palms, mate-producing Ilex paraguariensis, palo santo, cacti, thorn scrub, and medicinal plants. Wildlife and habitats have been pressured by agriculture, deforestation, logging, and land conflict, particularly in the eastern frontier.

## People, Language, and Religion

Most Paraguayans are mestizo and identify strongly with Guarani descent. Immigrant communities include Brazilians, Germans, Italians, Spaniards, Japanese, Koreans, Chinese, Taiwanese, and German-speaking Mennonites. Indigenous peoples include Pai-Tavytera, Mbya, Ache, Chiripa, Toba, Maskoy, Lengua, Nivakle, Tapiete, Ayoreo, and Chamacoco groups, some threatened by assimilation and land loss.

Spanish and Guarani are official languages. Guarani is spoken by nearly nine-tenths of the population, while Spanish dominates government and business; at least half the population is bilingual. About nine-tenths of Paraguayans are Roman Catholic, with evangelical Protestants forming an important minority. The constitution recognizes no official religion.

## Settlement and Demography

![[attachments/paraguay_003.webp|420]]

*Montage of Asuncion, Paraguay*

Paraguay has one of the world's lowest population densities, with most people living east of the Paraguay River. Asuncion is the largest city, with a metropolitan area including San Lorenzo, Luque, and Fernando de la Mora. Ciudad del Este and nearby cities grew rapidly after the 1970s. Population rose from 2.4 million in 1970 to more than 6 million by the mid-2000s, producing a young population and rising urbanization.

## Economy and Agriculture

Paraguay's economy ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economy)) changed after the 1970s through state companies, hydroelectric construction, roads, telecommunications, and air transport. Growth often failed to reduce rural poverty or inequality. Agriculture employs more than one-third of the workforce and produces soybeans, meat, cotton, corn, wheat, timber, dairy, and livestock. Land tenure is highly unequal, with late-20th-century estimates showing more than three-fourths of land owned by 1 percent of landholders.

## Energy, Manufacturing, and Trade

![[attachments/paraguay_002.webp|420]]

*Aerial view of Itaipu Dam on the Parana River*

Paraguay's key resource is hydroelectric power. Acaray began operating in 1968, while the Itaipu Dam, built with Brazil under a 1973 treaty and completed in 1982, made Paraguay one of the world's largest electricity exporters. Yacyreta, built with Argentina, added further capacity. Manufacturing remains limited and mostly processes agricultural products such as soybean oil, flour, sugar, meat, textiles, leather, alcohol, beer, and cigarettes.

Foreign trade shifted from tannin, meat, mate, tobacco, and cotton toward soybeans, meat, wheat, corn, and timber. Imports include machinery, petroleum, chemicals, and vehicle parts. Smuggling historically distorted trade statistics. Paraguay joined Mercosur through the Treaty of Asuncion in 1991.

## Services and Transportation

Services account for about two-fifths of GDP. Tourism includes Jesuit missions such as La Santisima Trinidad de Parana and Jesus de Tavarangue, Chaco parks, Iguazu Falls, Itaipu Dam, and Ciudad del Este shopping. Transport depends on rivers, roads, bridges, ports, barges, airports, and limited rail. Asuncion is the largest port, and river barges regained importance after 2000 because of fuel costs.

## Government and Politics

The 1992 constitution established Paraguay as a representative, pluralist democracy with separate legislative, executive, and judicial powers. The president serves a five-year term without immediate indefinite reelection, and the constitution created a vice presidency, abolished the death penalty, guaranteed civic liberties, and recognized indigenous rights. The Congress has a Chamber of Deputies and Senate.

Paraguayan politics long revolved around the Liberal Party and the Colorado Party. The Colorado Party was the only legal party from 1947 to 1962 and remained continuously in power until 2008. Under Stroessner, repression, dossiers, military influence, and controlled parties defined politics. Democratization after 1989 was uneven but expanded political freedoms.

## Justice, Security, Health, and Education

The Supreme Court heads the judiciary, but rulings have often been politically influenced. Voting is compulsory for citizens ages 18 to 75, and resident aliens may vote in municipal elections. The military includes army, navy, and air force, with compulsory male service reduced under the 1992 constitution. Health challenges include measles, tuberculosis, respiratory infections, dysentery, hookworm, hepatitis, Chagas disease, leishmaniasis, dengue, and yellow fever. Education is free and partly compulsory, but dropout rates and rural shortages remain problems.

## Culture

Paraguayan culture ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Culture)) fuses Guarani and Spanish traditions. Nanduti lace is a notable handicraft. Family, godparents, Roman Catholic traditions, folk festivals, Guarani identity, and cuisine shape social life. Foods include beef, river fish, soups, chipa, corn dishes, and sopa paraguaya. Guarani and Spanish influences appear in music, folklore, literature, and daily speech.

## Early and Colonial History

The Guarani occupied the region between the Paraguay and Parana rivers long before Europeans arrived. They farmed corn, cassava, and sweet potatoes, hunted, fished, and lived in defended villages. Alejo Garcia reached the area in 1524, and Sebastian Cabot sailed up the Parana in 1526. Domingo Martinez de Irala established Spanish settlements from 1536 to 1556, centered on Asuncion, creating a Guarani-Spanish rural society.

## Republic, Wars, and Reconstruction

Jose Gaspar Rodriguez de Francia, or El Supremo, dominated early independent Paraguay, which formally declared itself an independent republic in 1813. He isolated the country, suppressed elites, controlled the church, and expanded state property. After the catastrophic War of the Triple Alliance, Paraguay reconstructed under the 1870 constitution. U.S. President Rutherford B. Hayes arbitrated a Chaco boundary issue in Paraguay's favour in 1878.

## Chaco War, Stroessner, and Democracy

The Chaco War began in 1932 after Bolivian forces attacked a Paraguayan fort. Under Eusebio Ayala and Jose Felix Estigarribia, Paraguay pushed Bolivia back and gained three-fourths of the Chaco in the 1938 peace treaty. Political instability followed, including the 1947 civil war. Alfredo Stroessner seized power in 1954 and ruled until 1989. In 2008, Fernando Lugo ended Colorado dominance but was impeached in 2012; Horacio Cartes returned the Colorado Party to the presidency in 2013.
