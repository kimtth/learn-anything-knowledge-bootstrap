# Science Fiction

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> Science fiction explores how actual or imagined science and technology reshape individuals and societies. The source traces its emergence from satirical voyages and scientific romance through film, pulp fiction, dystopia, space opera, and postwar forms that use futures, aliens, robots, and imagined worlds to test social possibilities.

## Introduction

Science fiction ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Science_fiction)), often abbreviated SF or sci-fi, is fiction about the impact of actual or imagined science on society or individuals. The term was popularized, if not invented, in the 1920s by Hugo Gernsback. The Hugo Awards, given annually since 1953, are named for him.

Science fiction is a modern genre because it depends on scientific and technological plausibility. Ancient and early modern works sometimes used voyages, monsters, or imaginary worlds, but modern SF emerged when the Industrial Revolution made technological change a social force that writers could extrapolate into the future.

By the early 20th century, the genre had developed familiar themes: space travel, robots, aliens, time travel, utopias, dystopias, disasters, political agitation, and imaginary societies. SF can warn, satirize, dream, preach, parody, or express despair or cosmic hope about techno-social change.

## Antecedents

Early antecedents include Lucian's 2nd-century Trips to the Moon, which used lunar travel for satire. Cyrano de Bergerac later wrote of voyages to the Moon and Sun, making impossible things seem plausible while criticizing society and religion.

Jonathan Swift's Gulliver's Travels and [[Voltaire]]'s Micromegas show Cyrano's influence through strange beings, inverted normality, and harsh satire. Louis-Sebastien Mercier's The Year 2440, from around 1771, was important because it placed a utopian society in a realizable future on Earth rather than in myth or heaven.

## Jules Verne and Scientific Romance

![[attachments/science_fiction_002.webp|420]]

*Portrait of Jules Verne by Etienne Carjat*

Jules Verne was more important to the genre's formation than earlier precursors and is often called an inventor of science fiction. His unpublished early novel Paris in the Twentieth Century, written in 1863, predicted elevated trains, automobiles, facsimile machines, and computer-like banking machines, but its dystopian mood was too dark for his publisher.

Under Jules Hetzel's guidance, Verne wrote the Voyages extraordinaires, beginning with Five Weeks in a Balloon. These contemporary techno-thrillers described balloons, submarines, trains, mechanical elephants, and other marvels with technical accuracy and humour. Verne's success made scientific romance a permanent part of popular entertainment.

Albert Robida also anticipated future media, war, electricity, and social life in illustrated comic works such as The 20th Century and War in the 20th Century. His predictions were often closest when they were satirical rather than solemn.

## Early Film, Dystopia, and Pulp Traditions

![[attachments/science_fiction_001.webp|420]]

*Amazing Science Fiction Stories magazine cover from 1959*

Science fiction entered cinema early. Georges Melies filmed a trip to the Moon in 1902. Soviet and German films such as Aelita and Fritz Lang's Metropolis helped define visual SF through Constructivist design, robots, class struggle, and futuristic city imagery.

Yevgeny Zamyatin's We, written in 1920 and translated into English in 1924, described life under a totalitarian state and influenced Aldous Huxley's Brave New World and George Orwell's Nineteen Eighty-four. Dystopian SF became one of the genre's most powerful forms.

Pulp magazines and adventure traditions helped create space opera ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Space_opera)), robots, alien invasion, planetary romance, and heroic exploration. These forms could be crude, but they built passionate audiences and supplied the imagery that later film and television expanded.

## Science Fiction After World War II

![[attachments/science_fiction_003.webp|420]]

*The Magazine of Fantasy and Science Fiction cover from November 1959*

After [[World Wars (World War II)|World War II]], publishers shifted from pulps to paperbacks and digest magazines. The Magazine of Fantasy and Science Fiction and Galaxy Science Fiction began in 1949 and 1950, helping make American SF more sophisticated, urbane, satirical, and socially speculative.

The atomic bomb in 1945 and Sputnik in 1957 gave SF new urgency. Cold War fears shaped works such as Walter M. Miller's A Canticle for Leibowitz and Invasion of the Body Snatchers. Films such as The Day the Earth Stood Still, The War of the Worlds, Forbidden Planet, and Godzilla turned atomic anxiety and alien contact into popular images.

The great Anglophone writers of the period included Robert Heinlein, Isaac Asimov, Ray Bradbury, and Arthur C. Clarke. Outside English, Stanislaw Lem, Italo Calvino, Ivan Yefremov, Kir Bulychev, and Arkady and Boris Strugatsky showed the genre's global reach.

## New Wave, Cyberpunk, Film, and Fantasy

The New Wave, associated with Michael Moorcock and Harlan Ellison, challenged older SF in the 1960s and 1970s. It used taboo subjects, drugs, Eastern religion, sexuality, counterculture, and experimental literary style. It expanded soft SF, which focused more on society and inner space than on engineering.

Cyberpunk emerged later through writers such as William Gibson, Bruce Sterling, and Lewis Shiner. The rise of computers and the Internet in the 1990s made cyberpunk's alienated networks, artificial environments, and corporate futures feel immediately relevant.

Hollywood made science fiction film the genre's public face after the 1970s. The Alien, Terminator, and Jurassic Park series became global successes. At the same time, heroic fantasy grew commercially, with J.K. Rowling's Harry Potter books and J.R.R. Tolkien's Lord of the Rings adaptations showing the changing market for speculative fiction.

## Utopias and Dystopias

Utopian fiction imagines ideal societies. Thomas More's Utopia of 1516 used a no-place society to critique England. Later utopias included William Morris's News from Nowhere, Samuel Butler's Erewhon, James Hilton's Lost Horizon, Aldous Huxley's Island, and Ernest Callenbach's Ecotopia.

Modern utopias often stress ambiguity. Ursula K. Le Guin's The Dispossessed presents an anarchist society struggling to live up to its ideals, while Kim Stanley Robinson's Mars trilogy imagines settlers building new societies under Martian conditions.

Dystopias replace hopes for improvement with fear of present tendencies. Brave New World, Nineteen Eighty-four, Terry Gilliam's [[Brazil]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brazil)), E.M. Forster's The Machine Stops, Katharine Burdekin's Swastika Night, Joanna Russ's The Female Man, and Margaret Atwood's The Handmaid's Tale explore oppression, technology, misogyny, bureaucracy, and social control.

## Alien Encounters and Robots

Alien encounters became possible as natural history encouraged writers to imagine life evolving differently on other worlds. Camille Flammarion described alien biological environments, and J.H. Rosny Aine's Les Xipehuz imagined conflict between humans and crystal-based life. H.G. Wells's The War of the Worlds made aliens Darwinian competitors.

Robots and androids became central to SF cinema and fiction. Mechanical humans appear in works such as Metropolis, Westworld, The Stepford Wives, Star Wars, Bicentennial Man, A.I. Artificial Intelligence, and I, Robot. They raise questions about labour, identity, imitation, consciousness, and human control.

## Space Travel and Space Opera

Space travel is the classic SF theme. Verne's From the Earth to the Moon treated it as an engineering problem, recognizing weightlessness and airlessness, though his cannon-launched travellers only passed near the Moon. The actual Moon landing in 1969 reduced some romantic mystery by showing spaceflight as technical, cramped, and dangerous work.

Most SF avoids the hard realities of astrophysics through devices such as warp drive or faster-than-light travel. This allows alien-planet adventures and space opera. Writers such as Jack Williamson, A.E. Van Vogt, Jack Vance, Anne McCaffrey, Lois McMaster Bujold, and C.J. Cherryh helped develop the form.

The New British Science Fiction revived extravagant space opera ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Space_opera)) in the early 21st century through writers such as Iain M. Banks, Stephen Baxter, Justina Robson, Peter F. Hamilton, Charles Stross, and Ken MacLeod.

## Time Travel and Alternate Histories

Time travel complements space travel. Charles Dickens's A Christmas Carol offered a magical prototype, but H.G. Wells's The Time Machine of 1895 made time travel mechanical. The theme lets modern people enter the past or future and confront historical change directly.

Examples include Mark Twain's A Connecticut Yankee in King Arthur's Court, L. Sprague de Camp's Lest Darkness Fall, Ray Bradbury's A Sound of Thunder, Robert Silverberg's Up the Line, Poul Anderson's Guardians of Time, Fritz Leiber's The Change War, and the television series Doctor Who.

Alternate-history and parallel-universe stories explore causality, paradox, and multiple histories. The classic problem is the traveler who kills his own grandfather and prevents his own birth. Murray Leinster's Sidewise in Time expanded the idea by imagining many histories existing at once.

## Key Events & Developments

- 1516: Thomas More's Utopia helped shape utopian fiction.
- 1865: Jules Verne's From the Earth to the Moon treated spaceflight as engineering.
- 1895: H.G. Wells's The Time Machine made mechanical time travel central to SF.
- 1920s: Hugo Gernsback popularized the term science fiction.
- 1945: The atomic bomb changed the genre's themes and urgency.
- 1953: The Hugo Awards began.
- 1957: Sputnik intensified space-age SF.

## Interesting Facts

- Science fiction ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Science_fiction)) is separated from older fantasy by its concern for plausible science or technology.
- Utopia is a pun meaning both good place and no place.
- Warp drive is scientifically implausible but useful for alien-planet adventure stories.
- Doctor Who made a blue police box one of the most famous time machines in popular culture.
