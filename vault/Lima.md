# Lima

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> Lima is Peru's capital and commercial-industrial center, situated in a coastal desert beside the Rimac River. Spanish colonial foundations, central political power, rapid metropolitan growth, and environmental pressures such as congestion, pollution, and earthquakes shape the city's character.

Lima, Peru's capital and commercial-industrial centre, stands on the south bank of the Rimac River, about 8 miles inland from Callao. Its name comes from Quechua Rimac, meaning Talker, and its nickname El Pulpo, The Octopus, reflects its pull over Peru's people, capital, politics, and innovation.

Metropolitan Lima held 8,472,935 people in 2007. Its rapid growth also produced the darker nickname Lima la Horrible, associated with fog, congestion, pollution, dampness, and overcrowding.

The city site sprawls from the original Spanish settlement on the Rimac's alluvial cone into valleys, hills, and desert benches exposed to earthquakes, cliff erosion, flash floods, and barren sands.

## Climate

Lima's climate is temperate despite its tropical latitude because the cool Peru, or Humboldt, Current chills the coastal air. Winters from May to November are cool and damp, summers from December to April are hot and humid, and annual precipitation rarely exceeds 2 inches.

The garua, a dense sea mist, blankets the city during winter and traps pollutants, so residents can often taste the air. High humidity causes rust, and wealthier residents have often sought clearer winter air along the coast or eastward at La Molina.

Old Lima preserves a colonial checkerboard plan, the Plaza de Armas, Plaza Bolivar, cathedral, Archbishop's Palace, Torre Tagle Palace, and enclosed wooden balconies.

Urban growth after the 1930s transformed the city. Mansions became crowded inner-city housing, farms and open land became suburbs, and barriadas later called pueblos jovenes grew into districts that held about one-third of metropolitan Lima's population.

Lima's people make the city a microcosm of Peru, joining highland migrants, older elites, mixed populations, poor suburbs, affluent districts, Roman Catholic processions, sharp inequality, and a dominant industrial, financial, governmental, and informal economy.

## Transportation

[[Transportation]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transport)) links Lima historically to Callao through South America's oldest railway, while the line east past Vitarte reaches the highest point of any standard-gauge railway. Modern traffic, however, is heavily congested and often chaotic.

Most Limenos depend on minibuses, small buses, municipal buses, informal taxis, cars, trucks, motorcycles, and bicycles on an outdated street network; an elevated rail project was suspended in the early 1990s.

## Government

Metropolitan government is fragmented among the department of Lima, the province of Callao, and many autonomous districts. Citywide planning requires negotiation, and district autonomy has obstructed unified responses to growth.

A 1984 municipal law created a Metropolitan Council for Greater Lima, but revenue disparities remain severe between wealthy districts and poorer districts needing water, sewers, electricity, and paved streets.

## Services

Public services have been strained by rapid growth. Water once came from the Rimac and shallow wells, but modern supply must be brought from Andean lakes and diverted rivers, while affordable electricity arrived only after the Mantaro River hydroelectric project in the early 1970s.

Municipalities often lack money, planning capacity, and the ability to recover service costs, so Lima has relied on Peru's national government and agencies such as the World Bank.

Lima remains Peru's leading cultural centre, with the National University of San Marcos, founded in 1551, museums, huacas, Pachacamac nearby, El Comercio, television, radio, theatres, cinemas, penas, and restaurants.

Recreation and culture include football, volleyball, folk music, pisco, cevicherias, chifas, picanterias, and migrant clubs that keep provincial foods, songs, dances, and identities alive.

## History

![[attachments/lima_003.webp|420]]

*Huaca Pucllana archaeological site in Lima*

Lima's region has been inhabited for thousands of years. Pachacamac was an important pre-Inca and Inca religious centre, and much of Francisco Pizarro's demanded ransom for Atahuallpa came from there.

Francisco Pizarro founded Spanish Lima on Jan. 6, 1535, as Ciudad de los Reyes, or City of Kings. The name did not endure, but Lima became capital of the Viceroyalty of Peru because its coastal location eased communication with Spain.

Colonial Lima became the viceroyalty's centre of wealth, justice, Inquisition authority, learning, and prestige, including the University of San Marcos and a medical school founded by Jose Hipolito Unnue in 1808.

The city grew slowly from the late 17th century to the mid-19th century, was devastated by the 1746 earthquake, and remained conservative until Peru declared independence in July 1821.

## David J. Robinson

David J. Robinson is associated with the modern-city account. Lima's modernization began with the Lima-Callao railroad in 1851, followed by links to Miraflores, Ancon, and Chosica and by suburban movement of wealthier residents.

The guano boom enriched Lima and Callao, but the War of the Pacific brought Chilean occupation from 1881 to 1883 and the burning of the National Library. Literary life revived through Ricardo Palma and later writers such as Jose Carlos Mariategui and Cesar Vallejo.

Automobiles and road building in the 1920s and 1930s made Lima a magnet for migrants. From 1940 to 1980, about 2,000,000 people moved there, and self-help housing invasions led the government to grant land titles and extend basic services.

Lima continues to shape Peruvian national life. Works by Mario Vargas Llosa, Julio Ramon Ribeyro, and Jaime Bayly focus on the city, and the historic centre became a UNESCO World Heritage site in 1988.

Historic Lima faces air pollution, earthquakes, and fires, though many antique wooden balconies were repaired and restored in the 1990s.
