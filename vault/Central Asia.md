# Central Asia

> [!abstract] Reference navigation

## Introduction

Central Asia in the historical sense is the broad Eurasian interior where sedentary civilizations and nomadic peoples repeatedly met, fought, traded, and absorbed one another. The history of the region is best understood through the tension between the civilized and the barbarian, though that contrast is too simple if treated as an absolute.

The region lacks abundant native written sources, so much of its history must be reconstructed from Chinese, Greek, Latin, Persian, Arabic, and later Russian accounts. Central Asia therefore appears as a historical field shaped by outside observers as much as by the peoples who lived there.

During the late 4th century CE a powerful empire, the Juan-juan (Rouran), emerged in Mongolia, the political heartland of the region, stepping into the place vacated by the Xiongnu. Chinese sources barely distinguish them from their predecessors, and their history is an almost unbroken series of campaigns against neighbouring peoples, especially the Chinese.

In 552 the Juan-juan empire was destroyed by a revolution of lasting consequence. The tribe of the Turks, living within the empire and specializing in metallurgy, revolted and seized power. They were the first Central Asian people known to have spoken a Turkic language and the first to leave a written record: the Orhon inscriptions, funerary stelae near the Orhon River, reveal the internal stresses of a pastoral nomad state that stretched from China to Byzantium.

The founder, Bumin, bore the title khagan but died soon after his victory, and the empire split in two. The eastern half was centred on Mongolia; the western half, allied with the Sasanians, destroyed the Hephthalite empire in 560 and seized a vital portion of the Silk Road. Under Tardu the western Turks reached the borders of Byzantium before internal strife and Chinese pressure weakened the realm.

The Turk empire later split and then reunified under Elterish in 683. Though it disintegrated within decades, the Turks linked China, Iran, India, and Byzantium and gave their name to all the Turkic-speaking peoples, creating a durable identity across the steppe.

## The Uighurs and the Khitans

The Uighurs replaced the Turks in 744 in what amounted to little more than a coup d'etat, since the two languages were nearly identical. Relations with China dominated their foreign affairs: they aided the Tang against the rebellion of An Lushan (755) and traded horses for coveted Chinese riches. The khagan Mouyu visited Luoyang and was converted to Manichaeism, an Iranian religion whose Sogdian adherents gained influence at court. The Uighurs ruled from the fortified city of Karabalghasun on the Orhon, admired by the Muslim traveler Tamim ibn Bahr around 821.

The Khitans were the first people known to have spoken a Mongol language. Their leader Abaoji defeated the Kyrgyz in 924, and they conquered northern China as the Liao dynasty (907–1125) until ousted by the Juchen. The Western name Cathay derives from Khitan. Driven west in 1124 under Yelu Dashi, some founded the Karakhitan (Western Liao) state in the Chu valley, where the Muslim Karakhanids—who had occupied Bukhara in 992 from the Samanids—maintained steppe traditions more strongly than other Turkish dynasties.

## Timur and the Uzbeks

The rise of Timur marked a major shift in the late medieval world. He united the Turko-Mongol tribes between the Amu Darya and Syr Darya, then conquered Khorasan, Iran, Iraq, and the Caucasus. In 1398–99 he invaded India and sacked Delhi, and near Ankara in 1402 he captured the Ottoman sultan Bayezid I. Styling himself only emir and guregen ("son-in-law" of the Chagataid line), he died at Otrar in 1405 while marching on China. His son Shah Rukh later ruled from Herat, while his grandson Ulugh Beg, an enthusiastic astronomer, governed Samarkand from 1409 to 1447.

The Uzbeks then rose under Muhammad Shaybani, grandson of Abul-Khayr Khan (killed in battle in 1468). By 1500 Shaybani held Samarkand and the lands between the two rivers; he drove the Timurid Babur from Central Asia before being defeated and killed by Shah Ismail Safavi in 1510. His kinsmen ruled an aggressive khanate from Bukhara through the 16th century, feuding with the Shiite Safavids along Sunni–Shiite lines. Unlike the Ottomans, Safavids, and Mughals, however, the Uzbeks had only limited access to firearms, a lasting disadvantage against their rivals.

## Russian Conquest and Rule

The Russian advance began in Siberia, where in 1578 the Cossack Yermak Timofeyevich, acting for the Stroganov merchants, crossed the Urals and defeated the Shaybanid prince Kuchum. The march from west to east across Siberia, driven by the fur trade, was unparalleled for its speed, since the hunting populations cared little about the nationality of their tax collectors. Imperial rivalry with Britain—the fear of further penetration from across the Indus—later pushed Russia deeper into the south.

The khanate of Kokand was annexed in 1876, while Khiva and Bukhara became Russian protectorates in 1873 and 1868. Under tsarist rule the region was tied to imperial markets through railroads—the Trans-Caspian line reached Samarkand by 1888—administration, and cotton cultivation. Russia preserved the titular sovereignty of the emir of Bukhara and the khan of Khiva where convenient, but the balance of power had clearly shifted, and the old khanates became subordinate to Russian authority.

## Conclusion

Central Asia is a region of repeated conquest, state formation, fragmentation, and cultural exchange. Its long history is not a single straight line but a series of moments in which steppe power, urban civilization, and imperial expansion transformed one another.

## Key Events & Developments

- 552: The Turks overthrow the Juan-juan empire.
- 683: The Turk empire reunifies.
- 744: The Uighurs replace the Turks.
- 13th century: Mongol expansion reshapes the region.
- 1405: Timur dies during his campaign toward China.
- 1500: Shaybani takes Samarkand and shifts Central Asian power to the Uzbeks.
- 19th century: Russian conquest of Central Asia accelerates.
