# Organ and Tissue Transplants (Transplant)

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> Transplantation moves tissue or organs within or between bodies in order to restore viable function. This history ranges from grafts of skin, cornea, vessels, bone, marrow, nerves, and major organs to the biological problem of immune rejection and the development of immunosuppression and organ banking.

## Introduction

Transplant, also called a graft, is tissue or a complete organ moved from its natural site to another place in the same person or to another individual. The term came from horticulture, implying that a successful graft becomes nourished and viable in its new environment. Medical transplants range from skin, cornea, blood, bone marrow, bone, fascia, and nerves to kidneys, hearts, livers, pancreas, and heart-lung grafts.

## Historical Development

Myths imagined tissue transplantation ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tissue_transplantation)) long before surgery. Early Hindu surgeons developed nose reconstruction from arm skin flaps around the beginning of the 6th century BC. Gaspare Tagliacozzi introduced related flap methods into Western medicine in the 16th century. Thin free skin grafts, flaps, and later corneal grafts became central to plastic and reconstructive surgery.

## Skin and Flap Grafts

Most skin grafting ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Skin_grafting)) uses autografts from the same patient. Skin allografts from another person are useful for severe burns but are aggressively rejected. Flap grafts, left partly attached until new blood vessels grow, are valuable when fat as well as skin has been lost. Full-thickness grafts provide good cosmetic results but risk failure, while split-thickness grafts are the most common in plastic surgery.

## Cornea and Blood Vessels

Corneal transplants can restore sight when blindness is caused by corneal opacity and the rest of the eye is normal. Because the graft bed lacks blood vessels, many corneal allografts survive indefinitely. Blood-vessel grafts are most successful as autografts, often using a superficial leg vein to bypass arteries narrowed by atherosclerosis, including coronary or leg arteries.

## Bone, Fascia, Nerves, and Blood

Bone autografts help fractures unite; allograft bone is mainly a structural scaffold because its cells are dead or rejected. Fascia can repair hernias. Nerve regeneration is difficult because fibres may regrow along wrong paths, making limb grafts often unsatisfactory. Blood transfusion is a vital tissue graft that makes many surgeries and trauma treatments possible, while platelets and clotting factor VIII treat specific deficiencies.

Bone marrow grafting can treat aplastic anemia and some leukemias after diseased marrow is destroyed. Risks include rejection and graft-versus-host disease, in which donor immune cells attack the patient. Organ transplants are harder than many tissue grafts, but kidney transplantation became routine in developed countries, while heart, liver, pancreas, and combined heart-lung grafts became established or promising.

## Rejection and Immune Response

Rejection is the central problem in transplantation. The immune system attacks foreign cells because it cannot distinguish dangerous microorganisms from lifesaving grafts. T lymphocytes drive cell-mediated immunity by attacking antigens on foreign tissue, while B lymphocytes produce antibodies. Transplant rejection often develops over days or months as immune cells infiltrate and destroy the graft.

## Immunosuppression

Immunosuppression aims to let the recipient accept a graft with manageable side effects. Over time, dosages can sometimes be reduced as host and graft adapt. However, suppressing immunity increases vulnerability to infection and other complications. Transplant medicine therefore balances graft survival against toxicity, infection, cancer risk, and organ damage.

## Major Drugs and Serums

Azathioprine suppresses immunity but must be carefully dosed to avoid bone-marrow damage, infection, and bleeding. Corticosteroids such as prednisone and prednisolone are crucial during rejection crises but can cause infection, growth problems, diabetes, brittle bones, weight gain, and facial swelling. Antilymphocyte globulin can treat kidney-graft rejection when corticosteroids fail, though potency and side effects vary.

## Cyclosporine and Organ Banks

Cyclosporine, discovered from an earth fungus by Sandoz Laboratories, powerfully suppresses T lymphocytes and improved results in many organ grafts. It can damage kidneys, promote hair growth, and requires individualized dosing. Organs deteriorate rapidly without blood supply; cooling slows but does not stop damage. Brain, heart, liver, pancreas, lung, kidney, skin, and cornea differ greatly in how long they remain viable.

## Key Events & Developments

- 6th century BC: Hindu surgeons used skin flaps for nose reconstruction.
- 16th century: Gaspare Tagliacozzi introduced flap reconstruction to Western medicine.
- Blood transfusion: Became essential to modern surgery.
- Kidney transplantation: Became routine in many developed countries.
- Cyclosporine: Markedly improved organ-graft immunosuppression.

## Key People

- Gaspare Tagliacozzi ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gaspare_Tagliacozzi)): Pioneer of Western flap reconstructive surgery.
- Sandoz Laboratories ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sandoz_Laboratories)): Identified cyclosporine as an immunosuppressive agent.
- Transplant surgeons and immunologists: Developed grafting, matching, suppression, and organ-preservation methods.

## Interesting Facts

- Corneal grafts often avoid rejection because the cornea lacks blood vessels.
- Skin allografts are rejected especially aggressively.
- Bone marrow grafts can attack the recipient through graft-versus-host disease.
- Cooling slows organ deterioration but cannot stop it.
