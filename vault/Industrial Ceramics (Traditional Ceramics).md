## Introduction

Traditional ceramics ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ceramic)) are made from common natural materials such as clay minerals, quartz sand, silica, and feldspar. They include china tableware, brick, tile, abrasives, refractory linings, and portland cement. Their history reaches from Neolithic clay vessels about 10,000 years ago through fired pottery, straw-strengthened brick, quartz foundry molds, lime-mortar cement, Roman engineering, and Industrial Revolution kiln and processing improvements.

## Raw Materials

Clay minerals ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clay_mineral)), including kaolinite, form when rocks such as granite weather under water, carbon dioxide, and organic acids. Their tiny plate-shaped particles become plastic when mixed with water, so clay acts as the main former in traditional ceramics. Silica, added as quartz sand, sandstone, or flint, supplies green strength and shape stability. Feldspars act as fluxes that lower melting temperatures.

## Preparation and Forming

Beneficiation may be simple for common brick and tile clays, which can be used nearly as dug after blending, aging, and tempering. Whitewares need purer clays, silica, and feldspars, so washing, settling, flotation, gravity separation, and magnetic or electrostatic separation remove impurities. Batching fixes the formula, balancing workability, fusibility, fired colour, porosity, and strength.

Forming uses clay plasticity. Pressing compacts stiff mud in dies for brick, tile, and refractory shapes. Extrusion pushes plastic material continuously through a die to make repeated forms such as pipes and tiles. Slip casting pours a watery clay suspension into a porous mold, which draws out water and leaves a shaped clay layer.

## Firing and Finishing

![[attachments/industrial_ceramics_001.webp|420]]

*Fired ceramics being removed from a kiln*

Firing removes water, burns out binders, and consolidates the ware. Specialty pieces may use batch furnaces, while mass products usually move through tunnel kilns. Pyrometric cones deform at known temperatures and show the heat reached. In silicate ceramics, partial vitrification melts part of the material into glass while solid particles preserve shape and limit warping.

Glazing adds impermeability or decoration. A glass-forming slurry is applied to fired ware and refired at a lower temperature that vitrifies the glaze. Colour may come from transition-metal or rare-earth elements or from finely divided ceramic particles in the glaze.

## Products

Traditional ceramic products include whiteware, structural clay products, brick, tile, refractories, abrasives, and cement. Their shared logic is natural silicate raw materials, controlled blending, forming, firing, and finishing.

## Key Events & Developments

- Neolithic pottery: Useful clay vessels date to about 10,000 years ago.
- Fired ware: Burning clay made vessels stronger, harder, and less permeable.
- Roman cement: Lime-mortar cement supported major Roman civil engineering works.
- Industrial Revolution ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Industrial_Revolution)): Processing improvements in the 18th and 19th centuries increased ceramic reliability and scale.

## Key People

- Ceramic technologists: Developed traditional batching, forming, firing, glazing, and product-specific processing practices over centuries of craft and industrial refinement.

## Interesting Facts

- Clay plasticity comes from microscopic plate-shaped particles sliding over one another.
- Feldspar lowers firing temperatures by acting as a flux.
- Tunnel kilns let mass-produced ware move continuously through heating and cooling zones.
- Glaze is itself a glassy layer fired onto ceramic surfaces.
