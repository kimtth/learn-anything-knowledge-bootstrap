# The History of Western Painting (Painting, Western)

## Introduction

Western painting ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_painting)) is the history of painted forms on surfaces in Europe and regions shaped by European cultural traditions, from prehistoric cave images to modern art. It includes cave murals, pottery, frescoes, icons, manuscripts, panels, altarpieces, easel paintings, murals, and modern media.

Painting has been practiced for about 20,000 years. Unlike music and dance, some early examples survive, especially cave paintings in western Europe and related regions.

Western painting is often distinguished by its concentration on the human figure ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nude_%28art%29)), first in heroic and ritual contexts, then in Christian, Renaissance, secular, portrait, historical, landscape, and modern forms.

## Upper Paleolithic and Mesolithic Painting

Upper Paleolithic ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Upper_Paleolithic)) cave painting, especially in France and Spain, shows animals central to hunter life: bison, horses, cattle, reindeer, mammoths, and other game. Lascaux and related sites reveal remarkable draftsmanship, movement, shading, and colour washes.

Most Paleolithic figures are isolated [[Animals|animals]] rather than narrative scenes. Humans appear rarely, and clear interaction between humans and animals is uncommon, making interpretation difficult.

The technique of these early paintings was already complex. Simple outlines could be combined with shading, wash, varying brush thickness, and careful attention to horn, bone, hide, and volume. Many cave walls were built up over long periods, so apparent groupings can represent repeated additions rather than planned compositions. This helps explain why interpretation remains difficult even when the animal forms themselves are vivid and controlled.

In the Mesolithic, after Ice Age herds disappeared, human figures became central. Spanish rock-shelter paintings show hunters, archers, dancers, battles, ambushes, and group cooperation, reflecting new social conditions and hunting methods.

Mesolithic painting also marks a change in social imagination. Warmer forests replaced open Ice Age ranges, smaller animals were harder to hunt, and survival depended more on cooperation, foraging, fishing, and organized ambush. The art responded by showing lines of archers, coordinated drives, battle scenes, dancers, and figures whose clothing or careful depiction implies social status. Human action became the subject in a way that Paleolithic animal painting rarely allowed.

## Neolithic and Aegean Beginnings

The Neolithic introduced farming and settled village life from western Asia into Europe before 6000 BCE. Large-scale wall painting is scarce in Europe, but painted pottery developed rich geometric and textile-like patterns.

In the Aegean and eastern Mediterranean, the Metal Age brought towns, trade, and palatial cultures. Minoan, Cycladic, Helladic, and Cypriot traditions formed around Crete, the islands, mainland Greece, and Cyprus.

Early Minoan pottery moved from simple lines to dark-ground designs with cream and white spirals. Middle Minoan palace culture developed fresco, stucco relief, swirling floral patterns, and decorative wall painting.

Late Bronze Age Aegean frescoes used dark outlines and solid colour to show landscapes, birds, animals, flower gatherers, ceremonies, court rituals, and religious scenes. Akrotiri on Thera preserves vivid examples, including ship processions and festival scenes.

Minoan Marine style pottery, about 1500-1450 BCE, filled vessels with octopuses, dolphins, fish, rocks, waves, and sea life. Mycenaean painters later simplified Minoan motifs and developed a less successful figure style.

Cypriot painting in the Late Bronze Age followed its own pottery traditions, including handmade base-ring ware, white-slip ware, and local wheel-made imitations of imported Mycenaean pottery. These works show how the eastern Mediterranean mixed local habits with Aegean exchange.

The Aegean evidence also shows a lasting Western tension between decoration and representation. Pottery often favoured pattern, while palace frescoes explored ceremony, landscape, figure movement, and courtly or cultic power.

## Ancient Greek Painting

Greek art explored human experience, even when treating gods and heroes. Greek artists used art for gifts to gods, monuments to the dead, civic commemoration, and social identity; art for art's sake came much later.

Few Greek wall or panel paintings survive, so vase painting is the main evidence for Greek draftsmanship. Vases were not considered major art by ancient writers, but they preserve styles, myths, politics, and technique.

This evidence is indirect but unusually rich. Funerary paintings from Macedonia and northern Greece show what monumental painting could achieve, while vases preserve changes in line, anatomy, gesture, and narrative over centuries. Because ancient authors praised lost wall and panel painters more than vase painters, surviving pottery often stands in for an admired tradition that mostly disappeared.

After the Bronze Age collapse, Greece entered the Dark Ages. Protogeometric and Geometric pottery in Athens used circles, semicircles, zigzags, meanders, swastikas, and funerary or battle scenes, showing renewed order and aristocratic identity.

The Orientalizing period introduced animals, monsters, floral motifs, and narrative scenes from Syria and Phoenicia. Corinth developed Proto-Corinthian miniaturist style and black-figure technique.

The Archaic and Early Classical periods developed black-figure and red-figure vase painting, anatomical line, psychological scenes, and political narratives. Artists such as the Cleophrades Painter and Berlin Painter reached great refinement.

High Classical painting saw decline in vase painting but major advances in monumental painting. Skiagraphia, or shadow painting, introduced shading, volume, light, and contour modelling.

Late Classical painting was regarded as ancient Greek painting's high point. Zeuxis, Apelles, and Parrhasius were famous for illusion, colour gradation, highlighting, painterly effects, and linear mastery.

Greek painting also developed through public reputation. Painters signed works, experimented with illusion, and were praised by ancient writers, even though their most admired wall and panel paintings mostly vanished.

The later Greek interest in painterly illusion influenced Roman taste. Roman patrons valued Greek works, invited Greek artists, copied Greek images, and adapted Greek mythological and historical subjects to domestic, civic, and imperial settings.

## Hellenistic, Etruscan, and Roman Painting

![[attachments/the_history_of_western_painting_painting_western_003.webp|420]]

*Alexander Mosaic from Pompeii showing the Battle of Issus*

The Hellenistic period began after Alexander the Great's death. Painters worked for rulers and courts, and Greek style spread through the Mediterranean.

Etruscan tomb painting survives in Tuscany and Lazio. Tombs at Tarquinia, Chiusi, and Cerveteri show funerary games, banquets, dancers, musicians, wrestlers, and mythology, with strong Greek influence but local liveliness.

Roman painting absorbed Etruscan and Greek models. Greek paintings came to Rome as war booty, Greek artists followed patrons, and Roman temple and triumphal painting used art for political memory.

Pompeian wall painting moved from First Style imitation marble to Second Style illusionism, opening walls into landscapes, architecture, gardens, and mythological scenes. The Odyssey landscapes and Livia's villa garden frescoes show Roman interest in space, atmosphere, and visual fantasy.

Roman painting also included book illustration, mosaics, portraits, historical scenes, domestic decoration, and mythological cycles, though copying Greek painting is harder to trace than copying Greek sculpture.

## Early Christian and Byzantine Painting

![[attachments/the_history_of_western_painting_painting_western_004.webp|420]]

*Christ Pantocrator icon from Saint Catherine’s Monastery, Sinai*

Early Christian painting emerged clearly by the late 2nd century AD, especially in funerary contexts. It borrowed classical motifs, allegory, and moral scenes before developing distinct Christian iconography.

Christian art faced early limits: few monumental churches, Jewish suspicion of images, modest patronage, and continued elite appreciation of classical culture. Over time, biblical scenes, Christ, saints, and salvation themes became central.

Byzantine painting developed after Constantinople's foundation and became clearer from Justinian's reign. It expressed Orthodox Christianity through icons, church decoration, manuscripts, wall painting, and later regional traditions.

Byzantine art emphasized a timeless sacred realm more than naturalistic narrative. Icons of Christ, the Virgin, saints, and holy events became central to devotion.

Byzantine church programs used hierarchy and placement to teach theology. Christ, the Virgin, prophets, apostles, feast scenes, and saints occupied meaningful zones of domes, apses, walls, and screens, turning architecture into sacred order.

Early Byzantine manuscript illumination preserved Greco-Roman pictorial traditions in Christian and classical books. Works such as the Vienna Genesis, Rossano Gospels, Cotton Genesis, and Dioscorides manuscript show the survival and transformation of antique book painting.

Book illustration also shows how fragile painting's survival can be. Classical and Christian manuscripts preserve echoes of lost wall, panel, and codex traditions, from Virgilian illustration to Gospel cycles. Even fragments matter because they reveal how painters adapted antique narrative, architecture, landscape, and gesture to portable books, where image and text could travel across language, empire, monastery, and court.

Icons ranged from large cult images in Rome to intimate devotional panels at Sinai. The cleaning and discovery of these works revealed that early icon painting was more varied, refined, and emotionally direct than once assumed.

The Iconoclastic Age from 717 to 843 banned religious images of divine and saintly forms in many contexts. Crosses and nonfigural decoration continued, but figural mosaics and paintings were removed in Constantinople.

After 843, icons and church decoration returned. The Middle Byzantine period was brilliant, with mosaic programs, icons, manuscript painting, and walls covered with Old and New Testament scenes.

Russian, Georgian, Armenian, Coptic, Balkan, and post-Byzantine traditions adapted Byzantine models. Russian icons developed national features, bright colours, local saints, simplified silhouettes, and schools such as Novgorod and Stroganov.

Georgia and Armenia developed Christian painting with Byzantine, local, Persian, and western influences. Georgian frescoes covered church interiors, while Armenian manuscript illumination used gold grounds, rich ornament, lively Gospel scenes, and dramatic colour.

Coptic painting in Egypt emphasized simplified figures, animal and plant ornament, friezes of saints, enthroned Christ or Virgin images, and monastery wall paintings. Islamic conquest did not immediately end Coptic illumination, though later Islamic culture gradually predominated.

Post-Byzantine Orthodox painting remained active after the Ottoman conquests because Islamic rulers did not ban Christian icons and church painting. Crete, Mount Athos, the Balkans, Russia, and Greek communities kept icon traditions alive while absorbing local and western elements.

In late Byzantine and post-Byzantine art, the iconostasis gave panel painting new prominence. Icons were arranged in devotional ranks, joining private prayer, church ritual, and theological order in a single visual system.

## Western Dark Ages and Carolingian Revival

After western Rome's collapse, urban life and art patronage declined. Migrating peoples such as Franks, Vandals, Goths, Angles, and Saxons valued metalwork, leather, wood, and gemstones more than painting.

Painting revived through Christian conversion and the desire to imitate late Roman culture. Images taught scripture, proclaimed doctrine, and served worship.

Merovingian illumination was decorative and symbolic, using initials, crosses, birds, fish, and ornamental motifs rather than complex narratives.

Under [[Charlemagne]], Carolingian painting created a court culture rivaling Rome and Byzantium. Gospel books, the Coronation Gospels, Ebbo Gospels, Utrecht Psalter, and later scriptoria developed human figure modelling, classical revival, line energy, and lavish decoration.

Carolingian painting had two powerful tendencies. The Court school produced rich Gospel books with carefully modeled figures and brilliant drapery, while the Coronation Gospels style revived antique white-clad figures, chiaroscuro, and classical calm.

The Rheims scriptorium under Ebbo introduced nervous linear excitement and ecstatic movement. This energetic drawing tradition influenced later medieval manuscript styles across northern Europe.

## Ottonian, Anglo-Saxon, and Romanesque Painting

Late Anglo-Saxon England developed brilliant manuscript illumination at Winchester and other centres, with golden frames, foliage, animated drapery, expressive outline drawing, and iconographic invention.

Ottonian Germany revived imperial and ecclesiastical painting. The Master of the Registrum Gregorii used detailed figures and spatial settings, while royal manuscripts placed luminous figures against gold and coloured grounds.

Romanesque painting from the late 11th century emphasized bold design, symmetry, order, strong line, saturated colour, patterned drapery, and Byzantine influence.

[[Italy]] fused Eastern and Western styles, especially at Montecassino and Sant'Angelo in Formis. Rome revived classical and early Christian motifs in the 12th century.

France produced important Romanesque manuscripts and wall paintings at Cluny, Citeaux, Saint-Savin, Vicq, Tavant, and other centres, combining narrative vigour, sweeping folds, colour, and provincial expressiveness.

Spain produced Beatus manuscripts and powerful wall paintings with hieratic symmetry, strong colour, abstraction, and Islamic-influenced motifs such as horseshoe arches.

The Meuse Valley, especially Stavelot, became a major Romanesque centre. Its manuscripts combined Ottonian majesty, English outline drawing, Byzantine modelling, and complex theological programs that linked Old and New Testament scenes.

German and Austrian Romanesque books often used elaborate iconography, gold, purple, dark green, azure, ochre, and white. Scrolls, inscriptions, and dense compositions made manuscripts vehicles of theology as well as decoration.

## Gothic Painting

![[attachments/the_history_of_western_painting_painting_western_006.webp|420]]

*Giotto, Kiss of Judas, Scrovegni Chapel*

Gothic painting developed from Romanesque in the 12th century and dominated Europe by the mid-13th. It varied by region and phase: early, high, Italian, International, and late Gothic.

Early Gothic moved away from Byzantine severity toward softer realism. French Bibles moralisees, English psalters, Matthew Paris manuscripts, and German Zackenstil show different regional styles.

High Gothic painting in Paris under Louis IX adopted sculpture-like drapery, detailed faces, linear refinement, and eventually light-and-shade modelling. Master Honore and Jean Pucelle helped develop these changes.

Italian influence grew through perspective, spatial experiment, and renewed interest in naturalism. Gothic painting increasingly combined line, light, narrative, ornament, and courtly refinement.

The Gothic period also saw increasing importance of stained glass and manuscript illumination as vehicles of colour and story. Though not painting in the narrowest sense, these arts influenced painters' treatment of line, figure, and luminous surface.

Gothic manuscript painting also reflected court patronage. Royal psalters, moralized Bibles, Apocalypse cycles, and devotional books encouraged artists to refine small-scale narrative, decorative borders, facial detail, and complex page design.

In England, Gothic painting moved toward greater decorative elaboration, while in Germany sharper angular drapery and strong linear pattern remained important. Regional differences persisted even as Parisian and Italian models circulated.

Italian Gothic painting, especially through Cimabue, Duccio, Giotto, Simone Martini, and the Lorenzetti, pushed toward spatial coherence, human emotion, narrative clarity, and monumental wall cycles. This prepared the Renaissance more directly than northern Gothic did.

International Gothic later emphasized courtly elegance, delicate pattern, slender figures, rich colour, and luxurious surfaces. It spread through courts and manuscript workshops, preparing some regions for Renaissance naturalism while preserving aristocratic fantasy.

Late medieval panel painting and altarpieces gave painters new spaces for devotional narrative. Donor portraits, saints, Passion scenes, Marian imagery, gold grounds, and increasingly natural settings brought sacred subjects closer to human experience.

## Renaissance Painting

The Renaissance ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Renaissance)) transformed Western painting through naturalism, perspective, anatomy, classical revival, humanism, individual artistic identity, and new patronage.

Early Renaissance painters such as Giotto, Masaccio, Fra Angelico, Piero della Francesca, and Botticelli explored spatial depth, emotional narrative, human dignity, and balanced composition.

Florentine experiments depended especially on one-point perspective and on a new relation between painting and viewer. Masaccio's Trinity, Pisa altarpiece, and Brancacci Chapel frescoes joined sculptural figures, observed light, emotional drama, and rational space. Brunelleschi's architectural ideas and Donatello's sculptural force helped painters imagine figures as solid bodies acting in measurable settings. Religious subjects remained central, but they were increasingly staged as human events in coherent space.

Early Renaissance variety was as important as unity. Fra Angelico developed the sacra conversazione and devotional clarity at San Marco; Paolo Uccello explored decorative rhythm and perspective in battle scenes; Andrea del Castagno used sculptural light and antique references; Domenico Veneziano brought a distinctive colour sense; and Piero della Francesca connected proportion, calm structure, and luminous form. Florence gave the movement its centre, but Urbino, Ferrara, Padua, Mantua, Venice, Milan, and other cities adapted Renaissance painting to different courts and patrons.

High Renaissance masters [[Leonardo da Vinci]], Michelangelo, and Raphael developed idealized human form, scientific observation, harmonious design, sfumato, monumentality, and religious-human synthesis.

[[Leonardo da Vinci]] gave the High Renaissance a new unity of light, atmosphere, and composition. In the Virgin of the Rocks he used pyramidal grouping, subtle modelling, mysterious landscape, and sfumato to bind figures and space together. The Last Supper used perspective lines converging on Christ, grouped apostles in dramatic reaction, and combined rational architecture with psychological movement. Even its technical failure as a mural did not reduce its importance as a model for unified composition.

Raphael absorbed Florentine lessons and transformed them in Rome. The School of Athens arranged figures in varied poses within a stable architectural perspective, leading the eye toward Plato and Aristotle while preserving balance and clarity. [[Michelangelo]], though trained as a sculptor, made the Sistine ceiling and Last Judgment decisive statements about the painted body. The ceiling organized creation, prophets, sibyls, and heroic figures into monumental fresco, while the Last Judgment pushed later painting toward spiritual drama and Mannerist tension.

Venetian painting, through Giorgione, Titian, Tintoretto, and Veronese, emphasized colour, atmosphere, light, sensuality, and painterly surface.

Venice gave the Renaissance a different ideal. Giorgione used oil paint to suggest texture, light, air, and poetic landscape, making works such as The Tempest important for the rise of landscape as subject. [[Titian]] joined colour, atmosphere, mythological pleasure, religious grandeur, and late loose handling of paint. His Assumption, Bacchanal, portraits, Danae, Martyrdom of St. Lawrence, and Rape of Europa showed how Venetian colourism could rival Florentine and Roman design while preparing Baroque drama.

Northern Renaissance painting by Jan van Eyck, Rogier van der Weyden, Durer, Bosch, Bruegel, and others developed oil technique, detail, portraiture, symbolism, landscape, moral satire, and religious intensity.

The International Gothic legacy remained important in the early Renaissance, especially in courtly elegance, gold, fine pattern, and devotional intimacy. Artists such as Gentile da Fabriano and early Netherlandish painters adapted this refinement to new naturalism.

Renaissance perspective changed pictorial space. Linear perspective, mathematical proportion, anatomical study, and light modelling made painting a controlled window onto a constructed world.

Renaissance patrons included guilds, churches, courts, civic governments, confraternities, merchants, humanists, and private families. Their needs created altarpieces, chapel frescoes, portraits, mythologies, cassone panels, and public cycles.

Northern oil painting gave special power to surface detail. Reflections, jewels, fur, glass, wood, metal, architecture, landscapes, and tiny symbols could be rendered with luminous precision.

Renaissance portraiture expanded the role of the individual. Rulers, merchants, scholars, spouses, donors, and artists themselves appeared with objects, settings, inscriptions, and gestures that asserted status, piety, learning, or identity.

Mythological painting also grew as humanist patrons revived ancient stories. Venus, Mars, Apollo, Bacchus, nymphs, heroes, and allegories allowed painters to join classical learning with beauty, desire, politics, and philosophy.

Renaissance workshops joined craft training with intellectual ambition. Apprentices learned drawing, pigment preparation, gilding, fresco, panel technique, and collaboration, while leading masters increasingly claimed the status of learned creators rather than anonymous artisans.

The spread of prints helped painting ideas travel. Engravings and woodcuts carried compositions, ornament, anatomy, and devotional imagery across Europe, letting artists study works they had never seen directly.

The Reformation and Counter-Reformation altered patronage and imagery. Protestant regions often reduced or redirected religious painting, while Catholic regions used art for persuasion, devotion, instruction, and emotional force.

Mannerism after the High Renaissance stretched proportion, space, colour, and pose. Artists such as Pontormo, Parmigianino, Bronzino, and El Greco favoured elegance, complexity, spiritual tension, and artificial grace.

Mannerism was not only a decline from High Renaissance balance; it was also a deliberate experiment in difficulty. Painters elongated bodies, compressed or destabilized space, heightened colour, and made poses more intricate than natural movement required. The style suited courts, intellectual circles, and religious anxiety after the sack of Rome and the pressures of reform, because it could express learned artifice, unease, elegance, and spiritual intensity at once.

## Baroque, Rococo, and Neoclassicism

![[attachments/the_history_of_western_painting_painting_western_016.webp|420]]

Baroque painting used drama, movement, light, emotion, and theatrical composition. Caravaggio's tenebrism, Rubens's dynamic colour, Velazquez's realism, Rembrandt's psychological depth, and Poussin's classicism represent different paths.

The Dutch Golden Age produced genre scenes, portraits, still lifes, landscapes, interiors, and civic group portraits by artists such as Rembrandt, Vermeer, Hals, Ruisdael, and Hobbema.

Baroque Catholic art often served Counter-Reformation goals, making sacred stories immediate and emotionally compelling. In Spain, Zurbaran, Ribera, Murillo, and Velazquez combined realism, devotion, court portraiture, and dramatic light.

Caravaggio changed European painting with dramatic lighting, ordinary models, close-up sacred scenes, and intense realism. His influence spread through Italy, Spain, France, the Netherlands, and beyond.

The Baroque widened painting's audiences and functions. Church commissions, royal courts, civic institutions, private collectors, print culture, academies, and open markets supported different kinds of painting. Catholic regions often wanted persuasive religious images, while Protestant Dutch patrons encouraged landscapes, interiors, portraits, still lifes, and scenes of ordinary life. This variety made Baroque painting both theatrical and domestic, public and intimate.

[[Rembrandt]] transformed portraiture, biblical painting, etching, and self-portraiture through light, shadow, texture, and psychological depth. His late works often make rough surface and inward feeling more important than polished finish.

Vermeer and Dutch genre painters turned everyday interiors into meditations on light, domesticity, social role, music, letters, maps, and looking. Still-life painters used food, flowers, skulls, glass, and silver to combine material delight with moral reflection.

In France, classicism coexisted with Baroque drama. Poussin and Claude created ordered history and landscape painting, while court patronage under Louis XIV encouraged hierarchy, grandeur, and academic standards.

Rococo painting favoured elegance, lightness, pleasure, eroticism, pastoral fantasy, and decorative intimacy, especially in France through Watteau, Boucher, and Fragonard.

Neoclassicism, led by David and others, returned to antique severity, moral clarity, civic virtue, and historical subject matter, partly in response to Enlightenment and revolutionary ideals.

Neoclassical painting gave way to academic art and history painting in the 19th century, where state salons, academies, and public exhibitions shaped reputation. Painters balanced antique models, modern events, portraiture, and public moral drama.

The rise of the public exhibition changed painting's social life. Salons, critics, dealers, museums, reproductions, and later independent exhibitions made artists less dependent on single patrons and more exposed to public debate.

Neoclassicism's rival, Romanticism ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Romanticism)), turned toward storm, ruin, revolution, nightmare, nationalism, the exotic, and the sublime. Goya's disasters, Gericault's shipwreck, Delacroix's colour, Friedrich's solitude, and Turner's atmosphere all widened painting's emotional range.

Realists challenged academic hierarchy by making ordinary life worthy of large-scale seriousness. Courbet's peasants, Daumier's urban poor, Millet's rural labourers, and Manet's modern figures undermined inherited distinctions between noble and base subjects.

The 19th century also brought new tensions between official art and independent practice. Salon juries, state commissions, dealers, critics, and alternative exhibitions all shaped which paintings reached publics and how new styles were judged.

Academic painters continued to command prestige through technical finish, historical costume, mythological subjects, and public commissions, even while avant-garde painters attacked their values. The contrast between polished Salon surfaces and experimental brushwork became one of the defining conflicts of modern painting.

## Romanticism, Realism, and Impressionism

![[attachments/the_history_of_western_painting_painting_western_018.webp|420]]

*J. M. W. Turner, The Fighting Temeraire*

![[attachments/the_history_of_western_painting_painting_western_020.webp|420]]

*Vincent van Gogh, The Starry Night*

Romantic painting emphasized emotion, imagination, nature, the sublime, violence, exoticism, and individual vision. Goya, Delacroix, Gericault, Turner, Constable, Friedrich, and others reshaped landscape and history painting.

Realism turned toward modern life, labour, peasants, ordinary people, and social fact. Courbet, Daumier, Millet, and related artists challenged academic idealization.

Impressionism explored light, atmosphere, colour, fleeting perception, modern leisure, and visible brushwork. Monet, Renoir, Pissarro, Sisley, Degas, and Morisot changed the surface and subject of painting.

Impressionists used modern subjects: boulevards, railways, cafes, theatres, gardens, boating, dancers, workers, and suburbs. Their broken colour and outdoor painting challenged academic finish.

Impressionism also changed the meaning of finish. Rapid brushwork, open composition, and visible paint suggested that perception itself was temporary, mobile, and dependent on light rather than fixed contour.

Post-Impressionists such as Cezanne, van Gogh, Gauguin, and Seurat pushed beyond Impressionism toward structure, expressive colour, symbolism, pointillism, and modern abstraction's foundations.

Cezanne searched for durable structure beneath perception, helping inspire Cubism. Van Gogh used colour and brushwork for emotional intensity, while Gauguin pursued symbolic flatness, exotic subjects, and anti-natural colour.

Seurat's Neo-Impressionism used controlled dots of colour and optical mixture, joining modern leisure scenes to colour theory. This method made painting both scientific-looking and decorative.

Post-Impressionism opened several paths: Cezanne toward structure, van Gogh toward expression, Gauguin toward symbolism, and Seurat toward optical system. Later modernism drew on all of them.

Early modernism also drew from non-European art, including African sculpture, Japanese prints, Oceanic objects, and popular imagery. These sources were often misunderstood or appropriated, but they helped European painters question naturalism, perspective, anatomy, and academic beauty.

Symbolism and Art Nouveau turned away from literal realism toward dream, myth, decorative line, psychology, and spiritual suggestion. Puvis de Chavannes, Moreau, Redon, Klimt, and others expanded painting's symbolic possibilities.

The 19th century also strengthened landscape as an independent modern subject. Barbizon painters, Impressionists, Post-Impressionists, and national schools used landscape to explore light, labour, weather, memory, and modern perception.

Portraiture and genre painting changed under the pressure of [[Photography (Photography, History of)|photography]]. Some painters pursued realism and modern life; others emphasized brushwork, colour, structure, or psychological interpretation beyond photographic likeness.

## Modern Painting

Modern Western painting broke with Renaissance naturalism. Fauvism, Cubism, Expressionism, Futurism, Dada, Surrealism, Constructivism, De Stijl, abstraction, and later movements redefined image, space, colour, and meaning.

[[Picasso]] and Braque developed Cubism, fragmenting objects and space. Kandinsky, Malevich, Mondrian, and others explored nonrepresentational painting.

Surrealism used dreams, chance, automatism, fantasy, and psychoanalytic imagery. Dali, Ernst, Miro, Magritte, and others transformed the image-world of painting.

After World War II, Abstract Expressionism moved the avant-garde centre to New York through Pollock, Rothko, Newman, Still, de Kooning, and others. Later Pop art, Minimalism, Op art, Conceptual art, photorealism, postmodernism, and global art expanded painting's definitions.

Fauvism made colour independent and intense; Expressionism used distortion for inner emotion; Futurism praised speed and modernity; Dada attacked artistic convention; and Constructivism linked abstraction to social and technological ideals.

Cubism was especially decisive because it rejected single-point perspective and broke objects into shifting planes. It changed collage, abstraction, design, sculpture, and later modernist ideas of pictorial structure.

Early 20th-century Paris remained a meeting place for competing modernisms. [[Picasso]], a Spanish-born artist working largely in France, and Braque developed Cubism by treating objects as structures seen from multiple viewpoints rather than as forms fixed in Renaissance space. Matisse made colour, rhythm, and decorative harmony central to modern painting, while Dada and Surrealism challenged rational composition through chance, dream, absurdity, and psychic association.

After 1945, American painting moved from gestural abstraction to colour-field painting, hard-edge abstraction, Pop images, Minimal surfaces, shaped canvases, and conceptual approaches that questioned whether painting needed illusion, gesture, or even a traditional object.

The shift of avant-garde power from Paris to New York did not end painting's European arguments. Abstract Expressionism made gesture, scale, and surface into events, while later colour-field painters reduced composition to vast areas of colour. Pop art reintroduced mass media and consumer images; Minimalism stressed objecthood and repeated forms; photorealism tested painting against photography; and conceptual art asked whether the idea could outweigh the painted object. These movements widened painting's field without eliminating inherited genres.

Late 20th-century painting became plural. Figuration, abstraction, political painting, feminist art, neo-expressionism, photorealism, street influence, and global traditions coexisted rather than forming one dominant style.

Western painting ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_painting)) also became less purely Western as museums, markets, migration, colonial history, and global communication brought European traditions into dialogue with art from the Americas, Africa, Asia, and Oceania.

Modern and contemporary painters repeatedly reconsidered representation. Some revived the figure, narrative, history, and social criticism; others emphasized abstraction, material surface, conceptual rules, or the physical act of painting.

The 20th century also changed painting through new institutions. Commercial galleries, modern museums, art schools, photography, film, manifestos, criticism, and international exhibitions helped movements spread faster than earlier workshop or court systems.

Painting's modern history is therefore not a single march toward abstraction. It is a repeated argument among image, surface, material, perception, politics, memory, identity, and the artist's relation to older traditions.

Even when modern painters rejected tradition, they often argued with it. History painting, portraiture, landscape, still life, religious image, and decorative pattern returned in altered forms, showing that Western painting repeatedly renews old genres by changing their visual language.

By the late 20th century, painting's survival depended on this capacity for revision. It could absorb photography, mass media, political imagery, abstraction, installation, and older craft without losing its identity as coloured material arranged on a surface.

This long history also shows that Western painting ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_painting)) repeatedly changes when its social setting changes: cave ritual, temple and tomb, church and monastery, court and city, academy and salon, market and museum, studio and street all produced different kinds of painted meaning.

Across these shifts, [[The Art of Painting (Painting)|painting]] remained both object and image, craft and idea, public memory and private vision, continually balancing inherited forms with new techniques and new audiences.

Its continuity lies in that constant reinvention across changing places, patrons, beliefs, materials, techniques, institutions, markets, and visual expectations.

Western painting changed whenever its support and setting changed. Cave walls, pottery, plaster, parchment, wood panels, canvas, glass, manuscript pages, chapel walls, altarpieces, easel paintings, prints, and modern mixed media created different relations between image, viewer, patron, and ritual. Fresco favoured planned wall cycles; oil painting allowed slow glazing and detail; canvas helped large movable works circulate through studios, markets, and exhibitions.

Museums and conservation changed painting's afterlife. Once works moved from churches, palaces, workshops, or private houses into public museums and reproductions, they became part of national heritage, scholarship, tourism, restoration, and global art markets. Cleaning, attribution, photography, catalogues, loans, conservation science, and digital images now influence how paintings are valued and understood. The history of painting is therefore also a history of preservation, display, collecting, and interpretation.

The history is also a history of pictorial space. Paleolithic painters used cave surfaces and animal movement; Greek and Roman artists explored modelling and illusion; Byzantine painters organized sacred hierarchy; Gothic artists refined line and luminous surface; Renaissance painters constructed mathematical space; Baroque painters opened dramatic light and depth; modern painters questioned whether flatness, colour, gesture, or abstraction might be more truthful than illusion.

Painting's subject matter widened over time. Religious images dominated medieval and much Renaissance production, but portraits, landscapes, mythologies, still lifes, genre scenes, history paintings, political allegories, and modern everyday subjects gradually gained status. The rise of portraiture and landscape showed that individual identity and observed nature could become major carriers of meaning.

The painter's social role also changed. Medieval painters often worked as craftspeople in workshops serving church and court; Renaissance masters claimed intellectual and individual status; Baroque and academic painters negotiated academies, royal patrons, and churches; 19th-century painters faced salons, critics, and dealers; modern painters often worked through avant-garde groups, manifestos, galleries, museums, and independent exhibitions.

Colour, line, and surface created recurring conflicts. Disegno and colore, drawing and colour, Florentine structure and Venetian atmosphere, classical restraint and Romantic expression, academic finish and Impressionist brushwork, naturalism and abstraction all mark repeated debates about what painting should do. These debates make the tradition a sequence of technical discoveries and arguments about vision.

Modern painting did not simply reject the past. Impressionism, Post-Impressionism, Cubism, Expressionism, Surrealism, abstraction, and later movements continued to transform inherited subjects: landscape became colour and light, the body became structure or psychology, history became social critique, and the painted surface itself became a subject. Western painting therefore remains a record of seeing, belief, power, memory, and material invention.

## Materials, Patronage, and Changing Ways of Seeing

Western painting ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_painting)) changes when artists change both what they see and how they make images. Fresco, tempera, oil paint, watercolour, acrylic, collage, photography, and digital processes offer different possibilities for colour, texture, scale, speed, and correction. Oil paint, for example, dries slowly and can be layered or blended, helping artists create rich surface effects and subtle light. Technical change does not determine style, but it expands what artists can attempt.

For many centuries, patronage shaped painting. Churches commissioned altarpieces, frescoes, and devotional images; rulers and nobles wanted portraits, historical scenes, and displays of power; merchants and urban households bought private works. The Renaissance developed new interest in perspective, anatomy, classical learning, and the observable world, but religious painting remained central. Artists also began to gain greater individual status, becoming known not only as skilled artisans but as intellectual creators.

Perspective offered a systematic way to suggest three-dimensional space on a flat surface, yet it was never the only artistic goal. Renaissance, Baroque, Rococo, Neoclassical, Romantic, Realist, and Impressionist painters made different choices about composition, light, emotion, history, nature, and ordinary life. Baroque artists used drama, movement, and strong contrasts; Realists turned toward labour and social life; Impressionists explored fleeting light and modern urban experience. Each movement revised what subjects were considered worthy of serious art.

The nineteenth and twentieth centuries challenged the idea that painting must imitate visible reality. Modernism explored abstraction, expressive colour, fractured form, symbolism, unconscious imagery, and the material surface of paint. Cubism changed how objects could be shown by presenting multiple viewpoints; abstract art emphasized form, colour, line, and process; later artists used collage, text, photography, performance, and installation to question the boundary of painting itself.

Western painting is also connected to global exchange, colonialism, migration, and museums. Pigments, paper, prints, artistic ideas, and collectors moved across continents, while artists absorbed influences from Islamic, African, Asian, Indigenous American, and Oceanic traditions—sometimes respectfully, sometimes through unequal appropriation. Contemporary painting continues to address identity, ecology, memory, violence, technology, and the history of representation. Its history is therefore not a closed march of styles but a continuing argument about what an image can do.

## Key Events & Developments

- 15000-10000 BCE: Upper Paleolithic cave paintings flourished.
- c. 1600-1100 BCE: Aegean Bronze Age frescoes and pottery developed.
- c. 900-700 BCE: Geometric Greek vase painting flourished.
- 717-843: Byzantine Iconoclasm restricted figural religious art.
- 800: Carolingian court culture transformed northern manuscript painting.
- 12th-13th centuries: Romanesque and Gothic painting spread across Europe.
- 15th century: Renaissance perspective and oil painting transformed Western art.
- 19th century: Romanticism, Realism, Impressionism, and Post-Impressionism reshaped painting.
- 20th century: Cubism, abstraction, Surrealism, Abstract Expressionism, and later movements expanded painting radically.

## Interesting Facts

- Lascaux cave paintings still show remarkable movement and observation after millennia.
- Greek vase painting preserves much of what is lost from Greek wall painting.
- Icons became central devotional objects in Byzantine and Orthodox painting.
- Oil painting helped northern Renaissance artists achieve fine detail and luminous colour.
- Modern painting often rejected illusionistic depth in favour of flatness, process, colour, and concept.
