# Hong Kong

> [!abstract] Reference navigation

## Introduction

Hong Kong is a special administrative region ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Special_administrative_region)) of [[China]] on the south coast, east of the Pearl River estuary. It includes Hong Kong Island, ceded to Great Britain in 1842; southern Kowloon and Stonecutters Island, ceded in 1860; and the New Territories, leased from 1898 to 1997. The Chinese-British Joint Declaration of 1984 prepared the return to China on July 1, 1997.

The territory grew through political expansion and land reclamation ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Land_reclamation)). Hong Kong Island and nearby islets cover about 31 square miles, urban Kowloon about 18 square miles, and the New Territories more than 90 percent of the land. The [[Victoria and the Victorian Age (Victoria)|Victoria]] district became the administrative and economic center after the British landing in 1841, and growth first rested on the harbor, opium trade, China trade, labor, and added territory.

## Relief

Hong Kong has rugged relief that contrasts sharply with the low Pearl River Delta. Geologically it belongs to the eroded upland region of the South China massif, with structures formed about 150 million years ago in the later Jurassic Period. Volcanic rocks, granitic intrusion, and the drowning of a denuded dome helped form the protected harbor.

The landscape is a partially submerged upland cut by northeast-southwest ridges. Mount Tai Mo, at 3,140 feet, is the highest peak, while Lantau Peak, Kowloon Peak, Victoria Peak, and Mount Parker frame the territory and harbor. Lowlands, valleys, floodplains, and reclaimed land occupy less than one-fifth of the area.

Hong Kong has no large river system. The main exception is the Sham Chun, or Shenzhen, River, which forms the boundary with Guangdong and flows into Deep Bay. Most streams are short and run across the grain of the terrain, while reservoirs and catchment systems reduce downstream fresh water.

## Soils

*Hong Kong skyscrapers in a night of typhoon*

Most Hong Kong soils are acidic and low in fertility. The main exception is the alluvial soil of the Deep Bay area, where sediment from the Pearl River estuary settles into mudflats. Rice farming and later intensive vegetable cultivation changed these alluvial soils, while hill soils elsewhere are often thin, nutrient-poor, and vulnerable to sheet and gully erosion when exposed.

## Climate

Hong Kong lies on the northern edge of the tropical zone and has a monsoonal climate. Winters are cool and dry under northeast winds, while summers are hot, humid, and wet under ocean winds. Mean temperatures are about 60 F in January and 84 F in July, with annual rainfall around 88 inches, more than half falling in summer.

Typhoons usually occur between June and October. Of the 20 to 30 storms formed each year over the western North Pacific and South China Sea, about five or six may affect Hong Kong, sometimes bringing destructive rain and wind. The transitional climate supports both tropical and temperate-zone plant families, with mangroves, swamp cover, herbaceous growth, country parks, and protected landscapes surviving beside dense urban settlement.

## People

The overwhelming majority of Hong Kong's population is Chinese by origin, mostly from Guangdong and Hong Kong itself, with smaller numbers from other parts of China. Non-Chinese residents form only a small fraction and include mainly Asians, especially Filipinos, Indonesians, and South Asians, along with smaller groups of Americans, Canadians, Australians, and others.

## Languages

Chinese and English ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/English_language)) are both official languages, but spoken Cantonese is the common language and is almost universally understood in Hong Kong. Other dialects, including Teochew, Hakka, and Tanka, are used within communities connected to Guangdong and Hong Kong, while migrants from elsewhere in China and non-Chinese minorities often use their own languages. Mandarin use has risen with reintegration into China.

## Religion

![[attachments/hong_kong_001.webp|420]]

*Hong Kong Harbour Night 2019-06-11*

Most people in Hong Kong do not profess a religion, but religious life remains varied. Among Chinese believers, [[Buddha and Buddhism (Buddhism)|Buddhism]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buddhism)) and [[Taoism (Daoism)|Daoism]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taoism)) are the largest traditions, often mixed with Confucian beliefs and temple practices. Temples may honor several deities, especially those linked to the sea and weather, such as Dian Hau, Guanyin, Hong Shing, and Wong Daisin.

Christians form a small but important group in Hong Kong, with somewhat more Protestants than Roman Catholics. Smaller numbers of Muslims, Hindus, Sikhs, and Jews also live there. Settlement is highly urban and linear, following coastlines and transport routes, with older density around Victoria Harbour and newer towns in the New Territories.

## Economy

With limited natural resources, Hong Kong depends heavily on imports. Its free-port position once made entrepot trade with China central, but the [[United Nations]] embargo of 1951 sharply reduced that trade. Cheap labor, export needs, and laissez-faire policy then encouraged light industry, foreign investment, finance, and services.

As [[China]] opened its foreign policy, Hong Kong-China trade revived and Hong Kong became a manufacturing center, trading hub, shipping center, regional financial center, and agent of China's modernization. The services sector now makes up about four-fifths of GDP. Agriculture is small because only about 6 percent of land is arable, though vegetables, pond fish, flowers, fruit, livestock, and marine fishing remain part of primary activity.

## Manufacturing

Manufacturing ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manufacturing)) expanded rapidly in the 1950s when immigrant Chinese industrialists, many from [[Shanghai]], brought capital and technology. Foreign investment used cheap labor and nearby raw materials, and industry first concentrated in Kowloon before spreading to Kwun Tong, Tsuen Wan, Tuen Mun, Tai Po, and Yuen Long.

Manufacturing was once the leading part of the Hong Kong economy, but it later became much smaller beside services. Textiles and clothing remained the leading manufacturing activity and a major domestic export, while electronics became the second largest export earner. Shipbuilding and repair, aircraft engineering, steel rolling, plastics, machine parts, and cement served local and export needs.

## Finance

Since 1969, Hong Kong has become a major financial center in the Asia-Pacific region despite lacking a central bank. The Hong Kong Monetary Authority and selected commercial banks handle monetary functions and regulate the Hong Kong dollar. Foreign banks, deposit-taking companies, currency markets, the stock market, and the gold bullion market all helped build financial importance, while the absence of exchange controls supported growth.

Free trade made Hong Kong a major trade center. Imports include goods, fuels, raw materials, semimanufactures, and food, while exports and reexports connect Hong Kong with China, Japan, Taiwan, the European Union, Singapore, and other markets. Tourism benefits from transport, shopping, business, and culture.

## Legislative Council

The Basic Law gives executive authority to a chief executive under the central government in [[Peking (Beijing)|Beijing]]. Legislative authority belongs to the Legislative Council, whose members serve four-year terms. Electoral arrangements developed through election committees, geographic constituencies, and functional constituencies, while the Basic Law stated an eventual aim of universal suffrage.

In 2007, the Standing Committee of the National People's Congress ruled that universal suffrage would not occur before 2012, and Beijing later indicated that direct election for chief executive might be allowed in 2017. Civil and criminal law generally derive from the [[United Kingdom]], with the Court of Final Appeal above the High Court and lower courts.

## Housing

Housing ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Housing)) has long been a central problem in Hong Kong because space is limited and population pressure is high. Before [[World Wars (World War II)|World War II]], development was often unplanned: British residents lived around the Peak, wealthier groups on higher ground, and many Chinese residents in crowded lowland harbor areas with narrow streets, poor ventilation, limited water, and inadequate sanitation.

Postwar shortages were severe, with many families sharing cubicles, bed spaces, attics, roofs, and verandas. Government involvement grew after 1953, when resettlement blocks were built for fire victims, and expanded in the 1960s as squatters and the urban poor were relocated. Public housing once sheltered more than half the population, though by the early 21st century the share was about one-third.

## Education

Hong Kong has many subsidized, aided, and private schools, while education is compulsory through junior secondary level. Students take examinations at major transition points, and postsecondary education expanded despite pressure from small institutions. The University of Hong Kong, founded in 1911, and the Chinese University of Hong Kong, founded in 1963, grew strongly from the mid-1980s.

Other institutions include the Hong Kong Polytechnic, Hong Kong Baptist University, colleges of education, the City University of Hong Kong, and many vocational and technical programs. Many students study overseas. Nearly all residents have at least primary education, and cultural life blends East and West through festivals, opera, music, film, and public events.

## Recreation

Hong Kong's country park system covers about two-fifths of the land and makes outdoor recreation part of daily life. City residents use parks for walking, running, and tai chi chuan, while remote areas support hiking, cycling, camping, and water sports. Ocean Park, the Hong Kong Coliseum, and Hong Kong Stadium support sports and entertainment.

Libraries, museums, town halls, and cultural centers give Hong Kong a dense cultural infrastructure. The Hong Kong Central Library, opened in 2001, anchors public libraries. Museums cover history, art, science and technology, and space, while major cultural centers provide galleries, theatres, and concert spaces. Press and broadcasting developed into a wide communications network.

## History

Archaeological finds show Neolithic settlement in the Hong Kong area, and early modern peoples likely arrived from North China in the 2nd millennium BCE. Cantonese settlement began around 100 BCE, Hakka arrived later, and Hoklo were present by the mid-17th century. Before British rule, Hong Kong Island had a small fishing population, poor soil and water, mountains, and a reputation for pirates, but its deep sheltered harbor attracted British opium vessels by 1821.

After the First Opium War of 1839-42, the Treaty of Nanjing ceded Hong Kong Island to Britain. The Second Opium War led to the 1860 cession of southern Kowloon and Stonecutters Island, and the 1898 convention leased the New Territories and many islands for 99 years. Population rose from 120,000 in 1861 to more than 300,000 by century's end.

## Contemporary Hong Kong

After Japanese occupation ended, British troops returned on August 30, 1945, and civil government resumed in May 1946. Refugees from the Chinese civil war and later political upheavals joined returning residents. The 1951 embargo damaged entrepot trade, but light industries such as textiles revived the economy. Labor unrest and 1967 riots, influenced by China's Cultural Revolution, were followed by better labor laws, public housing, and public works.

From the mid-1970s, improved relations with [[China]] and economic growth raised the issue of the New Territories lease ending in 1997. The 1984 Joint Declaration, the Basic Law, and the formula of one country, two systems shaped the handover. After 1997, Hong Kong remained a major financial, trade, and service center under Chinese sovereignty, while questions of political authority, representation, and autonomy continued to define public life.
