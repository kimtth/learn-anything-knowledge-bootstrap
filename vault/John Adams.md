# John Adams

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> John Adams was an American revolutionary, constitutional thinker, diplomat, and the second president of the United States. His career joined advocacy for independence, diplomacy, constitutional government, and the difficult partisan politics of the early republic.

## Introduction

John Adams was born on October 30, 1735, in Braintree, Massachusetts, and died at Quincy on July 4, 1826. He was an early advocate of American independence, a major figure in the Continental Congress, author of the Massachusetts constitution of 1780, signer of the Treaty of Paris, first U.S. minister to the Court of St. James, first vice president, and second president of the United States.

His reputation declined in the 19th century but rose again in the late 20th century as his correspondence revealed his honesty, sharp language, political thought, realistic foreign policy, and role in one of the most important American political families.

## Early Life And Law

Adams was the eldest son of Deacon John Adams and Susanna Boylston. His father, a farmer, shoemaker, selectman, and community leader, hoped he would enter the ministry. Adams graduated from Harvard College in 1755, taught school in Worcester, then chose law and began practicing near Boston in 1758.

Abigail Smith, whom Adams married in 1764, became his confidante and political partner. Their letters preserve an unusually rich record of revolutionary-era marriage and thought, and their children included future president John Quincy Adams.

Adams entered resistance politics through legal and constitutional argument. His 1765 Dissertation on the Canon and Feudal Law opposed the Stamp Act by portraying Parliament's colonial intrusions as coercive. He also opposed the Townshend Acts, yet in 1770 defended the British soldiers charged after the Boston Massacre, insisting on legal rights even when unpopular.

## Revolution And Diplomacy

The Continental Congress made Adams central to independence. Elected in 1774, he and Samuel Adams led the radical faction. His Novanglus essays denied Parliament's authority to legislate for the colonies, and by 1775 he had become known as the Atlas of independence.

In Congress, Adams nominated [[George Washington]] as commander of the Continental Army, selected Thomas Jefferson to draft the Declaration of Independence, and defended the final break with Britain in debate. His Thoughts on Government guided state constitution-making, while his Plan of Treaties shaped early American foreign policy.

Adams's wartime work included heading the Board of War and Ordnance and helping create an American navy. Sent to Europe in 1778, he later returned for the Massachusetts Constitutional Convention, then went back to help negotiate peace with Britain.

The Treaty of Paris in 1783 benefited from the blend of Benjamin Franklin's discretion and Adams's forceful temperament. Adams later served as U.S. minister in London from 1785 to 1788. He also worked with Jefferson on a Dutch loan that helped consolidate American debts.

## Vice Presidency And Presidency

In 1789, Adams finished second to [[George Washington]] and became the first vice president. He disliked the office, but as president of the Senate he cast more tie-breaking votes than any later vice president and supported Washington's major policies.

The election of 1796 made Adams president by a narrow electoral majority over Thomas Jefferson, who became vice president and leader of the rival Republicans. Adams inherited Washington's cabinet and confronted a naval conflict with France known as the Quasi-War.

Adams's greatest presidential success was avoiding full war with France. After French officials demanded bribes from an American mission, he built up the navy but then sent another peace delegation against the advice of his cabinet and party. The decision damaged him politically but spared the young republic a costly war.

His chief domestic failure was signing the Alien and Sedition Acts in 1798, which allowed deportations and prosecutions of Republican writers. Adams later called the Sedition Act his worst political mistake.

In 1800, Adams lost to Jefferson after New York's electoral votes went to Jefferson and Aaron Burr. Before leaving office, he appointed Federalist judges, including John Marshall as chief justice. He was also the first president to live in the presidential mansion.

## Retirement

Adams retired to Quincy in 1801 and lived another quarter century. With encouragement from Benjamin Rush, he resumed correspondence with Jefferson in 1812, producing 158 letters on religion, aging, language, the French Revolution, and party conflict.

Adams and Jefferson died within hours of each other on July 4, 1826, the 50th anniversary of the Declaration of Independence. Their final exchange symbolized two revolutionary temperaments: Adams as realist, skeptic, and principled pessimist; Jefferson as idealist, romantic, and pragmatic optimist.
