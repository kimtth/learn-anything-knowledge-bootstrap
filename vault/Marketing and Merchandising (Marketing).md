# Marketing and Merchandising (Marketing)

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> Marketing directs goods and services from producers to consumers by studying exchange, customers, products, price, promotion, distribution, and market position. Developed from economics, it applies to commercial and nonprofit organizations, while merchandising focuses on stimulating retail sales.

## Introduction

![[attachments/marketing_001.webp|420]]

*Marketing mix diagram*

Marketing ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marketing)) is the set of activities that directs goods and services from producers to consumers by promoting and facilitating voluntary exchange. It applies beyond profit businesses to institutions such as hospitals, schools, and museums, while merchandising focuses more narrowly on promoting retail sale to consumers. As a discipline, marketing emerged in the early 20th century from economics by studying middlemen, demand, markets, and nonprice functions.

## Positioning

Positioning creates and communicates a clear brand or company place relative to competitors, as with Volvo's safety image or Mercedes-Benz engineering image. After strategy comes tactical marketing-mix planning, organized around product, price, place, and promotion.

## Product

![[attachments/marketing_002.webp|420]]

*Retail merchandising display*

The product is the offering made to customers, ideally designed from target-market input rather than only engineering preference. Products pass through life cycles of birth, growth, maturity, and decline, and new-product development involves production, finance, research, personnel, public relations, packaging, and branding. In industrialized markets, packaging and brands help communicate appeal, quality, identity, and differentiation.

## Price

Price reflects quality, performance, market valuation, and strategic message. High prices can signal quality or prestige, while discount strategies such as more for the same or the same for less force industries such as airlines and pharmaceuticals to rethink older brand-premium assumptions.

## Place

Place, or distribution, concerns where and how a product becomes available. Channels can include processors, warehouses, wholesalers, supermarkets, brokers, banks, direct mail, factory outlets, or Internet shopping. Each channel has capacity limits, so marketers may use direct channels when intermediaries restrict access or add unnecessary steps.

## Promotion

Promotion communicates with and influences customers through sales force, advertising, sales promotion, and public relations. Sales representatives are costly but effective for complex, expensive, or personal goods because they can personalize presentations, answer objections, secure commitments, and manage accounts.

## Advertising

Advertising is paid, nonpersonal communication by a known sponsor through print, broadcast, Internet, e-mail, Web sites, and other media. Sales promotion adds short-term incentives, often attracting brand switchers. Promotion grew in the late 20th century, but poorly coordinated tools can confuse customers when, for example, one message stresses luxury and another stresses rebates.

## Implementation and Control

Integrated marketing communications treats all promotional tools as one coordinated effort, while marketing control checks whether strategy still meets objectives through annual-plan, profitability, efficiency, and strategic control.

## Marketing Actors

Marketing actors—customers, distributors, and facilitators—interact with one another inside governmental, economic, social, and competitive environments that shape the whole marketing process.

## Customers

Understanding customers requires knowing who buys, uses, influences, decides, and pays. A purchase can involve several roles, such as initiator, influencer, decider, buyer, and user, and one person may fill multiple roles. Marketers therefore study what customers buy, why, how, when, where, and who participates in the decision.

## Intermediaries and Distribution Channels

Producers often use wholesalers, retailers, agents, brokers, distributors, and financial intermediaries because marketing channels perform information, promotion, negotiation, ordering, financing, risk taking, physical possession, payment, and title flows. Channel design depends on desired service outputs such as lot size, waiting time, proximity, variety, and backup service.

Manufacturers delegate channel tasks when intermediaries have resources, specialization, scale, or local market access. Channel management requires motivation and power bases such as reward, coercive, legitimate, expert, and referent power. Channel conflict can be vertical, horizontal, or multichannel, as when dealers, franchisees, or competing channels clash over markets and goals.

## Wholesalers

Wholesaling sells goods or services to other firms for resale or business use, usually in bulk and below retail prices. Merchant wholesalers take title and may offer full service or limited service; brokers and agents bring buyers and sellers together without taking title; manufacturers' and retailers' branches conduct wholesaling from within buyer or seller organizations. Wholesalers remain especially important for small retailers and in many less-developed distribution systems.

## Retailers

Retailing sells directly to consumers for personal, nonbusiness use through stores, mail, telephone, television, Internet, vending, street selling, or home selling. Marketplaces and peddlers long dominated retailing; modern chain stores trace to 1859 with A&P, while department stores, malls, and flagship stores became major modern retail forms.

## Supermarkets

Supermarkets are large, self-service, high-volume, low-margin stores that serve food and household needs. Michael Cullin opened the first true U.S. supermarket, King Kullen, in 1930. After [[World Wars (World War II)|World War II]], supermarkets displaced many small grocers worldwide, while convenience stores served nearby residents with long hours, limited lines, and fill-in purchases.

## Superstores

Superstores, combination stores, hypermarkets, discount stores, off-price retailers, warehouse clubs, direct sellers, direct marketers, vending operations, and retail organizations broadened retailing. Discounting uses high-volume, low-profit, low-service strategies; off-price retailers buy below wholesale; direct marketing uses measurable contact by mail, telephone, or Internet; and corporate chains, voluntary chains, retailer cooperatives, consumer cooperatives, franchises, and conglomerates organize ownership and coordination.

## Consumer Cooperatives

Consumer co-ops became especially popular in [[Denmark]], Finland, Iceland, Norway, Sweden, and Great Britain, often where residents saw local prices as high or service as poor. Franchise organizations contract franchisers with independent franchisees, while merchandising conglomerates combine diversified retail lines under central ownership.

## Marketing Facilitators

Marketing facilitators include advertising agencies, market research firms, transportation firms, and warehousing firms. Independent facilitators can provide specialized expertise and objectivity when firms do not need or cannot afford permanent internal departments.

Advertising agencies manage paid communications through account management, creative, research, and media-planning departments. Market research firms gather quantitative and qualitative data on customers, competitors, distributors, and marketplace forces. Transportation firms move goods through channels, while storage and distribution warehouses inspect, track, repackage, ship, and invoice goods.

## Marketing Across Sectors

Marketing principles apply across industries, but practices vary by product, service, buying behaviour, and sector, including government, consumer goods, services, business markets, nonprofit activity, social causes, and places.

## Markets and Buying Establishments

The U.S. civilian buying establishment includes departments, administrations, agencies, boards, commissions, and the executive office, alongside bodies such as the Tennessee Valley Authority. The military buying establishment centers on the Department of Defense and supply agencies. Government purchasing uses open bids or negotiated contracts, while consumer goods are classified as convenience, shopping, specialty, or unsought goods. Services differ from goods because they are intangible, inseparable from production, variable, and perishable.

## Business and Nonprofit Marketing

Business marketing sells to organizations such as construction, manufacturing, mining, transportation, utilities, communications, and distribution firms, often in very large transactions. Nonprofit marketing helps museums, hospitals, universities, churches, and other tax-exempt or donation-supported organizations pursue growth, funding, and mission goals.

Social marketing applies marketing to beliefs, attitudes, values, and practices intended to change behaviour for social benefit, and it expanded through Internet social networking in the early 2000s. Place marketing promotes towns, cities, regions, states, or nations to tourists, businesses, investors, and residents while adapting to competition, technology, decline cycles, and external shocks.

## Criticisms of Marketing

Critics argue that marketing can raise prices, encourage excessive markups, use deceptive practices, create false wants, promote nonessential consumption, or give private interests too much influence. Responses include FTC guidelines, guarantees, cooling-off periods, professional standards, and arguments that brands provide symbolic, quality, and informational value.

## Marketing and Society

The societal marketing concept balances company profit, consumer satisfaction, and public interest. Examples include The Body Shop's natural products, recycled packaging, and social donations, and Ben & Jerry's caring capitalism. Marketing also supports economic development, jobs, technological progress, and consumer choice.

## Key Events & Developments

- 1859: A&P marked the birth of the modern chain store.
- 1930: Michael Cullin opened the first true U.S. supermarket.
- 1969: Scholars began extending marketing to nonprofit organizations.
- 2000s: Internet social networking reshaped social marketing.
