# Humour and Wit (Humour)

> [!abstract] Reference navigation

## Introduction

Humour ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Humour)) is communication or stimulation that tends to produce amusement and the reflex of laughter. Spontaneous laughter is a motor reflex involving about 15 facial muscles, altered breathing, and patterned movements from a faint smile to explosive laughter. Conventional smiling can be controlled by intention, but spontaneous laughter is treated as a reflex that creates puzzles because it has no obvious survival use and seems mainly to release tension.

The central paradox of laughter is that a complex mental stimulus, such as a comic story, can trigger a simple bodily response. Unlike most arts, humour has a visible physiological test: if the joke works, the body answers. This makes humour useful for studying creativity, because the step from serious insight to ridicule can often be reversed.

## The logic of laughter

![[attachments/humour_and_wit_humour_001.png|420]]

*'The feast of reason, and the flow of soul,' - ie - the wits of the age, setting the table in a roar by James Gillray*

Humour is explained through bisociation, a term used for perceiving one situation in two self-consistent but incompatible frames of reference at once. In jokes about a masochist, a widow, a doctor, a daughter's hand, or a marquis finding his wife with a bishop, the comic effect comes from a sudden switch between two different rule systems.

The marquis story shows the pattern clearly: sexual morality would call for outrage, but the marquis applies the logic of division of labour and blesses the crowd because the bishop is performing his function. The joke forces the mind to leap from one plane of meaning to another. The same structure appears in literal versus metaphorical meaning, statistical logic versus common sense, faith versus reason, and normal logic versus reversed masochistic logic.

## Laughter and emotion

Bisociation ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Act_of_Creation)) gives humour its intellectual structure, but emotion gives it force. A comic story builds tension and then lets the punch line cut across expected development. The emotion has lost its original object and is discharged in laughter. When sympathy replaces detachment, the same scene can become pathetic rather than funny.

Aggression is therefore treated as a basic ingredient of comic emotion. It may be crude in children's jokes, tavern laughter, sick jokes, or satire, and almost invisible in refined wit. Older theories also point that way: [[Aristotle and Aristotelianism|Aristotle]] connected laughter with ugliness, Cicero with baseness, [[Descartes and Cartesianism (Descartes)|Descartes]] with joy mixed with surprise or hatred, and [[Francis bacon|Francis Bacon]] with deformity.

The superiority tradition is traced through Thomas Hobbes, who in 1651 called laughter sudden glory at another's weakness, and Alexander Bain, who stressed triumph over a rival. Henri Bergson saw laughter as society's corrective humiliation of the unsocial person, Max Beerbohm found delight in suffering and contempt for the unfamiliar, and William McDougall called laughter an antidote to sympathy.

The tension-release tradition is also central. Immanuel Kant defined laughter as the sudden transformation of tense expectation into nothing. Herbert Spencer described liberated nervous energy flowing into bodily movements, and Sigmund Freud connected laughter with release from repression, infantile satisfaction, and de-tension. Large laughter can therefore be triggered by small events when stored aggression, fear, sexual tension, boredom, or group contagion amplifies the response.

## Patterns of association

Some comic reactions depend on associative memory. A stage prop, catchphrase, family joke, Charlie Chaplin's shoes, Groucho Marx's cigar, or Falstaff's entrance may work because earlier emotions and expectations are already stored. A cartoon may seem instantly funny, but the viewer still reconstructs a miniature story.

The combined theory says that humour joins incompatible contexts, abruptly redirects thought, and leaves emotion behind. The mind moves quickly, but emotion has inertia because it is tied to older bodily systems, hormones, and the sympathetic nervous system. Aldous Huxley argued that human glandular equipment reflects more dangerous evolutionary conditions; laughter becomes one harmless outlet for aggression that might otherwise turn inward or become violence.

## Verbal humour

Verbal humour is analyzed by identifying the frames of reference that collide. Puns are the most primitive form, tying two ideas by sound, as in examples from John Milton, Sigmund Freud, or the joke about Christmas as the alcoholidays. Their crudeness may explain both their popularity with children and their link with punning mania.

More complex jokes move from sound to words and ideas. Groucho Marx's joke about shooting two bucks depends on money and animals sharing one word. The marquis story is superior because it plays on ideas, not just language. The same grammar covers metaphor versus literal meaning, professional logic versus common sense, exalted versus trivial ideas, or opposed chains of reasoning.

Sustained literary humour does not rely on one punch line. Don Quixote and Sancho Panza create continuing amusement by moving between fantasy and practical sense. Comic verse uses incongruity between elevated form and low content, as in Lewis Carroll's absurd rhymes, limericks, bogus proverbs, nonsense verse, and mock-heroic contrasts.

Satire is a verbal caricature. It exaggerates and simplifies disliked features of a person, institution, or society so the reader sees familiar reality through an absurd mirror. Jonathan Swift's Gulliver's Travels works because the Yahoos are not literal truth but an overshot image. [[Aristophanes]], Swift, Anatole France, and George Orwell use such distortion to expose accepted social deformities.

## Situational humour

Situational humour often degrades dignity into mechanism. A practical joke that removes a chair turns an important person into a body obeying gravity. Soldiers acting like machines, a pedant behaving like a robot, or Hamlet getting hiccups all deflate aspiration through flesh, matter, and accident.

Bergson's phrase about the mechanical encrusted on the living explains one group of comic situations, though not all humour. Man-machine, man-animal, disguise, impersonation, and parody all depend on double perception. Walt Disney's animals act human while remaining animal; caricaturists find animal features in human faces; parody attacks pretense by forcing the sublime to meet the trivial.

Laughter at puppies, deformity, accents, stuck records, faulty spelling, and the centipede paradox follows the same logic. These cases isolate a part from its normal context or show imitation as pretense. Charles Darwin's explanation of tickling is extended: tickling becomes funny only when perceived as a mock attack. Yale University experiments found babies laughed far more when tickled by mothers than by strangers, because the attack had to feel safely unreal.

## Humour in the visual arts

Visual humour uses the same structures. A distorting mirror shows the viewer both as familiar self and absurd material. Caricature exaggerates selected features and simplifies the rest, like satire. Its malice remains harmless only when viewers know the distortion is not literal suffering; otherwise grotesque faces by [[Leonardo da Vinci]], Hogarth, or Honore Daumier can become tragic instead of comic.

## Humour in music

Musical humour is harder to explain in words, but several analogies are clear. A misplaced trumpet blast, an out-of-tune performer, animal sounds, a Chopin nocturne turned into hot jazz, or a street song played with Wagnerian pathos can create comic incompatibility. More subtle examples include Maurice Ravel's La Valse, Zoltan Kodaly's Hary Janos, and lighthearted scherzos by [[Mozart]], which may produce smiles rather than loud laughter.

## Styles and techniques in humour

Judgments of comic style depend on taste, period, and technique, but three criteria recur: originality, emphasis, and economy. Originality supplies surprise. Since true originality is rare, humorists often heighten tension through emphasis, repetition, local colour, ethnic caricature, exaggeration, and simplification.

Higher humour often prefers economy ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economy)). Economy does not mean mere shortness; it means hints instead of explanations and oblique allusion instead of frontal attack. Older cartoons hammered their message, while modern cartoons often pose a riddle that the viewer must solve. Emphasis and economy are complementary ways to guide comic response.

## Relations to art and science

Humour is presented as part of a continuum with art and science ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Science)). Scientific discovery, artistic creation, and joking all combine previously separate areas of experience. William Harvey's comparison of the fish heart to a pump, a poet's analogy, a caricaturist's cucumber-like nose, and the deciphering of the Rosetta Stone all use related structures of connection and selection.

The difference lies in purpose and emotion. The scientist seeks synthesis and feels the Aha reaction; the artist creates juxtaposition and evokes the Ah reaction; the humorist engineers collision and releases the Haha reaction. These are not sealed worlds, because wit blends into epigram, caricature into portrait, and comedy into tragedy.

## Key Events & Developments

- ancient Greece: [[Aristotle and Aristotelianism|Aristotle]] connected laughter with ugliness and debasement.
- 1651: Thomas Hobbes defined laughter as sudden glory in Leviathan.
- 19th century: Herbert Spencer and Alexander Bain gave major tension-release and superiority accounts.
- 1905: Sigmund Freud linked jokes and laughter to repression, release, and infantile sources.
- 1961: A survey of American children showed that discomfort, hoaxing, and mortification caused more laughter than witty remarks.
