## Introduction

![[attachments/shinto_001.webp|420]]

*Shinto sanctuary at Ise Grand Shrine*

Shinto ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shinto)) is the indigenous religion of Japan. Its name means "the way of kami," where kami refers to sacred, superior, mystical, or divine powers, including gods and deities. The term came into use to distinguish native Japanese practices from Buddhism, which entered Japan in the 6th century CE.

Shinto ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shinto)) has no founder, no official scripture in the strict sense, and no fixed dogma. It is seen more clearly in Japanese social life, personal motivation, values, and habits of thought than in a formal system of doctrine. Its guiding beliefs have survived through ritual practice, shrine life, myth, family custom, and attitudes toward nature, purity, sincerity, and community.

Shinto is often divided into three main types. Shrine Shinto ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shrine_Shinto)) is the oldest main current and includes the now-defunct State Shinto, which joined religion and state and was closely tied to the Imperial family. Sect Shinto ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sect_Shinto)) consists of organized religious bodies, many formed in the 19th century. Folk Shinto includes local practices such as roadside images, agricultural rites, and household or village beliefs without formal doctrine.

## Neo-Confucian Shinto

After the Tokugawa shogunate was founded in 1603, Shinto again interacted strongly with Confucian thought. Scholars interpreted Shinto through Neo-Confucianism, especially the teachings of Chu Hsi and Wang Yang-ming. Neo-Confucian study became important for the warrior class.

Yoshikawa Koretaru and Yamazaki Ansai were major figures in Confucian Shinto. They connected traditional Shinto theories with Neo-Confucian ideas, including the Supreme Ultimate, which they identified with the first kami of the Nihon shoki. Yoshikawa stressed political philosophy, imperial virtues, loyalty, and filial piety. Yamazaki developed a stronger emperor worship and mystical piety.

## Fukko Shinto

Fukko Shinto, or Restoration Shinto, belonged to the Kokugaku movement that began near the end of the 17th century. Its advocates argued that Shinto norms should come from ancient Japanese classics and ancestral life-attitudes, not Buddhist or Confucian interpretation.

Motoori Norinaga represented this school. He emphasized musubi, the mystical power of becoming or creation, and a this-worldly view of life in which the world develops through constant change. His teaching supported respect for the Imperial line and absolute trust in kami for matters beyond human ability.

Hirata Atsutane, Motoori's most important Shinto successor, developed philological study and trained many disciples. He wrote prayers, created formulas for family worship of tutelary kami and ancestors, and promoted Shinto practice. His spirituality, emperor reverence, and desire to restore ancient Shinto helped inspire support for the 1868 Meiji Restoration.

## Formation of Sect Shinto

During the later 19th century, new religious movements appeared amid social confusion and unrest. Some grew from mountain worship, some stressed purification and ascetic practice, and others combined Confucian and Shinto teachings. Many aimed at healing disease or offering spiritual salvation through personal religious experience.

Important Sect Shinto ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sect_Shinto)) groups included Kurozumi-kyo, founded by Kurozumi Munetada; Konko-kyo, founded by Kawate Bunjiro; and Tenri-kyo, founded by Nakayama Miki. During the Meiji period, 13 sectarian Shinto groups were recognized, including purification sects, mountain-worship sects, and faith-healing sects.

## Shinto Literature and Mythology

Shinto has no scripture equivalent to the Bible ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bible)) or Quran ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quran)), but the Kojiki of 712 and the Nihon-gi or Nihon shoki of 720 are treated as sacred in a broad sense. They compile oral traditions of ancient Shinto while also recording early Japanese history, geography, and literature.

The core myths concern Amaterasu Omikami, the sun goddess and ancestress of the Imperial Household. Other important kami include Izanagi and Izanami, who gave birth to the Japanese islands and many kami; Tsukiyomi no Mikoto, the moon god; and Susanoo no Mikoto, ruler of the nether regions.

According to mythology, Jimmu, a descendant of Amaterasu, became the first emperor of Japan. The Three Sacred Treasures, the mirror, sword, and jewels, were first given by Amaterasu to her grandson and remain revered symbols of the Imperial Household. The Inner Shrine of Ise-jingu is dedicated to Amaterasu and is the most venerated Shinto shrine.

## Doctrines

At Shinto's centre are beliefs in the creating and harmonizing power of musubi and the truthful will of kami, called makoto. Kami cannot be fully explained in words because they exceed ordinary human understanding. Devotees understand kami through faith and usually recognize many kami in polytheistic form.

Each shrine community honours a tutelary kami as a source of life and existence. A kami has a divine personality, responds to truthful prayer, and reveals makoto as a way of living. Truth is not mainly an abstract idea; it appears in concrete life, changing through time and space in the encounter between human beings and kami.

Shinto ethics emphasize makoto no kokoro, or the true heart. This means sincerity, purity, uprightness, and doing one's best in work and relationships. Loyalty, filial piety, love, and faithfulness matter, but the deeper aim is a sincere heart rooted in awareness of the divine.

Purification is another major principle. Harae removes pollution and restores proper relation with kami. Shinto does not focus on original sin in the same way as some religions; impurity is often treated as something to be cleansed so life can return to harmony.

The tradition also stresses the sacred value of the present moment, called naka-ima, or middle present. By living each moment fully, people participate in the eternal development of the world. After World War II, Shinto teaching again emphasized harmony, peace, national development, and world co-prosperity.

## Ritual Practices and Institutions

![[attachments/shinto_002.webp|420]]

*Floating torii gate at Itsukushima Shinto Shrine*

Shinto has no weekly service. People visit shrines when they wish, often on the first and fifteenth days of the month or during festivals. A devotee may also pay respect every morning. Rites of passage and seasonal festivals connect family, community, agriculture, and shrine life.

Major festivals include the Spring Festival for good harvest, the Autumn Harvest Festival, the Annual Festival, and the Divine Procession. In a procession, a portable shrine, or mikoshi, is carried through the parish so the kami's presence moves among the people.

A grand festival usually follows a set order: purification, adoration, opening the inner sanctuary, offerings of food such as rice, sake, fish, vegetables, salt, and water, prayer, sacred music and dance, symbolic offerings, removal of offerings, closing the sanctuary, final adoration, and a feast. Animal meat is avoided because shedding blood in sacred space is taboo.

Prayers, called norito, follow ancient models and reflect the belief that spoken words have spiritual power. The feast, or naorai, once expressed communion with kami through sharing offered food; today communion often occurs through drinking offered sake after the festival.

## Shrine Arts, Music, and Sacred Objects

![[attachments/shinto_003.webp|420]]

*Historical image of Kagura dance in Shinto*

Shinto visual representation differs from Buddhist statuary. Kami images were not used in ancient Shinto until after Buddhism entered Japan, and even where they exist, they are placed deep inside the shrine and are not directly worshiped by the public.

Shrines preserve picture scrolls, votive plaques, calligraphy, sculptures, swords, arms, and other gifts from imperial families, nobles, feudal lords, and worshipers. Many shrine buildings and objects have been designated by the Japanese government as national treasures or important cultural properties.

Traditional shrine music and dance were performed to entertain and appease kami. Gagaku, elegant court music with vocal and instrumental forms, was patronized by the Imperial Household and later used in solemn shrine and temple ritual. Other forms include bugaku dance, kagura for blessing and purification, ta-asobi, and dengaku.

## Key Events & Developments

- 6th century CE: Buddhism entered Japan, and the term Shinto ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shinto)) later helped distinguish native beliefs from Buddhism.
- 712: The Kojiki compiled ancient oral traditions.
- 720: The Nihon shoki recorded mythology, early history, and religious traditions.
- 1603: The Tokugawa shogunate began, encouraging renewed Shinto-Confucian interpretation.
- 1868: The Meiji Restoration drew support from revivalist Shinto ideas and emperor reverence.
- 1956: The Association of Shinto Shrines proclaimed General Principles of Shinto Life stressing harmony, peace, and co-prosperity.
