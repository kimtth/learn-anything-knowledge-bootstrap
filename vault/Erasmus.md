# Erasmus

## Introduction

Erasmus ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Erasmus)) was one of the most influential scholars of the northern Renaissance, a humanist, editor, and critic of religious and intellectual life in early modern Europe. Born in 1469 in Rotterdam and dying in 1536 in Basel, he became famous for his work in classical learning, biblical scholarship, and the reform of education. He helped lay the foundations of historical-critical study by using philological methods to examine ancient texts, especially the Greek New Testament ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Testament)) and the writings of the early Church Fathers. His broad learning and sharp wit made him a central figure in the intellectual life of the age.

## Early life and career

Erasmus was the second illegitimate son of a priest and a physician's daughter. He received an education at the school of St. Lebuin's in Deventer, where one teacher, Jan Synthen, and the headmaster, Alexander Hegius, helped shape his humanist interests. He later entered a monastery, first at Steyn near Gouda, but he never felt at home in monastic life. His early training in classical Latin gave him a strong taste for the humane letters, and he soon began to write in a style that combined learning, irony, and moral seriousness.

In 1499, Erasmus visited England at the invitation of William Blount, Lord Mountjoy. There he met Thomas More, whose friendship became one of the most important of his life. He also encountered John Colet, who inspired him to study Scripture in a more historical and rhetorical way, rather than through the abstract logic of scholastic theology. Colet's example strengthened Erasmus's wish to return to the earlier evidence of Christianity.

## Humanist and educational writings

![[attachments/erasmus_003.webp|420]]

*Erasmus statue in Rotterdam*

Erasmus's educational writings expressed an ambitious vision of learning. In De pueris instituendis, he argued that education could form not only the mind but also moral character. He believed that classical and Christian literature could encourage peace, discipline, and humane behavior, and that the old scholastic curriculum often produced quarrelsome and narrow minds. His famous Praise of Folly ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/In_Praise_of_Folly)), written during a journey across the Alps, showed a different and more ironic side of his thought. It mocked the vanity, pretension, and foolishness of human institutions, including the Church, while also revealing his own deep concern for reform.

## The Protestant challenge

The rise of Martin Luther brought Erasmus into a painful conflict. At first, he admired some aspects of Luther's critique of ecclesiastical abuses, and he even described Luther as a powerful voice of Gospel truth. Yet he also feared that Luther's movement would destroy the careful balance of reform and tradition that he himself wanted to preserve. In his letters and writings, Erasmus tried to distinguish his own position from that of the reformers.

## Final years and legacy

During the 1520s and 1530s, Erasmus continued to write, edit, and polemicize. He lived in Basel for a time, worked on his Greek New Testament and the Paraphrases, and produced editions of the Church Fathers. He also attacked scholars who insisted on excessive linguistic purism, a sign of his preference for clarity, usefulness, and historical judgment over rigid formalism.

## Key Events & Developments

- 1469: Erasmus is born in Rotterdam.
- 1495: He studies theology in Paris but grows dissatisfied with scholastic learning.
- 1499: He visits England, meets Thomas More and John Colet, and deepens his humanist outlook.
- 1516: His Annotationes in Novum Testamentum and other scholarly work give him a major place in biblical studies.
- 1524: He publishes De libero arbitrio, defending free choice against Luther.
- 1525: Martin Luther replies with De servo arbitrio.
- 1529: He moves to Freiburg after the religious conflict intensifies in Basel.

## Interesting Facts

- Erasmus became one of the first major scholars to use philological methods on the Greek New Testament.
- His Praise of Folly ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/In_Praise_of_Folly)) combined serious criticism with satire, making it one of the most memorable works of the Renaissance.
- He believed that education could shape character as much as knowledge.
- His religious position was independent, which made him suspicious to both Protestants and Catholics.
