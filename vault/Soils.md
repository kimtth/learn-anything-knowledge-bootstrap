## Introduction

Soil is the biologically active, porous medium in the upper layer of Earth's crust. It supports life by storing water and nutrients, filtering and breaking down harmful wastes, and participating in carbon, nitrogen, and other element cycles. It develops through weathering shaped by biological, climatic, geologic, and topographic influences.

Since agriculture and forestry began in the 8th millennium BCE, people have needed practical knowledge of soils. In the 18th and 19th centuries, the Industrial Revolution increased pressure on soils to supply raw materials, while quantitative science improved soil management. Soil science grew from the study of plant-growth materials into the study of soils as dynamic biogeochemical systems.

## The Soil Profile

![[attachments/soils_002.png|420]]

*Soil horizons diagram*

Soils vary in thickness and properties because geology, climate, time, deposition, erosion, and landscape history differ. Yet soils share a vertical structure called a soil profile ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soil_horizon)), made of horizons produced by percolating water and living organisms.

The A horizon is an upper weathered layer containing humus, microbial biomass, and mineral particles mixed into aggregates. Above it may lie an O horizon of litter and decomposed organic matter. In heavily leached soils, an E horizon forms below the A horizon as water removes materials.

The B horizon often accumulates clay, humus, carbonates, or metal oxides carried downward from upper layers. It may form blocks, columns, or prisms. The C horizon contains little humus or structure and often consists of parent material. Below it lies the R horizon of consolidated rock.

The A, E, and B sequence is called the solum, the main zone of soil-forming processes and the principal habitat of soil organisms. Soil scientists study small landscape units called pedons, and groups of similar pedons form polypedons that can serve as taxonomic units.

## Soil Behaviour

Soil behaviour depends on physical, chemical, and biological features. Texture, structure, porosity, and permeability affect how soils hold and move air, water, and dissolved materials. Fine-textured soils have more total pore space but lower permeability because their pores are smaller.

Aggregates called peds create structure in natural soils. Cultivation and urban traffic can break surface peds into clods or make the A horizon dense and unstructured. Dense or low-porosity soils increase surface runoff, erosion, flooding, and waterlogging.

Chemically, soils are reactive because mineral and organic particles have charged surfaces. Weathering of minerals such as feldspar releases bases, silica, and oxides of iron and aluminum. Leaching can remove nutrients and increase acidity through hydrogen and aluminum ions, harming organisms and plant growth.

Humus is the dark, decomposed organic matter that remains after organisms transform biomass. It helps control acidity, nutrient cycling, and detoxification of hazardous compounds. Humus can bind or immobilize pesticides, fertilizers, wastes, and degradation products.

Soils are full of soil life. One kilogram of fertile soil may contain hundreds of billions of bacteria, billions of actinomycetes, nearly a billion fungi, and many nematodes, mites, springtails, insects, and earthworms. Plant roots also add huge biomass; a single plant can have hundreds of kilometres of roots in the top metre of soil.

## Soil Formation

Soil formation is controlled by five state factors: parent material, topography, climate, organisms, and time. These factors interact, so a soil's properties reflect both the original material and the continuing effects of water, heat, life, relief, and age.

Parent material is the initial solid matter of a soil. It may be rock, river alluvium, lake or marine sediment, glacial till, loess, volcanic ash, or organic material. Mineral composition, texture, and layering influence horizon development, clay formation, water movement, and whether a soil is monogenetic or polygenetic.

## Topography

Topography affects soil through elevation, aspect, slope form, and position from summit to toeslope. It controls runoff, percolation, drainage, and evapotranspiration. Convex upper slopes often lose water and soil, while toeslopes receive soluble and suspended materials moving downslope.

Related soils shaped by local relief form toposequences. Upper convex slopes tend to have shallower profiles and less distinct subsurface horizons. Lower concave slopes often have more organic matter, clay, soluble compounds, and wetter horizons. Water and gravity can also move whole profile sections downslope in landslides.

## Climate

In pedology, [[Climate and Weather (Climate)|climate]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Climate)) means long-term precipitation, temperature, and storm patterns. Water supplies the solvent for chemical reactions, supports soil organisms, and transports particles through erosion or percolation. Solar energy controls the speed of many water-mediated processes.

Climate patterns create global soil patterns. From pole to equator, polar desert soils may give way to leached Podzols or Spodosols, temperate soils rich in organic matter, arid soils low in humus but enriched in salts, and red or yellow tropical soils rich in resistant iron oxides. Continental transects also show how evapotranspiration and rainfall shape soil properties.

## Organisms

Organisms shape soils through vegetation, soil animals, microbes, and humans. A set of nearby soils influenced by different local plants and animals is called a biosequence. Soils under trees may be more acidic and lower in humus than soils under grass because litter type and decomposition differ.

Human beings are also soil-forming agents. Agriculture, forestry, drainage, irrigation, grazing, and construction can change humus levels, compaction, nutrient supply, erosion rates, and drainage. Human management can improve soil productivity or degrade soil structure and fertility.

## Time

[[Time]] marks the evolution of soil characteristics. A set of soils differing mainly by age is a chronosequence. In young soils, humus in the A horizon may increase for thousands of years, but after about 10,000 years the rate slows, nutrients leach away, and very old soils may lose humus.

Other age indicators include clay and lime moving downward, better-developed E and Bt horizons, hardpans, and redder colours. Agricultural practices can interrupt natural trends by lowering stored humus by 25 percent or more, affecting nutrient retention, acidity, and aeration.

## U.S. Soil Taxonomy

U.S. Soil Taxonomy classifies soils in six hierarchical categories, with soil order as the highest level. Order names end in -sol and reflect parent material or processes tied to the five formation factors and diagnostic horizons.

Parent-material orders such as Andisols, Histosols, and Vertisols occupy less than 5 percent of soil-covered continental land. Young or weakly developed Entisols and Inceptisols cover nearly 33 percent. Soils with salt, acidity, aluminum, or strong weathering limitations, including Aridisols, Spodosols, Oxisols, and Ultisols, make up almost 40 percent. More naturally fertile Alfisols and Mollisols occupy about 15 percent, mainly in strategic middle-latitude belts.

## FAO Soil Groups

The FAO soil classification uses a two-level system: a soil group name and a modifying adjective identifying a soil unit. It was designed to compare national systems rather than replace them. Some FAO groups match U.S. orders closely, such as Andosol, Cambisol, Histosol, and Vertisol, while others correspond to lower taxonomic levels.

FAO groups are based on field and laboratory observations. Some are defined mainly by parent material, some by topography, and most by climate, organisms, and time. This global comparison helps map major soil regions and relate local names to broader patterns.

## Soils in Ecosystems

Soils are central to carbon and nitrogen cycles. Dead biomass is decomposed by microbes. Aerobic organisms produce carbon dioxide, anaerobic organisms produce organic acids or alcohols, and under very anaerobic conditions bacteria produce methane. Plants and photosynthetic microbes then use carbon dioxide to make new biomass.

Nitrogen in dead proteins becomes ammonium, which plants can absorb directly in some systems. Bacteria often convert ammonium to nitrite and then nitrate, the mobile form commonly absorbed by roots and microbes. Rhizobium bacteria in legumes, cyanobacteria, and Azotobacter convert atmospheric nitrogen into biomass nitrogen, closing the cycle.

Soils store very large carbon pools. Soil organic matter contains about three times as much carbon as land vegetation, and arid soils also store inorganic carbon as calcium carbonate. Soil respiration releases carbon dioxide each year, but plant biomass returns carbon to the soil. Agriculture can upset this balance and release extra carbon dioxide.

## Erosion and Pollution

![[attachments/soils_003.webp|420]]

*Historic photograph of soil erosion in the southern United States*

Soil erosion removes surface horizons and can expose lower horizons to weathering. It is driven by water, wind, slope, vegetation loss, land use, and soil structure. Erosion reduces fertility because it removes humus-rich and nutrient-rich surface material.

Soil pollution comes from pesticides, fertilizers, wastes, industrial materials, and toxic compounds. Humus and mineral surfaces can sometimes immobilize or detoxify pollutants, but soils can also transport contaminants through runoff, leaching, and erosion. Soil management therefore affects both food production and environmental health.

## Key Events & Developments

- 8th millennium BCE: Agriculture and forestry made practical soil management essential.
- 18th-19th centuries: Industrial demands and quantitative science pushed soils into systematic study.
- Modern soil science: Soils came to be understood as dynamic biogeochemical systems, not just plant-growth media.
- About 10,000 years: Many soils show slowing humus accumulation and increasing nutrient leaching after this age.

## Key People

- Pedologists: Soil scientists who study soil formation, classification, horizons, and landscape patterns.
- Rhizobium bacteria ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rhizobium_bacteria)): Nitrogen-fixing organisms living in legume roots.
- Nitrosomonas ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nitrosomonas)) and Nitrobacter bacteria: Microbes that convert ammonium to nitrite and nitrate in the nitrogen cycle.

## Interesting Facts

- One kilogram of soil may contain hundreds of billions of bacteria and nearly a billion fungi.
- Humus helps soils hold nutrients and can reduce harm from some pollutants.
- Pedons are the smallest landscape units that can be called soil.
- Alfisols and Mollisols make up only about 15 percent of soil-covered land but include many naturally fertile soils.
- Soil organic matter stores about three times as much carbon as land vegetation.
