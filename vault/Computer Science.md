## Introduction

![[attachments/computer_science_002.png|420]]

*Diagram of Turing’s first machine*

Computer science is the study of computers, including hardware design, software, algorithms, data structures, systems, and the theory of computation. It is both an engineering discipline and a mathematical one, spanning architecture, software, and theory.

It also studies performance, reliability, availability, and experimentation because large systems are often too complex to predict without testing.

Computer science ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_science)) is a way of understanding how computing systems are built and how they behave. It is concerned with both practical machines and the abstract rules that make them work.

## Major areas

![[attachments/computer_science_001.png|420]]

*Von Neumann computer architecture with CPU, memory, input, and output*

Computer science ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_science)) organizes the field around architecture, software, and theory. It covers computer design, operating systems, programming languages, databases, artificial intelligence, graphics, numerical methods, and the analysis of reliability and performance.

Architecture ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Architecture)) deals with how machines are organized, while software turns that hardware into useful systems. The theoretical side asks what can be computed and how efficiently it can be done.

A digital computer normally includes a control unit, arithmetic-logic unit, memory, and input/output devices, with the processor coordinating instructions and data.

Algorithms and data structures are core intellectual tools. Algorithms describe step-by-step procedures for solving problems, while data structures organize information so those procedures can operate efficiently. Their analysis considers correctness, time, memory, and scalability.

This makes abstraction central to the field. A program can be studied as source code, machine instructions, mathematical procedure, data transformation, or interaction with users and hardware. Computer science moves among these levels so that systems can be designed, proved, optimized, debugged, and maintained.

Software systems include compilers, operating systems, databases, networks, graphics, and user interfaces. Each area has its own engineering problems: translating languages, scheduling processes, managing memory, storing records, protecting data, displaying images, and allowing people to interact with machines.

Computer scientists also build CAD tools and formal methods that help design chips, floor plans, and software before physical production or deployment.

## Development

Computer science ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_science)) developed from roots in electrical engineering and mathematics to the rise of digital computers in the mid-20th century and the growth of specialized software and networked systems. The field shifted from isolated calculators to complex, general-purpose information systems.

The development of the field followed the appearance of programmable machines, then compilers, operating systems, databases, and communication networks. Computer science emerged as an independent discipline only about 1960, drawing its roots from electrical engineering and mathematics; the term "artificial intelligence" was coined in 1956, computer graphics appeared in the early 1950s, and the invention of magnetic-disk storage led in the 1960s and '70s to the database and modern database management systems. Each stage widened what computers could do and deepened the need for theory and design.

The theory of computation is another pillar. Questions about computability, automata, formal languages, and complexity show that some problems can be solved efficiently, some only with great cost, and some not at all. This theoretical side links computer science to mathematics and logic.

Artificial intelligence, numerical analysis, simulation, and graphics show the field’s applied side. They use computation to model reasoning, solve scientific problems, represent images, and automate tasks. Computer science therefore moves constantly between abstraction and implementation.

Programming languages are a major field. Languages must balance human readability with machine execution, and their design involves syntax, semantics, typing, control structures, data abstraction, and translation by interpreters or compilers. This evolution runs from the early FORTRAN (Formula Translator) and the business-oriented COBOL, through teaching languages like BASIC — written by John Kemeny and Thomas Kurtz at Dartmouth — to imperative descendants such as Pascal and C, functional languages like LISP favoured for artificial intelligence, declarative logic languages such as Prolog, and concurrent languages such as Ada, the U.S. Department of Defense standard from 1983 to 1997.

Operating systems manage processors, memory, storage, files, devices, and users. They make hardware usable by coordinating many tasks and protecting programs from interfering with one another. Databases similarly organize large bodies of information so that data can be stored, searched, updated, and protected.

Computer networks add another layer. They require protocols, addressing, routing, error control, and security. Modern computer science increasingly studies distributed systems rather than isolated machines.

The field also developed around the problem of reliability. As computers entered business, government, transportation, medicine, and communication, errors became costly. Software engineering, verification, testing, fault tolerance, and secure design emerged to make complex systems dependable.

Real-time systems add a stricter requirement: sensor data, computation, and control output must occur quickly enough to meet deadlines in cars, aircraft, factories, or other controlled processes.

Parallel and distributed computing became important because single processors could not solve every large problem efficiently. Supercomputers, multiprocessors, and networked systems divide work among many components, but this creates new problems of synchronization, communication, and performance.

Human-computer interaction is also part of the field, because useful systems must be understandable and controllable by people. Interfaces, graphics, input devices, and usability connect technical design to human perception, work habits, and communication.

This makes computer science a discipline of both formal systems and practical human use.

## Conclusion

Computer science ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_science)) is the discipline that explains, designs, and improves the machinery and abstractions behind modern computing.

Computer science ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_science)) is not a single specialty. It is a collection of linked problems about speed, correctness, scale, representation, and automation.

## Core Equations

Computer science commonly measures algorithms and information with asymptotic and binary notation:

$$T(n)=O(n\log n)$$

Big-O notation describes how running time grows with input size, here at a rate typical of efficient divide-and-combine algorithms.

$$\text{bits}=\log_2 N$$

This expresses how many binary choices are needed to distinguish N equally possible states.
