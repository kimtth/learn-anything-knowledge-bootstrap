## Introduction

Egypt is a country in the northeastern corner of Africa, but it is much more than a place on a map. Its core is the Nile River valley and delta, where one of the earliest urban and literate civilizations of the ancient Middle East developed beside Mesopotamia.
Pharaonic Egypt stands at the start of a long chain of later powers. Native dynasties ruled for about 3,000 years, then foreign rule came in several waves: [[Alexander the Great]] in 323 BC, the Ptolemaic dynasty, [[Rome]] in 30 BC, the Byzantine Empire, and finally Arab Muslim armies in AD 639-642.

One major theme is continuity and change. Before the Muslim conquest, rural Egyptian agrarian life changed slowly because village life was tied to the yearly rise and fall of the Nile; after the conquest, both cities and villages gradually absorbed Arab culture, and Arabic replaced the older Egyptian language as the everyday spoken language.
Egypt as part of larger worlds. After the Arab conquest it became part of the broader [[Islamic World|Islamic world]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islamic_world)), while still being ruled at different times by Arab, Kurdish, Circassian, Turkish, French, British, military, and elected or semi-elected governments.

Egypt became an intellectual and cultural centre of the Arab and Islamic world, especially after the Mongols destroyed Baghdad and the Abbasid caliphate in the mid-13th century. Under the Mamluk sultans, Cairo became a powerful political and cultural centre, even though Mamluk legitimacy was partly symbolic and military.

The opening also stresses strategic geography. The Suez Canal ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Suez_Canal)), opened in 1869, turned Egypt's location between Africa, Asia, and Europe into an even more important route for trade and empire, and this drew stronger involvement from [[France]] and the [[United Kingdom]].

Modern Egypt is described as a country shaped by the military coup of 1952, socialism, Pan-Arab nationalism, wars with [[Israel]], the peace treaty of 1979, and long authoritarian rule. Popular frustration with corruption, repression, and poverty helped produce the 2011 uprising that removed Hosni Mubarak.

Herodotus famously called Egypt the gift of the Nile. Agriculture remains important, but modern Egypt is largely urban, with [[Cairo]] as one of the world's largest urban areas and with manufacturing, trade, services, and tourism increasingly central to the economy.

## Land

Egypt borders Libya in the west, [[Sudan]] in the south, and [[Israel]] in the northeast. Its northern coast on the Mediterranean Sea is about 620 miles, while its eastern coast on the Red Sea and Gulf of Aqaba is about 1,200 miles.
land is the physical base of Egyptian history. Most of the country is desert, but the Nile valley and delta create the narrow green zone that supports population, farming, cities, and political power.

## Relief

Egyptian relief is dominated by the Nile. For about 750 miles inside Egypt, the river runs north through bare desert in a trenchlike valley, bordered by cliffs until it reaches Cairo and spreads into the delta.

The country is divided into four main physical regions: the Nile valley and delta, the Eastern Desert, the Western Desert, and the Sinai Peninsula. The Nile cuts the desert plateau into the Western Desert between the river and Libya, and the Eastern Desert between the river and the Red Sea region.

Egypt is not entirely flat. Mountains appear along the Red Sea, in the southern Sinai, and in the far southwest near the Uwaynat mountain mass, while most coasts outside the delta are narrow, dry, and thinly populated.

The Nile delta, or Lower Egypt, covers about 9,650 square miles. It runs about 100 miles from Cairo to the Mediterranean and has a coast about 150 miles long from Alexandria to Port Said.

The delta once had as many as seven branches of the Nile ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nile)), but the river now mainly flows through the Damietta and Rosetta branches. Canals, drainage channels, and brackish coastal lagoons such as Maryut, Idku, Burullus, and Manzilah make the flat delta highly managed rather than featureless.

The cultivated Nile valley between Cairo and Aswan varies from a few hundred yards to about 14 miles wide. Since the Aswan High Dam was completed in 1970, the valley has had perennial irrigation, and Lake Nasser replaced the old Nubian valley, forcing about 100,000 Nubians into planned villages in New Nubia.

## The Eastern Desert

The Eastern Desert covers almost one-fourth of Egypt, about 85,690 square miles. Its northern part is a limestone plateau of rolling hills that breaks near Qina into cliffs about 1,600 feet high and deeply cut wadis.

South of Qina, a sandstone plateau is also cut by ravines, but some of these ravines can be used as routes. Farther east, the Red Sea Hills form complex mountain systems with peaks above 6,000 feet, including Mount Shaib al-Banat at 7,175 feet.
The desert's geological variety. Ancient igneous and metamorphic rocks, including granite near Aswan, cross the Nile valley and form the First Cataract, while the Red Sea coast has a narrow plain that widens southward and is bordered offshore by coral reefs.

## The Western Desert

The Western Desert ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_Desert)) covers about two-thirds of Egypt, roughly 262,800 square miles. It is part of the Libyan Desert and slopes from the high plateau of Al-Jilf al-Kabir in the southeast toward depressions and oases farther north.

Important oases include Al-Kharijah, Al-Dakhilah, Al-Farafirah, Al-Bahriyyah, and Siwa. Siwa, near the Libyan border, is the largest and most populous oasis and has been inhabited for thousands of years.
The Qattara Depression is one of the Western Desert's major physical barriers. It is uninhabited, difficult to cross by modern vehicles, and bordered by stony tracts and great ridges of windblown sand.

## Sinai Peninsula

The Sinai Peninsula is a wedge-shaped territory with its base on the Mediterranean and its apex between the gulfs of Suez and Aqaba. It covers about 23,000 square miles.

Southern Sinai is mountainous and sharply serrated, with peaks above 8,000 feet. Mount Catherine, or Jabal Katrina, is Egypt's highest mountain at 8,668 feet.

Central Sinai contains the plateaus of Al-Tih and Al-Ajmah, both deeply cut and sloping north toward Wadi al-Arish. Along the Mediterranean coast, dome-shaped hills and long dune lines lead to Lake Bardawil, a salt lagoon about 60 miles long.

## Drainage

Outside the Nile ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nile)), Egypt has almost no natural permanent surface drainage. A few small streams exist in the southern Sinai mountains, but most valleys in the Eastern Desert and Sinai are normally dry and carry water only after heavy rain.

The wadis of the Eastern Desert mostly drain west to the Nile or east to the Red Sea. In Sinai, drainage moves toward the gulfs of Suez and Aqaba or north toward Wadi al-Arish, while the Western Desert is so dry that it has almost no drainage lines.

The Western Desert still has an important underground water table. Where that water comes near the surface, wells have made life possible in some oases.

## Soils

Egypt's useful [[Soils|soils]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soil)) depend on water and rock type outside the Nile silt zone. Nubian sandstone covers about one-third of the country, Eocene limestone covers about one-fifth, and ancient igneous and metamorphic rocks form important areas in Sinai, the Red Sea region, and the southwest.

The fertile cultivated soil of the Nile valley and delta comes from silt carried from the Ethiopian Highlands by the Blue Nile and Atbarah rivers. Deposits are more than 30 feet deep in the northern delta and about 22 feet deep near Aswan.

Egyptian silt is not uniformly easy to farm. High clay content makes some soil difficult to work, sodium carbonate can create infertile black-alkali soils, and salinization has produced barren areas in the northern delta.

## Climate

*All Gizah Pyramids*

Egypt lies in the North African desert belt, so its climate has low rainfall, strong seasonal and daily temperature ranges, and sunshine almost all year. Winter runs mainly from November to March, summer from May to September, and short transition periods separate them.

The khamsin dust storms occur most often from March to June. They bring tropical air from the south, sharply raise temperature, lower humidity, thicken the air with dust, and may produce gale-force winds.

Temperatures vary by place and season. Alexandria has January temperatures roughly from 48 to 65 F, Aswan from 48 to 74 F, while June midday highs range from about 91 F in Cairo to 106 F in Aswan.

Rainfall is mostly a winter event and is low and uneven. Alexandria averages about 7 inches a year, Cairo about 1 inch, Aswan almost none, and the Red Sea coast and Western Desert are nearly rainless, while northern Sinai receives somewhat more.

Plant life is varied despite the dry climate. The Western Desert ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_Desert)) is often bare, but places with water support grasses and perennials; the Eastern Desert and Sinai wadis support tamarisk, acacia, succulents, aromatic herbs, and other sparse desert vegetation.

The Nile ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nile)), canals, and ditches support water plants, reeds, grasses, and palms. Date palms occur across the delta, valley, and oases, while papyrus, important in ancient Egypt, now survives mainly in botanical gardens.

Egyptian animal life includes domestic buffalo, camels, donkeys, sheep, and goats. Ancient animals such as hippopotamuses, giraffes, and ostriches no longer live in Egypt, and crocodiles are found only south of the Aswan High Dam.

Wildlife includes the aoudad, Dorcas gazelle, fennec, Nubian ibex, Egyptian hare, jerboa, jackal, hyrax, mongoose, lizards, vipers, cobras, scorpions, rodents, insects, and many birds. Egypt has more than 200 migrating bird types and more than 150 resident birds, while the Nile contains about 190 fish varieties.

## People
The population of the Nile valley and delta is broadly homogeneous, formed from indigenous African ancestry mixed with Arab ancestry. Urban populations, especially in northern delta towns, show more visible traces of older foreign communities such as Greeks, Romans, Turks, Circassians, and Europeans.

The Saidi, or Upper Egyptians, live roughly from Cairo to Aswan and are culturally more conservative than Lower Egyptians, though ethnically similar. Nubians in the far south differ more clearly in culture, kinship, and physical ancestry, and their social structure includes clans and broader segments.

The desert populations include nomadic, seminomadic, and settled groups. In the Sinai and northern Eastern Desert many groups are recent Arabian immigrants with tribal organization, while the southern Eastern Desert is associated with the Beja, especially the Ababdah and Bisharin.

The Western Desert ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_Desert)) population outside the oases is of mixed Arab and Amazigh descent. The Saadi, including the Awlad Ali, claim descent from Banu Hilal and Banu Sulaym, while the Murabitin have client status and may descend from older Amazigh inhabitants.

Foreign communities once had major influence in Egypt. In the 1920s, foreigners numbered more than 200,000, especially Greeks, Italians, British, and French, but their numbers fell sharply after independence.

Settlement patterns differ by region. Delta people have had more contact with the outside world and are generally less traditional, while Saidis and Bedouin groups place strong value on family honour, tribal identity, and sometimes vendetta as an illegal but persistent way to settle disputes.

Rural villages in the Nile valley and delta are compact settlements surrounded by intensively cultivated fields. Houses are often made of mud brick or, in wealthier villages, fired brick and concrete, and village features commonly include a mosque or church, a school, shops, and government or service buildings.

Urban settlements have grown into fields and often show weak planning. [[Cairo]] is dense and historically layered, while Alexandria, Port Said, Ismailia, and Suez have more modern features tied to the coast and the Suez Canal.

Most Egyptians live near the Nile ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nile)), where density is extremely high. The population is young, more than two-fifths live in urban areas, family planning lowered the birth rate after the 1960s, and health improvements raised life expectancy to about 70 years.

## Languages

Arabic ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arabic)) is Egypt's official language, and most Egyptians speak regional vernaculars of Arabic. The spoken language differs strongly from Modern Standard Arabic, which is learned in school and used by educated people across the Arab world.

Egyptian Arabic contains many regional forms. The Bedouin of Sinai and the Eastern Desert have one dialect group, the Bedouin of the Western Desert another, Upper Egypt has a separate vernacular, and the Cairo dialect is widely understood because of Cairo's film industry.
A multilingual tradition among educated Egyptians. Many educated Egyptians know English or French as well as Arabic, partly because of long foreign contact and foreign-language schools.

Minority languages include To Bedawi among the Beja, a Berber-related language at Siwa Oasis, Eastern Sudanic languages among Nubians, and smaller Greek, Italian, and Armenian communities. Coptic, once used in daily and religious life, had by the 12th century survived mainly as the liturgical language of the Coptic Orthodox Church.

## Religion

[[Muhammad and the Religion of Islam (Islam)|Islam]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islam)) is the official religion of Egypt, and most Muslims are Sunni. Cairo's al-Azhar University is presented as one of the world's leading centres of Islamic learning, and al-Azhar's sheikhs are widely respected in the Sunni world.

The Muslim Brotherhood, founded in Egypt in 1928, is described as a transnational religio-political organization that promotes conservative Muslim values. Sufism is also widely practiced.

The largest Christian community is the Copts. They are generally similar to Muslim Egyptians in language, dress, and daily life, but their church traditions go back before the Arab conquest and the Coptic Orthodox Church has remained autonomous since its break with the Eastern Church in the 5th century.

Copts have long been associated with handicrafts, accountancy, banking, commerce, and civil service, and there are wholly Coptic villages as well as mixed Coptic-Muslim villages. They are especially numerous in the middle Nile valley governorates of Asyut, Al-Minya, and Qina, and about one-fourth live in Cairo.

Other religious communities include Coptic Catholic, Greek Orthodox, Greek Catholic, Armenian Orthodox and Catholic, Maronite, Syrian Catholic, Anglican, and Protestant groups. The Jewish community is now very small.

## Economy

Egypt's economy ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economy)) moved from free-market conditions before 1952 to heavy state control after the revolution. Major nationalization from 1961 to the early 1970s affected industry, communications, banking, finance, cotton, foreign trade, and other sectors.

The state controlled production, distribution, prices, rent, investment, foreign exchange, foreign travel, and corporate leadership. Even after some restrictions were relaxed under infitah, or the opening policy of the early 1970s, public boards and state ministries continued to guide many enterprises.

Foreign investment became important after the early 1970s. Arab aid was suspended after Egypt's 1979 peace treaty with Israel, but Western and Japanese investment increased as relations with Israel normalized.

The [[The Forms Of Government Their Historical Development (Government)|government]] reduced defence spending after the treaty and directed more resources to roads, bridges, pipelines, telephones, and other infrastructure. New oil and gas discoveries and Western aid helped make the economy more resilient, though living standards stayed low and population growth strained limited resources.

Political uncertainty after the 2011 uprising hurt much of the economy. Tourism, construction, and manufacturing suffered especially, showing how closely economic performance was tied to security and political stability.

Agriculture remains important because only about 3 percent of Egypt's land is arable, yet that land is highly fertile and often cropped more than once a year. Agriculture contributes about one-seventh of GDP, employs about one-fourth of the labour force, and earns foreign exchange through exports.

Egyptian farming is highly commercial and intensively managed. Canals, drains, dams, pumps, barrages, fertilizers, pesticides, crop rotation, and government controls all help produce high yields from very limited land.

Field crops provide about three-fourths of agricultural value. Cotton is the main summer crop and Egypt is the main producer of long-staple cotton, while other important crops include maize, rice, wheat, sorghum, fava beans, sugarcane, tomatoes, sugar beets, potatoes, onions, citrus, and other fruits.

The Aswan High Dam transformed agriculture by controlling floods and allowing perennial irrigation. By 1975, more than 1,000,000 acres had been reclaimed and about 700,000 acres converted from basin irrigation, though urban and industrial growth consumed almost as much farmland.

Land reform is described as one of Egypt's major successes. Ownership limits fell from 200 acres in 1952 to 100 acres in 1961 and 50 acres in 1969, and by 1975 owners with 50 acres or more held less than one-eighth of cultivated land.

Egypt's biological resources centre on the Nile ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nile)). Livestock includes water buffalo, cattle, asses, goats, and sheep, while fisheries expanded after the High Dam, especially through projects at Lake Nasser, even as sea catches near the delta declined because Nile water flow changed.

Mineral resources are limited compared with Egypt's size and population. Petroleum began on a small scale in 1908, expanded significantly in the mid-1970s in the Gulf of Suez and Western Desert, and was joined by important natural gas discoveries in the delta, Western Desert, Mediterranean shelf, and Gulf of Suez.

Energy development includes refineries, oil pipelines, and hydroelectric power. The Sumed pipeline, opened in 1977, links the Gulf of Suez with the Mediterranean, while the Aswan High Dam power station and Aswan II added capacity, though thermal plants fueled by natural gas now produce most electricity.

Other resources include phosphate, coal, manganese, iron ore, chromium, uranium, and gold. These resources support industry, but Egypt's main natural wealth remains its narrow strip of cultivated land.

## Manufacturing

Manufacturing ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manufacturing)) became one of Egypt's largest economic sectors during the 20th century, and by the 21st century manufacturing and mining together produced roughly one-fourth of GDP. Before about 1930, free trade policies weakened domestic industry by favouring imported goods.

A customs tariff in 1930 encouraged Egyptian manufacturing, and the Bank of Egypt supported entrepreneurs in printing, cotton ginning, transport, textiles, vegetable oil, pharmaceuticals, and rayon. World War II cut Egypt off from many European imports and further stimulated textile and other production.

After the 1950s, large-scale manufacturing was nationalized, and heavy industry received strong support, especially after trade and aid agreements with the Soviet Union. Projects included the Hulwan iron and steel complex, an aluminum complex powered by the High Dam, ammonium nitrate production, and fertilizer plants.

By the early 21st century, many large enterprises were still state-owned or state-operated, although privatization had begun. Major industries included textiles, food processing, chemicals, petroleum refining, cement, metals, engineering, and consumer goods.

## Finance

Modern banking in Egypt began in the mid-19th century with institutions such as the Bank of Egypt, the Anglo-Egyptian Bank, Credit Lyonnais, the Ottoman Bank, the National Bank of Egypt, and the Agricultural Bank of Egypt. The first purely Egyptian bank was Banque Misr in 1920.

The National Bank of Egypt performed central-bank functions before the Central Bank of Egypt was formally separated from it in 1961. Banks and insurance companies were nationalized in the late 1950s and early 1960s, concentrating finance into state institutions.

The banking system was reorganized again in the early 1970s. Some major banks were merged, specialized functions were assigned, two new banks were created, and foreign banks were allowed back as part of economic liberalization.

## Trade

Egypt's trade ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trade)) has long shown a large deficit: imports usually equal about one-third of GDP, while exports equal about one-tenth. Since World War II, imports have usually exceeded exports, and the deficit became especially large in the 1960s and again in the early and mid-1980s.

The trade deficit was partly offset by Western aid and remittances from Egyptians working abroad. Imports include raw materials, mineral and chemical products, capital goods, foodstuffs, and consumer goods, while exports include petroleum products, raw cotton, cotton yarn, fabrics, rice, onions, garlic, and citrus.

Important trading partners include the [[United States of America (United States)|United States]], [[Italy]], [[Germany]], and [[France]]. Trade is dependent on both Egypt's own production and its external political relationships.

## Services

The service sector ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tertiary_sector)), including retail, tourism, and government services, is one of the largest parts of the economy. The government itself is a major employer, and public contracts continue to support other parts of the economy.

Tourism brings foreign exchange and draws visitors with warm winters, beaches, casinos, ancient monuments, and cultural sites. Visitor numbers rose in the 1990s and early 21st century, but security shocks such as the 1997 Luxor massacre and the 2011 uprising caused major drops.

Labour is divided among agriculture, manufacturing and mining, services, trade, finance, and transport. Underemployment appeared early in agriculture because land was scarce, and later nonagricultural jobs did not grow fast enough for the expanding labour force.

The lowest living standards belong especially to landless agricultural workers, while better wages appear in petroleum, manufacturing, and industries with social insurance or health and housing benefits. Inflation since the late 1970s weakened the older advantage of low living costs.

Before 2011, trade unions were closely controlled by the government through official federations. After Mubarak's removal, hundreds of independent unions appeared, often linked to the Egyptian Federation of Independent Trade Unions or the Egyptian Democratic Labour Congress.

The tax system depends heavily on a small wealthy minority because most people earn low incomes. Direct income taxes, sales taxes, customs duties, and Suez Canal fees all support revenue, but the income gap between rich and poor has widened since the 1960s.

Transport follows the country's geography. Roads, railways, canals, ports, air routes, and telecommunications mostly follow the Nile, the delta, the Mediterranean coast, and the Suez Canal, with Cairo acting as a central hub.

The Suez Canal ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Suez_Canal)), closed after the 1967 war and reopened in 1975, is a major route between the Mediterranean and Red seas. The Nile and canals also carry heavy freight, while ports such as Alexandria, Port Said, and Suez handle trade and passenger traffic.

[[Telecommunications Systems (Telecommunication)|Telecommunications]] developed early, beginning with telegraph and telephone systems in the 19th century. Later Egypt expanded landlines, cellular service, Nilesat satellite broadcasting, Internet access through cafes, and privately available satellite television.

## Justice

The 2014 constitution emphasizes judicial independence. Judges are subject only to the law, cannot be dismissed except as provided by law, and are appointed by the state with approval from the Supreme Judicial Council.

The court system includes district tribunals, tribunals of first instance, courts of appeal, and the Court of Cassation, which is the highest appeal court. Sessions are public unless public order or decency requires otherwise, and sentences are given in open court.

Special courts include military courts and public security courts, while the Council of State handles administrative disputes and disciplinary actions. The Supreme Constitutional Court in Cairo reviews constitutionality and resolves judicial conflicts.

Egypt abolished the Shariah court system in 1956, becoming the first Arab country to do so. Personal-status matters such as marriage, divorce, and inheritance are handled by civil courts, though French-based civil and penal codes remain influenced by Shariah.

## Security

Egypt maintains one of the strongest military forces in the region. About three-fourths of military strength is in the army, with the rest divided among the air force, air defence command, and navy.
Egypt's military industrial complex. Government-linked firms make light armoured vehicles and missiles and assemble some heavy armoured vehicles under foreign contracts.

The officer corps has long played a political role. As part of the peace process with [[Israel]], the [[United States of America (United States)|United States]] has provided large amounts of military aid.

Paramilitary and intelligence bodies are important for internal security. The Central Security Forces report to the Ministry of the Interior, while the National Guard, Border Guard Forces, Coast Guard, and intelligence services also support state control.

Conscription begins for males at age 18, followed by reserve obligations. Poor pay and living conditions, especially in the Central Security Forces, helped produce a short rebellion in the mid-1980s that caused several hundred deaths.

## Housing

Egypt has faced a serious urban housing shortage since World War II, made worse by migration from villages to cities. Public and private construction has struggled to keep up with demand.

Large amounts of private investment went into housing in the mid-1980s, and the late 1990s brought major spending on slum improvement and new cities. Even so, demand stayed higher than supply, and many units remained empty because of high prices or legal and bureaucratic obstacles.

In rural areas, villagers often build low-cost homes with local materials. The government has supported self-help projects with loans and has built planned villages with utilities on newly reclaimed land.

## Education

At the end of the 19th century, Egypt had only three state secondary schools and nine higher schools, while primary and higher learning still depended strongly on Quran schools and Islamic colleges. A 1923 law promised free compulsory education for ages 7 to 12, though it was not fully enforced until the early 1950s.

Education expanded sharply after World War II and especially after the 1952 revolution. Women's education grew strongly, more women entered universities, and new careers opened to women in professions, public life, and politics.

The expansion of [[Education|education]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Education)) created an intellectual elite and a larger middle class of professionals, officials, and businesspeople. Literacy rose gradually, with about two-thirds of men literate and about half of women, while female literacy continued to increase quickly.

State general education has three stages: six years of primary school, three years of preparatory school, and three years of secondary school. Primary education is compulsory from ages 6 to 12, and secondary schools are either general or technical.

The al-Azhar system operates beside the Ministry of Education's system. It gives instruction equivalent to state levels but adds time for Islamic subjects, and since 1961 its university-level curriculum has been modernized to compare with other state universities.

Higher education includes state universities such as Cairo University, Alexandria, Ayn Shams, and Asyut, plus later state universities, private universities, and specialized institutes. The American University in Cairo, founded in 1919, is the oldest private university mentioned.

## History

The history section covers Egypt from the Islamic conquest in the 7th century to 's present. It frames this history as a long process of Islamization, Arabization, imperial ambition, foreign occupation, nationalism, military rule, authoritarian stability, uprising, and uncertain democratic transition.

From AD 639-642, an Arab force led by Amr ibn al-As conquered Byzantine Egypt. The conquest succeeded quickly because Byzantine authority was divided and weak, Coptic support for Byzantine rule was limited, and the Arab armies had already defeated Byzantine and Persian power in nearby regions.

Early Arab rule changed little at first for ordinary Egyptians. Treaties protected the native population and churches in exchange for tribute, Copts continued to administer taxation, and the Arabs avoided settling in older cities such as Alexandria by founding Al-Fustat as a garrison town.

Al-Fustat became a centre of Arabization because it had an Arab majority and required local people to use Arabic. Yet the change was slow: Arabic replaced Greek as an official language only in 706, while Coptic remained spoken and later survived mainly in church ritual.

For more than two centuries, Egypt was governed by caliphal governors under the Umayyad and Abbasid caliphates. Egypt supplied taxes and grain, served as a base for naval and land expansion, and supported campaigns against Rhodes, Cyprus, Sicily, Nubia, and North Africa.

Tax pressure, tribal politics, Coptic unrest, Kharijite rebellions, and rapid governor turnover weakened caliphal rule. Repression after a Coptic and tribal rebellion in 829-830 helped accelerate conversion to Islam.

Autonomy returned under Ahmad ibn Tulun, who governed from 868 and built an independent army, took control of finances, conquered Syria and Palestine, and kept Egyptian revenues inside Egypt. His mosque in Cairo is treated as a lasting sign of restored Egyptian power.

The Tulunid dynasty collapsed after Khumarawayh's extravagance and murder, and Abbasid control briefly returned in 905. The Ikhshidid dynasty later restored stability under Muhammad ibn Tughj, but after Kafur's death in 968, disorder opened the way for Fatimid conquest.

The Fatimid dynasty founded Cairo as a palace city and made Egypt a rival centre to the Abbasid caliphate. At its height, Fatimid authority extended over coastal North Africa, Sicily, the Hejaz, Yemen, and southern Syria and Palestine, though firm control was strongest in Egypt.

## Islamization

Fatimid Islamization was complex because the ruling elite promoted Ismaili Shiism while most Egyptian Muslims remained Sunni. The official doctrine claimed that the Fatimid caliph was the only legitimate leader, but the Ismaili minority could not fully impose its will.

Non-Muslims were usually treated with tolerance under the Fatimids, with Copts and Jews serving in the bureaucracy. The exception was the reign of al-Hakim, who ordered destruction of churches and pressured non-Muslims toward conversion or exile.

Saladin restored Sunni rule in 1171 with little public outcry, suggesting that Ismaili Shiism had not deeply reshaped the wider population. Cairo nevertheless became important for Ismaili teaching because Fatimid propaganda encouraged learning and institutional development.

## Arabization

The Arabization of Egypt continued slowly. Fatimid reliance on Amazigh troops was balanced by Turkish, Sudanese, and Arab contingents, and Arab tribes were settled in Upper Egypt to weaken rival Ismaili support.

Fatimid trade transformed Egypt's place in the Mediterranean and Red Sea world. As power declined in Iraq, Mesopotamia, and Syria, Egypt's strong government, Red Sea control, navy, low tariffs, and tolerance of merchants helped make Al-Fustat a great international entrepot.

Fatimid power weakened when military uprisings and court rivalries made caliphs dependent on strong generals and outside help. In 1169, a Syrian army entered Egypt, and one commander, Saladin, became vizier before abolishing the Fatimid caliphate and founding the Ayyubid dynasty.

Under Saladin, Egypt returned to the Sunni world and became a champion against the Crusaders. He built Cairo's citadel, expanded walls, remitted un-Islamic taxes, treated local Christians and Jews with tolerance, and used Egypt as the base for a wider empire including Syria, Yemen, the Hejaz, and much of the Holy Land.

The Ayyubid dynasty was weakened by family fiefdoms that encouraged provincial power struggles. Al-Kamil reunited Syria and Egypt for a time and made a truce with Frederick II in 1229, trading Jerusalem and other territory for political support.

The rise of mamluk armies changed Egyptian history. After al-Salih Ayyub expanded the purchase of Turkish military slaves, mamluk soldiers became powerful enough to defeat the Crusade of Louis IX, assassinate Turan-Shah, raise Shajar al-Durr to power, and create a new military slave regime in 1250.

The Mamluks made Egypt the political, economic, and cultural centre of the eastern Arabic-speaking Muslim world. They restored a symbolic Abbasid caliphate in Cairo in 1261 and legitimized themselves through victories over Mongols and Crusaders.

Mamluk politics were brutal and unstable, with 45 sultans in 264 years and military force as the real basis of legitimacy. Yet rulers such as Baybars I, al-Malik al-Nasir, and Qait Bay created periods of stability, conquest, trade, and cultural patronage.

Mamluk decline came from repeated plague after 1348, Timur's victory in Syria, loss of Indian Ocean trade to the Portuguese, Bedouin raids, and factional military weakness. Still, Mamluk Cairo produced major architecture, chronicles, biographical dictionaries, encyclopaedias, and religious institutions.

Religious life under the Mamluks grew harder for Christians. Copts and Jews had served in government, but popular resentment, anti-Christian writing, suspicion linked to Mongol-Christian contacts, church closures, and repeated dismissal of Copts from offices helped speed conversion to Islam.

Economically, the Mamluk period was the high point of medieval Egyptian commerce. Trade linked Egypt with Mediterranean ports, the Black Sea, and India, but plague, state interference, Bedouin raids, and Portuguese competition reduced this importance by the 15th century.

## The Ottomans (15171798)

The Ottomans defeated the Mamluks in 1516-1517, making Egypt a province ruled from Constantinople. Egypt again became a source of taxes and a base for foreign expansion, while economic and cultural decline continued from the late Mamluk period.

Ottoman rule was shaped by surviving Mamluk power. The Ottomans installed governors and armies, but Mamluk elites entered the bureaucracy and military, joined the advisory council, and gradually reasserted practical dominance.

The kanun-name of 1525 organized Egypt under an Ottoman viceroy, advisory divan, army corps, inspectors, and provinces. Over time, Mamluk beys became a powerful local elite, sustained by the old system of buying, training, freeing, and attaching military slaves to great houses.

By the early 18th century, the Mamluks were again the strongest force in Egyptian politics. Ali Bey and Abu Dhahab won de facto autonomy, conducted campaigns in Syria and the Hejaz, and helped show how weak Ottoman control had become.

## Expansion

During the 16th century, the Ottomans used Egypt as a base for expansion. They extended authority into Nubia as far as the Third Cataract and tried to restore control over the Red Sea.

Ottoman fleets and troops captured Yemen and Aden between 1536 and 1546, then established a colony at Mitsiwa on the Abyssinian coast in 1557. These outposts lost importance in the 17th century as Ottoman and Portuguese power declined and the Dutch took over much spice trade.

## Culture

Ottoman Egyptian culture lacked the vitality of Mamluk culture because of political instability and economic decline. One sign was the dramatic fall in historical writing; the main historian of note was Abd al-Rahman al-Jabarti in the late 18th and early 19th centuries.

Ottoman architecture also did not match Mamluk achievements. Public buildings were still constructed, but even the best often imitated the Byzantine basilica model adopted for mosques.

Religiously, the Ottomans continued to employ Copts in financial offices. That the story of the last Abbasid caliph transferring authority to the Ottoman sultan is generally considered an 18th-century invention.

The French occupation from 1798 to 1801 began a new era of Western impact. Napoleon I invaded Egypt to harm British trade, threaten India, and gain bargaining power, while also claiming that French rule would regenerate Egypt.

Napoleon defeated Mamluk forces at Shubrakhit and the Battle of the Pyramids, entered Cairo, and tried to win religious leaders through councils and public claims of sympathy for Islam. But Nelson destroyed the French fleet at Abu Qir in the Battle of the Nile, cutting French communications.

The French occupation produced revolt, Ottoman and British counterattack, and eventual French evacuation in 1801. Its deeper importance was that it exposed Egypt to European power and exposed Europe to Egypt through scholars, the Rosetta Stone, and the Description de l'Egypte.

After the French left, Ottoman, British, Mamluk, and Albanian forces struggled for control. In 1805, Cairo's ulama invested Muhammad Ali as viceroy, and the Ottoman sultan confirmed him.

Muhammad Ali built power by weakening the Mamluks and ulama, ending tax farming, directly collecting taxes, surveying land, monopolizing crops and trade, and massacring many Mamluk leaders at the citadel in 1811. He became effectively the sole landholder and built a centralized state.

His military expansion included campaigns in Arabia against the Wahhabis, conquest in northern Sudan, intervention in Greece and Crete, and invasion of Syria. His army defeated Ottoman forces, but European intervention in 1840 forced his son Ibrahim to evacuate Syria.

Muhammad Ali's reforms included a conscript army of Egyptian peasants trained by European instructors, new administrative councils and departments, centralized provincial rule, technical schools, missions to Europe, a printing press in 1822, and the translation work of Rifaah Rafi al-Tahtawi.

## Abbas I and Said, 1848-63

The reign of Abbas I showed how fragile westernization was after Muhammad Ali. Abbas was traditionalist, closed the school of languages and translation bureau, exiled al-Tahtawi to Sudan, and turned from French influence toward British support.

The British gained from Abbas I a concession to build a railway from Alexandria to Cairo, completed between 1851 and 1856 and later extended to Suez. This strengthened British communications with India.

Said, who ruled from 1854 to 1863, reversed policy again and was more sympathetic to Westernizers. His most important act was granting Ferdinand de Lesseps a concession in 1854 to cut a canal through the Suez isthmus, creating conflict with both the Ottoman sultan and the British.

## Ismail, 186379

Ismail, son of Ibrahim Pasha, continued modernization with energy but also with financial recklessness. The American Civil War raised demand and prices for Egyptian long-staple cotton, but the postwar price collapse damaged the economy and worsened debt.

Ismail supported the Suez Canal ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Suez_Canal)) but modified earlier terms by rejecting a large land cession and refusing unlimited unpaid peasant labour. After arbitration and Ottoman approval, the canal opened in 1869, symbolizing Egypt's western orientation and strategic importance.

Ismail expanded autonomy within the Ottoman system. He secured succession by primogeniture in 1866, gained the title khedive in 1867, and had his privileges confirmed in 1873, but these concessions cost money and deepened debt.

In Africa, Ismail extended Egyptian authority in Sudan, Darfur, the Red Sea, the Somali coast, and Harer, using figures such as Samuel White Baker and Charles George Gordon. Attempts to invade Abyssinia in 1875 and 1876 failed and marked the limits of expansion.

Financial crisis brought European control. The sale of Suez Canal shares to Britain in 1875 did not prevent bankruptcy, and the Caisse de la Dette Publique and Dual Control placed Egyptian revenue under European supervision.

Opposition to European control grew among the Assembly of Delegates, Egyptian army officers, and nationalist circles. Ahmad Urabi and allied officers formed part of this resistance, but Britain intervened, bombarded Alexandria, defeated the Urabists at Tall al-Kabir, and occupied Cairo in 1882.

The British occupation marked the culmination of European influence after 1798. Britain kept the facade of khedivial government but ruled through advisers and the British agent, especially Lord Cromer, while defending strategic interests around the Suez Canal.

## Abbas Hilmi II, 18921914

The accession of Abbas Hilmi II in 1892 began a new phase of opposition to British occupation. The young khedive challenged Cromer's control, dismissed Mustafa Fahmi, and criticized British officers, but Cromer forced him to retreat.

Nationalism grew through Mustafa Kamil, who used speeches, writings, and his newspaper al-Liwa to build patriotism around opposition to British rule. The reconquest of Sudan, the 1899 Anglo-Egyptian Condominium, and the Fashoda Incident shaped nationalist feeling and British-French diplomacy.

The Dinshaway Incident of 1906 intensified anti-British feeling after harsh sentences were imposed on villagers following a clash with British officers. Cromer retired in 1907, and later officials tried, with limited success, to conciliate Egyptians and reform political institutions.

World War I changed Egypt's status. Britain declared a protectorate in 1914, deposed Abbas, appointed Husayn Kamil as sultan, imposed martial law, and suspended the Legislative Assembly.

After the armistice of 1918, Sad Zaghlul and other politicians demanded autonomy and formed the Wafd delegation. British refusal and Zaghlul's arrest provoked the 1919 revolt, leading Britain to send Lord Allenby and later to declare Egyptian independence in 1922 while reserving control over defence, imperial communications, foreign interests, minorities, and Sudan.

## The Kingdom of Egypt (192252)

*Egypt, Alexandria*

The Kingdom of Egypt began as a constitutional monarchy. The 1923 constitution gave the king executive power, created a bicameral legislature, and established universal male suffrage with indirect election of deputies.

In practice, independence and constitutional rule were limited by a struggle among the king, the Wafd, and the British. King Fuad used both nationalists and British support to defend royal power, while the Wafd claimed to stand for independence and constitutional government but often compromised to gain office.

British policy focused on protecting imperial communications through the Suez Canal ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Suez_Canal)). Elections repeatedly gave the Wafd majorities, but parliaments were dissolved, governments changed, and treaty negotiations broke down over British interests and Sudan.

The 1936 Anglo-Egyptian Treaty created a mutual defence alliance and helped Egypt enter the League of Nations. Yet royal conflict, party splits, youth movements, and British intervention kept politics unstable.

[[World Wars (World War II)|World War II]] deepened instability. Britain pressured King Farouk over government appointments, the 1942 intervention damaged both royal and Wafd legitimacy, and postwar politics became more radical.

The Muslim Brotherhood, founded in 1928, became a militant mass organization, while nationalist pressure demanded British evacuation and control over Sudan. Egypt also became more involved in Arab affairs and helped form the Arab League in 1943-44.

The defeat in the first Arab-Israeli war of 1948-1949 intensified disillusion. Violence by and against the Muslim Brotherhood, failed negotiations with Britain, guerrilla warfare in the canal zone, British reprisals at Ismailia, and the burning of Cairo in January 1952 left the monarchy deeply weakened.

## The revolution and the Republic

The 1952 revolution began when the Free Officers led by Col. Gamal Abdel Nasser overthrew the monarchy on July 23. The coup was driven by strong but vague Egyptian nationalism and later became a revolution from above.

Muhammad Naguib served first as the older figurehead and president after Egypt became a republic in 1953. In 1954 he lost a power struggle to Nasser, who drew on the military, police, and controlled labour support, while parties, the middle class, and the Muslim Brotherhood rallied to Naguib.

Nasser's victory created a regime based on military and security power plus carefully managed civilian mobilization. Political parties had already been abolished, and Naguib was placed under house arrest.

## The Nasser regime

Gamal Abdel Nasser first pursued moderation on Sudan, British forces, and Israel. Sudan became independent in 1956, and the 1954 Anglo-Egyptian Agreement provided for gradual British evacuation from the canal zone.

An attempted assassination by a Muslim Brotherhood member in 1954 allowed Nasser to crush the organization. Executions, imprisonment, and prison radicalization later influenced figures such as Sayyid Qutb and helped shape modern Islamist militancy.

Conflict with [[Israel]] escalated through Gaza raids, Israeli retaliation, and Egypt's failed attempt to obtain Western arms. In 1955, Nasser turned to a Czechoslovak arms agreement backed by the Soviet Union after refusing to join the Baghdad Pact.

When the United States and Britain withdrew funding for the Aswan High Dam, Nasser nationalized the Suez Canal Company in July 1956. Britain, France, and Israel attacked Egypt, but U.S. and Soviet pressure ended the Suez Crisis and left Nasser in control of the canal.

After 1956, Nasser became more radical. He created the National Union, later the Arab Socialist Union, promoted scientific socialism, nationalized major industries and banks, and doubled manufacturing's share of national output by 1970, though agriculture and population growth weakened gains.

Nasser also pursued Pan-Arab leadership. Egypt and Syria formed the United Arab Republic in 1958, but Syrian resentment of Egyptian dominance led to its collapse in 1961, after which Nasser moved further left and intervened in Yemen's civil war.

The 1967 Six-Day War was a disaster. After Nasser ordered the UN force out of Sinai and closed the Gulf of Aqaba to Israeli shipping, Israel launched a preemptive strike, destroyed much of Egypt's air force, occupied Sinai, and left about 10,000 Egyptians dead.

Nasser offered to resign after the defeat, but public support kept him in office. Egypt then rearmed, fought the War of Attrition along the Suez Canal, and Nasser began a turn to the right that his successor would expand.

## The Sadat regime

Anwar el-Sadat succeeded Nasser after his death on September 28, 1970. First seen as temporary, Sadat quickly outmaneuvered rivals in the Corrective Revolution of 1971 and prepared a new strategy toward Israel.

The 1973 October, or Yom Kippur, War was launched with Syria to change diplomatic conditions rather than destroy Israel. Egypt's early crossing of the canal let Sadat claim honour and seek negotiations, even though Israel later counterattacked west of the canal.

Sadat signed the Sinai I and Sinai II disengagement agreements in 1974 and 1975. His dramatic trip to Jerusalem in 1977 led to the Camp David Accords of 1978 with Menachem Begin and Jimmy Carter, and to the Egyptian-Israeli peace treaty of March 26, 1979.

Peace brought the return of Sinai, U.S. aid, and normalization with [[Israel]], but it also cost Egypt Arab financial support and membership in the Arab League. Many Arabs believed the Palestinian issue had not been solved.

Domestically, Sadat linked peace to the infitah open-door economic policy and a controlled return to party politics. But economic hardship produced deadly demonstrations in January 1977, and widening inequality weakened his legitimacy.

Islamist activism increased under Sadat. He allowed Muslim activists and the Brotherhood more room as a counterweight to leftists and Nasserites, but groups such as al-Takfir wa al-Hijrah, Islamic Jihad, and the Islamic Group turned toward violence against the state and Copts.

Sadat arrested about 1,500 political figures in September 1981. On October 6, 1981, militant soldiers linked to Islamic Jihad assassinated him, and his vice president, Hosni Mubarak, came to power with a mandate for cautious change.

## The Mubarak regime

Hosni Mubarak kept peace with Israel and close ties with the United States while working to regain Sinai, which returned to Egyptian sovereignty in April 1982. He also released many of Sadat's political prisoners while prosecuting militants involved in the assassination.

Israel's invasion of Lebanon in 1982 strained Egyptian-Israeli relations and caused critics to accuse Mubarak of allowing Israel to exploit Egypt's disengagement. His cautious policy nevertheless helped Egypt rebuild relations with moderate Arab states, return to the Arab League, and sell military supplies to Iraq during the Iran-Iraq War.

In the late 1980s, Egypt suffered from falling oil prices and reduced remittances from millions of workers abroad. In 1991, the government signed an Economic Reform and Structural Adjustment Program with the IMF and World Bank, devalued the pound, raised interest rates, and reduced food and fuel subsidies.

These reforms hurt the poor and strengthened the social role of Islamist groups. Extremist groups, including Islamic Jihad and the Islamic Group, attacked politicians, secular writers, Copts, and tourists, including the severe Luxor attack in 1997.

Mubarak's politics remained authoritarian. He was reelected without opposition in 1987, 1993, and 1999, won again in the contested 2005 election, and his National Democratic Party dominated the People's Assembly amid claims of rigging.

The Muslim Brotherhood could not become a legal party but supported candidates through other parties or as independents. Press freedom was wider than under Nasser or Sadat, yet laws, censorship, al-Azhar pressure, Islamic courts, detention, and alleged torture limited political speech.

The Persian Gulf War of 1990-1991 brought mixed effects. Egypt received debt relief for joining the U.S.-led coalition, but many workers lost jobs in Iraq, and hopes for reconstruction contracts or regional peacekeeping roles were disappointed.

Uneven economic development, IMF influence, dependence on Western technology, and widening rich-poor gaps strained national unity. Growing Muslim-Coptic tension, even though most Egyptians tried publicly to minimize religious differences.

## Unrest in 2011

After Tunisia's 2011 uprising, protests against Hosni Mubarak began on January 25 in Cairo and other cities. Demonstrators denounced corruption, repression, and poverty and used social media networks outside traditional opposition leadership.

The protests grew more violent on January 28 after Friday prayers, when police used tear gas, water cannons, rubber bullets, beatings, and arrests. The government imposed curfews and cut Internet and telephone service in many places, while police stations and NDP buildings were attacked or burned.

Mohamed ElBaradei, the National Association for Change, and the Muslim Brotherhood joined or supported the demonstrations as they expanded. The army deployed in Cairo, but its attitude remained uncertain and it announced on January 31 that it would not use force against protesters.

Mubarak promised on February 1 that he would not run in the September election, but violence followed when pro-Mubarak supporters attacked demonstrators in Tahrir Square. At least five people died and about 1,500 were injured.

Talks led by Omar Suleiman offered concessions such as releasing political prisoners and increasing media freedom, but the opposition demanded Mubarak's immediate departure. Strikes by doctors, textile workers, transport workers, and others added pressure.

On February 10, Mubarak promised constitutional changes and delegation of powers but refused to resign. On February 11, Suleiman announced that Mubarak had stepped down, leaving the Supreme Council of the Armed Forces to administer Egypt.

The Supreme Council of the Armed Forces, led by Mohamed Hussein Tantawi, imposed martial law, suspended the constitution, dissolved parliament, kept Mubarak's cabinet temporarily, and promised elections. It also said Egypt would continue to respect treaties, including the 1979 peace treaty with Israel.

Investigations soon targeted Mubarak, his family, NDP figures, and officials suspected of corruption. Mubarak, Alaa, and Gamal were arrested in April, and prosecutors announced in May that they would be tried for ordering the killing of protesters as well as corruption and abuse of power.

## Transition to an elected government

Post-Mubarak elections began in November 2011. The Muslim Brotherhood's Freedom and Justice Party won about 47 percent of People's Assembly seats, and the hard-line Nur Party won about 25 percent.

The presidential election of 2012 was disrupted by legal reversals. Omar Suleiman and Khairat al-Shater were disqualified, and the runoff was between Ahmed Shafiq, a Mubarak-era minister, and Mohammed Morsi of the Freedom and Justice Party.

Just before the runoff, the Supreme Constitutional Court invalidated the parliamentary elections, dissolving the Islamist-dominated assembly. The Supreme Council of the Armed Forces then issued a declaration expanding its own authority and limiting the incoming president.

Mohammed Morsi won the presidency and took office in June 2012, then moved in August to retire senior military council members and revoke the military's June declaration. The central struggle then shifted to the drafting of a new constitution.

Islamist dominance in the Constituent Assembly alarmed liberal, secular, and Christian members, who boycotted sessions and challenged the assembly in court. Morsi's November 22 edict exempting himself from judicial oversight caused mass protests because many saw it as dictatorial.

The draft constitution passed the assembly without boycotting Christian and secular members and was approved by referendum in December 2012. Violence and protests continued into 2013 amid economic decline, poor services, unemployment, inflation, sectarian incidents, and polarization.

On June 30, 2013, massive anti-Morsi protests took place on the first anniversary of his inauguration. Gen. Abdel Fattah al-Sisi warned that the military would intervene if the crisis was not resolved, and on July 3 the military removed Morsi, suspended the constitution, and installed Adly Mansour as interim leader.

Security forces arrested Morsi and Brotherhood leaders, shut Brotherhood-affiliated television stations, and violently dispersed pro-Morsi demonstrations. More than 1,000 people were killed over several days in August 2013 when security forces broke up sit-ins in Cairo.

The interim regime declared a state of emergency and appointed a committee to amend the 2012 constitution. The new committee reduced Islamist language and strengthened the autonomy of the military and police; the amended constitution passed in January 2014 with more than 98 percent of the vote.

## Government and society

Egypt has operated under several [[Constitution and Constitutional Government (Constitution)|constitutions]] as both monarchy and republic. The 1923 constitution declared Egypt independent, sovereign, Islamic, and Arabic-speaking; later constitutions and declarations reflected the changing balance among monarchy, military rule, Arab nationalism, Islamism, and elected government.

After the 1952 revolution, parties were abolished and replaced by state organizations such as the National Union and Arab Socialist Union. Women received the franchise in the 1956 constitution, and Pan-Arabism shaped experiments such as the United Arab Republic with Syria and the failed Federation of Arab Republics.

The 1971 constitution was amended in 1980, 2005, and 2007, suspended after the 2011 uprising, and followed by an interim declaration. A 2012 constitution was approved under Morsi, suspended after his removal in 2013, and amended into the 2014 constitution.

The constitutional framework defines the Arab Republic of Egypt as a democratic state with [[Muhammad and the Religion of Islam (Islam)|Islam]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islam)) as state religion and Arabic as the national language. It recognizes public and private ownership, equality before the law, peaceful assembly, unions, voting rights, and a ban on parties based on religion.

The president is head of state and shares executive authority with the cabinet. The president appoints the prime minister and ministers, commands the armed forces, may grant amnesty, and can declare war only with National Defense Council consultation and approval from two-thirds of the House of Representatives.

Legislative power lies in the House of Representatives, whose members are elected for five-year terms. It approves laws and the budget, questions the cabinet, can dismiss ministers or the prime minister by no-confidence motions, and can impeach the president by a two-thirds majority.

Local administration was decentralized in 1960 through governorates, districts, and villages. Each level has elected people's councils and appointed executive councils, though the central government keeps strong control.

Egypt has 27 governorates, and Cairo, Alexandria, Port Said, Suez, and Luxor have governorate status. Governors are appointed and dismissed by the president and supervise local policy in education, health, utilities, housing, agriculture, communications, cooperatives, and national-plan implementation.

## Cultural life

Modern Egyptian culture ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Culture)) is Arab and Islamic but also deeply shaped by contact with the West. The main cultural story is how traditional systems responded to Western conquest, ideas, technology, reform, rejection, and selective adoption.

Daily rural life still shows the importance of the fellahin, agricultural labour, family, and village routines. Men work fields with tools such as the fas and minjal, women and children help in some regions, and older patterns of dress and public modesty remain visible.

Urban life differs sharply from village life and has been influenced by global urban culture. Since the early 1980s, modest dress and hijab have remained important, but city clothing, food, arts, and values have also absorbed Western and Mediterranean influences.

The family is the strongest social link in Egypt. In rural Upper Egypt and among Bedouin groups, tribal identity, blood relationships, patronage, honour, and vendetta remain socially important, while urban life relies more on extended patronage networks of relatives and friends.

Egyptian food reflects local and Mediterranean traditions. Ful mudammis, made from fava beans, is widely considered the national food, while mulukhiyyah, kuftah, aysh baladi, aysh shami, falafel, fish, mutton, chicken, Turkish-influenced sweets, figs, dates, coffee, and tea are also important.

Public holidays include Labor Day, Revolution Day, Armed Forces Day, Id al-Adha, Id al-Fitr, the Prophet's birthday, and Coptic Christmas on January 7. This mix reflects secular, Muslim, and Christian calendars.

Egypt is one of the Arab world's major literary centres. Writers such as Tawfiq Hakim, Yahya Haqqi, Muhammad Husayn Haykal, Abd al-Rahman al-Sharqawi, Yusuf Idris, and Naguib Mahfouz explored Western impact, rural life, urban poverty, and modern Egyptian identity.

Naguib Mahfouz is presented as Egypt's greatest modern novelist and won the Nobel Prize for Literature for The Cairo Trilogy. Later writers such as Sonallah Ibrahim, Nawal El Saadawi, Bahaa Taher, and Alaa Al Aswany gained international prominence.

The modern Egyptian theatre began as a European import, with Arabic-language plays first performed in 1870. Mahmud Taymur and Tawfiq al-Hakim dominated early development, while younger dramatists reflected changing society.

Because literacy rates were low, electronic media became central to mass culture. Egyptian television shaped tastes across the Arab world, and Egyptian actors, films, and programs gained wide regional influence.

Egyptian cinema dates to World War I and expanded after Misr Studios was founded in 1934. Directors such as Youssef Chahine, Salah Abu Sayf, and Muhammad Khan, and actors such as Omar Sharif, helped make Egyptian film important across the Arab world and beyond.

Music and dance are deeply rooted in Egyptian culture. Traditional instruments include the ud, qanun, nay, riqq, and rabab, while Sufi dhikr, zar ceremonies, martial dances, raqs sharqi, and professional popular music show wide variety.

Modern music stars such as Sayyid Darwish, Muhammad Abd al-Wahhab, Umm Kulthum, and Abd al-Halim Hafiz became cultural icons. Western-style music also became familiar from the 19th century and was adapted by composers such as Yusuf Greiss and Abu Bakr Khayrat.

Visual art and architecture draw from ancient, Islamic, and European traditions. Mahmud Mukhtar's The Awakening of Egypt, UNESCO sites such as Memphis, Giza, Thebes, Abu Simbel, Philae, Abu Mina, Saint Catherine's, and Islamic Cairo, and modern projects like Bibliotheca Alexandrina all show this range.

Cultural institutions include the Institut d'Egypte, Academy of the Arabic Language, National Research Centre, Atomic Energy Organization, Academy of Scientific Research and Technology, Egyptian National Library, National Archives, Egyptian Museum, Coptic Museum, Museum of Islamic Art, Greco-Roman Museum, and fine-arts museums.

Sports culture reaches back to ancient practices such as wrestling, weightlifting, stick fencing, and ball games. After the 1952 revolution, the government invested in sports facilities to raise Egypt's international standing.

Modern sport includes wrestling, weightlifting, boxing, swimming, basketball, volleyball, martial arts, and especially football. Cairo clubs al-Ahli and Zamalik have large followings, and Egypt's national team was the first African representative at the World Cup in 1934.

Egypt's media and publishing remain influential. Newspapers and magazines are supervised by the Supreme Press Council, state broadcasting is powerful, private satellite companies began in the 1990s, and Cairo has long been the Middle East's largest publishing centre, though challenged by Beirut and other Arab cities.

- 868-905: The Tulunid dynasty made Egypt virtually autonomous and kept more revenue inside the country.

- 1171: Saladin abolished the Fatimid caliphate, restored Sunni rule, and began the Ayyubid dynasty.
- 1250: Mamluk rule began after military slaves displaced the Ayyubid succession and created a new ruling system.
- 1260: The Mamluks defeated the Mongols at Ayn Jalut, strengthening their legitimacy and Egypt's regional power.
- 1516-1517: The Ottomans defeated the Mamluks and made Egypt an Ottoman province.
- 1798-1801: The French occupied Egypt, beginning a stronger era of European intervention and modern Western impact.
- 1805: Muhammad Ali became viceroy and began building a centralized, military, and reforming Egyptian state.
- 1854: Said granted Ferdinand de Lesseps the concession to build the Suez Canal.
- 1869: The Suez Canal ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Suez_Canal)) opened, increasing Egypt's strategic and commercial importance.
- 1882: Britain defeated the Urabists, occupied Egypt, and began a long period of British domination.
- 1919: The Wafd-linked nationalist revolt followed the arrest of Sad Zaghlul and pushed Britain toward limited independence.
- 1922: Britain declared Egypt independent while reserving major powers over defence, communications, minorities, foreign interests, and Sudan.
- 1952: The Free Officers overthrew the monarchy and began the revolutionary republic.
- 1956: Nasser nationalized the Suez Canal Company, triggering the Suez Crisis.
- 1967: The Six-Day War ended in Egypt's defeat and Israel's occupation of Sinai.
- 1973: The Sadat government launched the October War to force a new diplomatic situation with Israel.
- 1979: Egypt signed a peace treaty with Israel and became the first Arab state to make peace with it.
- 1981: Sadat was assassinated by militants linked to Islamic Jihad, and Hosni Mubarak became president.
- 2011: Mass demonstrations forced Hosni Mubarak to step down and left the Supreme Council of the Armed Forces in control.
- 2012: Mohammed Morsi became president after post-Mubarak elections, and a new constitution was approved by referendum.
- 2013: The Egyptian military removed Morsi after mass protests and installed an interim administration.
- 2014: An amended [[Constitution and Constitutional Government (Constitution)|constitution]] passed by referendum, reducing Islamist language and strengthening military and police autonomy.

## Interesting Facts

- Egypt is often called the gift of the Nile because the river's water and silt made dense settlement and agriculture possible in a desert country.
- Only about 3 percent of Egypt's land is arable, yet that small area supports intensive farming and much of the population.
- The Nile delta once had as many as seven river branches, but its waters are now mainly concentrated in the Damietta and Rosetta branches.
- Lake Nasser formed after the Aswan High Dam and led to the resettlement of about 100,000 Nubians into New Nubia.
- Khamsin storms can raise temperatures by 14 to 20 F, drop humidity to about 10 percent, and fill the air with dust.
- Papyrus, famous from ancient Egypt, is now found in Egypt mainly in botanical gardens rather than in the wild landscape.
- Al-Azhar has been an Islamic teaching centre for more than 1,000 years and remains one of Sunni Islam's most important institutions.
- Ful mudammis, made from fava beans, is widely regarded as Egypt's national food.
- The Suez Canal was closed after the 1967 war and reopened in 1975 after the Egyptian-Israeli disengagement process.
- The Cairo Metro is described as a commuter rail system with expansion under way, showing how modern transport follows the needs of the dense capital.
- Misr Studios, founded in 1934, helped make Egypt the centre of Arabic-language cinema.
- Naguib Mahfouz won the Nobel Prize for Literature for The Cairo Trilogy, marking Egypt's central role in modern Arabic fiction.
- Umm Kulthum was the leading female vocalist of Egypt and the Arab world for almost 50 years.
- Football is Egypt's most popular sport, and the national team was Africa's first World Cup representative in 1934.
- The 2014 constitution kept Egypt's state identity Arab, Islamic, and Arabic-speaking while changing the political order after the 2013 military intervention.
