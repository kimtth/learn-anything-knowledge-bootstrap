# Bolivia

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> Bolivia is a landlocked South American country whose high Andean plateaus, tropical eastern lowlands, indigenous societies, and mineral and hydrocarbon resources have shaped uneven development. From Tiwanaku and Inca origins through Spanish silver extraction, independence, land reform, and resource nationalism, its history centres on controlling land, labour, wealth, and political power across sharply different regions.

## Introduction

![[attachments/bolivia_001.png|420]]

*Location of Bolivia in South America*

Bolivia ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bolivia)) is a landlocked country in west-central [[South America]], extending some 950 miles north-south and 800 miles east-west and bordered by [[Brazil]], [[Paraguay]], [[Argentina]], [[Chile]], and [[Peru]]. It shares Lake Titicaca, South America's largest inland lake, with Peru, and it has been landlocked since losing its Pacific coast to [[Chile]] in the War of the Pacific ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/War_of_the_Pacific)) (1879-84). The constitutional capital is the historic city of Sucre, seat of the Supreme Court, while La Paz is the administrative capital, home to the executive and legislature.

Bolivia is traditionally regarded as a highland country: although only one-third of its territory lies in the Andes, most large cities and centuries of mining and investment have been concentrated there. In the late 20th century the eastern lowlands, especially the department of Santa Cruz, developed rapidly. The land was once the centre of the ancient Tiwanaku empire and part of the Inca empire from the 15th century; under Spanish rule it provided immense wealth in silver within the Viceroyalty of Peru ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Viceroyalty_of_Peru)).

## Geography and Relief

![[attachments/bolivia_002.webp|420]]

*Salt piles on the Salar de Uyuni in Bolivia*

Bolivia's mountainous west is one of the highest inhabited areas in the world. The Andes here form two great parallel ranges: the Cordillera Occidental along the Chilean border, holding active volcanoes, the Uyuni Salt Flat, and the country's highest peak, Mount Sajama (21,463 feet); and the Cordillera Oriental, whose northern section near La Paz, the Cordillera Real, carries snowcapped peaks above 20,000 feet. Between them lies the Altiplano, a flat plateau about 500 miles long at elevations of 12,000 to 12,500 feet.

The eastern lowlands, the Oriente, are tropical plains, rivers, and seasonally flooded land that became increasingly important late in the 20th century. Relief shapes where people live and where power is concentrated: La Paz, the mining districts, and the older population centres lie in or near the Andes, while the lowlands developed later as a frontier of agriculture, settlement, and investment.

## Climate and Environment

Although Bolivia ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bolivia)) lies wholly within the tropics, it has every gradation of temperature from equatorial lowland heat to arctic cold, because in the Andes contrasts depend more on elevation and cloud cover than on latitude. Cold winds sweep the Altiplano year-round, where daytime averages of 45 to 52 F fall below freezing at night, while Lake Titicaca moderates the north. The rainy season runs from December to March.

Climate underlies the country's agricultural diversity. The cold Altiplano supports one set of crops and livestock; the humid Yungas, with up to 53 inches of rain a year, and the warm Valles support another; and the hot Oriente, averaging 73 to 80 F and occasionally swept by cold southern winds called surazos, supports yet another. Temperature and rainfall thus shape settlement, farming, and transport across the country.

## Population and Economy

Bolivia is well endowed with natural resources but has long been one of the poorest countries in South America. Mining, hydrocarbons, agriculture, and trade dominate the economy, though development has been limited by high production costs, weak transport infrastructure, and the landlocked location. The revolutionary program of 1952-53 brought agrarian reform and nationalized the mines, but agricultural and mineral output initially fell sharply.

Population and urbanization have grown together. From an estimated 1,800,000 people in 1900, the population reached about 5,300,000 by the mid-1970s as the death rate fell and birth rates stayed high. Urban dwellers rose from under one-tenth in 1900 to nearly two-thirds by the early 21st century. Economic change has sharpened rather than erased Bolivia's strong regional and ethnic divisions, making them more politically visible.

## Minerals and Energy

![[attachments/bolivia_003.webp|420]]

*Mine entrance at Cerro Rico near Potosi, Bolivia*

Mining has shaped Bolivia for centuries. The country is a major producer of tin and gold and also holds zinc, silver, antimony, tungsten, lead, copper, and lithium. Tin long dominated, but Bolivia remained dangerously exposed to world prices: a glut in the 1980s forced the state corporation COMIBOL to slash output and lay off more than two-thirds of its workers. Privatization later passed many mines to cooperatives of former employees working in harsh conditions.

Hydrocarbons grew central later. Oil exploration began in 1920 under Standard Oil, whose operation was nationalized in 1937 to form YPFB; natural gas became Bolivia's most valuable legal export by the mid-1980s, carried after 1998 through the Bolivia-Brazil pipeline. In 2006 President Evo Morales nationalized the gas fields and oil industry, ordering the military to occupy the fields and giving the state control of energy production.

## Agriculture and Industry

Agriculture engages about two-fifths of the workforce but yields only about one-seventh of GDP. On the Altiplano, potatoes—grown in thousands of varieties since pre-Columbian times—and the grains quinoa and canahua are staples, alongside llamas and alpacas. The Yungas produce coffee, cacao, citrus, and coca; the Valles around Cochabamba, called the garden of Bolivia, grow grapes, vegetables, and grain; and the Oriente around Santa Cruz grows soybeans, sugarcane, rice, and cotton.

Agriculture is also a social history: the 1953 Agrarian Reform Act broke up the large estates, the latifundios, and gave plots to the Indian campesinos, creating new market towns. Industry remains small. Mineral processing and food preparation long dominated, but the early 21st century brought investment in soybean processing, textiles using alpaca and cotton, wood products, and gold jewelry, concentrated in La Paz, El Alto, Cochabamba, and Santa Cruz.

## Government and Society

Bolivia was declared independent in 1825 and adopted its first constitution in 1826, but its history has been marked by frequent military coups within a unitary state. Under the heavily revised constitution, a directly elected president serves a five-year term, and a bicameral National Congress combines a Chamber of Senators and a Chamber of Deputies. A new constitution approved in January 2009 allowed consecutive presidential terms and required that judges be elected.

The country is divided into nine departamentos, subdivided into provinces and cantons. Since the Popular Participation Law of 1994, municipios (municipalities) manage 20 percent of the public-sector budget, letting long-neglected communities begin public works. The growing political weight of indigenous communities and campesino groups, who increasingly speak for the nation, has made Bolivian society politically contested as well as culturally diverse; women have voted since 1938.

## History

Bolivia ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bolivia)) traces its origins to the Tiwanaku empire, which centred on the Altiplano from the 7th century and reached its height by the 11th, and later to the Inca empire, which absorbed the Aymara-speaking peoples in the 15th century. After the Spanish conquest, the discovery of silver at Potosi in 1545—the largest silver mines then known—drove forced Indian labour and the founding of towns such as Sucre, La Paz (1548), and Cochabamba.

After independence in 1825, Bolivia lost its Pacific coast to Chile, accepting that loss in the peace treaty of 1904, and sold the Acre region to Brazil in 1903. A tin-based economy, repeated military rule, and conflicts over natural gas dominated the 20th century, leading to the resignations of presidents amid gas protests and to the election of Evo Morales, Bolivia's first Indian president, on December 18, 2005. Quechua and Aymara remain central to national identity.

## Conclusion

Bolivia ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bolivia))'s history, economy, and politics are inseparable from its geography and from resource extraction. Its central tension is the effort to build national stability across very different regions and social worlds—the Andean west and the eastern lowlands, highland Indian communities and lowland frontier cities.

The country is rich in resources and cultural depth but repeatedly challenged by uneven development and regional division. [[Extraction And Processing Industries (Mining)|Mining]], coca cultivation, and regional inequality remain linked parts of one national story of land, labour, and control of wealth.
