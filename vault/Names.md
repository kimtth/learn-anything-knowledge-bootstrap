# Names

## Introduction

A name is a word or group of words that refers directly to an individual entity, real or imaginary. Unlike a common noun or descriptive phrase, a name points to a specific entity rather than identifying it as a member of a class. For example, Colorado can name a specific river, while a phrase such as the river that flows through Austin identifies a river by description.

Names and appellatives, or common nouns, can overlap. A common noun such as river may refer to one particular river in a situation, but that does not make it a name. Some names, such as Big River or Red River, may originate from descriptive phrases that become fixed as names. The distinction is usually clear but can blur when names become common nouns or when groups, institutions, brands, churches, or nations are treated as individualized entities.

## Names and Descriptions

The difference between direct naming and descriptive reference is central. A name singles out an entity without listing its properties, while a noun phrase identifies it by a unique feature. Even if several entities share the same name, each use can still be a proper name when context or added detail identifies the intended individual.

## Onomastics

![[attachments/names_001.webp|420]]

The science of onomastics, or onomatology, studies names in all their aspects. Because almost anything can be named, its field includes many languages, regions, cultures, and historical periods. Practical divisions include personal names, family names, place-names, names of institutions, names of peoples, commercial names, and many other categories.

## Personal and Family Names

![[attachments/names_002.webp|420]]

*Personal name document*

Family names ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surname)) in Europe developed from several sources: personal names, patronymics, place-names, occupations, nicknames, physical traits, and social roles. English patronymics often use -son, as in Richardson, Dickson, Henryson, and Gibson. Scottish names use Mac or Mc, Irish names use O, Mc, or Mac, and Welsh names may use P-, as in Powell from son of Howel.

## Surnames from Places

![[attachments/names_003.webp|420]]

*Surname distribution map*

Many surnames come from places and landscape words. English examples include Murray, Hardes, Whitney, Fields, Holmes, Brookes, and Woods. French family names show similar patterns: some derive from Jacques, some from places such as Avignon or Caen, some from districts such as Breton or Norman, some from occupations such as butcher, baker, or mason, and some from traits such as red hair.

## Patronymic Surnames

Patronymic names preserve descent from a father's name or its variants. Richardson, Dickson, Dixon, Dickinson, Henryson, Harrison, Henderson, Gilbertson, Gibson, Gregson, and Grigson all show such processes. Older English families may use Norman French Fitz-, as in Fitzgerald. Modern Greek forms use suffixes such as Dimitriopoulos, meaning son of Dimitrios.

## Occupational Surnames

Occupational and descriptive family names show how social identity became inherited. Clerk, Leclerc, Duclerc, Auclerk, Clergue, and Clark relate to clerical roles. Boucher, Boulanger, and Masson indicate butcher, baker, and mason. Other surnames arose from nicknames whose original incidents are now obscure, such as Czech names meaning Don't eat bread or Jump into the field.

## Jewish Family Names

Jewish family names developed differently because many Jews in ghettos used only given names until the late 18th and early 19th centuries. Later names were chosen or assigned, often from religious roles, tribes, places, or nicknames. Cantor, Kantorowicz, Kohn, Cohen, Cahen, Kaan, Kahane, Levi, Halevy, and Lowy reflect religious or tribal associations. Some imposed names in Austria were derisive or artificial.

Royal and religious naming customs differ from ordinary surnames. Some royal houses have family names, such as Hohenzollern, while the British royal family adopted Windsor only in 1917. Popes abandon their personal names after election and choose a single papal name, sometimes to signal religious or missionary intention.

## Other Patterns of Naming

Many cultures use theophoric names, names containing divine references. Assyrian, Babylonian, Phoenician, Hebrew, Aramaic, Arabic, and other naming traditions include meanings such as a god created, protected, gave, or healed. Ashurbanipal, Nebuchadrezzar, Hannibal, Jonathan, Rafael, Peter, Muhammad, and Ibrahim belong to wider patterns where names preserve religious, linguistic, familial, and social history.

## Place-names

Place-names include descriptive, commemorative, honorific, wishful, religious, and historically layered names. Descriptive names identify features, as in Rocky Mountains, North Sea, or Newcastle. Commemorative names honour persons or earlier places, such as Constantinople for Constantine, Cartagena after older Cartagena and Carthage, and New York after the duke of York. Wishful names include Vladivostok, Cape of Good Hope, and Pontus Euxinus.

Place-names also record historical influence. In the United States, Spanish names such as Florida, San Antonio, Santa Fe, and San Diego show Spanish Catholic influence in the South and Southwest. New populations often adopt older place-names into their own languages, leaving layered evidence of conquest, settlement, religion, trade, colonization, and cultural contact.

- 3rd century BC: Greek grammatical tradition used onoma for both name and noun.
- Late 18th century: Many Jews in Europe began choosing or receiving inherited family names.
- 1917: The British royal family adopted the name Windsor.
