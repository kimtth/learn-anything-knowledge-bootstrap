## Introduction

Western philosophy ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_philosophy)) is the history of philosophical thought from ancient Greece to the present. Its history includes attempts to define philosophy, connect ideas to cultural settings, and trace shifting conceptions of philosophy's task. Philosophy has meant the love of wisdom, the study of the universe, moral inquiry, theology, foundations of science, criticism of knowledge, analysis of consciousness and will, inquiry into truth, goodness, and beauty, and codification of rational thought.

Histories of philosophy can be organized by problems, schools, or great thinkers. A full history must consider the logic of ideas, the cultural and historical context in which they arise, and the personality and situation of philosophers themselves. Western philosophy repeatedly alternates between system-building and criticism, reason and experience, metaphysics and skepticism, theology and secular inquiry, and individual freedom and social order.

The writing of philosophical history therefore has its own method. Ideas are not detached slogans; they depend on earlier doctrines, local institutions, available texts, political crises, religious controversies, and the temperament of individual thinkers. A chronological story must still notice recurring patterns, such as Platonism and Aristotelianism in the Middle Ages, ancient skepticism in the Renaissance, and rationalism against empiricism in the Enlightenment.

## Ancient Greek Beginnings

The earliest Greek philosophers were cosmologists or naturalists who sought nonmythological explanations of the physical world. Thales of Miletus explained the world through water and practical mathematical reasoning. Later pre-Socratics developed monistic and pluralistic theories of matter, change, number, being, and nature, laying foundations for metaphysics, science, and rational inquiry.

## Socrates

[[Socrates]] transformed philosophy from cosmology toward ethical self-examination. He claimed no wisdom but pursued it through questioning, exposing inconsistencies in opinions and actions. His life rested on refusing wrongdoing and believing that real knowledge of the good prevents evil action. His trial and death made him the model of philosophical integrity and inspired multiple schools, including Megarian, Cynic, Cyrenaic, and Platonic traditions.

Socrates' political importance came from action as well as argument. In 406 BC he resisted the unlawful condemnation of Athenian generals after Arginusae, and under the Thirty Tyrants he refused an order to arrest an innocent man. These episodes made philosophical conscience visible as obedience to justice rather than to majority pressure or oligarchic command.

## Plato

*Plato and Aristotle in Raphael's School of Athens*

[[Plato and Platonism (Plato)|Plato]], Socrates' greatest disciple, connected ethics, politics, metaphysics, knowledge, and education. Disillusioned by Athenian politics, he argued that political life would improve only when philosophers ruled or rulers became philosophers. His doctrine of Forms treated changing things as intelligible through eternal realities. The Academy, his school, institutionalized philosophical inquiry and shaped later metaphysics, mathematics, political theory, and theology.

## Aristotle

[[Aristotle and Aristotelianism|Aristotle]], Plato's student and later teacher of [[Alexander the Great]], founded the Lyceum and created a systematic philosophy of logic, nature, ethics, politics, metaphysics, biology, psychology, rhetoric, and poetics. He rejected separate Platonic Forms, developed formal logic, treated form as immanent in things, and gave Western philosophy a durable model of disciplined inquiry grounded in observation, classification, and explanation.

Aristotle's break with Plato was also a change in philosophical style. Instead of seeking a separate realm of Forms, he analyzed categories, causes, substances, living things, constitutions, arguments, and poetic structures in the world available to inquiry. That combination of empirical collection and theoretical ordering made him a recurring authority whenever philosophers wanted knowledge to be both systematic and attached to experience.

## Hellenistic Schools

Stoicism, founded by Zeno of Citium, taught that happiness lies in virtue and living according to nature, while externals such as health, wealth, illness, and poverty are ultimately indifferent. Epicureanism, founded by Epicurus, made pleasure central but understood happiness through simple living, friendship, freedom from fear, and liberation from religious terror. Skepticism, associated with Pyrrhon and later Sextus Empiricus, questioned certainty and preserved much knowledge of Hellenistic thought through criticism of dogmatic schools.

## Late Antiquity and Neoplatonism

Late antique schools continued for centuries until Justinian I closed pagan philosophical schools in 529. Neoplatonism, founded by Ammonius Saccas and developed by Plotinus, reinterpreted Plato through a hierarchy of reality flowing from the One. It deeply influenced Christian, Islamic, Jewish, and medieval philosophy by linking metaphysics, mysticism, intellect, and the soul's ascent.

## Early Middle Ages

The early medieval period followed the collapse of the Western Roman Empire and the rise of Christian culture. Monasteries became centres of learning. [[Augustine]], shaped by Neoplatonism and Christianity, identified eternal truth with God and offered powerful analyses of memory, knowledge, love, time, history, and the soul. Boethius transmitted Aristotelian logic and raised the medieval problem of universals through translations and commentaries.

## Anselm and Scholastic Revival

Anselm of Canterbury represented the revival of reasoned theology, arguing that faith seeks understanding. His Monologion and Proslogion offered rational proofs of God, including the famous argument from the being than which none greater can be conceived. The 11th and 12th centuries saw cathedral schools and universities replace monasteries as major centres of learning.

## Aristotelianism and Arabic Thought

The translation of Aristotle and Arabic philosophical works in the 12th and 13th centuries transformed medieval thought. Avicenna influenced metaphysics, psychology, logic, and medicine, while al-Ghazali and the Liber de causis shaped Christian debate. Scholastic method used authoritative texts, lectures, and disputations, creating a disciplined philosophical theology based on argument and commentary.

The new university method joined authority with contest. Masters read prescribed texts, commented on them, and then staged disputations in which objections and replies forced a question into sharper form. This made medieval philosophy both conservative and dynamic: it revered Aristotle, Augustine, Boethius, Pseudo-Dionysius, and Avicenna, yet it also trained thinkers to test interpretations against rival arguments.

## High Scholasticism

Robert Grosseteste and Roger Bacon emphasized mathematics, optics, induction, deduction, and experiment. Bonaventure defended an Augustinian and Franciscan path from creatures to God. Albertus Magnus helped introduce Aristotelian science and philosophy to Christian Europe. Thomas Aquinas reconciled Aristotle with Christian doctrine, distinguished philosophy from theology, and argued that reason and faith cannot ultimately conflict because both come from God.

## Averroism and Late Medieval Thought

Latin Averroists, including Siger de Brabant, pursued Aristotle through Averroes even when conclusions appeared to conflict with Christian teaching. Condemnations in 1277 showed growing anxiety about rationalism. John Duns Scotus emphasized God's infinite being, contingency, will, and haecceity, earning the title Doctor Subtilis. William of Ockham developed nominalist tendencies, while Meister Eckhart advanced speculative mysticism and Nicholas of Cusa explored learned ignorance and the coincidence of opposites.

## Renaissance Humanism

Renaissance humanism revived Classical literature, rhetoric, medicine, mathematics, and human dignity. Giovanni Pico della Mirandola celebrated human self-creation, while Lorenzo Valla developed textual criticism. Marsilio Ficino translated Plato and served the Platonic Academy of Florence. Humanism favoured dialogue, rhetoric, moral reflection, and the dignity of human beings over narrow Scholastic disputation.

Humanism's philosophical force came from philology as well as moral enthusiasm. Recovering Greek and Latin texts changed what could be argued, how authorities were read, and which ancient schools returned to use. Plato, Stoicism, skepticism, atomism, and Epicureanism reentered early modern debate not as curiosities but as living alternatives to late scholastic forms of reasoning.

## Renaissance Science and Method

Renaissance philosophy helped prepare modern science through empiricism, mechanism, and mathematical explanation. [[Leonardo da Vinci]] emphasized experience, mechanical science, and mathematics. Andreas Vesalius exemplified anatomical observation, while [[Copernicus]], [[Kepler]], and [[Galileo]] showed the power of mathematical physics. Ancient atomism, skepticism, Stoicism, and Platonism reentered modern thought.

## Bacon and Hobbes

[[Francis bacon|Francis Bacon]] championed experience and induction, calling for systematic tables of presence, absence, and degree to discover causes and forms. Thomas Hobbes combined Baconian method and Galilean mechanics into a materialist system. For Hobbes, reality is matter in motion, philosophy is reasoning about causes and effects, and politics must constrain human passions to prevent civil disorder.

## Descartes and Rationalism

Rene Descartes introduced systematic doubt, clear and distinct ideas, and the certainty of self-consciousness in I think, therefore I am. Cartesian rationalism treated mathematical clarity and logical consistency as tests of truth and made the subject central to philosophy. Continental rationalism continued through Spinoza and Leibniz, using systematic treatises, deductive methods, and metaphysical arguments.

## Enlightenment and Empiricism

The Enlightenment ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Age_of_Enlightenment)) was shaped by Isaac Newton's physics and John Locke's theory of understanding. Locke traced ideas to sensation and reflection and distinguished primary from secondary qualities. George Berkeley denied matter as an independent substance and reduced objects to perceived qualities. David Hume applied experimental reasoning to human nature, dissolving causal necessity into habit and the self into a bundle of perceptions.

## Enlightenment Politics and Sciences

The Enlightenment produced materialism, natural science, social science, economics, ethics, aesthetics, and political liberalism. Montesquieu, Vico, [[Adam Smith]], Bentham, Burke, and others specialized new fields. [[Locke]] defended natural freedom, equality, consent, property, and limited government. [[Jean-Jacques Rousseau]] radicalized social-contract theory through the general will, citizenship, equality, and obligation to resist usurped authority.

Enlightenment specialization changed philosophy's map. Natural history, chemistry, political economy, jurisprudence, aesthetics, psychology, and moral theory began to separate into recognizable fields, yet they still shared confidence in reason, progress, nature, and reform. Political theory shifted from securing order under sovereign power toward explaining rights, consent, representation, equality, and resistance to injustice.

## Professional Philosophy and Kant

In the 18th century philosophy became more professional, especially in German universities. Christian Wolff helped create systematic terminology and textbooks. Immanuel Kant culminated Enlightenment philosophy by asking what reason can and cannot do. His critical method examined a priori knowledge, proposed a Copernican revolution in which objects conform to human knowing, and created the philosophical form of the critique through analytic, dialectic, and methodology.

Kant's critical philosophy answered a double pressure. Newtonian science seemed to show necessary order in nature, while Hume's empiricism undermined the idea that necessity is simply observed. Kant therefore asked how experience is possible at all, arguing that sensibility and understanding actively structure the objects humans can know.

## Nineteenth Century

After Kant, the 19th century discovered the irrational and generated powerful oppositions: pragmatism against idealism, positivism against irrationalism, Marxism against liberalism. Romanticism elevated feeling over reason, the Industrial Revolution inspired social reform, the revolutions of 1848 brought class conflict to consciousness, and Charles Darwin's evolution reshaped views of nature and humanity.

German idealism developed through Fichte, Schelling, and [[Hegel and Hegelianism (Hegel)|Hegel]], who interpreted history as the unfolding of spirit. Auguste Comte developed positivism and the law of three stages. [[John Stuart Mill]] defended empiricism, liberalism, utilitarianism, and scientific method. British idealists such as T.H. Green, F.H. Bradley, and Bernard Bosanquet revived Hegelian themes. Peirce and William James developed pragmatism, testing meaning and truth through practical consequences. Kierkegaard, Schopenhauer, and [[Nietzsche]] explored faith, will, suffering, individuality, and value.

The 19th-century turn also changed philosophy's subject matter. History, society, economics, biology, psychology, art, culture, and religion became philosophical problems, not just background conditions. Marx connected consciousness to labour and class, Mill connected liberty to individuality and public debate, and Nietzsche treated morality as something with a history rather than a timeless code.

This widened philosophical criticism beyond metaphysics alone.

It also changed the figure of the philosopher. Earlier modern thinkers had often been courtiers, clergy, gentlemen, or independent scholars; the 19th century increasingly tied philosophy to universities, public controversy, political movements, and specialized sciences. The result was a more fragmented but more socially alert philosophical culture.

## Twentieth Century

The 20th century included analytic philosophy, phenomenology, logical positivism, existentialism, pragmatism, process philosophy, and critical theory. G.E. Moore and Bertrand Russell reacted against idealism through analysis and logic. Ludwig Wittgenstein reshaped language, logic, and ordinary-language philosophy. Husserl founded phenomenology, Heidegger reinterpreted being and human existence, Sartre developed existentialism, and later thinkers such as Derrida questioned metaphysics, language, and presence.

Analytic philosophy emphasized logical clarity, language, science, and argument. Russell and Moore treated analysis as a way to dissolve inherited metaphysical confusions, while logical atomism sought the structure of the world through the structure of propositions. Logical positivists later tied meaningfulness to verification and scientific language, though their own criterion became controversial. Ordinary-language philosophers then turned attention to everyday uses of words, resisting the idea that philosophy must imitate mathematics or natural science.

This analytic development kept logic ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logic)) close to science and mathematics, but it also produced self-criticism. Questions about verification, reference, private language, rule-following, and ordinary use showed that clarity could require attention to practice as well as formal structure.

Continental philosophy developed through phenomenology, existentialism, hermeneutics, structuralism, poststructuralism, and critical theory. Husserl sought rigorous description of consciousness, Heidegger shifted inquiry toward being and Dasein, Sartre made freedom, responsibility, and bad faith central, and later French thinkers examined language, power, difference, interpretation, and cultural systems. These movements retained older questions about subjectivity, history, and meaning while challenging universal systems.

Bergson, Dewey, and Whitehead represented other modern alternatives. Bergson stressed duration, intuition, and creative evolution; Dewey developed pragmatism as experimental inquiry tied to democracy, education, and social practice; Whitehead built a process metaphysics in which events and becoming are more basic than static substances. Together they show that 20th-century Western philosophy was not simply divided between analytic and continental camps.

Across the century, Western philosophy ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_philosophy)) also changed its institutional and literary form. It became increasingly professional, journal-based, university-centred, and technically specialized, yet it also remained connected to politics, art, science, religion, and social criticism. Questions about language, logic, mind, science, power, violence, gender, culture, and interpretation carried forward the older philosophical task of examining human experience as a whole.

The continuing pattern is one of recurring problems rather than simple replacement. Ancient questions about being, knowledge, virtue, and happiness reappear in medieval theology, modern science, Enlightenment politics, idealism, pragmatism, phenomenology, analysis, and postmodern criticism. Western philosophy therefore remains a conversation across schools, methods, historical worlds, changing ideals of reason, and disputed visions of human flourishing, truth, justice, meaning, and freedom.
