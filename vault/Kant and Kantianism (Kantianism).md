# Kant and Kantianism (Kantianism)

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> Kantianism is the diverse body of later philosophies developed from Kant's critical thought. Its schools reconsidered a priori knowledge, the thing-in-itself, moral action, values, and scientific method while opposing dogmatism and speculative naturalism in different national settings.

## Introduction

![[attachments/kantianism_001.webp|420]]

*Portrait of Immanuel Kant*

Kantianism ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Immanuel_Kant)) means either Kant's own system or the later philosophies inspired by his writings. This summary concerns the later movement. Kantian philosophies are diverse, but they share a concern with the nature and limits of human knowledge and with the hope of making philosophy scientific in a way comparable to mathematics and physics.

The Kantian movement opposed dogmatism, speculative naturalism, and usually irrationalism. Its schools selected different parts of Kant's thought: empirical knowledge, a priori structure, the thing-in-itself, knowledge and morality, or the conditions of moral action. Kant's own synthesis drew on British empiricism, Newtonian science, and German rationalism, so later readers could reconstruct it in many directions.

## Early Kantianism: 17901835

Early Kantianism developed from the claim that the Critique of Pure Reason was a critique of metaphysics and a methodological preparation for science. Kant's Copernican revolution held that knowledge is partly constituted by the knowing subject. The mind imposes forms and concepts on experience, so knowledge is not a simple copy of external reality.

Transcendental elements appear in three levels: space and time as forms of intuition, categories such as substance and causality, and ideas of reason such as the I, the world, and God. Phenomena arise when sensory data meet these forms, while things-in-themselves remain unknown.

Post-Kantian disputes focused on the thing-in-itself, limits of knowledge, and the relation between Kant and idealism. Some interpreters moved toward German idealism, while others held closer to critical limits.

## Nineteenth-century Neo-Kantianism

Neo-Kantianism revived Kant partly as a response to positivism. Many thinkers wanted a foundation for science that respected phenomena without accepting materialist metaphysics. Kant offered such a model because he limited scientific knowledge to phenomena and rejected speculation.

The return to Kant was strengthened by Kuno Fischer's historical work and by Otto Liebmann's call, "Back to Kant!" in 1865. The revival produced empiricist, logicist, realist, metaphysical, axiological, and psychological currents. The most important forms survived into the 20th century.

## Epistemological Neo-Kantianism

Epistemological Neo-Kantianism included empiricist, logistic, and realist forms. Hermann von Helmholtz argued that perception represents external things indirectly, that space and time are frameworks of the perceiving subject, and that causality is an a priori law. Friedrich Albert Lange likewise kept science at the phenomenal level and rejected the thing-in-itself.

The Marburg school made the logicist form most influential. Hermann Cohen treated the transcendental subject as a logical function that constructs knowledge. Nothing is simply given; everything is posed as a task for thought. Paul Natorp extended this logic, while Ernst Cassirer emphasized human symbolizing powers.

## Metaphysical Neo-Kantianism

Metaphysical Neo-Kantianism tried to recover critical metaphysics. Otto Liebmann moved toward this approach, Johannes Volkelt argued that subjective consciousness points beyond itself, and Friedrich Paulsen claimed that Kant remained a metaphysician even in the Critique of Pure Reason.

## Axiological Neo-Kantianism

Axiological Neo-Kantianism, or the Baden school, developed around Wilhelm Windelband and Heinrich Rickert. It treated values as central: action answers to the Good, and thought answers to the True. Rickert argued that criticism should ground not only physics but also the sciences of spirit and culture.

The Baden school reconnected Kant with German idealism and cultural philosophy. It drew near to Wilhelm Dilthey's historical understanding, Georg Simmel's life-philosophy, Hugo Munsterberg's values, Richard Kroner's culture and religion, and Bruno Bauch's general work.

## Psychological Neo-Kantianism

Psychological Neo-Kantianism interpreted transcendental philosophy through inner experience. Jurgen Bona Meyer began this path, while Leonard Nelson founded the Neo-Friesian school. Nelson held that the mind's a priori equipment is revealed through analysis of the inner self.

Kant philology became a major scholarly field after 1860. Hans Vaihinger's commentary, Kantstudien in 1896, the Kant-Gesellschaft in 1904, and the Academy edition from 1900 made Kant studies a historical enterprise as well as a movement.

## Non-German Kantianism

Non-German Kantianism spread through Western philosophy. In France, Victor Cousin helped introduce German thought, while Charles Renouvier influenced Octave Hamelin, Jules Lachelier, and Emile Boutroux. In Britain, Kantianism was weaker than Hegelianism, though Edward Caird, Robert Adamson, Norman Kemp Smith, Herbert J. Paton, and Peter F. Strawson drew on Kant.

American and Italian Kantianism took distinct forms. Ralph Waldo Emerson helped make Kant known in the [[United States of America (United States)|United States]] around 1840, though he was not a Kantian. Charles Sanders Peirce used Kant against Hegelianism, and William H. Werkmeister followed Marburg themes. In Italy, Alfonso Testa, Carlo Cantoni, Francesco Orestano, Augusto Guzzo, and Pantaleo Carabellese advanced Kantian study.

## Assessment of Kantianism

Kantianism's strength is that it made philosophy confront the empirical and the a priori at once. Its theory of science remained influential because science needs experience but also relies on conditions of knowledge. The central difficulty is whether synthetic a priori knowledge is possible.

Later philosophers returned to, revised, or rejected Kant. Edmund Husserl returned to transcendental themes. Ludwig Wittgenstein briefly adopted a semi-Kantian limit on reason. Martin Heidegger gave Kant an existential interpretation. Nicolai Hartmann defended a realist metaphysics opposing Kant on mathematics, ontology, and the thing-in-itself.

Major objections accused Kantianism of narrowing philosophy to philosophy of science, being too formal for concrete concerns, and becoming subjectivist. Critics especially attacked Kant's formal ethics and the idea that knowledge is constructed by the subject.

Kantianism declined after World War I compared with its strength from 1860 to 1918, yet it did not disappear. Even critics continued to work with its problems, and Kant remained comparable in importance to Plato and Aristotle.

## Key Events & Developments

- 1790-1835: Early Kantianism debated the thing-in-itself and post-Kantian idealism.
- 1865: Otto Liebmann popularized the call "Back to Kant!"
- 1871: Hermann Cohen gave the Marburg school a major foundation.
- 1896: Kantstudien was founded for Kant scholarship.
- 1904: The Kant-Gesellschaft was founded.
- After World War I: Kantianism ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Immanuel_Kant)) declined as philosophy moved toward more concrete problems.

## Key People
