# Horses and Horsemanship (Horse)

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> The horse is a hoofed herbivorous mammal domesticated on the Eurasian steppes and historically central to transport, agriculture, hunting, warfare, status, art, and ritual. Human selection produced many breeds, while the long partnership between horses and people reshaped societies before mechanized transport.

## Introduction

The horse, Equus caballus ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Horse)), is a hoofed herbivorous mammal of the family Equidae. It is a single species with many varieties called breeds. Before mechanized vehicles, horses were central as draft animals and as one of the main forms of transportation.

Wild horses were probably first hunted for food, and domestication likely occurred about 6,000 years ago among Indo-European peoples of the steppes north of the Black and Caspian seas. Climate, food, and human selection helped the horse acquire its modern form quickly. Its partnership with humans became unusually close: it plowed fields, hauled goods, carried riders, hunted game, managed cattle, fought in war, and supported exploration.

The horse-human relationship shaped language, ritual, status, art, and myth. Terms such as chivalry and cavalier carry horse-related meanings of honor and manners. Horses were buried with Scythian kings and Egyptian pharaohs, linked to supernatural beliefs, and made central in Greek ideas such as the Centaur. Famous horses, including Bucephalus, Incitatus, El Morzillo, Roan Barbery, and Copenhagen, became inseparable from famous riders.

Horses also provided materials and food. Horsemeat has been eaten in parts of Europe and Iceland, bones and cartilage have supplied glue, horse serum has supplied tetanus antitoxin, hides have made leather, foal coats have made fur, horsehair has served upholstery and violin bows, manure has served fuel and mushroom cultivation, and mare's milk has been drunk by peoples such as the Scythians, Mongols, and Arabs.

## Form and function

A mature male horse is a stallion, a female is a mare, a breeding stallion is a stud, and a castrated male is a gelding. Young horses are foals; male foals are colts and female foals are fillies. Geldings have largely replaced stallions as riding horses, while earlier practice often reserved mares for breeding and geldings for work or riding.

The primitive horse was probably about 12 hands high and dun colored. Domestic horses gone wild, such as North American mustangs, tend to revert toward primitive traits. Modern anatomy shows adaptation for speed, grazing, and flight: long legs, a single hoof on each foot, an elongated head and neck, and a body suited to rapid movement over open ground.

## Senses

The senses of the horse serve its main defense, flight. Large eyes set far back on the head and a long neck allow wide visual warning even while grazing. Vision is binocular only directly forward, and evidence suggests limited color perception. Smell and hearing are keener than in humans, helping horses detect water, fire, danger, and familiar signals.

George Gaylord Simpson summarized the survival pattern by saying that legs for running and eyes for warning helped horses endure despite predators. Hearing and memory also support training: horses can learn repeated words, tones, and sounds, while visual memory can make them shy from places or objects linked to earlier fear.

## Colour and pattern

Horse [[Colour|colour]] developed from primitive dun into many coats and patterns. Important colors include black, bay, chestnut, sorrel, palomino, cream, and white. Black horses may still have white markings; bay includes shades of brown with black mane, tail, and usually stockings; chestnut lacks the black overtones of bay; and palomino ranges from cream to bronze with a light mane and tail.

White and patterned coats are especially varied. Gray horses are born dark and whiten with age; roan mixes white with another color from birth; pinto refers to many spotted white-and-color patterns; and appaloosa patterns are associated with spotted horses. These markings became important in breed identity, selection, and visual classification.

## Nutrition

The natural food of the horse is grass, while stabled horses usually receive hay and grain. Feeding immediately before or after work can cause digestive problems, and fresh water is important, though overheated horses should not be watered at once. Oats are especially valuable, while crushed oats, chaff, barley, hay, mash, corn, salt, and commercial feed mixes serve different needs.

Food choices also reflect condition and poverty. Older horses or horses with poor teeth may need crushed grain, and animals with digestive troubles may receive mash. In difficult times, horses have adapted to potatoes, beans, leaves, and even fish in Iceland, though such foods are not preferred when normal feed is available.

## Behaviour

Horse behaviour shows instinct, memory, judgment, and a strong flight response. Foals stand soon after birth and can follow their mothers within hours. Horses rarely attack unless flight is impossible or they have been brutally treated. Habit governs many reactions, while smell, hearing, and directional sense help them return to stables even after long absence.

Training depends on memory and consistency. The same word and tone can become linked with a desired movement, and intelligent horses may anticipate a rider's wishes. Many horses sense a rider's uncertainty or fear, while highbred animals may show confidence, obedience, jealousy, or resistance. Individual temperament matters because instinct is shared by the species but expressed differently in each animal.

## Breeds

In time, two broad breed groups emerged: the southerly Arab-Barb types and the northerly cold-blooded types. Modern breeds may be classified by origin, use, appearance, or size. Light, fast, spirited breeds are typified by the Arabian; heavy, slower working breeds by the Belgian; and intermediate breeds by the Thoroughbred.

## Arabian

The Arabian ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arabian_Peninsula)) breed, developed in Arabia by the 7th century CE, is prized for stamina, intelligence, and character. It is compact, with a small head, wide nostrils, marked withers, short back, strong legs, fine hooves, and silky coat, mane, and tail. Many colors occur, though gray is common, and important breeding centers include Najd and the United States.

## Thoroughbred

The English Thoroughbred developed through long selective breeding for racing. Arab and Barb horses entered England early, and later the Royal Mares and three foundation sires, the Byerly Turk, Darley Arabian, and Godolphin Barb, shaped the breed. Thoroughbreds have a fine head, deep chest, straight back, long easy stride, and coats usually bay or chestnut.

## Asian

Asian breeds were strongly influenced by Arabian, Persian, and steppe horses, producing small, plain-looking animals of intelligence and endurance. They include the Turkoman, Akhal-Teke, Tartar, Kirghis, Mongol, and Cossack horses. The Orlov trotter ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orlov_Trotter)) was produced in 1778 from a Persian stallion and a Dutch mare at Count Orlov's stud farm.

## Anglo-Arab

The Anglo-Arab originated in France by crossing English Thoroughbreds with pure Arabians. It is larger than the Arabian, smaller than the Thoroughbred, easy to maintain, and able to carry considerable saddle weight. Chestnut and bay are common coat colors.

## American breeds

American breeds include harness racers, sprinting horses, ranch horses, color breeds, and gaited riding horses. The Standardbred, developed in the 19th century and traced largely to Messenger, excels at pacing and trotting. The American Quarter Horse, linked to Janus, was bred for quarter-mile racing and became useful for polo and ranch work. The Morgan, descended from a stallion owned by Justin Morgan, became a versatile riding, carriage, farm, army, and cattle horse.

The Appaloosa is a color breed associated with spotted patterns and with horses descended from Spanish animals in North America. Gaited American breeds include the American Saddlebred, trained for three or five gaits, the Tennessee Walking Horse, known for the running walk, and the Missouri Fox Trotting Horse, whose fox-trot suits rough Ozark terrain.

## Other light breeds

Other light breeds include the English Hackney, Cleveland Bay, Yorkshire Coach horse, Holstein, Hanoverian, East Prussian or Trakehner, Andalusian, and Lipizzaner. Some were bred for carriage, riding, sport, or academic horsemanship. The Lipizzaner, linked to a stud farm near Trieste founded in 1580, became famous for graceful training at the Spanish Riding School of Vienna.

## Heavy breeds

Heavy breeds descended from medieval war horses and were used for heavy loads and farm labor before tractors replaced most of their work. They include the English Shire, Suffolk, Clydesdale, French Percheron, Belgian horse, German Noriker, and Austrian Pinzgauer. These horses usually stand well over 16 hands, and some exceed 19 hands.

## Ponies

Ponies are generally horses under 14.2 hands, excluding Arabians. They are sturdy, intelligent, energetic, sometimes stubborn, and useful for carriages, pack loads, children's riding, and pets. Varieties include Welsh, Dartmoor, Exmoor, Connemara, New Forest, Highland, Dale, Fell, Shetland, Iceland, Norwegian, Indian, Java, Manila, Argentina, and the mountain Haflinger of South Tyrol.

## Evolution of the horse

The evolution of the horse is one of the best documented lineages in paleontology. The horse family began during the Eocene Epoch, about 55.8 million to 33.9 million years ago, with Hyracotherium, commonly called Eohippus. This small browsing animal, found in North America and Europe, had padded feet with four functional hooves in front and three behind, a small brain, and teeth suited to a general browsing diet.

The line from Eohippus to modern horses shows increases in size, reduction of toes, loss of footpads, longer legs, fused lower-leg bones, longer muzzle, larger and more complex brain, and high-crowned teeth suited to grazing. This was not a simple straight progression but a branching evolutionary history shaped by changing environments. Modern horses represent the surviving end of a long equid development.

## Key Events & Developments

- about 55.8 million years ago: The horse family began in the Eocene with Hyracotherium, or Eohippus.
- about 6,000 years ago: Horse domestication likely took place among steppe peoples north of the Black and Caspian seas.
- 7th century CE: The Arabian ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arabian_Peninsula)) breed was known to have developed in Arabia.
- 1580: The stud farm associated with the Lipizzaner was founded near Trieste.
- 1689, after 1700, and about 1730: The Byerly Turk, Darley Arabian, and Godolphin Barb entered the Thoroughbred foundation.
- 1778: The Orlov trotter ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orlov_Trotter)) was produced from a Persian stallion and Dutch mare.
- 19th century: American breeds such as the Standardbred developed for specialized racing and harness uses.

- Xenophon ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Xenophon)): Greek writer who recorded the connection between well-trained horses, gods, and heroes.

- George Gaylord Simpson ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Gaylord_Simpson)): Biologist who summarized the horse's survival through running legs and warning eyes.
- Count Orlov ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Count_Orlov)): Owner of the stud farm where the Orlov trotter ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orlov_Trotter)) originated.
- Justin Morgan ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Justin_Morgan)): Vermont owner connected with the founding stallion of the Morgan breed.

## Interesting Facts

- Mustangs that returned to the wild often moved back toward primitive horse traits.
- Gray horses are born dark and become whiter as they age.
- Horse serum has been used to obtain tetanus antitoxin.
- The Shetland pony can be under seven hands high.
