# The History of Western Philosophy (Philosophy, Western)

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> Western philosophy is the historical tradition of systematic reflection on reality, knowledge, reasoning, ethics, politics, and human life. The source follows its development from Greek thought through medieval Christianity and modern debates, showing how changing intellectual institutions and problems reshaped philosophical inquiry.

## Introduction

![[attachments/western_philosophy_001.webp|420]]

*Bust of Socrates*

Western philosophy ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_philosophy)) is the history of philosophical thought from ancient Greece to the modern period. It studies changing ideas about wisdom, reality, knowledge, truth, goodness, beauty, God, nature, society, reason, language, mind, and human life.

Philosophy has meant many things: love of wisdom, understanding the universe, examining moral duties, knowing divine purposes, grounding science, testing human ideas, studying will and consciousness, judging values, and codifying rules of clear thought.

A broad definition treats philosophy as rational, methodical reflection on human experience and on the most general questions that underlie knowledge, action, and value.

Writing philosophical history requires attention to ideas, cultural context, and individual thinkers. Philosophers inherit problems from predecessors, respond to social circumstances, and express their own personalities and situations.

This makes philosophical history a mixed discipline. It can be organized by schools, by recurring problems, or by great philosophers, but each choice highlights something different. Late ancient writers often grouped thinkers into sects, while later historians sometimes treated the sequence as a gallery of major minds. A careful account must move between both habits.

Western philosophy is also shaped by recurring methods. Some periods prized systematic metaphysics, while others stressed dialectic, commentary, experiment, criticism, ordinary language, or historical interpretation. Philosophical schools often defined themselves by their answer to an inherited problem, such as whether knowledge begins in reason or sense experience, whether universals are real, or whether ethics depends on nature, God, happiness, duty, or freedom.

## Pre-Socratic Philosophy

The earliest Greek philosophers were often cosmologists or naturalists. They sought the origin and structure of the physical world without relying on mythological explanation.

Thales of Miletus is traditionally considered the first Greek philosopher because he offered a natural explanation of the world, saying everything came from water. He also used mathematical knowledge for practical problems.

Early thinkers moved from monism, explaining reality by one substance, to pluralism, explaining it by several principles. Their questions concerned matter, change, permanence, number, being, and order.

Parmenides emphasized unchanging being, while Heracleitus stressed change and flux. Pythagoreans linked reality with number and harmony.

## Socrates and the Socratic Schools

[[Socrates]] shifted philosophy toward ethics, self-examination, and the search for definitions. He claimed no wisdom but questioned others to reveal contradictions in their beliefs and actions.

[[Socrates]] held that one must never do wrong and that no one who truly knows the good will act against it. He refused unlawful actions under both Athenian democracy and the Thirty Tyrants.

His refusal made [[Ethics|ethics]] practical rather than merely verbal. He would not support a mass death sentence after Arginusae, and he would not help the Thirty Tyrants seize an innocent citizen. These choices connected inquiry, conscience, law, and courage, which is why his death became a philosophical event rather than only an Athenian trial.

His death in 399 BCE became a defining event for philosophy. It showed the conflict between moral conscience, public opinion, political authority, and philosophical inquiry.

Socratic followers founded different schools. Eucleides of Megara stressed theoretical aspects; Antisthenes and Diogenes founded Cynicism and rejected material wants; Aristippus founded Cyrenaic hedonism, treating pleasure as the good.

## Plato

![[attachments/western_philosophy_002.webp|420]]

*Plato's Academy mosaic*

[[Plato and Platonism (Plato)|Plato]], Socrates' greatest disciple, linked ethics, metaphysics, politics, knowledge, and education. He believed political life could not improve unless philosophers ruled or rulers became philosophers.

[[Plato and Platonism (Plato)|Plato]] reacted to Athenian democracy, the Thirty Tyrants, and Socrates' execution. His dialogues attack rhetoric, demagogy, moral confusion, and unstable opinion.

The theory of Forms holds that changing sensible things participate in eternal intelligible realities. Knowledge aims beyond appearances toward truth, goodness, beauty, justice, and being.

In [[The History of Western Political Philosophy (Political Philosophy)|political philosophy]], Plato sought the education of the soul and the city. The Republic presents philosopher-rulers, ordered classes, justice as harmony, and the Form of the Good as the highest principle.

## Aristotle

[[Aristotle and Aristotelianism|Aristotle]], Plato's student, founded the Lyceum after teaching Alexander the Great. He rejected a separate realm of Forms and studied beings through substance, form, matter, cause, purpose, and change.

Aristotle invented formal logic ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logic)) and analyzed arguments used in Socratic and philosophical discussion. His works on categories, interpretation, analytics, topics, and fallacies shaped logic for centuries.

His philosophy was encyclopedic: metaphysics, biology, ethics, politics, psychology, rhetoric, poetics, and natural science. He grounded knowledge in experience while seeking universal causes and forms.

In ethics, Aristotle treated happiness as eudaimonia, flourishing through virtue, practical wisdom, friendship, and rational activity. In politics, he viewed humans as political animals and studied constitutions comparatively.

## Hellenistic Philosophy

Stoicism, founded by Zeno of Citium, taught that happiness lies in living according to nature and virtue. External things such as wealth, health, pain, and death are indifferent, though some are preferable.

Stoics emphasized reason, self-control, providence, duty, and freedom from destructive passions. They became advisers of rulers and later influenced Roman ethics and Christian thought.

Epicureanism treated pleasure as the good but meant simple, stable pleasure, freedom from fear, friendship, and peace of mind. Epicurus taught that gods do not intervene in human affairs and that philosophy frees people from religious terror.

Skepticism, associated with Pyrrhon and later Sextus Empiricus, denied certainty and attacked dogmatic claims. Sextus preserved much knowledge of Hellenistic philosophy by quoting doctrines he sought to refute.

Neoplatonism, founded by Plotinus and inspired by Plato, taught the soul's ascent from the sensible world to the One. It shaped late ancient pagan philosophy, Christian theology, Islamic philosophy, and medieval mysticism.

## Early Medieval Philosophy

The early Middle Ages preserved philosophy through Christian thinkers, monasteries, and Latin learning. Philosophy became closely tied to theology, scripture, and the problem of faith and reason.

[[Augustine]] was deeply shaped by Neoplatonism. He identified eternal Truth with God and argued that necessary truths of mathematics and ethics require divine illumination.

Augustine analyzed [[Memory|memory]], knowledge, love, will, perception, sin, grace, time, and history. The City of God presents history as a drama between earthly and heavenly cities.

Boethius transmitted Aristotle's logic to the Latin Middle Ages and raised the problem of universals. His Consolation of Philosophy treated providence, fate, chance, happiness, and divine foreknowledge.

Greek Fathers such as Origen, Gregory of Nyssa, Pseudo-Dionysius, and Maximus the Confessor transmitted Christian Neoplatonism, later influencing John Scotus Erigena and medieval mystical thought.

## Anselm and Scholasticism

Anselm combined faith and reason. He held that faith comes first, but reason should seek understanding of what faith believes.

His Monologion gave Neoplatonic proofs of God from degrees of goodness, being, and perfection. His Proslogion presented the famous ontological argument: God is that than which nothing greater can be conceived.

The 12th and 13th centuries brought a knowledge explosion through translations of Aristotle, Arabic philosophy, and Greek science. Universities developed lecture, commentary, and disputation as philosophical methods.

Scholastic practice depended on authorities but did not simply repeat them. Augustine, Aristotle, Boethius, Pseudo-Dionysius, Avicenna, and later commentators supplied texts; masters then argued over their meaning, scope, and compatibility. Formal disputation trained philosophers to state objections strongly before resolving them, creating a demanding classroom method for theology, logic, science, and metaphysics.

Avicenna influenced Christian thinkers through metaphysics of being, essence, existence, psychology, logic, and natural philosophy. The Book of Causes and other Arabic materials transmitted Neoplatonic and Aristotelian ideas.

## Grosseteste, Bacon, Bonaventure, and Albert

Robert Grosseteste at Oxford emphasized scientific method as both inductive and deductive. He used observation, mathematics, and experiment, especially in optics and natural philosophy.

Roger Bacon popularized experimental science and argued that knowledge requires both reasoning and experience. He envisioned a universal wisdom organized by theology but strengthened by mathematics and experiment.

Bonaventure defended Augustinian and Platonic themes against excessive Aristotelianism. He held that philosophy needs faith's guidance and that all creatures bear marks of the Creator.

Albertus Magnus recognized the value of newly translated Greek and Arabic works, taught Aristotle to his contemporaries, and contributed to botany, zoology, mineralogy, and philosophical synthesis.

## Thomas Aquinas and Late Medieval Thought

![[attachments/western_philosophy_003.webp|420]]

*Thomas Aquinas reading*

Thomas Aquinas used Aristotle to enrich Christian thought while distinguishing philosophy and theology. Reason begins from sense experience and seeks first causes; theology begins from revealed scripture.

Aquinas argued that faith and reason cannot contradict because both come from God. He transformed Aristotle's unmoved mover into the God of Christianity and defended the soul's immortality within an Aristotelian framework.

Latin Averroists at Paris, including Siger de Brabant, followed Aristotle and Averroes even where conclusions seemed to conflict with Christian doctrine. Condemnations in 1277 showed anxiety over rationalism and naturalism.

Duns Scotus opposed the self-sufficiency of philosophy, emphasized God's infinite being, contingency, divine will, and the individuality of things through haecceity. He earned the title Doctor Subtilis.

Scotus also sharpened the medieval debate over will and intellect, making contingency and individual existence more central than in many earlier scholastic systems.

William of Ockham developed nominalism, while Meister Eckhart developed speculative mysticism. Nicholas of Cusa later spoke of learned ignorance and God as the coincidence of opposites.

## Renaissance Humanism

Renaissance humanism ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Renaissance_humanism)) revived classical literature, rhetoric, moral reflection, and the dignity of human beings. It was more literary and moral than narrowly technical philosophy.

Pico della Mirandola's Oration on the Dignity of Man expressed the new stress on human self-creation. Lorenzo Valla used rhetoric and textual criticism against authority.

Marsilio Ficino translated Plato into Latin and led Florentine Platonism. Plato, Plotinus, Stoicism, skepticism, atomism, and Epicureanism all returned through classical recovery.

Humanists such as [[Erasmus]], Thomas More, and [[Montaigne]] emphasized education, moral judgment, irony, skepticism, civic life, and self-reflection.

Humanism gave philosophy a renewed rhetorical and civic voice. It valued persuasion, textual criticism, moral character, and educated judgment, not only syllogistic demonstration. That mattered for later political and scientific writing because dialogue, essay, satire, philological correction, and public controversy became legitimate philosophical instruments.

## Science, Method, and Early Modern Philosophy

![[attachments/western_philosophy_004.webp|420]]

*Portrait of Rene Descartes*

Renaissance science and philosophy rested on experience, mechanism, and mathematics. Leonardo da Vinci formulated principles of empiricism, mechanical science, and mathematical certainty.

[[Francis bacon|Francis Bacon]] made experience and induction central. His tables of presence, absence, and degree aimed to organize facts so causes and forms could be discovered.

Thomas Hobbes built a mechanistic materialism. Reality is matter in motion; philosophy is reasoning by words about causes and effects; politics requires power to restrain human passions and preserve peace.

Rene Descartes founded modern rationalism through methodic doubt, clear and distinct ideas, and the certainty of self-consciousness: I think, therefore I am. He grounded knowledge in God, mind, and mathematical clarity.

Cartesianism dominated much of continental Europe, while Spinoza and Leibniz developed rationalist systems. Spinoza used a geometric method; Leibniz stressed necessary truths, logic, mathematics, and a universe of substances or monads.

## Enlightenment and Empiricism

Enlightenment ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Age_of_Enlightenment)) philosophy was shaped by Newton and Locke. Newton showed the power of mathematical reason in nature, and Locke turned philosophy toward the origin, extent, and certainty of human knowledge.

John Locke argued that ideas come from sensation and reflection. His genetic criterion of knowledge replaced Cartesian self-evidence with inquiry into how ideas originate.

Locke distinguished primary qualities, such as extension and motion, from secondary qualities, such as colour and taste. George Berkeley rejected this distinction and reduced matter to perceived qualities, founding phenomenalism.

David Hume pushed empiricism into skepticism. Cause and effect, selfhood, and necessity are not directly perceived but arise from habit, association, and mental custom. His critique created a crisis for Enlightenment thought.

Hume's importance lay in turning Newtonian ambition toward the mind. He wanted a science of human nature, but the result made reason look dependent on custom, expectation, and association. This forced later philosophers to ask whether science, morality, and personal identity could be justified without returning to dogmatic metaphysics.

## Enlightenment Ethics and Politics

Enlightenment philosophy also shaped social science, economics, history, law, aesthetics, and political theory. Turgot, Montesquieu, Vico, Adam Smith, Bentham, Burke, Shaftesbury, Gottsched, and Baumgarten all contributed to specialized fields.

[[Locke]]'s political philosophy denied divine-right monarchy and defended natural rights to life, liberty, and property. Government exists by consent and must serve the common good.

Rousseau radicalized social-contract theory. Natural liberty is exchanged for civil liberty, and legitimate law expresses the general will. Citizens must rebel when government usurps popular sovereignty.

Together, Locke and Rousseau contain central seeds of modern liberalism, representative democracy, civil liberties, equality, and the dignity of human beings, though Rousseau's collectivism differs sharply from Locke's individualism.

## Later Developments

![[attachments/western_philosophy_005.webp|420]]

*Portrait of Immanuel Kant*

The 19th century was philosophically diverse. It discovered the irrational, history, will, society, and culture, preparing later oppositions between logical atomism and phenomenology, logical positivism and existentialism.

Kant responded to Hume by asking how knowledge is possible. German idealism, utilitarianism, positivism, Marxism, pragmatism, existentialism, phenomenology, analytic philosophy, and later language philosophy all developed from problems inherited from earlier periods.

Kant's answer created a critical turn: philosophy no longer simply described objects but examined the conditions under which objects can be known. German idealists then expanded this into accounts of spirit, history, freedom, and culture, while later positivists and empiricists resisted such speculation by demanding closer ties to science and experience.

Kant's critical philosophy argued that the mind actively structures experience through forms and categories. This made him a turning point between Enlightenment empiricism and later German idealism.

Kant also reorganized [[Metaphysics|metaphysics]] by limiting it. Instead of proving supersensible objects directly, philosophy had to analyze the conditions of possible experience and the claims of practical reason. This preserved space for freedom, morality, God, and immortality while denying that they could be known like objects in nature.

[[Hegel and Hegelianism (Hegel)|Hegel]] treated philosophy historically and dialectically, seeing thought, society, and spirit as developing through conflict and resolution. His influence shaped Marxism, existentialism, theology, history, and political theory.

The 19th century also brought utilitarianism through Bentham and Mill, German pessimism through Schopenhauer, religious and existential critique through Kierkegaard, and the challenge of Nietzsche, who attacked morality, metaphysics, and inherited values.

Twentieth-century analytic philosophy emphasized logic, language, science, and conceptual clarity, while phenomenology and existentialism emphasized consciousness, lived experience, freedom, anxiety, embodiment, and meaning.

Both sides still answered older questions. Analytic thinkers asked how propositions, evidence, and concepts work; phenomenologists and existentialists asked how the world is disclosed to lived persons. Their disagreement was partly about method, not simply about subject matter.

This division was never absolute. Pragmatism connected meaning with practical consequences, Marxism linked thought to social and economic life, and later language philosophy made ordinary speech a philosophical source. Across these movements, inherited questions about truth, value, mind, and community survived but were recast through science, history, politics, and language.

Early modern philosophy also changed its literary forms. Renaissance thinkers revived dialogue, while Hobbes, Descartes, and Spinoza used more systematic treatises shaped by mathematical order. The form mattered because philosophical authority increasingly came from method, sequence, demonstration, and clarity rather than inherited office.

The Enlightenment expanded philosophy into social inquiry. Natural rights, civil liberty, political economy, historical development, aesthetics, and moral psychology all became objects of systematic analysis. Newton's success encouraged confidence in reason, while Locke and Hume turned that confidence back upon the mind that reasons.

Later Western philosophy repeatedly returned to crisis: Hume's skepticism about causality, Kant's limits on knowledge, Hegel's historical reason, Marx's social critique, Nietzsche's genealogy of values, and 20th-century disputes over logic, language, existence, power, and interpretation. The tradition is continuous less because it agrees than because it keeps reopening inherited questions under new pressures.

The history of Western philosophy is therefore both chronological and recurring. [[Plato and Platonism (Platonism)|Platonism]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Platonism)) and Aristotelianism, rationalism and empiricism, skepticism and dogmatism, faith and reason, individual and community, nature and freedom continually reappear in new forms.

## Key Events & Developments

- 6th century BCE: Thales gave a natural account of the world.
- 399 BCE: Socrates was executed in Athens.
- 529: Justinian closed pagan philosophical schools.
- 1077-1078: Anselm wrote Monologion and Proslogion.
- 12th-13th centuries: Aristotle and Arabic philosophy entered Latin Europe.
- 1277: Paris condemned 219 rationalist and Aristotelian propositions.
- 1486: Pico wrote the Oration on the Dignity of Man.
- 1632: Galileo published his Dialogue.
- 1690: Locke published Essay Concerning Human Understanding and Two Treatises.
- 1739-1740: Hume published Treatise of Human Nature.
- 1762: Rousseau published The Social Contract.
