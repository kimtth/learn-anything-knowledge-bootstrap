# Lisbon

> [!abstract] Reference navigation

## Portuguese Lisboa

Lisbon, or Lisboa, is the capital, chief port, largest city, and main commercial, political, and tourist centre of [[Portugal]]. It stands on the Tagus River estuary, making it the westernmost capital city in continental Europe.

The city's ancient name, Olisipo or Ulyssipo, was linked by legend to Ulysses or Elisha, but Phoenician settlement is more credible. Lisbon became a base for exploration by the 15th century, was rebuilt after the 1755 earthquake, and was renewed for Expo '98 in 1998.

## Landscape

![[attachments/lisbon_002.webp|420]]

*Lisbon and the Tagus River*

Lisbon lies on the north bank of the Tagus, about 8 miles from the Atlantic entrance. The river widens east of the city into the Mar de Palha, or Sea of Straw, a sunlit bay supporting port trade.

The city rises in terraces from the river and the Mar de Palha toward low hills and the Sintra Mountains. Older waterside districts look over the river and southern cliffs. Faults cross the region, but after the 1755 earthquake, activity has mostly been slight tremors.

## Climate

Lisbon's climate is mild and even, shaped by the Atlantic Ocean and the Gulf Stream. The mean annual temperature is about 17 C, January averages about 10 C, and August rarely exceeds 28 C.

Rainfall is strongly seasonal, with very little summer rain and about 100 mm in winter months. The Tagus also shapes the city's appearance, beginning at the formal river entrance of Commerce Square, whose marble stair, arcades, and statue of King Joseph I introduce the rebuilt Baixa.

The Cidade Baixa, or Lower City, was rebuilt after 1755 under Sebastiao de Carvalho, later the marques de Pombal. Its grid, Rossio, and Avenida da Liberdade contrast with older, hillier districts.

## People

The Lisbon metropolitan area covers only about 3 percent of Portugal's territory but holds more than one-fourth of its population. The city grew in the 1970s through rural migration and the return of Portuguese citizens from African colonies that became independent in 1975.

Immigration later made Lisbon more cosmopolitan. By the mid-1990s, Africans, especially people from Cape Verde, were the largest immigrant group, joined by residents from [[Brazil]], Angola, Guinea-Bissau, European countries, and smaller South Asian communities.

## Economy

Lisbon's economy began with fishing but shifted strongly toward services after the 1970s. Heavy industries were nationalized after the 1975 revolution, later reprivatized in the 1980s, and multinational firms became important in technology and pharmaceuticals.

## Manufacturing

Older heavy industry such as shipbuilding, steelworks, and oil refining declined or became obsolete. Modernized plants produced automotive parts, chemicals, electronics, tobacco, paper, and foodstuffs, while foreign-owned automotive assembly and food plants opened in new metropolitan industrial centres.

Traditional cork and textile industries stayed competitive through innovation. Services employed more than three-fourths of the labour force, and tourism, commerce, banking, and foreign corporations expanded after Portugal entered the European Economic Community in 1986.

Expo '98 changed Lisbon's image by renewing derelict waterfront areas. Slaughterhouses, waste centres, and oil refineries gave way to museums, hotels, housing, marinas, an oceanarium, and entertainment venues along the Tagus.

## Transportation

Lisbon is linked by rail and road to the Portuguese interior and Europe. The 25th of April Bridge, opened in the mid-1960s, became the main road entry, while the cable-stayed Vasco da Gama Bridge, opened for Expo '98, connected the city and eastern metropolitan area to the southern shore and eased congestion.

Transport improvements in the 1990s included a new subway line, refurbished historic trolleys, and modernization of Portela de Sacavem airport. New highways and parking increased automobile use, traffic, and pollution.

## Government

As capital, Lisbon houses Portugal's main government institutions. The city is divided into concelhos, or municipalities, which are divided into bairros and freguesias, or parishes.

Local representatives are elected to parish assemblies for four-year terms, and those assemblies choose parish committees. Lisbon's municipal assembly combines parish representatives with directly elected members and elects the municipal council and mayor.

## Health

As a major urban centre, Lisbon has a higher share of doctors and health professionals than the rest of Portugal. Its hospitals include state, private, and military institutions.

Since the 1990s, public hospitals have been modernized, and branches of larger hospitals have opened in other city areas. This network reflects Lisbon's role as national capital and metropolitan service centre.

## Education

Education in [[Portugal]] is free and compulsory for children ages 6 through 15. Lisbon has public schools and many private schools, including American, French, German, Spanish, and Swedish schools.

## Universities

The medieval University of Lisbon was founded in 1288, moved between Lisbon and Coimbra, and settled permanently in Coimbra from 1537. Lisbon did not regain a university until 1911, followed by the Technical University in 1930, the Catholic University of Portugal in 1968, and the New University in the 1970s.

University education before the 1974 coup was largely limited to the elite. Applications rose in the 1990s, then declined as many Portuguese students studied abroad and some immigrants entered the workforce.

Cultural life expanded through Expo preparations. The Belem Cultural Centre, opened in 1992, the Calouste Gulbenkian Foundation and Museum, and Culturgest added performance, exhibitions, research, music, ballet, and visual arts.

## History

![[attachments/lisbon_001.webp|420]]

*Lisboa - Portugal (52597836992)*

The valley at Lisbon's core was once a forked branch of the Tagus. The first residents are unknown, but a Phoenician trading station about 1200 BCE is possible. Under the Romans, from 205 BCE to about 409 CE, Julius Caesar made the settlement a municipium called Felicitas Julia.

After Roman rule, the area passed through the Alani, Suebi, and Visigoths. The Moors took Lisbon in the 8th century and remained for 433 years, despite attacks by Normans and by Alfonso VI of Castile and Leon.

In 1147, Crusader forces and the Portuguese under Afonso I captured Lisbon. The Moorish alcazar became a royal palace, the cathedral was linked by legend to a converted mosque, and the city became increasingly central to the Portuguese kingdom.

## The Age of Discovery

![[attachments/lisbon_003.webp|420]]

*Belem Tower in Lisbon*

The first Portuguese census of 1527 counted 65,000 people in Lisbon. After Vasco da Gama reached India in 1498, Lisbon broke the Venetian monopoly on Oriental trade and attracted German, Flemish, Dutch, English, French, Greek, Lombard, and Genoese merchants.

Under King Manuel I, Portugal developed the ornate Manueline style. The Tower of Belem, built in 1515, and the Jeronimos Monastery, begun in 1502, became Lisbon's most important monuments and later World Heritage landmarks.

## Spanish Rule and Rebuilding

King Sebastian's death in Morocco in 1578 led to Spanish rule when Philip II became king of both countries in 1580. The Invincible Armada sailed from Lisbon in 1588, and the city benefited for a time from riches of the Spanish Main.

A conspiracy of Lisbon nobles restored Portuguese independence in 1640. British influence later grew through the 1654 Cromwellian treaty and the British Factory, which negotiated trade privileges until 1810.

Brazilian gold, diamonds, plantations, manufacturing, aqueduct construction, and churches brought optimism in the early 18th century. On November 1, 1755, earthquakes, a tsunami, and fires devastated Lisbon; Joseph I's minister, the marques de Pombal, directed the practical rebuilding that created the modern Baixa.

## Interesting Facts

- Lisbon is the westernmost capital city in continental Europe.
- The Mar de Palha means Sea of Straw, named for the way the bay shimmers in sunlight.
- The Tower of Belem was built in the Tagus as a fort, but the river later changed course.
- The 1755 disaster struck on All Saints' Day when churches were crowded.
- Expo '98 caused the largest renewal project in Lisbon since the Pombaline rebuilding.
