# Prague

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> Prague is the Czech capital whose Vltava setting, layered Romanesque-to-modern architecture, and thousand-year Bohemian history make it a major Central European city. Its historic core, intellectual institutions, music, literature, and recurrent role in religious and political conflict connect urban identity with national history.

## Czech Praha

![[attachments/prague_001.webp|420]]

*Charles Bridge and Prague Castle above the Vltava*

Prague ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prague)), Czech Praha, is the capital of the Czech Republic and one of the great cities of central Europe. It lies on the Vltava River and serves as the major Czech economic and cultural centre. Its architecture reflects more than 1,000 years of Bohemian history and gives the city its famous identity as the city of a hundred spires.

The city's most visible landmarks include Charles Bridge, the Vltava's changing views, and Prague Castle at Hradcany, which dominates the left bank from a high hill. Older quarters with narrow streets, taverns, and restaurants contrast with Wenceslas Square, modern parks, and housing developments. Baroque palaces add another layer of splendour.

After 1945, planning helped preserve Prague's ancient core while supervising modern construction. In 1992, the historic city centre was added to UNESCO's World Heritage List. This protected status reflects the concentration of Romanesque, Gothic, Baroque, Rococo, Classical, Neoclassical, and modern monuments in one urban landscape.

Prague is also famous for cultural life ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Culture)). Wolfgang Amadeus Mozart lived there, and his Prague Symphony and Don Giovanni were first performed in the city. Czech composers Bedrich Smetana, Antonin Dvorak, and Leos Janacek are celebrated in the annual spring music festival. Jaroslav Hasek's Schweik and Franz Kafka's modern dilemmas are also inseparable from Prague's literary image.

## Physical and Human Geography

![[attachments/prague_003.webp|420]]

*Panorama of Prague along the Vltava River*

From small riverside settlements, Prague ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prague)) spread over hills, river valleys, and terraces. The metropolitan area covers 192 square miles, or 496 square kilometres. Its core contains historic buildings, bridges, museums, employment, and traffic congestion, while surrounding zones combine industry, housing, new development, parks, recreation, sports facilities, agriculture, and open countryside.

The city ranges from 623 feet above sea level to 1,247 feet on White Mountain. Its [[Climate and Weather (Climate)|climate]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Climate)) is mid-continental, with cold winters and warm summers. Prague's physical growth reflects both the Vltava valley and the pressures of modern urban expansion.

## Transportation

Inner-city [[Transportation|transportation]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transport)) depends heavily on buses, trams, and the subway, or metro. These services are subsidized and relatively inexpensive. Growth in passenger cars and commercial vehicles has created pressure for a major urban motorway system with radial roads connecting Prague to the national network.

Prague is one of the country's main railway junctions, with three main stations and three freight circuits. The international airport at Ruzyne was expanded and modernized in the 1960s to serve central Europe. A new port was built where the Vltava and Berounka rivers meet, and summer passenger boats on the Vltava became a tourist attraction.

## Administration and Social Conditions

Prague is the seat of government for the Czech Republic and the administrative centre for the Central Bohemian Region. Municipal and regional committees historically coordinated town planning, environmental control, and other projects. The city is divided into districts, each with its own local committee structure.

Municipal services such as gas, electricity, water, sewage, and refuse disposal were consolidated after World War II and modernized through urban planning. Authorities also expanded nurseries and recreation facilities.

Housing has been a persistent urban problem. Much inner-city housing consisted of small apartments needing renovation, while new construction lagged behind demand. Large peripheral developments such as North Town, South Town, and Southwest Town were built to respond to shortages.

## Education and Health

Prague's most famous educational institution is Charles University, founded in 1348 and the oldest university in central Europe. The Academy of Arts, the Academy of Music, and the conservatory founded in 1811 are also important. The Czech Academy of Sciences, founded in 1952 as the Czechoslovak Academy of Sciences, sponsors research and international congresses.

The city has been linked with scholars such as Tycho Brahe, Johannes Kepler, and Albert Einstein, who taught there. As the capital, Prague also contains major hospitals, specialized clinics, and outpatient clinics, including noted facilities for plastic surgery, orthopedics, and urology.

## Cultural Life

![[attachments/prague_002.webp|420]]

*Old Town Square in Prague viewed from the Old Town Hall Tower*

Prague's musical life is internationally known. The Prague Symphony and Czech Philharmonic have strong reputations, and the annual spring festival is a high point. The city also has more than 20 theatres, museums, galleries, and a Palace of Culture completed in 1981.

The city contains about 2,000 officially recognized monuments. Important Romanesque and Gothic sites include the Church of St. George, St. Vitus's Cathedral, Tyn Church, the Powder Tower, Bethlehem Chapel, St. Agnes Convent, the Old-New Synagogue, and the Old Jewish Cemetery. Baroque treasures include Valdstejn and Clam-Gallas palaces, St. Nicholas Church, and the Antonin Dvorak Museum.

## History

The Prague region was crossed for thousands of years by trade routes linking northern and southern Europe. Paleolithic relics, Neolithic farmers, Celtic settlements, Slavs, and Avars all preceded the first Prague settlement, traced to the second half of the 9th century. Vysehrad and Hradcany became early fortified centres.

Legend credits Princess Libuse and Premysl with founding Prague, and the Premyslid dynasty ruled from about 800 to 1306. St. Wenceslas was murdered by his brother Boleslav about 939, and Boleslav I strengthened power against German pressure. By 965, Ibrahim ibn Yaqub described Prague as a busy trading centre, and the bishopric was founded in 973.

Medieval expansion produced the Old Town, especially after the first stone bridge, and Prague became central to Bohemian politics. The sermons of Jan Hus at Bethlehem Chapel from 1402 helped make the city important in the Reformation. Hus was burned at Constance in 1415, and popular unrest led to the first Defenestration of Prague in 1419.

Hussite forces led by Jan Zizka won at Vitkov Hill in 1420. Later, the Habsburgs tried to suppress Czech Protestantism. The second Defenestration of Prague in 1618 helped trigger the Thirty Years' War, followed by Protestant defeat at White Mountain in 1620 and executions in Old Town Square in 1621.

Prague declined after war, occupation, and plague, but later recovered. In 1784, the Old Town, New Town, Mala Strana, and Hradcany were united. Modern Prague grew through industrialization, planning, culture, and national government.
