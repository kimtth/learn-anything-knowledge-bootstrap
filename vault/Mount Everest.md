# Mount Everest

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> Mount Everest is Earth's highest peak, located in the Himalayas between Nepal and Tibet. Its tectonic uplift, extreme high-altitude environment, indigenous names and reverence, Survey of India measurement, and evolving mountaineering history connect physical geography with exploration and environmental pressure.

## Introduction

Mount Everest ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mount_Everest)), called Sagarmatha in Sanskrit and Nepali, Chomolungma in Tibetan, and Zhumulangma Feng in Chinese, lies on the crest of the Great Himalayas between [[Nepal]] and the Tibet Autonomous Region of [[China]]. At 29,035 feet, or 8,850 metres, it is the highest point on Earth. Local peoples long revered it; Chomolungma means Goddess Mother of the World or Goddess of the Valley, and Sagarmatha means Ocean Mother. Its status as the world's highest mountain was established by the Survey of India in 1852, and in 1865 Peak XV was renamed for Sir George Everest.

Everest's [[Geomorphic Processes (Geology)|geology]] reflects the collision of the Indian-Australian Plate with the Eurasian Plate about 50 million years ago. The Himalayas began rising about 25 to 30 million years ago, and the Great Himalayas took modern form during the Pleistocene. Everest continues to move northeast and rise slightly each year. Its rocks include folded metamorphic schists and gneisses, granites, and higher sedimentary layers, all shaped by tectonic uplift, erosion, snow, ice, and extreme altitude.

## Habitation

Everest's summit environment cannot support permanent human habitation, but Tibetan-speaking peoples live in the valleys below. The Sherpas of the Khumbu valley traditionally farmed, traded, grazed livestock seasonally, and treated the mountains as sacred. They built Buddhist monasteries, placed prayer flags on slopes, and protected valley wildlife. Because gods, demons, and the Yeti were believed to inhabit the high peaks and lower slopes, Sherpas traditionally did not climb the mountains.

Foreign expeditions changed Sherpa life. Beginning with British exploration in the early 20th century, surveying, portering, guiding, and later trekking and commercial climbing created new livelihoods. Sherpas' high-altitude adaptation made them essential to expeditions, though they were often placed in subordinate roles and not always credited for pioneering achievements. Tourism and climbing brought income but also cultural, economic, and environmental change.

## Environmental Issues

On the Nepalese side, Everest lies within Sagarmatha National Park ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sagarmatha_National_Park)), created in 1976 and designated a UNESCO World Heritage site in 1979. Rhododendron, birch, pine, alpine vegetation, glaciers, snow leopards, lesser pandas, Tibetan bears, and many birds are part of the surrounding ecosystem. Overgrazing, excessive resource use, and mountaineering waste damaged habitats, prompting reforestation and cleanup programs.

Everest's climbing waste includes abandoned tents, cans, crampons, oxygen containers, and human waste. Many supplies have been removed and recycled or discarded, but bodies of climbers who died high on the mountain usually remain because recovery is dangerous and difficult. Eco Everest Expeditions, beginning in 2008, publicized cleanup and climate concerns, including melting in the Khumbu Icefall.

## History of Exploration and Mountaineering

Everest is difficult to reach and more difficult to climb. Before airstrips in the Khumbu valley, goods on the Nepalese side had to be carried by humans and pack animals; higher areas still require foot access. The best climbing window is usually April-May, before the monsoon, with a shorter possible window after the monsoon in September. Above about 25,000 feet, the death zone ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Death_zone)) causes hypoxia, rapid pulse and breathing, poor digestion, poor sleep, confusion, and dangerous judgment.

Traditional Everest climbing used siege climbing: large teams established camps up the mountain, climbed high, slept lower, acclimatized gradually, and supported a summit assault team. The common southern route begins at Base Camp on the Khumbu Glacier, with camps spaced upward toward the South Col and summit. Sherpa logistical support became central. Some climbers, such as Eric Shipton, criticized large oxygen-supported expeditions as less aesthetic than lighter climbing.

## Early Expeditions

British interest in Everest grew from the 1890s through the Royal Geographical Society and the Alpine Club. Tibet was closed to Westerners, but J.B.L. Noel secretly entered in 1913 and saw the summit. In 1921, an expedition under C.K. Howard-Bury explored northern approaches. George Mallory and G.H. Bullock investigated the Rongbuk valleys and identified the North Col route.

The 1922 expedition made the first serious summit attempts from the north. George Finch and Captain Bruce used oxygen and reached about 27,300 feet, while a later avalanche killed seven Sherpas on the North Col slopes. In 1924, Edward Norton reached 28,100 feet without oxygen, a documented height unsurpassed until 1953. Mallory and Andrew Irvine disappeared on June 8 while attempting the summit; whether they reached it remains unresolved.

## 1930s Reconnaissance and Attempts

The 1933 expedition found Irvine's ice ax and made high attempts along the Northeast Ridge and Great Couloir but turned back. Airplane flights over Everest that year photographed the summit and surrounding landscape. Maurice Wilson, an inexperienced climber, died in 1934 while attempting Everest alone. Reconnaissance in 1935 and expeditions in 1936 and 1938 faced monsoon snow, storms, avalanche danger, and difficult North Col conditions.

## Golden Age of Everest Climbs

After [[World Wars (World War II)|World War II]] and China's takeover of Tibet in 1950, the northern approach closed and Nepal permitted southern reconnaissance. In 1951, Eric Shipton's party, including Edmund Hillary, found a route through the Khumbu Icefall toward the Western Cwm. Swiss expeditions in 1952 tested the South Col route and came close to the summit, with Raymond Lambert and Tenzing Norgay reaching high on the mountain.

The historic 1953 British expedition, led by John Hunt, used careful training, load ferrying, oxygen equipment, and staged camps. Tom Bourdillon and Charles Evans reached the South Summit on May 26 but turned back. On May 29, Edmund Hillary and Tenzing Norgay left their high camp, crossed the South Summit and final ridge, overcame the obstacle later called the Hillary Step, and reached the summit.

## Everest-Lhotse, 1956

In 1956, a Swiss expedition achieved ascents of both Everest and Lhotse. Ernst Reiss and Fritz Luchsinger reached Lhotse on May 18, while Swiss teams reached Everest on May 23 and May 24 using oxygen and a route close to the British South Col line. The expedition showed that the southern route could be repeated and that neighbouring high peaks could be integrated into Himalayan climbing strategy.

## Attempts of 1960 and the U.S. Ascent of 1963

In 1960, an Indian expedition from the south was stopped by wind and weather, while a Chinese expedition claimed the first ascent from the north; its account was doubted at first but later generally accepted. The first American Everest expedition, led by Norman Dyhrenfurth in 1963, combined summit goals with studies in physiology, psychology, glaciology, and meteorology related partly to the U.S. space program.

On May 1, James Whittaker and Nawang Gombu reached the summit by the South Col route. On May 22, William Unsoeld and Thomas Hornbein climbed the previously unclimbed West Ridge and descended by the Southeast Ridge, completing the first major Himalayan traverse. Barry Bishop and Luther Jerstad also summited that day; all four bivouacked high and suffered frostbite.

## Developments Since 1965

In 1965, an Indian expedition led by M.S. Kohli placed nine climbers on the summit, and Nawang Gombu became the first person to climb Everest twice. After Nepal reopened its mountains in 1969, climbers turned toward new routes, especially the Southwest Face and its Rock Band. Japanese expeditions tried in 1969-1970, and a British team led by Chris Bonington succeeded on the Southwest Face in 1975.

Reinhold Messner transformed Everest climbing by emphasizing lighter, bolder ascents. In 1978, he and Peter Habeler made the first ascent without supplemental oxygen. In 1980, Messner made the first solo ascent, from the Tibetan side, showing that Everest could be climbed in a radically different style from siege expeditions.

## Further Exploration From Tibet

After Messner, climbers pursued harder Tibetan-side routes. In 1982, Peter Boardman and Joe Tasker disappeared on the Northeast Ridge. In 1983, an American team climbed the East Face from the Kangshung Glacier, with Carlos Buhler, Lou Reichardt, and Kim Momb summiting first, followed by others. Later teams tried the North Face, Northeast Ridge, and other difficult lines.

## Since 1990

*Climbers on the summit of Mount Everest*

Since the 1990s, commercial Everest climbing has expanded rapidly. Permits increased, north and south routes opened, and multiple expeditions often operated at once, creating traffic jams at narrow places such as the Hillary Step. On May 19, 2012, crowding contributed to dangerous delays and four deaths. Commercial teams depend on fixed ropes, satellite weather forecasts, oxygen, Sherpa support, and client logistics.

Everest also became a site for records and extraordinary feats. Sherpa climbers such as Apa Sherpa and Phurba Tashi reached 21 ascents, while speed records, youngest climber records, and oldest climber records changed repeatedly. Jordan Romero summited at age 13 in 2010, Miura Yuichiro reached the summit at age 80 in 2013, and Watanabe Tamae set women's age records.

## Key Events & Developments

- 1852: The Survey of India identified Everest as Earth's highest point.
- 1921: British reconnaissance explored the northern approaches.
- 1924: Mallory and Irvine disappeared high on Everest.
- 1953: Hillary and Tenzing made the first confirmed ascent.

- George Mallory ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Mallory)): Early British climber who disappeared in 1924.
- Andrew Irvine: Mallory's climbing partner on the fatal 1924 attempt.
- Edmund Hillary ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edmund_Hillary)): First confirmed summiter with Tenzing Norgay in 1953.
- Tenzing Norgay ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tenzing_Norgay)): Sherpa mountaineer and first confirmed summiter with Hillary.
