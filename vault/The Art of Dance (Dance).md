## Introduction

Dance ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dance)) is rhythmic movement of the body, usually to music and within a defined space, used to express ideas or emotions, release energy, or enjoy movement itself. It is both a natural human impulse and an art shaped by training, choreography, and performance.

The art of dance joins two ideas: dance as impulse and dance as skilled composition. People may dance from inner need, ritual demand, social pleasure, or excitement, while professional dancers turn movement into forms that can delight spectators who do not dance themselves.

Writers have defined dance differently according to familiar styles. [[Aristotle and Aristotelianism|Aristotle]] saw dance as rhythmic movement representing character, action, and suffering, reflecting Greek theatre. John Weaver emphasized elegant, regular, harmonious movement, reflecting courtly ballet. John Martin stressed physical expression of inner emotion, reflecting modern dance.

A universal definition must include the body and its movement range. Dance may borrow from work, travel, ritual, or survival movements, but it adds qualities such as self-expression, aesthetic pleasure, entertainment, symbolism, and rhythmic shaping.

## Functions of Dance

Dance has served religious, military, social, educational, recreational, and theatrical functions. In religious dances, performers may enter trance, enact divine stories, imitate sacred beings, or participate in prayer and procession.

War dances and hunting dances have trained bodies, stirred courage, and sought magical power. Warriors might dance with weapons, while hunters might wear animal skins and imitate prey to acquire skill or spiritual force.

Social dance supports celebration, courtship, recreation, and entertainment. Courtship dances allow dancers to display strength, grace, vigour, and attractiveness, while also offering controlled physical contact.

Fertility dances often use sexual symbolism, including leaps, lifts, and mimed gestures. Many traditional social dances also preserve older ritual meanings even when later enjoyed mainly as recreation.

## Historical Attitudes

In ancient [[Greece]], dance was part of drama, education, and civic life. Plato linked good singing and dancing with education, health, beauty, agility, goodness of soul, and balance of mind. Aristotle saw dance as useful for shaping emotion and leisure.

Romans often considered dance decadent or effeminate, and early Christian leaders sometimes repressed pagan dance customs. Yet some Christian communities included processions, circle dances, and line dances in worship, and St. Basil compared sacred dance to angelic movement.

During the Renaissance ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Renaissance)), dance became essential at court. Courtiers needed to move gracefully, and amateur court ballets flourished. In England, dance was popular across classes, though Puritan movements later suppressed exuberant customs.

In the United States, the Shakers developed choreographed worship dances with complex religious symbolism. Over time, folk, social, theatre, swing, ballroom, Irish, Balkan, English country, and urban dance forms gained or regained popularity.

## The Dancer

Dancers are performing artists whose bodies are the medium. They require strength, flexibility, coordination, rhythm, discipline, memory, expressiveness, and often intense training.

In many cultures, sacred or formal dances required special training. Errors could be treated seriously because dance was linked to prayer, magic, or communal success. Some religious dancers formed special castes, followed taboos, and underwent spiritual discipline.

In Europe, professional dance was long associated with wandering performers of low status, while early court ballets were danced by amateurs. Louis XIV recognized that dance needed trained professionals and helped establish standards for ballet training.

## Dance Technique

Classical ballet uses turnout, pointed feet, erect posture, controlled arms, high leg extensions, jumps, turns, balances, and pointe work. Partner work, or adagio, includes lifts, supported balances, and pirouettes.

Ballet compositions use enchainements, or combinations, and often arrange dancers in lines, circles, symmetrical groupings, pas de deux, solos, group dances, and codas.

Modern dance uses some classical positions but changes their style. It may use turned-in legs, flexed feet, loose feet, twisting torsos, crouching, rolls, falls, floor work, and ordinary actions such as walking and running.

Folk dance often uses basic hops, skips, running steps, gallops, pivots, lifts, relaxed arms, simple swaying, and group formations. Its technical simplicity can still support rich rhythm, community meaning, and regional style.

## Choreography

Choreography is the art of making dances by gathering and organizing movement into order and pattern. Western theatre dances are often credited to single choreographers, while social, folk, and many non-Western dances evolve through group innovation and tradition.

Choreographers may create for function, such as celebration, opera, ritual, or prayer, or from stimuli such as music, painting, literature, emotion, a concept, a movement idea, or a particular dancer.

Dance structure may follow [[Musical Forms and Genres (Musical Form)|musical forms]], plot, theme, repetition, contrast, floor pattern, dancer number, spatial design, or organic development of movement ideas. Even narrative ballets often use formal motifs that work both dramatically and visually.

Choreographers vary rhythm to create movement qualities. Slow even rhythms may suggest softness and fluidity; fast asymmetrical rhythms may create tension, excitement, or anguish. Dance rhythms may follow or resist the natural rhythm of movements.

## Music and Design

Most dance is accompanied by [[The Art of Music (Music)|music]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Music)), and terms such as tempo, dynamics, and beat come from music. Music may determine a dance's length, structure, phrasing, mood, character, or dramatic meaning.

Choreographers use music differently. Ruth St. Denis visualized music, George Balanchine translated music into spatial terms, and Vaslav Nijinsky used Debussy's music in Afternoon of a Faun mainly for atmosphere.

Dance may also use silence, spoken text, natural sound, electronic sound, popular music, non-Western music, or experimental scores. Modern dance and contemporary ballet often mix musical traditions.

Visual design includes costume, makeup, masks, props, lighting, and stage sets. These elements can clarify character, mood, period, ritual power, social identity, or formal shape.

In ritual dances, clothing, body paint, weapons, animal skins, feathers, masks, and symbolic colours may have magical or religious meaning. In theatre dance, design became increasingly integrated with choreography, especially through reformers such as Michel Fokine and collaborators such as Leon Bakst and Alexandre Benois.

## Drama and Abstraction

Dance often lies between dramatic expression and formal pattern. Dramatic dance tells stories, expresses character, imitates animals, enacts courtship, or communicates emotion through gesture and movement.

Movement itself carries expressive force: stamping can suggest aggression, jumping exhilaration, dragging despair. Mime can be realistic or symbolic, such as pointing to a ring finger to mean marriage.

Formal dance stresses line, rhythm, spatial pattern, symmetry, group design, and movement invention. Even dramatic ballets are stylized, and even abstract dances may suggest mood or meaning.

Modern theatre experiments blurred dance, mime, acting, singing, film, visual art, and conceptual performance. Avant-garde works often avoided chronological narrative and mixed reality, symbolism, and fantasy.

## Ballet

![[attachments/the_art_of_dance_dance_001.webp|420]]

*Ballet dancer foot en pointe*

Ballet has dominated Western theatre dance since the 17th century. Its movement style grew from court dances of the 16th and 17th centuries and is based on turnout, verticality, pointe work, symmetry, controlled posture, and graceful extension.

Classical ballet often values lightness, height, speed, balance, and clear lines. Women may dance en pointe, and dancers frequently move upward rather than close to the floor.

Ballet design is often symmetrical, with formal groupings around principal dancers. Reformers such as Jean-Georges Noverre and Michel Fokine argued that rigid symmetry could weaken dramatic truth.

Fokine's Ballets Russes works showed ballet's stylistic range. Chopiniana used soft lyricism; Prince Igor and The Firebird absorbed Russian folk energy; he favoured shorter works shaped by theme rather than padded full-length spectacle.

Vaslav Nijinsky and Bronislava Nijinska made more radical ballets. Jeux used modern dress and sport imagery; The Rite of Spring used turned-in legs, massed groups, heavy arms, and harsh primitive movement; Les Noces used crouched, weighted bodies and blocklike groupings.

## Modern and Postmodern Dance

Modern dance developed in the early 20th century as a reaction against ballet's formalism and light subject matter. Isadora Duncan rejected ballet as unnatural and sought movement inspired by nature, antique sculpture, flowing rhythm, bare feet, and free tunics.

Modern choreographers such as Martha Graham and Doris Humphrey made the whole body expressive. Graham emphasized the back, pelvis, spirals, contractions, and inner conflict. Humphrey described movement through fall and recovery.

Merce Cunningham reacted against expressionism by making dance about movement, rhythm, phrasing, space, and structure rather than narrative or emotion. Music, design, and movement could be created independently, leaving spectators to relate them freely.

Postmodern dance ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Postmodern_dance)) in the 1960s and 1970s pushed further toward ordinary movement, simple tasks, walking, running, rolling, skipping, repetition, accumulation, street clothes, galleries, lofts, outdoor spaces, and reduced spectacle.

## Indian Classical Dance and Asian Forms

![[attachments/the_art_of_dance_dance_002.webp|420]]

*Bharatanatyam dancer performing with hand gestures and ankle bells*

Indian classical dance developed from religious ritual and includes natya, or dramatic imitation; nritta, or pure dance; and nritya, or mood expressed through face, hands, legs, and feet.

Indian dance differs from ballet by using bent legs, flat feet, low jumps, elaborate stamping, mobile head and torso, expressive eyes, and complex hand gestures. Bells on the ankles add rhythmic accompaniment.

Asian dance traditions often use symbolic objects. In Japan, a fan can suggest a book, falling leaves, or the rising Moon. In Java, dancers use impassive faces, elaborate hands, and scarves to create floating qualities.

## Tribal, Ethnic, Folk, and Social Dance

Tribal dance belongs to self-contained communities and may be participatory, ritual, ceremonial, or identity-preserving. Categories include weapon, fertility, Sun and Moon, initiation, war, hunting, and worship dances.

Many tribal dances have declined or transformed, but some survive among communities such as the Hopi. They cannot simply be treated as unchanged evidence of ancient dance, because all traditions develop over time.

Ethnic dance is characteristic of a cultural group. Flamenco, Indian dances, the polka, and many regional forms may be called ethnic, though some are theatrical, some social, and some classical.

European folk dances often developed from older ritual dances. The Maypole dance preserves tree symbolism, while morris dance may descend from weapon or worship dances. Collectors such as Cecil Sharp and Bela Bartok helped revive some national dances.

Social dance moved from group formations to couples in forms such as the waltz and polka. In the 20th century, tango, fox-trot, Charleston, Black Bottom, swing, ballroom, jazz, break-dancing, and music-video styles expanded social dance vocabulary.

African American influence brought syncopation, bent knees, crouched torsos, pelvic and hip movement, improvisation, and freer individual dancing into Western popular dance.

## Key Events & Developments

- 1721: John Weaver defined dance through elegant and harmonious courtly movement.
- 18th-19th centuries: The Shakers developed choreographed religious dances in the United States.
- 1908-1910: Fokine's Ballets Russes works expanded ballet's expressive range.
- 1912: Nijinsky's Afternoon of a Faun used music for mood and a radically stylized movement world.
- 1913: The Rite of Spring shocked audiences with primitive, anti-classical movement.
- 1960s-1970s: Postmodern dance emphasized ordinary movement, task, space, time, and bodily weight.
