# Adam Smith

> [!abstract] Reference navigation

## Introduction

Adam Smith, baptized June 5, 1723 in Kirkcaldy, Fife, Scotland and died July 17, 1790 in [[Edinburgh]], was a Scottish social philosopher and political economist. He is best known for An Inquiry into the Nature and Causes of the Wealth of Nations, published in 1776, but he was more than an economist. His economic ideas were part of a wider view of moral life, law, government, and social change.

Smith was the son of a customs official and Margaret Douglas. He studied first in Kirkcaldy, then entered the University of Glasgow at age 14 in 1737. There he was shaped by Francis Hutcheson, a professor of moral philosophy. In 1740 he won the Snell Exhibition ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Snell_Exhibition)) and went to Balliol College, Oxford, where he found the teaching poor and educated himself through wide reading.

## Edinburgh Lectures

After returning from Oxford, Smith gave public lectures in [[Edinburgh]] with support from Lord Henry Kames. These lectures covered [[Rhetoric|rhetoric]], history, and economics, and they impressed important listeners. This period helped him move from private study into public intellectual life.

## Glasgow

In 1751, Smith became professor of logic at the University of Glasgow. In 1752 he moved to the chair of moral philosophy, where he stayed for twelve years. He later called this the happiest and most honourable period of his life. He lectured in English rather than Latin, taught demanding material to young students, and also took part in university administration, becoming dean of faculty in 1758.

At Glasgow, Smith lived in a rich intellectual and commercial setting. His circle included Joseph Black, James Watt, Robert Foulis, and his close friend David Hume. He also met merchants active in the colonial trade after the union of Scotland and England in 1707. From figures such as Andrew Cochrane, he learned practical details about trade and business that later gave The Wealth of Nations ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Wealth_of_Nations)) a strong sense of the real world.

## The Theory of Moral Sentiments

In 1759, Smith published The Theory of Moral Sentiments ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Theory_of_Moral_Sentiments)). The book explains how people make moral judgments even though they are driven by self-interest and self-preservation. His answer is the idea of an impartial spectator, an inner judge that approves or condemns actions and helps people regulate their passions.

The work also presents humans as both passionate and social. For Smith, people compete with one another, but they also have reason and sympathy. These powers allow them to build institutions that reduce conflict and sometimes turn private motives toward public benefit. The book contains an early version of the idea later called the invisible hand, where self-seeking action can unintentionally advance the interest of society.

## The Wealth of Nations

Although The Wealth of Nations ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Wealth_of_Nations)) is famous as the first great work of political economy, it is better understood as a continuation of The Theory of Moral Sentiments ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Theory_of_Moral_Sentiments)). It asks how the struggle between passion, reason, and moral judgment works in history and society. Smith describes four broad stages of social organization: hunters, nomadic agriculture, feudal or manorial farming, and commercial interdependence.

Each stage has institutions suited to its needs. In a hunting society there is little property and little regular government. With flocks and settled property, law and order become more important. Smith did not simply praise property as natural; he saw civil government as protecting property and therefore defending the rich against the poor. His historical analysis links economic forms with law, class power, and political institutions.

In commercial society, Smith argued that markets, division of labour, and exchange could greatly increase wealth, but he also criticized monopoly, bad policy, and restrictions that distorted economic life. His larger point was that social order can develop from many individual actions, yet it still depends on institutions, justice, and government policy.

## Assessment

Much more is known about Smith's thought than about his private life. He never married, and many papers were destroyed, including some as late as 1942. Contemporary accounts describe him as absent-minded, awkward in speech before warming to a subject, and unusual in manner, but also kind, tactful, and respected.

Smith gained fame during his own lifetime. Students came to Glasgow from distant places, and later European thinkers and British officials admired his work. His reputation as a social philosopher has remained strong because of the range of his knowledge, the force of his general ideas, and the boldness of his historical vision.

## Key Events & Developments

- 1723: Adam Smith is born in Kirkcaldy.
- 1737: Smith enters the University of Glasgow and studies under Francis Hutcheson.
- 1740: Smith wins the Snell Exhibition ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Snell_Exhibition)) and studies at Oxford.
- 1751-1752: Smith takes the logic chair and then the moral philosophy chair at Glasgow.
- 1759: Smith publishes The Theory of Moral Sentiments ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Theory_of_Moral_Sentiments)).
- 1776: Smith publishes The Wealth of Nations ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Wealth_of_Nations)).

## Key People

- Adam Smith ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adam_Smith)): Scottish social philosopher and political economist whose works joined moral theory, history, law, and economics.
- Francis Hutcheson ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Francis_Hutcheson_%28philosopher%29)): Smith's Glasgow teacher and an important early influence.
- David Hume ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Hume)): Philosopher and lifelong friend in Smith's intellectual circle.
- Andrew Cochrane ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andrew_Cochrane)): Glasgow merchant whose commercial experience helped inform Smith's understanding of trade.

## Interesting Facts

- As a child, Smith was said to have been briefly carried off and then abandoned.
- At Oxford, Smith relied heavily on self-education because he found the university much less stimulating than Glasgow.
- Smith once joked that he was a beau in nothing but his books, referring to his library of about 3,000 volumes.
