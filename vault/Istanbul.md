# Istanbul

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> Istanbul, on the Bosporus between Europe and Asia, succeeded Constantinople as a major Byzantine and Ottoman imperial city. Its walls, churches, mosques, urban landscape, commerce, and transport record its role as a crossroads of imperial power and cultural exchange.

## Introduction

Istanbul ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Istanbul)), formerly Constantinople and ancient Byzantium, is Turkey's largest city and seaport. It was the capital of the Byzantine Empire, the Ottoman Empire, and, until 1923, the Turkish Republic. Its old walled city stands on a triangular peninsula between Europe and Asia, making it both bridge and barrier among religions, cultures, trade routes, and empires for more than 2,500 years.

The city's names reflect its long history. Byzantium may derive from Byzas, the legendary Megarian founder who took the peninsula from Thracian peoples about 657 BCE. [[Constantine the Great]] dedicated it in 330 CE as New Rome, but Constantinopolis became its lasting imperial name. Istanbul developed from a Greek phrase meaning in the city and became official in Turkish postal use in 1930.

## Landscape and Climate

![[attachments/istanbul_001.webp|420]]

*Historical peninsula and modern skyline of Istanbul*

The old city covers about 9 square miles on a peninsula washed by the Golden Horn, the Bosporus, and the Sea of Marmara. Its seven hills made it an apt New Rome for Constantine. Many mosques and historic landmarks on those hills were collectively designated a UNESCO World Heritage site in 1985.

The Golden Horn ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Golden_Horn)) separates old Stamboul from Beyoglu, while the Bosporus divides European Istanbul from Asian districts such as Uskudar and Kadikoy. Black Sea water flows south through the Bosporus, while a warmer, saltier Mediterranean undercurrent pushes north beneath it.

Climate and nature are shaped by winds and water. The poyraz blows from the Black Sea, the winter karayel can freeze the Golden Horn and Bosporus, and the lodos can raise storms on the Sea of Marmara. Fire, earthquake, riot, and invasion repeatedly damaged the city, leaving rebuilt avenues, old alleys, wooden houses, and surviving walls side by side.

## Walls, Byzantine Monuments, and Churches

![[attachments/istanbul_002.webp|420]]

*Galata Tower in Istanbul*

The land walls isolate the peninsula from the mainland and were breached only once, by the cannon of Mehmed II in 1453. Their inner and outer ramparts, moat, towers, and gates made Constantinople one of the world's hardest cities to conquer. Sea walls once protected the Golden Horn and the Sea of Marmara sides.

Byzantine monuments include Constantine's Burnt Column, the Hippodrome's remaining curved end, the obelisk of Theodosius I, the masonry obelisk of Constantine VII, the serpent column from Plataea, the Valens aqueduct, and more than 80 closed cisterns.

The Basilica Cistern, called Yerebatan Saray or Yerebatan Sarnici, is one of Istanbul's most striking Byzantine survivals. Its 336 columns rise from dark water to a vaulted roof near Hagia Sophia.

Hagia Sophia, dedicated to Divine Wisdom, is the greatest surviving Byzantine church. Built and rebuilt across centuries, it took its essential 6th-century form under Justinian. Its dome became one of world architecture's great achievements. It became a mosque in 1453 and a museum in 1935.

Other Byzantine remains include St. Irene, SS. Sergius and Bacchus, the Chora church with its mosaics and frescoes, the Palace of Constantine near the land walls, the Golden Gate, and the Galata Tower built by Genoese traders in 1349.

## Ottoman Architecture and Urban Character

Ottoman Istanbul changed the skyline of the seven hills with domes and minarets. The Ottoman dynasty built major structures from the mid-15th to the mid-16th century, blending Seljuq, Hellenic, and Byzantine traditions.

Mimar Koca Sinan, the greatest Ottoman architect, designed the Suleymaniye Mosque from 1550 to 1557, inspired by but not copied from Hagia Sophia. The Blue Mosque of Ahmed I, famous for its six minarets, became one of the best-known monuments of the city.

## People, Economy, and Transportation

![[attachments/istanbul_003.webp|420]]

*Metropolitian Istanbul - Landscapes of Turkey 12*

Istanbul's population grew through migration from the countryside, especially after World War II. Many migrants built gecekondu settlements with limited sanitation, power, and water. Christian and Jewish minorities declined in proportion and number, while Kurds became the largest ethnic minority.

Industry makes Istanbul Turkey's largest port and industrial hub. Textiles, flour milling, tobacco processing, cement, and glass have been important manufactures, and tourism has become a growing source of income.

[[Transportation]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transport)) joins rail, ferry, bus, maritime, and air systems. Haydarpasa station on the Asian side begins the Baghdad Railway. Ferries cross the harbour and reach the Princes Islands, while Istanbul Ataturk Airport historically provided domestic and international service close to the city centre.

## Government, Services, and Culture

Municipal government descends from Constantine's organization of the city into 14 districts, though modern Istanbul is divided into district municipalities with elected mayors and councils. These bodies manage local budgets, waste removal, and construction permits.

Public services struggled to keep pace with growth. Water supply, sewage disposal, hospitals, and electricity expanded but faced pressure from migration and industrialization. Istanbul University traces its predecessor to 425 and was refounded in 1453, while the city also has technical, fine arts, commercial, and foreign educational institutions.

Cultural life ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Culture)) includes the Ataturk Cultural Center in Taksim Square, municipal and private theatres, opera, ballet, research institutes, learned societies, libraries, museums, and a publishing tradition shaped by both Ottoman and republican eras.

## History

Byzantium began as a Greek colony on the Bosporus and passed through Persian, Athenian, Spartan, Macedonian, and Roman influence. It was rebuilt by Septimius Severus after 196 CE and survived invasions and civil wars before Constantine chose it as imperial capital.

Constantinople ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constantinople)) was inaugurated on May 11, 330. Constantine's decision shifted the Roman centre eastward, created a lasting unification of Greece, and produced a Christian, Roman, and Greek-speaking imperial capital. Its gold solidus remained a monetary standard for more than a thousand years.

The Byzantine capital endured for 1,130 years. It became a centre of imperial power, commerce, religion, wealth, and art, even as imperial territory eventually shrank. Its walls, churches, palace life, and religious authority made it one of the chief cities of the medieval world.

Modern Istanbul emerged from the Ottoman Empire's collapse and the founding of the Turkish Republic. Young Turk forces occupied the city in 1908, the Balkan Wars nearly brought Bulgarian capture, World War I ended with Allied occupation from 1918 to 1923, and Mustafa Kemal Ataturk's Nationalists abolished the sultanate in 1922. Ankara became capital in 1923, but Istanbul remained Turkey's largest city.

Late 20th-century growth transformed the city. Rural migration caused nearly tenfold population increase, straining housing, infrastructure, sanitation, and public services. Earthquake risk became especially serious because rapid, substandard, and unregistered construction raised death tolls, as shown by the 1999 tremor near Istanbul.

## Key Events & Developments

- 657 BCE Byzantium: Greek settlers traditionally founded the city.
- 196 CE Septimius Severus: The Roman emperor destroyed and rebuilt the city.
- 330 CE [[Constantine the Great]]: New Rome was inaugurated as imperial capital.
- 413 and 447 Land walls: Major inner and outer defenses were built.
- 532 Nika Insurrection: Hagia Sophia burned and was rebuilt under Justinian.
- 1453 Mehmed II: Ottoman forces captured Constantinople.
- 1923 Republican shift: Allied occupation ended and Ankara became Turkey's capital.
- 1930 Istanbul ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Istanbul)): The modern name became official in postal usage.
