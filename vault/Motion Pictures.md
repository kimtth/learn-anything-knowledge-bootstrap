## Introduction

Motion pictures ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Film)), also called films or movies, are sequences of still photographs projected rapidly to create the illusion of continuous movement. They became one of the most popular and influential media of the 20th century, combining drama, emotion, photography, sound, optics, acting, writing, design, music, and industrial organization. Film quickly became a mass entertainment form while also developing as a serious art of directing, screenwriting, cinematography, editing, design, performance, and music.

The motion-picture experience depends on the hypnotic illusion of movement, the apparent accuracy of photographed images, and the sense of presence created by projected light and sound. Film can vary from a handheld documentary made by one person to a multimillion-dollar epic requiring hundreds of performers and technicians. Its history includes major changes such as the arrival of sound, widescreen processes, television competition, animation, computer imagery, and shifting national styles.

## Particularity

Film images show particular things rather than abstractions. A word such as house can remain general, but a camera shows one specific house. This makes film concrete and vivid, though sometimes less open to imagination than language. Yet images can also be ambiguous because they show without explaining, so viewers search for meaning. This explains the power of documentary without commentary and the force of montage, where viewers connect one photographed image to the next.

## Neutrality

The film image seems neutral because the camera and microphone appear to receive sights and sounds without human emotion. Courts may trust film evidence more than sketches or reports for this reason. Yet neutrality is partly an illusion: focus, framing, direction, lens choice, editing, and sound manipulation shape what viewers see and feel. In cinema, the mind often follows the eyes because directors control visual attention.

## Luminosity

Luminosity ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Luminosity)) gives projected film a special force. Bright light on a screen transforms ordinary reality and allows a wide tonal range from deep black to brilliant highlight. This power matters in both black-and-white and colour cinema, where delicate gradations of light, shade, and colour can create atmosphere, emphasis, beauty, and emotional contrast.

## Movement

Movement is the defining addition film makes to graphic art. Composition is kinetic, not static: the meaning of one frame depends on its relation to frames before and after it. Film narrative develops through time, so plots, gestures, camera movement, and changing visual relations matter more than any single image alone.

## Realism

Realism gives motion pictures much of their power. Photographed moving images feel tied to actual presence, whether they record an event or stage a drama for the camera. Synchronous sound strengthens this reality by letting hearing confirm what the eyes see. Animated films lack photographic realism and are often received as fantasy, though they can develop their own expressive reality.

## Montage

Montage is film editing: assembling shots to create meaning, rhythm, and dramatic effect. Soviet filmmakers after the 1917 Revolution studied editing because film stock was scarce and cinema had propaganda value. Lev Kuleshov showed that an identical face could seem hungry, tender, or sorrowful depending on the following image. Sergey Eisenstein argued that montage creates a new concept greater than the sum of shots, while Vsevolod Pudovkin stressed editing as a central language of film.

## Early Cinema and Spectatorship

The Kinetoscope, associated with Thomas A. Edison and William Dickson in 1891, belongs to the early history of mechanically reproduced moving images. Cinema changed spectatorship because audiences watched projected reproductions rather than living performers. Moviegoing became informal, with repeat showings, food sales, and later mass theatrical habits. After [[World Wars (World War II)|World War II]], television reduced theatre attendance, but films also became central to television programming and promotion.

## From Invention to Widescreen

Thomas A. Edison and William Dickson represent the invention and early commercialization of moving-image devices. Later competition from television pushed theatres to emphasize large-screen spectacle through CinemaScope and other widescreen formats. Television versions of widescreen films were often altered by panning and scanning, which selected parts of the original frame for narrower screens.

## Framing

Framing excludes what is unessential and directs attention to what matters. Aspect ratios shape composition, from the Academy ratio of 1.33:1 to CinemaScope and wide-screen formats. Wider frames can create expansiveness but also require careful composition to preserve intimacy and tension. Directors such as Kurosawa Akira, Francois Truffaut, and Miklos Jancso used frame shape and arrangement expressively.

## Scale

Scale changes constantly from shot to shot and can alter emotional meaning. Long shots may make figures lonely or helpless, while close-ups can make them powerful, threatening, or intimate. Films such as King Kong used models to create size illusions, and Orson Welles's Citizen Kane used foreground-background scale relationships to show power and control.

## Symbolic Colour and Cinematography

Cinematography records the director's visual ideas and creates the atmosphere and look of a film. Cinematographers control framing, lenses, camera angle, movement, focus, slow or accelerated motion, and visual effects. Colour may be symbolic, as in Eisenstein's Ivan the Terrible, Part II, where red shifting toward blue conveys fear. Trick photography began with simple camera stoppage and reversal, but later depended on laboratory work, prepared backgrounds, and complex technical teams.

## Editing

Editing trims and joins shots into a concise, complete film. A shot records part of the physical world, but editing reshapes that record into an imagined world or argument. Even calm fiction films may contain hundreds of cuts. Editors often hide their work by cutting on action and by anticipating the spectator's questions, releasing information just when needed. Establishing shots, medium shots, close-ups, and dialogue cutting form common conventions.

## Tempo

Film tempo comes from movement, cutting rhythm, music, and story content. Fast action, quick cutting, and lively music can create excitement; immobility and solemn music can create sadness or boredom. Suspense is unusual because it combines excitement with the feeling that time is slowing. Directors such as Satyajit Ray and Ozu Yasujiro used deliberate pacing to produce serenity and wisdom.

## Sound

[[Sound]] was mechanically possible early but became practical only in the late 1920s, when amplification and synchronization problems were solved. Early talkies often imitated theatre and restricted camera movement, but sound removed intertitles, made music integral, and allowed isolated noises to carry dramatic power. Silence also became expressive, sometimes more powerful than words or music.

## Music

Film music moved from live silent-film accompaniment to composed soundtracks. Early sound-era music was often overemphatic, but filmmakers learned restraint. Music can heighten climaxes, relieve unbearable intensity, symbolize characters or forces, and structure emotion. From the 1960s, electronic music became common. Sound engineers select and modify noises as carefully as cinematographers select images.

## Literature, Acting, and Design

Film frequently adapts [[The Art of Literature (Literature)|literature]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Literature)), but successful adaptation requires cinematic rather than purely verbal expression. Some films preserve plays, ballets, or operas almost intact; others transform novels into visual structures. Film acting differs from stage acting because the camera studies small facial and bodily details. Screen performance often requires restraint, apparent naturalness, and star presence rather than theatrical projection.

## Sets and Expressionism

Set design can support realism, fantasy, spectacle, or psychological atmosphere. German Expressionist films such as The Cabinet of Dr. Caligari, Nosferatu, and Metropolis made settings central to horror, doom, and distorted inner worlds. Later cinema used sets in epics, science fiction, musicals, thrillers, and intimate drama, while directors such as Jean Renoir and Marcel Pagnol sometimes preferred authentic locations or constructed spaces to naturalize performance.

## Lighting

Lighting is a primary tool of film composition. Daylight is strong and cheap, but artificial lighting controls highlights, shadows, colour, and dramatic emphasis. Dark lighting often suggests tragedy, while bright lighting suggests comedy or romance; lighting from above, below, front, side, or behind changes the face's meaning. Styles moved from flat early lighting to German Expressionist and film noir contrast, then toward more balanced emphasis.

## Settings

Film can reproduce natural scenery and make landscape an active dramatic force. Swedish directors after [[World Wars (World War I)|World War I]] used mountains, weather, lakes, and seasons as participants in simple tales, while documentaries such as Robert Flaherty's work made environment central. Portable equipment later made location shooting easier and more common, though studios remained useful for historical, remote, or controlled settings.

## Costume

Costume has shaped character, spectacle, fashion, and realism from early epics to modern cinema. Historical dramas, Hollywood glamour, and fashion promotion made costumes highly visible, while Italian Neorealism showed the power of ordinary dress. Stars such as Charlie Chaplin, Buster Keaton, and Mae West used costume as part of screen identity.

## Makeup

Film makeup must withstand close camera scrutiny and often aims at heightened reality. It can create beauty, age, dirt, monsters, or naturalism. The cosmetics industry grew with cinema in the 1920s, and Max Factor became important in adapting makeup to lighting and colour film. Prosthetics, contact lenses, and chemistry later transformed science-fiction, fantasy, and horror imagery.

## Robert Sklar

Robert Sklar frames directing as the role most responsible for a film's style, structure, and quality, though cinema remains collaborative. Early filmmakers such as Georges Melies handled producing, design, acting, and camera work together; later producers assigned specific directors. By about 1910, expanding production required directors to coordinate actors, designers, technicians, budgets, and schedules.

## Directing and Personal Style

Modern directing can shape narrative, image, sound, performance, and postproduction. Some directors are known for visual style, episodic structure, or sound design, including Federico Fellini, Andrey Tarkovsky, Werner Herzog, Francis Ford Coppola, Robert Altman, Martin Scorsese, and Bernardo Bertolucci. Even when cinematographers, editors, and sound engineers execute technical work, the director often defines the total conception.

## Types of Motion Pictures

Motion pictures serve several social and artistic functions. The main modes are documentary, experimental, and fictional. Documentary films rely on cinema's ability to relay events in the world; experimental films test the medium's technological and expressive possibilities; fictional films are the narrative movies most audiences associate with cinema.

## Documentary

Documentary film grew from public interest in journalism, travel, lectures, and visual records around the turn of the 20th century. Early films showed places, battles, ceremonies, and unfamiliar cultures. Nonfiction production declined because documentary depends on unpredictable world events, while fiction could be controlled in studios. Later documentaries used observation, dramatization, propaganda, journalism, television footage, and direct cinema.

## Wartime and Direct Documentary

British wartime documentaries, Pare Lorentz's American films on the Dust Bowl, Frank Capra's Why We Fight, French cinema verite, and American direct cinema expanded documentary practice. Television trained documentary makers and supplied footage. Films such as Point of Order, Don't Look Back, and Titicut Follies showed that documentary could be theatrical, political, observational, and controversial.

## Propaganda

Propaganda film deliberately tries to influence opinion or behaviour. The Soviet Union used cinema powerfully after the 1917 Revolution, though Stalinist control later restricted major directors. Nazi Germany used film for spectacle and ideology, especially Leni Riefenstahl's Triumph of the Will. Educational, advertising, promotional, religious, and political films may also function as propaganda.

## Experimental and Animated Film

Experimental film explores cinema beyond mainstream narrative and documentary. In the 1920s, Dada, Surrealist, and avant-garde artists such as Fernand Leger, Marcel Duchamp, Rene Clair, Man Ray, Luis Bunuel, and Salvador Dali made abstract or nonnarrative works. In the United States, Maya Deren's Meshes of the Afternoon helped shape post-[[World Wars (World War II)|World War II]] avant-garde film around dream, psychology, and inner states.

## Animation

Animation became both experimental form and popular entertainment. Cartoon characters such as Mickey Mouse, Bugs Bunny, Popeye, and Woody Woodpecker became cultural icons. Walt Disney's Snow White and the Seven Dwarfs in 1937 led feature animation. Later revivals included Who Framed Roger Rabbit, Beauty and the Beast, The Lion King, and computer animation such as Pixar's Toy Story in 1995.

## Fictional Genres

Fictional genres helped studios regularize production and audiences understand expectations. Westerns, gangster films, police thrillers, science fiction, social problem films, musicals, melodramas, disaster films, and comedies reuse settings, stars, plots, music, lighting, and character types. Genre history reflects Hollywood production and American popular culture, though countries with more independent production may use genre less rigidly.

## Genre Revision and New Waves

Genres can be mixed, revised, or overturned. Films such as Moulin Rouge!, Bonnie and Clyde, and M*A*S*H play with conventions. The French New Wave of the late 1950s and early 1960s, including Francois Truffaut, Jean-Luc Godard, Claude Chabrol, and Eric Rohmer, transformed storytelling and visualization while still engaging genre expectations.

## Films of Art and Art Cinema

Art cinema includes recordings of plays, operas, ballets, concerts, and films aimed at audiences concerned with artistic value. Some works once dismissed as uncinematic later showed that filmed performance could use inventive cinematic techniques. Art houses after [[World Wars (World War II)|World War II]] showed European films by directors such as Federico Fellini, Ingmar Bergman, Luis Bunuel, and Michelangelo Antonioni, while museums and campuses supported avant-garde film.

## Film Societies, Festivals, and Awards

Film societies and festivals helped create serious film culture, journals, books, and university film study. Cine-clubs flourished in the 1920s, including groups linked to Louis Delluc, Ricciotto Canudo, and the Film Society of London. Festivals at Venice, Cannes, Berlin, Moscow, Toronto, Sundance, Telluride, and elsewhere became marketplaces, cultural meetings, sites of discovery, and arenas of political and artistic dispute. By the early 21st century, more than 700 festivals were scheduled annually worldwide.

## Key Events & Developments

- 1891: [[Edison]] and Dickson developed the Kinetoscope.
- 1917: Soviet cinema began developing montage theory after the Russian Revolution.
- Late 1920s: Practical synchronized sound transformed cinema.
- 1937: Disney released Snow White and the Seven Dwarfs.
- 1995: Pixar's Toy Story became the first fully computer-animated feature.
