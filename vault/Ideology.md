# Ideology

## Introduction

Ideology ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ideology)) is a form of social or political philosophy in which practical aims are as important as theoretical explanation. It tries both to explain the world and to change it. In a strict sense, an ideology offers a broad theory of human experience, proposes a program for social or political organization, treats its realization as a struggle, recruits committed adherents, addresses a wide public, and often gives intellectuals a special role.

Modern ideology differs from ordinary opinion because it organizes ideas into an action-oriented system. In a looser sense the word can mean any political theory guided by ideas, but in the stricter sense it describes systems such as positivism, socialism, communism, fascism, Nazism, nationalism, and some forms of liberalism or conservatism when they become comprehensive programs of belief and action.

## Origins and Characteristics

A.-L.-C. Destutt de Tracy introduced the French word ideologie during the French Revolution as a name for his science of ideas. He drew on John Locke, Etienne Bonnot de Condillac, and especially [[Francis bacon|Francis Bacon]], whose view of science joined knowledge with practical improvement. Destutt de Tracy's science of ideas was meant to free minds from prejudice and prepare society for reason.

The ideologues combined individual liberty with state planning and a national education program. Under the Directory of 1795-1799, their teaching briefly became official doctrine in France. [[Napoleon]] first supported them and then denounced them, even blaming them in 1812 for French military defeats. From the beginning, ideology therefore carried both praise and abuse: it could mean rational liberation or dangerous abstraction.

The 19th century is often described as an age of ideology because secular systems increasingly tried to fill roles once occupied by religious faith. Ideologies were not simply philosophical explanations; they were programs, appeals, and mobilizing structures that sought to organize society around a future to be achieved.

## Religion and Early Political Thought

Ideology and religion resemble each other as total systems concerned with truth and conduct, but they differ sharply. Religion usually rests on divine order, faith, worship, and spiritual purification. Ideology normally claims to rely on reason, focuses on the group, nation, or class, and seeks practical political transformation in this world.

Girolamo Savonarola in Florence showed an early ideological dimension within Christianity. He tried to turn a puritan Christian community into a political model to be realized immediately, using popular appeal and state power to regulate economic and private life. His militancy influenced later reforming communities such as Calvin's Geneva and Puritan settlements in the New World.

Niccolo Machiavelli was a critic of Savonarola but also a precursor of modern ideologists. His republican ideal looked back to ancient Rome and imagined political renewal through revolutionary will. In the 17th century, English political conflict encouraged theories that justified radical action; John Locke's Two Treatises of Government in 1690 defended rights against absolutism and helped create an ideological style built around principles such as liberty and right.

## Hegel, Marx, and False Consciousness

G.W.F. Hegel used a historical philosophy in which people enact roles assigned by forces they do not fully understand. Critics saw this as an ideology of the status quo because it seemed to reconcile people to existing circumstances. Karl Marx developed a sharper critique in The German Ideology, written in 1845-1846, where ideology could mean false consciousness: beliefs through which people misunderstand their real social condition.

[[Marx and Marxism (Marx)|Marx]] did not use the term consistently. Sometimes ideology was self-deception; at other times it could describe a more general system of ideas. Later Marxists often dropped the purely pejorative sense and called Marxism itself an ideology. Communist states even created ideological institutes and treated party philosophers as party ideologists.

## Sociology of Knowledge

Sociology of knowledge extended the idea that belief systems express interests. Writers such as Max Weber and Karl Mannheim examined how social conditions shape ideas, and some theorists treated economics, psychology, religion, and political theory as rationalizations of class or group interests. From this angle, the task of research was to uncover the life conditions that produce ideologies.

The problem was self-reference. If all philosophy is ideology and all truth claims are disguised interests, then the sociology of knowledge might also be only another ideology. Weber partly reversed Marx by showing that ideas, such as Protestant beliefs, could help generate economic structures. Mannheim distinguished ideology from utopia and imagined a socially unattached intelligentsia capable of less biased knowledge, but that hope itself looked elitist and uncertain.

## Rationalism, Romanticism, and Terror

Political rationalism treats politics as something deduced from abstract ideas. Critics such as Michael Oakeshott argued that liberty makes sense within inherited practices and can become a dangerous abstraction when detached from experience. Edward Shils emphasized romanticism, claiming that its cult of the ideal and contempt for compromise made civil politics appear weak and drove people toward ideological extremism.

Ideology and violence are linked by critics who stress ideology's totalizing and militant style. Albert Camus rejected revolutionary doctrines that justify cruelty for future justice; he preferred reform and human decency over what he called logical crimes. Karl Popper criticized historicist ideologies for pretending that history can become a predictive science, arguing instead for trial, error, falsification, and piecemeal social engineering.

Militant language often fills ideological writing: struggle, march, resist, victory, and overcome. Georges Sorel, Frantz Fanon, and Jean-Paul Sartre each gave violence or revolutionary action an important place, though in different ways. Sartre later distinguished major philosophies from marginal ideologies and tried to renew Marxism by adding existentialist elements.

## Pragmatism and International Relations

![[attachments/ideology_003.webp|420]]

*Far right ideological hierarchy*

Pragmatism is often contrasted with ideology because it treats problems on their merits rather than applying a fixed doctrine. The contrast is not absolute: many political approaches are belief systems, but only some become highly structured, recruiting, programmatic ideologies. Liberalism, for example, can become more ideological at one extreme and more pragmatic at another.

International relations became visibly ideological in the 20th century, though earlier religious and political wars also had ideological elements. World War I began as a more traditional national war but increasingly required ideological justification, including democracy, culture, and civilization. The Russian Revolution of 1917, Woodrow Wilson's League of Nations vision, fascism, the Spanish Civil War, and World War II all strengthened ideological interpretations of war and diplomacy.

The Cold War was largely an ideological confrontation between communism and Western anticommunism. The West did not always possess a single positive ideology, but opposition to communism became negatively ideological. Modern ideological conflict therefore differs from older wars mainly by degree: public opinion, mass politics, universal abstractions, and competing social visions made ideology a dominant feature of modern international life.

## Key Events & Developments

- French Revolution: Destutt de Tracy coined ideologie as a science of ideas.
- Directory period: The ideologues briefly influenced official French doctrine in 1795-1799.
- Napoleonic rejection: [[Napoleon]] turned ideology into a term of abuse by blaming ideologues for French failures.
- Marxist critique: [[Marx and Marxism (Marx)|Marx]] gave ideology the influential sense of false consciousness.
- Cold War ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cold_War)): Ideological conflict became a major frame for international relations after World War II.

## Interesting Facts

- Ideology ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ideology)) began as a technical term but quickly became both a compliment and an insult.
- Ideologues ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ideology)) once tried to redesign national education to make France rational and scientific.
- False consciousness made ideology one of the central concepts of Marxist criticism.
- Political language often reveals ideology through military words such as struggle, march, and victory.
- Cold War anticommunism could be ideological even when it opposed ideology rather than offering a single doctrine.
