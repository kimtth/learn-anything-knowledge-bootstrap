# Physical and Chemical Analysis and Measurement (Chemical Analysis)

> [!abstract] Reference navigation

## Introduction

Chemical analysis ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Analytical_chemistry)) is the determination of physical properties or chemical composition of matter. It uses systematic procedures that developed alongside the physical sciences. Classical, or wet, analysis uses chemical reactions and a balance rather than mechanical or electronic instruments. Instrumental analysis uses devices to characterize reactions or directly measure analyte properties.

The object being measured is the analyte, and the method may detect a visible product, a precipitate, an electrical signal, absorbed radiation, emitted radiation, or a separated chromatographic peak.

Quantitative analysis can be gravimetric or volumetric. Gravimetric methods depend on critical mass measurements, such as precipitating chloride as silver chloride, filtering, drying, and weighing it. Volumetric methods measure volume, commonly through titration, in which reagent quantity reveals analyte amount.

## Sampling and Preparation

Sampling removes a portion of bulk material for assay, ideally representing the whole. Statistical planning determines sample size, number, location, timing, accuracy, cost, and required information. Homogeneous materials may need one sample; heterogeneous materials need more. Sample preparation may preserve unstable components, prevent contamination, dissolve solids, adjust concentration, or isolate analytes before measurement.

Poor sampling can make even a precise assay misleading, as with a lake pollutant measured only at the centre and surface rather than around edges and at depth.

For that reason, analytical chemistry treats sampling, preservation, blanks, standards, and calibration as part of measurement itself rather than as preliminaries. A sensitive instrument cannot correct a specimen that was contaminated, evaporated, segregated, or taken from an unrepresentative location.

## Interference Removal

An interference is a substance other than the analyte that is measured by the chosen method or prevents the analyte from being measured. Interferences create erroneous results and may be removed by distillation, selective precipitation, filtration, complexation, osmosis, reverse osmosis, extraction, electrogravimetry, or chromatography. Some separation techniques also serve as assays.

## Distillation and Selective Precipitation

Distillation separates components by boiling point. More volatile substances vaporize, condense, and are collected as distillate, while less volatile substances remain behind. Distillation columns improve separation through repeated vaporization-condensation steps. Selective precipitation uses a reagent that reacts with an interference to form a solid precipitate, which can then be removed by filtration or centrifugation.

## Filtration, Complexation, Osmosis, and Extraction

Filtration separates particles by size through filter paper, fritted glass, glass fibre, or vacuum systems. Complexation masks an interference by forming a dissolved complex with different chemical behaviour. Osmosis and reverse osmosis use semipermeable membranes and solvent flow for separation. [[Extraction And Processing Industries (Mining)|Extraction]] separates solutes by their relative solubilities in immiscible solvents and may be performed in batch or countercurrent form.

Chromatography ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chromatography)) separates mixture components by relative attraction to a stationary phase while a mobile phase moves past it. Liquid chromatography uses a liquid mobile phase; gas chromatography uses a gas. In elution chromatography, components more attracted to the stationary phase move more slowly, while less attracted components exit earlier and are collected separately.

## Absorptiometry and Refractometry

Absorptiometry measures electromagnetic radiation absorbed by an analyte. Absorption raises molecules to excited states, reducing beam intensity in proportion to concentration and path length. Methods include nuclear magnetic resonance, electron spin resonance, thermal, infrared, ultraviolet-visible, and X-ray absorptiometry. Refractometry measures refractive index, useful because pure substances have characteristic values and mixtures change with composition.

## Luminescence

Luminescence methods measure radiation emitted after excitation. Fluorescence occurs when an excited electron returns quickly to a lower orbital without spin inversion, stopping immediately when excitation ceases. Phosphorescence involves spin inversion and delayed emission. Both phenomena can identify or quantify analytes through characteristic emission behaviour.

## Electroanalysis

Electroanalysis uses electrodes and electric or electronic devices to measure parameters related to analyte identity or quantity. Major methods include potentiometry, amperometry, conductometry, electrogravimetry, voltammetry, polarography, and coulometry. Most depend on electron transfer between electrodes and analyte species through oxidation or reduction.

## Conductometry and Voltammetry

Conductometry measures a solution's ability to conduct current, which depends on dissolved ions, ion size, charge, and concentration. It is useful for total conductance and titration end points but is not very selective. Voltammetry varies electrode potential while measuring current. Polarography uses a mercury or liquid-metal electrode; waves and peaks identify substances and quantify concentrations through working curves.

## Electrogravimetry and Coulometry

Electrogravimetry deposits an analyte-derived solid on a preweighed electrode, then measures mass increase after rinsing and drying. Constant-current methods are faster but more interference-prone; constant-potential methods are more selective. Coulometry measures the total electricity required to complete an analyte reaction, either directly or through electrochemically generated titrants.

## Amperometry and Potentiometry

Amperometry holds electrode potential constant and relates current to analyte concentration or titration end point. Potentiometry measures potential between an indicator electrode and reference electrode at nearly zero current. It is probably the most common electroanalytical method and includes inert indicator electrodes and ion-selective electrodes such as glass pH electrodes, liquid-ion-exchanger, solid-state, neutral-carrier, coated-wire, field-effect transistor, gas-sensing, and biomembrane electrodes.

## Gas and Liquid Chromatographic Analysis

In gas chromatography, a gas mobile phase moves analyte through a column, and a detector records separated peaks by retention time. Qualitative analysis compares retention times with standards; quantitative analysis uses peak height or area. Liquid chromatography operates similarly in columns or on planes, sometimes using retention volume rather than retention time.

## Core Equations

Absorptiometry often uses the Beer-Lambert law:

$$A=\varepsilon lc$$

The Beer-Lambert law relates absorbance to molar absorptivity, path length, and concentration, making concentration measurable from light absorption.

Coulometry relates total charge to the amount of substance transformed:

$$Q=nF$$

This coulometry relation connects total electric charge with the amount of substance transformed through Faraday's constant.

Gravimetric precipitation of chloride can be represented as:

$$\mathrm{Ag^+ + Cl^- \rightarrow AgCl(s)}$$

Read the left side as the reactants or starting materials and the right side as the products; the coefficients show the proportions needed to conserve atoms or charge.

## Key Events & Developments

- Classical analysis: Wet chemical techniques established gravimetric and volumetric foundations.
- Instrumental analysis: Spectral, electrochemical, and chromatographic tools expanded sensitivity and selectivity.
- Chromatography ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chromatography)): Stationary-mobile phase partitioning became central to separations.
- Ion-selective electrodes: Potentiometry became widely useful for pH and ion assays.

## Key People

- Analytical chemists ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Analytical_chemists)): Developed systematic methods for sampling, separation, detection, and quantification.
- Instrument designers: Extended chemical analysis through spectrometers, electrodes, chromatographs, and detectors.

## Interesting Facts

- Wet analysis may use no instrument other than a balance.
- Silver chloride precipitation is a classic gravimetric example.
- Reverse osmosis is useful for water purification and analytical separations.
- Retention time helps identify compounds in gas chromatography.
