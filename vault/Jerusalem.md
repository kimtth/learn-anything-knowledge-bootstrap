# Jerusalem

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> Jerusalem is a city sacred to Judaism, Christianity, and Islam whose landscape, monuments, religious communities, and political status have made it a focal point of pilgrimage, memory, and conflict. Its modern development remains inseparable from Israeli-Palestinian relations and the city's layered ancient history.

## Hebrew Yerushalayim , Arabic Bayt al-Muqaddas or Al-Quds

Jerusalem is an ancient Middle Eastern city that has been wholly under Israeli rule since 1967. It has been revered, fought over, and governed by many dynasties and states. In the 20th century, it became a focus of rival Zionist and Palestinian Arab national claims. The United Nations proposed making it a corpus separatum ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Corpus_separatum)), or separate entity, but the 1948 Arab-Israeli war divided it into Israeli west Jerusalem and Jordanian east Jerusalem.

The city's status remained one of the central issues in the Israeli-Palestinian dispute. Israel declared Jerusalem its capital in 1949, occupied east Jerusalem in the Six-Day War of 1967, expanded municipal boundaries, and later reaffirmed the unified city as its capital by law in 1980. These actions were repeatedly condemned by international bodies. The municipal area covered about 49 square miles, and the population estimate for 2004 was 693,200.

Jerusalem's meaning is religious as well as political. Jews see it as the centre of ancient national life and religious yearning. Christians connect it with the suffering, death, and triumph of Jesus. Muslims revere it as the place of Muhammad's night journey and as the home of major Islamic shrines. This layered holiness gives the city unusual emotional power and makes ordinary urban questions inseparable from memory, faith, and sovereignty.

## Landscape

Jerusalem's site stands on hills about 2,575 feet above sea level. The city looks east toward the Dead Sea, the Jordan River, and the mountains of Moab, and west toward the coastal plain and the Mediterranean Sea about 35 miles away. The Old City is a walled medieval enclosure of less than half a square mile, and the modern municipality spreads far beyond it into Israeli and West Bank-adjacent urban areas.

The Old City is a walled quadrilateral about 3,000 feet on each side and is believed to have been continuously inhabited for nearly 5,000 years. Its dominant platform is the Temple Mount, known to Jews as Har Ha-Bayit and to Muslims as Al-Haram al-Sharif. It contains the Dome of the Rock, Al-Aqsa Mosque, synagogues and yeshivas nearby, churches, convents, hidden courtyards, bazaars, and religious institutions packed into a dense Middle Eastern urban fabric.

Modern expansion began outside the walls in the 1860s. Early Jewish suburbs and Christian or foreign compounds grew west and northwest of the Old City, including Mishkenot Shaanannim, Yemin Moshe, the Russian Compound, the German Colony, the American Colony, Mea Shearim, and Mahane Yehuda. After 1967, large Jewish housing developments were built on the city's southern, eastern, and northern edges, while Arab housing construction inside the city remained more restricted.

## Climate

Jerusalem's climate is mixed subtropical and semiarid, with warm dry summers and cool rainy winters. Annual precipitation averages about 24 inches, while snow is generally light and irregular. Average daily temperatures range from about 50 degrees F in January to about 75 degrees F in August.

The sharav, or khamsin, is a hot dry desert wind common in autumn and spring. Summer sunlight is intense because of low humidity, few clouds, and the high angle of the sun. The city has little heavy industry and serious air pollution is limited, though automobile exhaust contributes to urban pollution.

## Housing

Housing ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Housing)) ranges from ancient stone buildings in the Old City to 19th-century neighbourhoods, modern tree-lined quarters, and government-built housing for immigrants. The typical Old City dwelling is a complex of structures around an inner courtyard, reached by a narrow corridor and often arranged on different levels.

Urban renewal after 1967 restored and gentrified the Jewish quarter of the Old City and older Jewish neighbourhoods in the New City. Housing density remained unequal, with Arab households averaging about 1.8 persons per room and Jewish households about 1 person per room. This difference reflected economic inequality, building restrictions, family size, and uneven municipal development.

## The Western Wall

Jerusalem's architecture is defined by the coexistence of old and new, sacred and secular. The Ottoman wall built by Suleyman the Magnificent in 1538-1540 still surrounds the Old City, largely on older Crusader, Byzantine, Herodian, and Hasmonean foundations. Seven gates remain open, while the Golden Gate is sealed and linked in Jewish legend with the Messiah's entrance.

The Western Wall ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_Wall)) became the holiest Jewish shrine during centuries when Jews were excluded from the Temple Mount. Since 1967, more of the wall has been exposed and a large plaza cleared before it. On the Temple Mount itself stand the gold-capped Dome of the Rock, completed in 691, and the silver-domed Al-Aqsa Mosque, built in the early 8th century.

Architectural layers include the Citadel near Jaffa Gate, Byzantine and Crusader churches, Mamluk madrasahs and ornament, Ottoman structures, rock-cut tombs, British mandate buildings, and modern Israeli public architecture. A city ordinance from the mandate period requires buildings to be faced in stone, giving much of Jerusalem a common visual character despite religious and political diversity.

## People

Jerusalem's population has traditionally been classified by religion because Judaism, Christianity, and Islam all claim sacred ties to the city. Jews and Arabs live largely in separate districts, and Jewish neighbourhoods are further divided among ultraorthodox, traditional, and secular communities. Armenian Christians form a distinct enclave in the Old City.

The modern conflict is often expressed in religious terms but is mainly shaped by two national claims: Jewish Israeli and Palestinian Arab. Jews from many parts of the Diaspora have made the Jewish population ethnically diverse. Muslims are almost all Arab, while Christians are the smallest and most internally diverse religious community, though most local Christians are also Arab.

## Jews

Jewish Jerusalem is divided by origin and religious practice. Ashkenazim and Sephardim represent broad cultural lineages, while secular, traditional, and ultraorthodox groups each form roughly a third of the Jewish population. Disputes over Sabbath observance, traffic, and kashruth affect local politics, and some ultraorthodox neighbourhoods are closed to traffic on the Sabbath.

Jewish holy places include the Temple Mount, the Western Wall, the reputed tomb of King David on Mount Zion, the Mount of Olives cemetery, and tombs in the Valley of Kidron. Many Orthodox Jews avoid entering the Temple Mount itself for fear of profaning the place of the Holy of Holies. Jerusalem also contains scores of yeshivas and is one of the world's major centres of rabbinic learning.

## Muslims

Muslim Jerusalem is overwhelmingly Sunni. Muslims revere the city as the third holiest place on earth, and taqdis, the pilgrimage to Jerusalem, is an optional complement to the hajj to Mecca. The Nabi Musa pilgrimage, once the largest mass pilgrimage in Palestine and timed with Easter, declined after the early 1950s.

Al-Haram al-Sharif and Muslim religious trusts have been administered since 1967 by Muslim authorities that do not recognize Israeli sovereignty over east Jerusalem. Since 1995, the Palestinian Authority has exercised effective control over many Muslim institutions, courts, and appointments in east Jerusalem.

## Christians

Christian Jerusalem is the smallest but most diversified religious community. Eastern Orthodox, Monophysite, Roman Catholic, Protestant, Armenian, Coptic, Abyssinian, Syrian Orthodox, Greek Catholic, Armenian Catholic, Anglican, Lutheran, and Evangelical groups all have institutions in the city. About four-fifths of local Christians are Arabs.

The Church of the Holy Sepulchre is shared by major denominations according to the Ottoman status quo of 1852. The Greek Orthodox patriarchate owns large properties and has jurisdiction over the Holy Land, while the Franciscans have guarded Roman Catholic rights since 1334. The Armenian patriarchate, established in the 6th century, anchors a community centred on the Cathedral of St. James.

## Economy

Jerusalem's economy depends heavily on government, public service, tourism, construction, and services. Since 1967, housing growth and pilgrim traffic have stimulated investment, though political tension can sharply reduce activity. Poverty is concentrated among parts of the Muslim population, ultraorthodox Jews, and Jews from Africa and Asia.

Manufacturing ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manufacturing)) remains limited because heavy industry would threaten the city's traditional character and because transport and marketing are difficult. Small industries include electronics, wood, furniture, printing, food products, textiles, clothing, chemicals, religious objects, and tourist goods. Services employ about two-thirds of the workforce, and tourism brought more than one million visitors by 2000.

Transport links Jerusalem north-south through the West Bank and east-west with Tel Aviv-Yafo, the Jordan Valley, and Amman. Roads improved after the mid-1970s, but congestion remained severe. Jewish and Arab bus systems operate separately, Sabbath observance affects Jewish services, shared taxis fill gaps, and the historic Jaffa-Jerusalem railway opened in 1892.

## Government

Municipal government is led by a 31-member council elected every four years and a directly elected mayor since 1975. Israeli Jews are the most politically active group. Most Arabs in east Jerusalem identify as Palestinians and usually refuse to vote in municipal elections because they reject Israeli sovereignty. No Arab has served on the council since 1967.

Jerusalem ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jerusalem)) is the seat of Israel's president, Knesset, ministries, Supreme Court, Chief Rabbinate, Jewish Agency, World Zionist Organization, and Yad Vashem. Most countries have not recognized it as Israel's capital. The city also remains under complex religious administration, with holy places cared for by their respective religious authorities and protected by law.

## Health

Health care is supplied by major hospitals and subsidized services. Hadassah Medical Centre at En Kerem and Hadassah Hospital on Mount Scopus serve patients from Israel, the West Bank, Gaza, and Jordan. Other institutions include Shaare Tzedeq, St. John's Ophthalmic Hospital, Al-Maqasid al-Khayriyyah, Al-Mutalla, and specialized facilities for mental health, children, terminal cases, and disability.

Emergency and public health are supported by Magen David Adom, the Red Crescent, municipal clinics, school health supervision, and obligatory medical insurance for Israeli citizens. Clinics expanded into east Jerusalem after 1967, and a modern medical centre serving Arab residents opened in El-Sheikh Jarrah in 1982.

## Education

[[Education]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Education)) is complex because of high birth rates and strong religious identities. Hebrew is used in most Jewish schools and Arabic in Arab schools, with English the main second language. Separate Jewish religious systems emphasize sacred texts, while Arab government schools generally follow the Jordanian pattern. Religious organizations also maintain many private schools.

Higher education includes the Hebrew University of Jerusalem, opened in 1925, with campuses at Mount Scopus and Givat Ram; Al-Quds University, founded in 1995, with headquarters at Abu Dis; Bezalel Academy of Arts and Design; the Samuel Rubin Academy of Music; Hebrew Union College; teachers colleges; a Mormon university; and an Armenian seminary.

Cultural institutions include the Israel Museum, Shrine of the Book, Rockefeller Archaeological Museum, Islamic art collections, research institutes, theatres, libraries, broadcasting, parks, and Teddy Kollek Stadium.

Archaeology in Jerusalem began with 19th-century European surveys and became modern and scientific with Kathleen Kenyon's excavations from 1961 to 1967. Since 1968, excavations around the Old City and Temple Mount have revealed Herodian pavements, Temple steps, priests' entrances, First Temple-period artifacts, Roman destruction layers from 70 CE, an Umayyad palace, the Cardo, and remains from Crusader and Ayyubid periods.

Ancient Jerusalem shows settlement from about the late Chalcolithic and Early Bronze Age around 3000 BCE. The early name Urusalim probably meant Foundation of Shalem. Biblical tradition links the city with Melchizedek, Abraham, and the Jebusites. Around 1000 BCE, David captured it and made it the capital of Israel and Judah; Solomon expanded it and built the Temple.

Destruction and restoration shaped the city's early history. Jerusalem was attacked by Egyptians, Philistines, Arabians, Israel, Assyria, and Babylon. Nebuchadrezzar II destroyed the city and Temple in 587/586 BCE, beginning the Babylonian captivity. Cyrus II permitted Jewish return in 538 BCE, the Temple was restored in 515 BCE, and Nehemiah rebuilt fortifications around 444 BCE.

Hellenistic and Roman Jerusalem passed through Greek influence, Ptolemaic and Seleucid rule, Maccabean revolt in 167 BCE, Hasmonean independence, and Roman capture by Pompey in 63 BCE. Herod the Great transformed the city with enlarged walls, a palace, a citadel, an amphitheatre, and the grand rebuilt Temple. Under Pontius Pilate, Jesus of Nazareth was put to death.

Roman destruction followed Jewish revolt. In 70 CE, Titus besieged and devastated the city and destroyed the Temple. After another failed Jewish revolt in 132-135, Hadrian founded Aelia Capitolina. Christian pilgrimage expanded after Constantine I's conversion and St. Helena's pilgrimage in 326, leading to major shrines, especially the Church of the Holy Sepulchre. Persian invasion in 614 ended a prosperous Christian era.

Islamic Jerusalem began when the caliph Umar I entered the city in 638. The Dome of the Rock was built in 688-691. Umayyad and Abbasid rulers generally allowed Christians and Jews to remain. Fatimid instability, the destruction of Christian shrines under al-Hakim in 1010, and Seljuq control after 1071 helped stimulate the Crusades.

Crusader Jerusalem began in 1099, when forces under Godfrey of Bouillon conquered the city and massacred Muslims and Jews. The city became capital of the kingdom of Jerusalem, Muslim shrines became churches, and Jews and Muslims were barred. Saladin captured the city in 1187; brief Christian returns followed in 1229-1239 and 1240-1244.

Mamluk and Ottoman Jerusalem returned the city to Muslim rule. From 1247, Mamluks built mosques, madrasahs, and tombs, while Jews returned to their quarter and Franciscans represented Latin interests. Ottoman rule began in 1517 under Selim I, and the 16th century brought walls, water repairs, madrasahs, waqfs, and charities. Later economic decline and disputes over holy places drew European powers into local affairs.

## Modern Jerusalem

British Jerusalem began when Edmund Allenby's troops entered in 1917. Under the League of Nations mandate, Jerusalem again became a capital. Zionist immigration and Palestinian Arab nationalism intensified conflict. Hajj Amin al-Husayni, the grand mufti, led Arab politics, while clashes occurred in 1920, 1929, and during the Arab revolt beginning in 1936.

The mandate crisis deepened after World War II. Jewish underground groups attacked British forces, and the Irgun blew up part of the King David Hotel in 1946. In 1947, the UN voted to partition Palestine and make Jerusalem and Bethlehem a separate international entity, but the plan was not implemented. When Britain withdrew on May 14, 1948, war followed.

Divided Jerusalem lasted from 1948 to 1967. Israel held west Jerusalem, Jordan held the Old City and most of east Jerusalem, and ethnic separation became almost complete. Israel declared Jerusalem its capital in 1949, but major powers did not recognize the move. Barbed wire, walls, gun positions, and limited access to holy places marked the divided city.

Unified Israeli rule began after the Six-Day War of 1967, when Israel captured east Jerusalem and the Old City from Jordan. East Jerusalem's municipal council was dissolved, Israel governed the enlarged city as sovereign territory, and many countries regarded east Jerusalem as occupied. Mayor Teddy Kollek tried to strengthen Israeli control while limiting friction with Arabs.

Late 20th-century conflict centred on sovereignty, settlement, institutions, and violence. Israel's 1980 Basic Law reaffirming Jerusalem as capital caused international controversy. The first intifadah from 1987 to 1993, clashes in 1990, Palestinian institutional growth after the 1993 peace accord, new Jewish neighbourhoods, attacks in 1996, failed negotiations in 2000, and the second intifadah deepened the divide.
