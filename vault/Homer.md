# Homer

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> Homer is the traditionally named author of the Iliad and Odyssey, though little certain biographical evidence survives. These epics formed the basis of Greek education and culture, traveled through Roman, Byzantine, Renaissance, and translation traditions, and became foundational works of Western literature.

## Introduction

![[attachments/homer_001.webp|420]]

*Ancient-style bust of Homer*

Homer, who may have flourished in the 9th or 8th century BCE in Ionia, is the traditional author of the Iliad ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iliad)) and the Odyssey ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Odyssey)). Little certain knowledge survives about him beyond the ancient Greek attachment of his name to those poems. It is still probable that an epic poet called Homer played the primary role in shaping them, and if so he stands among the greatest literary artists and among the most influential authors in Western tradition.

The Homeric epics ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homer)) formed the basis of Greek education and culture through the Classical age and remained central to humane education into the Roman Empire and the Christian era. Their influence moved indirectly through [[Virgil]]'s Aeneid ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aeneid)), which was loosely modeled on both epics, and directly through Byzantine preservation, the movement of Greek scholars into Italy after Ottoman pressure, Renaissance recovery, and later translations. The poems became central to the Classical European tradition and shaped Western ideas through their earlier role in Greek culture.

For the Greeks, the Iliad ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iliad)) and Odyssey ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Odyssey)) were more than literature. They were symbols of Hellenic unity and heroism, texts memorized by many people, and sources of moral and practical instruction. Early references and quotations appear by the 7th century BCE in poets such as Archilochus, Alcman, Tyrtaeus, and Callinus. By the end of that century, Theagenes of Rhegium was already producing allegorical interpretation, showing that Homeric scholarship ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homeric_scholarship)) began early.

Ancient writers quickly filled the lack of firm facts with legend. Heracleitus of Ephesus used a story about Homer's death from failing to solve a boys' riddle, while traditions imagined a contest between Homer and Hesiod. Herodotus credited Homer and Hesiod with shaping Greek theology and placed them no more than 400 years before his own 5th century BCE lifetime, unlike the common ancient assumption that Homer lived soon after the Trojan War.

The belief that Homer came from Ionia is reasonable because the poems are mainly in Ionic dialect ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ionic_Greek)). Cities such as Smyrna and Chios competed for the honor of being his home, but no authenticated local memory survived. In the Alexandrian period of the 3rd and 2nd centuries BCE, scholars developed fantastic pseudobiographies, later refined under the Roman Empire. Modern scholars accept little of this except the broad connection with Ionia and the probability that the poems reached their form through a long oral tradition.

## The Iliad

The Iliad ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iliad)) is not a full account of the Trojan War but a concentrated poem about the wrath of Achilles. Its opening conflict with Agamemnon removes Achilles from battle, and for thousands of lines his absence shapes the war. The poem explores the heroic ideal in contradictory form: pride, violence, magnificent strength, animal force, and a final human tenderness.

Much of the Iliad ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iliad)) is battle poetry built from recurring motifs but varied through powerful set pieces. These include the catalogue of forces, formal duels between [[Paris]] and Menelaus and between Ajax and Hector, Helen's identification of Achaean leaders, Agamemnon's inspection of troops, the triumph of Diomedes, Hector's meeting with Andromache, the embassy to Achilles, the night expedition, and divine interventions by Hera, Zeus, and Poseidon.

The death of Patroclus brings Achilles back into the war after the recovery of the body, the making of divine armor, and formal reconciliation with Agamemnon. Achilles kills Hector in book 22, restores heroic standing through funeral games for Patroclus, and then meets the grief of Priam. The poem ends not with the fall of Troy but with Hector's funeral, turning heroic rage toward mortality, pity, and shared human loss.

## The Odyssey

The Odyssey ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Odyssey)) is more diffuse in action but has an even more complex and harmonious structure than the Iliad. Its main situation is in Ithaca, where Penelope and Telemachus are powerless before arrogant suitors while they doubt Odysseus's return from Troy. Telemachus secretly travels to the Peloponnese and meets Nestor, Menelaus, and Helen while searching for news of his father.

Odysseus moves from Calypso's island to the land of the Phaeacians under the hostility of Poseidon. There he tells of his adventures after Troy, including his escape from Polyphemus the Cyclops. The poem then brings him back to Ithaca alone at night, where Athena guides disguises, recognition scenes, and strategy.

The return story depends on concealment, testing, and restoration. Odysseus reveals himself first to the faithful swineherd Eumaeus and then to Telemachus; together they plan the killing of the suitors. After the violent fulfillment of that plan, Penelope recognizes him, he recounts his adventures, meets his aged father Laertes, and regains stability in his island kingdom through Athena's help.

The Odyssey ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Odyssey)) deepens themes of cunning, endurance, family loyalty, household order, and divine-human partnership. Its adventures contain folk-tale elements, but the structure links them to a serious social goal: the recovery of rightful authority in Ithaca after long absence, disorder, and testing.

## Homeric influence and poetic form

Homer's influence is strongest in the formal and imaginative components of the poems. The gods dignify human events while sometimes making them seem small or tragic. Divine assemblies in the Iliad ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iliad)) and the personal, ambivalent relation between Odysseus and Athena in the Odyssey show the main composer's range and taste.

The battle scenes, realistic deaths, speeches, catalogues, similes, recurring formulas, and epic rhythm reflect a long oral tradition ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oral_tradition)) shaped into large poetic architecture. The poems were not just inherited tales; they were organized works whose structure, characterization, and emotional pattern gave lasting form to heroic myth. Their effect on Greek education made them a cultural foundation, while later transmission made them central to European literature.

## Key Events & Developments

- 9th or 8th century BCE: Homer may have flourished in Ionia.
- 7th century BCE: Early poets such as Archilochus and Alcman already referred to or quoted Homeric poetry.
- late 7th century BCE: Theagenes of Rhegium began allegorical interpretation of Homer.
- 5th century BCE: Herodotus placed Homer and Hesiod within 400 years of his own time and credited them with shaping Greek theology.
- 3rd-2nd centuries BCE: Alexandrian scholars expanded both genuine scholarship and legendary pseudobiography around Homer.
- late 8th century CE onward: Byzantine culture helped preserve and revive the Homeric epics ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homer)).
- Renaissance: Greek scholars moving west helped transmit Homeric poetry into Italian and European culture.

## Key People

- Homer ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homer)): Traditional author and chief shaping figure of the Iliad ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iliad)) and Odyssey ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Odyssey)).
- Achilles ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Achilles)): Central warrior of the Iliad, whose wrath and grief shape the poem.
- Hector ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hector)): Trojan hero killed by Achilles and mourned at the Iliad's close.
- Odysseus ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Odysseus)): Hero of the Odyssey, whose return restores his household and kingdom.
- Penelope ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Penelope)): Odysseus's faithful wife, who resists the suitors in Ithaca.
- Telemachus ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Telemachus)): Odysseus's son, whose search for his father begins his own growth.
- Athena ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Athena)): Goddess who protects Odysseus and helps restore order in Ithaca.
- [[Virgil]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Virgil)): Roman poet whose Aeneid carried Homeric patterns into later tradition.

## Interesting Facts

- The Iliad ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iliad)) covers only part of the Trojan War and ends with Hector's funeral, not Troy's fall.
- Ancient cities such as Smyrna and Chios competed to claim Homer as their own.
- Some ancient legends about Homer were already fictional by the 5th century BCE.
- The Odyssey ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Odyssey)) reaches Ithaca at about its midpoint, leaving much of the poem for disguise, recognition, and revenge.
- Greek readers treated the Homeric epics ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homer)) as sources of education, unity, heroism, morality, and practical instruction.
