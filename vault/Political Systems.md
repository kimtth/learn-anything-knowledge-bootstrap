# Political Systems

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> Political systems include formal state institutions, the informal habits and relationships through which states work, and the broader processes linking politics to society. This account compares constitutional, empirical, and systems analysis and considers sovereignty, nation-states, supranational and subnational politics, authority, succession, and conflict.

## Introduction

A political system ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Political_system)) can mean the formal legal institutions of a government or state. It can also mean the actual behaviour through which the state functions, including informal rules, political habits, and relationships among groups. In the broadest sense, it is a set of interacting processes within society, connected with economic, social, and other nonpolitical systems.

These definitions lead to different kinds of study. Constitutional analysis examines legal structures and official offices. Empirical analysis compares how governments really operate. Systems analysis studies politics as a process of interaction within the larger social order. The full study of political systems needs all three approaches.

The most important modern political form is the nation-state. The world is divided into more than 190 countries, each claiming sovereignty, final authority, and the right to command obedience within its territory. Yet political life also exists above and below the nation-state, so political systems can be distinguished as supranational, national, and subnational.

## Legitimacy and Authority

Legitimacy is the belief that political authority has a rightful claim to obedience. A government can use police, armies, courts, taxes, and bureaucracy, but coercion alone rarely sustains a political system for long. Rulers seek legitimacy through tradition, religion, law, elections, revolution, nationalism, ideology, performance, security, or economic success. Political systems become unstable when official authority loses credibility, when rival groups claim a better right to rule, or when institutions can no longer convert conflict into accepted decisions.

## Typologies of Government

![[attachments/political_systems_001.png|420]]

*Political system diagram*

Political systems may be classified by level, structure, and method of rule. Supranational systems include empires, leagues, commonwealths, regional unions, and global organizations. National systems include unitary and federal states. Subnational systems include cities, regions, provinces, and local communities.

Traditional categories such as monarchy, tyranny, oligarchy, and constitutional government still matter historically, but they do not fully describe modern politics. A monarch may be ceremonial or absolute. A republic may be democratic or dictatorial. A constitution may describe formal offices while real power lies elsewhere.

## Empires

[[The History of European Overseas Exploration and Empires (European Exploration)|Empires]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Empire)) are supranational systems made of peoples with different cultures and ethnic backgrounds. Because they rule over diversity, they are often held together by coercion and the threat of reconquest. They centralize power and usually give little effective representation to their component peoples.

Ancient empires such as [[Egypt]], China, Persia, and imperial Rome could maintain order for long periods. They were based on military force and religious belief, but they also built bureaucracies, legal structures, irrigation systems, roads, and conditions for high civilization. They often claimed to provide universal order.

Modern colonial empires were different. Some colonies were settled by immigrants from the imperial country and built institutions similar to the metropolitan centre. Other territories were ruled by imperial bureaucracies over native populations. These governments were usually more coercive and often focused on extraction and administration.

## Leagues and the Commonwealth

![[attachments/political_systems_002.webp|420]]

*United Nations General Assembly*

Leagues are associations of states that combine forces against common threats. Ancient Greek leagues, European city leagues, and the League of Nations shared features such as a charter, an assembly, an executive organ, and some judicial or arbitral machinery.

The League of Nations was a major 20th-century experiment in supranational organization. Its Covenant followed World War I, with Woodrow Wilson as a leading advocate. It had an Assembly, Council, Secretariat, and specialized agencies, but its ability to prevent conflict depended on member cooperation that often failed.

The Commonwealth developed from the British Empire into a voluntary association of equal members. The Statute of Westminster in 1931 established equality of status, and the London Declaration in 1949 allowed republics to remain members while recognizing the British monarch symbolically. Later members included former colonies and even states without direct colonial ties to Britain.

## The European Union and United Nations

![[attachments/political_systems_003.webp|420]]

*European Parliament*

The European Union is difficult to classify because it has both confederal and federal elements. Its predecessors included the European Coal and Steel Community, the European Economic Community, and Euratom. These institutions developed regulatory and directive powers that went beyond ordinary cooperation among states.

The [[United Nations]] is another supranational system that does not fit older categories. Founded in 1945, it grew from 51 members to more than 190 by the early 21st century. Its purposes include international peace, friendly relations, and cooperation on economic, social, cultural, and humanitarian problems.

The UN includes the Security Council, General Assembly, secretary-general and Secretariat, Economic and Social Council, Trusteeship Council, International Court of Justice, and specialized agencies. Its weaknesses are serious, especially the difficulty of agreement among the five permanent Security Council members. Still, it remains an important forum for debate, negotiation, and functional cooperation.

## National Political Systems

A nation-state claims independent power to compel obedience within a territory. It may be large or small, populous or tiny, culturally unified or highly diverse. Its distinctive features include compulsory membership for citizens, a claimed monopoly over armed force, and government officers who act in the name of the land and people.

Power alone does not fully define a state. Some states cannot make their claims effective because rival groups challenge them. States also differ in institutions, purposes, and methods. These differences appear especially in the contrast between unitary and federal systems.

A unitary state concentrates legal sovereignty in the central government, though it may delegate authority to regions or localities. Examples include Britain, France, Japan, Poland, Spain, many Scandinavian countries, and many Latin American and African states. Some unitary systems allow broad local autonomy in practice, while others supervise local governments closely.

A federal state divides authority constitutionally between national and regional governments. Federalism may respond to territory, diversity, history, or administrative need. In both federal and unitary states, modern government requires some territorial distribution of administration because no central authority can manage all local affairs directly.

## Cities

[[Cities]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/City)) were among the earliest complex political systems. They emerged in river valleys such as the Euphrates and Tigris, the Nile, the Huang He, and the Yangtze. They replaced tribal kinship with class, occupation, religious institutions, kingship, specialized officials, taxation, and organized military power.

Early city civilizations could project power into surrounding countryside and build empires such as Sumer, Egypt, China, Babylonia, Assyria, and Persia. The Greek polis was different because it developed forms of citizen participation and public debate that contrasted with many earlier city monarchies.

[[Cities]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/City)) remain important subnational systems because they concentrate population, wealth, administration, and conflict. Local governments must manage services, planning, taxation, order, and representation. Large cities often become political forces in their own right, not merely administrative subdivisions.

## Regions

Regions stand between local and national communities. Some, such as the Swiss canton, German Land, English county, or American state, have their own political institutions. Others are cultural, linguistic, economic, ethnic, or geographic communities without full political structures.

[[France]] shows how regional systems can change. Before the French Revolution, France had old provinces such as Burgundy, Gascony, Brittany, Normandy, Provence, Anjou, and Poitou. Napoleonic governments replaced them with departements partly to weaken regional patriotism. Later planning revived larger regions that often resembled older provincial identities.

Regionalism can support autonomy, administrative efficiency, or cultural identity, but it can also raise fears of separatism. Italy illustrates both sides: some regions such as Valle d'Aosta, Sardinia, Sicily, Trentino-Alto Adige, and Friuli-Venezia Giulia received special status, while national governments sometimes resisted regional structures because of political competition.

## Succession and Transfer of Power

Every political system must solve the problem of [[Inheritance and Succession (Inheritance)|succession]]. Rule may pass by heredity, constitutional prescription, election, force, or some combination. Orderly succession preserves continuity, while disputed succession can produce crisis, violence, or collapse.

The [[United States of America (United States)|United States]] illustrates constitutional succession through Article II and the Twenty-fifth Amendment, ratified in 1967. These rules allowed vice presidents such as Chester A. Arthur, Theodore Roosevelt, Calvin Coolidge, Harry S. Truman, Lyndon B. Johnson, and Gerald R. Ford to assume office under defined arrangements.

Election can also create succession problems when results are contested or procedures are unclear. The Great Schism in the papacy and disputes over Hungary's elective kingship show older failures of elective succession. In some modern states, rulers seize power and then receive only formal electoral confirmation.

The transfer of power is related but distinct. It asks how a present ruler may be removed. Constitutional methods, votes, revolutions, coups, civil wars, secession, rebellions, guerrilla war, terrorism, class warfare, and peasant revolts all show ways in which authority can be challenged.

## Revolution, Coup, and Internal Conflict

A revolution usually follows growing strain within a political system. Challenges to authority increase, legitimacy declines, power becomes more coercive, and conflict reaches a climax in the destruction of the old order. Revolutions change not only rulers but the political order itself.

A coup d'etat overthrows a government without immediately changing the whole political order. It is usually carried out by an individual or group inside government or the ruling class. Sometimes a coup becomes a preliminary stage to revolution, as with Napoleon I, Napoleon III, Mussolini, and Hitler.

Internal conflict has many causes. Rapid social and economic change can create new classes and grievances. Established elites may refuse to share power. Wealth distribution, intellectual alienation, ideological conflict, factional struggles, charismatic leadership, oppressive rule, and weak rule can all produce violent challenges to government.

## Monarchy

Monarchy ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monarchy)) has changed greatly in the modern world. A king or queen may be a ceremonial head of state in a parliamentary democracy or an absolute ruler in an autocratic regime. The same title can therefore conceal very different political realities.

In modern [[Europe]], royal lines survived mainly where monarchs lost or failed to acquire effective governing power. Dynasties such as the Hohenzollerns, Habsburgs, and Romanovs lost their thrones because they continued to rule as well as reign. In Britain, the Netherlands, and Denmark, monarchs became symbols of unity and state authority while cabinets, parliaments, ministries, and parties govern.

The 20th century also saw many non-Western hereditary monarchies disappear. Thrones fell in Turkey, China, many Arab countries, Indian principalities, African tribal kingdoms, and parts of Southeast Asia. Modern monarchy survived best when it became ceremonial or adapted to constitutional limits.

## Dictatorship

Dictatorship flourished even as hereditary monarchy declined. The 20th century included figures such as Ataturk, Benito Mussolini, Juan Peron, Tito, Gamal Abdel Nasser, Sukarno, Kwame Nkrumah, and Charles de Gaulle, making it an age of plebiscitary dictatorship in many settings.

In many new African and Asian states, constitutional systems inherited from colonial powers failed. Presidents and prime ministers banned opposition parties, while armies seized power and created military dictatorships. Weak middle classes, traditions of autocracy, social disorder, and the army's organization all helped dictatorships emerge.

Latin American caudillismo was a different form of monocratic rule. After central authority collapsed in former Spanish colonies, army officers with private armies and peasant support often seized control. Such regimes reflected personal leadership, military force, and weak national institutions.

## Oligarchy

Oligarchy means rule by the few. Although the term is less often used for modern systems, small irresponsible ruling groups have not disappeared. In some preindustrial societies, elites came from hereditary castes or ruling classes separated by religion, kinship, wealth, prestige, or language.

[[Modernization and Industrialization (Industrialization)|Industrialization]] weakened older oligarchies by requiring skill, merit, and technical ability. Yet it also created new elites in bureaucracy, business, administration, and parties. In advanced societies, governing elites may no longer come from one class, but they may still escape effective restraint and use state power to protect their interests.

Oligarchic tendencies also appear inside large bureaucratic structures. Political parties, legislatures, agencies, and other institutions can develop leadership groups that preserve influence. This makes oligarchy a recurring risk even in formally democratic systems.

## Legislatures and Divisions of Government

Modern governments divide authority among institutions such as executives, legislatures, courts, and bureaucracies. Legislatures differ in structure and procedure. Some are bicameral, as in the United States, Australia, Brazil, Canada, Germany, and India; others are unicameral, especially in small or unitary countries such as New Zealand and Denmark.

Legislative procedure varies widely. The United States House of Representatives uses elaborate rules partly derived from a manual by Thomas Jefferson. Voting may be by formal division, teller vote, or electronic methods. Presiding officers may be neutral umpires, majority-party leaders, or mostly ceremonial officials.

Legislative parties assign committee seats, fill offices, and organize debate. In the U.S. House, committee membership reflects party strength, but party discipline is weaker than in many British and European legislatures. These differences shape how laws are made and how governments are held accountable.

## Self-Preservation and Political Socialization

The first right of individuals and countries is self-preservation. For a state, this means more than protection from foreign violence. It also means maintaining the attachment of citizens and preventing civil war, which destroys the government's monopoly over legitimate force.

Political authority is most secure when the community agrees on basic regime principles. [[Ideology]], custom, religion, shared need, fear of enemies, and patriotism can help create that agreement. Governments therefore use education and civic socialization to strengthen attachment to the regime.

Political socialization begins early, through family, school, and public institutions. Education teaches duties and loyalties as well as skills. A regime that neglects civic attachment risks losing the consent and cooperation needed for order.

## Political Change

Political systems change through revolution, gradual ideas, and structural revision. The American Revolution, French Revolution, and Russian Revolution changed not only their own societies but also political ideas worldwide. Their influence reached later independence movements and revolutionary ideologies.

Change can also occur through the slow force of [[The History of Western Political Philosophy (Political Philosophy)|political philosophy]]. Ideas about liberty, representation, socialism, nationalism, development, and social justice can transform expectations without a single violent break. New beliefs may gradually undermine old regimes or reshape institutions.

Structural revision changes the formal and informal arrangements of government without necessarily replacing the regime. Reformers and revolutionaries both understand that changing institutions can change policy outcomes. Electoral rules, territorial divisions, legislative procedures, executive powers, and administrative structures can all alter political results.
