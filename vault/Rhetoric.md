# Rhetoric

## Introduction

![[attachments/rhetoric_001.webp|420]]

*Cicero drawing*

Rhetoric ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rhetoric)) is the art and theory of training communicators who seek to persuade or inform. Traditionally it focused on the speaker or writer who creates discourse. In the 20th century, emphasis shifted toward the auditor or reader and toward how audiences receive, interpret, and are moved by language.

Classical rhetoric began in ancient Greece around the 5th century BC as instruction in public speaking for citizens of Greek republics and later for wealthy Roman students. For about 2,000 years, it stood near the centre of western education. Quintilian's Institutio oratoria ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Institutio_Oratoria)), written before AD 96, became one of the most influential educational texts.

Modern rhetoric is not only a list of stylistic terms. It studies how discourse works in relation to audience, situation, argument, interpretation, and persuasion. It overlaps with literary criticism, philosophy, history, communication, and social sciences but keeps its special concern with the effects of language.

## Figures, Tropes, and Schemes

Classical rhetoricians invented many terms still used in literary criticism ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Literary_criticism)). A trope changes or turns meaning locally, while a scheme shapes structure or arrangement. The categories can overlap, but they show how rhetoric studies both the texture of words and the larger form of discourse.

Important tropes include metaphor, simile, personification, irony, hyperbole, understatement, and metonymy. A metaphor may compare unlike things directly, as in John Donne's comparison of separated lovers to the legs of a geometer's compass in A Valediction: Forbidding Mourning.

Important schemes include allegory, parallelism, antithesis, congeries, apostrophe, enthymeme, rhetorical question, and gradatio. John Bunyan's Pilgrim's Progress extends the metaphor of salvation as a journey so fully that it becomes the structure of the whole work.

## Ancient Greece and Rome

![[attachments/rhetoric_002.webp|420]]

*Portrait of Cicero*

Rhetoric ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rhetoric)) has long been linked with democracy. Ancient accounts locate its beginnings in Syracuse in the 5th century BC, when citizens returning after tyranny needed to argue legal claims over confiscated land. Teachers began offering systematic instruction to help litigants persuade judges and audiences.

In [[Athens]], rhetoric became tied to education, politics, and philosophy. The Sophists taught argumentation and public effectiveness, making rhetoric central to civic life. Later attacks by [[Plato and Platonism (Plato)|Plato]] made sophistic seem like shallow or deceptive reasoning, but the Sophists also treated rhetoric as a serious art of analysis and invention.

Plato's [[Socrates]] criticized false persuasion but imagined an ideal rhetoric joined to truth and knowledge of the soul. Isocrates offered a broad educational ideal that united verbal skill, practical wisdom, and civic judgment. [[Aristotle and Aristotelianism|Aristotle]] gave rhetoric a systematic place beside dialectic by analyzing persuasion, audience, and kinds of proof.

## The Five Offices

Classical rhetoric came to include five offices. Invention finds arguments and materials; disposition arranges them; elocution fits words to topic, speaker, audience, and occasion; pronunciation or action delivers the speech; and memory stores the material for performance.

These offices made rhetoric a productive art for creating discourse. Orations, poems, plays, letters, and many other language forms could fall under rhetorical training. Only logic or dialectic remained clearly separate. When free public deliberation declined, however, the civic role of rhetoric became less secure.

## The Middle Ages

St. Augustine created one of the first Christian rhetorics in Book IV of On Christian Doctrine. He adapted rhetoric for ministers who needed to make revealed truth effective in speech. The first three books addressed biblical interpretation, making interpretation part of rhetorical invention.

In the 13th century, Thomas Aquinas and Peter of Spain helped place logic above rhetoric in advanced education. Thomas shaped theological curriculum through abstract symbolic reasoning, while Peter influenced arts education through dialectic and disputation. Rhetoric remained useful but no longer served as the highest professional discipline.

## Renaissance, Ramism, and Fragmentation

The 16th century brought strong interest in vernacular rhetorics as European writers became more self-conscious about native languages. Petrus Ramus and his followers fragmented classical rhetoric by assigning invention and disposition to dialectic and leaving rhetoric mainly with style and delivery.

This shift weakened the older unity of rhetorical theory. Parts of rhetoric became separate specialties: logic handled invention, grammar and style handled language, and elocution handled spoken performance. The older art of public discourse became a collection of disconnected arts.

## Elocution and Interpretation

Later elocutionists tried to restore voice, gesture, and performance to language. Some of their theories of posture and expression became exaggerated or mechanical, but they were important because they made interpretation central. They asked how written words became meaningful spoken discourse.

Thomas Sheridan drew on John Locke's idea that words are signs of ideas and tones are signs of passions. This created a split between printed discourse and spoken language. Other rhetoricians used faculty psychology and associationism to recover parts of rhetoric, such as emotional appeal and the passions.

## Rhetoric in Philosophy: The New Rhetoric

The new rhetoric rejects the idea that rhetoric is merely ornamental expression. It treats rhetoric as a practical theory of argumentation that studies techniques used to gain or increase the adherence of minds to claims presented for assent. Its focus is persuasive action on audiences.

This approach continues [[Aristotle and Aristotelianism|Aristotle]]'s concern with hearers and dialectical reasoning, but it expands rhetoric to all kinds of audiences and arguments. It studies how argumentation begins, develops, and produces effects. It also opposes purely stylistic rhetoric, which reduces the field to figures of speech.

The new rhetoric is interested in presence, the way discourse brings ideas, values, or facts before the audience's mind. It studies figures and forms only insofar as they help persuade, organize attention, or make something matter to hearers and readers.

## Key Events & Developments

- 5th century BC: Rhetoric ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rhetoric)) emerged in Greek civic and legal contexts.
- Before AD 96: Quintilian wrote Institutio oratoria ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Institutio_Oratoria)), a major educational work.
- The Middle Ages: St. Augustine adapted rhetoric for Christian preaching and interpretation.
- 16th century: Petrus Ramus helped fragment rhetoric by moving invention and arrangement into dialectic.
- 20th century: Modern and new rhetorics shifted attention toward audiences, readers, argumentation, and interpretation.

## Key People

- [[Plato and Platonism (Plato)|Plato]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plato)), Isocrates ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isocrates)), and [[Aristotle and Aristotelianism|Aristotle]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aristotle)): Defined major ancient debates over rhetoric, truth, education, and persuasion.
- Quintilian ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quintilian)): Made rhetoric a comprehensive educational ideal for the orator.
- St. Augustine ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St._Augustine)): Joined Christian interpretation and rhetorical preaching.
- Petrus Ramus ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Petrus_Ramus)): Fragmented classical rhetoric into separate arts.
- Thomas Sheridan: Helped develop elocution as the study of voice and expression.

## Interesting Facts

- Enthymeme is a rhetorical argument in which the audience supplies a missing premise.
- Allegory can become the structural principle of a whole work, not just a local figure.
- Rhetoric ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rhetoric)) once included much more than speeches, extending to letters, poems, plays, and other discourse.
- New rhetoric ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_rhetoric)) studies argumentation and audience adherence rather than ornament alone.
