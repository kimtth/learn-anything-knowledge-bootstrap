# International Relations, 20th-Century

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> Twentieth-century international relations were shaped by two world wars, the unstable Versailles settlement, decolonization, the Cold War, nuclear crisis management, and the post-Cold War search for regional order. The subject explains how ideology, power, institutions, and economic interdependence transformed global politics.

## Introduction

![[international_relations_20th_001.webp|420]]

*United Nations Security Council meeting*

Twentieth-century international relations describe the shifting relations among states, especially the great powers, from the late imperial age through World War I, the peace settlement, the interwar breakdown, World War II, the Cold War, decolonization, detente, renewed confrontation, the collapse of Soviet power, and the unsettled post-Cold War order. The century opened with Europe still at the centre of world politics and closed with a more dispersed system in which the United States, Russia, China, the European Union, Japan, regional powers, international organizations, economic networks, ethnic conflicts, and transnational movements all mattered.

The central transformation was the destruction of the old European balance of power and its replacement by a global system. The first half of the century was dominated by European rivalries, imperial competition, total war, and ideological conflict. The second half was shaped by the superpower confrontation between the United States and the Soviet Union, the nuclear age, the division and later reunification of Germany, the emergence of Communist China, decolonization across Asia and Africa, Middle Eastern wars, and the gradual erosion of bipolar certainty.

## Roots of World War I

The long peace after 1871 rested on a European balance shaped by German unification, British maritime strength, French resentment over Alsace-Lorraine, Russian ambition, Austro-Hungarian fragility, Ottoman decline, and expanding nationalism. Otto von Bismarck treated the new German Empire as a satisfied power and tried to isolate France while preventing hostile coalitions. His diplomacy produced arrangements such as the Dreikaiserbund, the German alliance with Austria-Hungary in 1879, the Triple Alliance with Italy in 1882, and the Reinsurance Treaty with Russia in 1887.

Bismarck's system depended on restraint, secrecy, and personal skill. It was vulnerable because Austria-Hungary and Russia competed over the Balkans, France sought recovery, Italy pursued Mediterranean ambitions, Britain guarded imperial routes, and Germany's political structure combined modern industry with authoritarian rule. Once Bismarck fell from office in 1890, German policy became less coherent, more naval and colonial, and more alarming to other powers.

The Habsburg and Ottoman problems made southeastern Europe dangerous. Austria-Hungary held Germans, Magyars, Czechs, Slovaks, Croats, Serbs, Romanians, Poles, Ruthenes, Slovenes, Italians, and others under a dynastic framework increasingly challenged by nationalism. The Ottoman Empire's retreat encouraged Balkan peoples to seek independence or expansion. Russia claimed a protective role toward Slavs and Orthodox Christians, while Austria-Hungary feared that South Slav nationalism would destroy the empire from within.

The New Imperialism ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Imperialism)) of the late 19th century intensified rivalry. European powers partitioned large parts of Africa and Asia, sometimes for markets and resources but often for prestige, strategic position, domestic politics, and technological confidence. Britain occupied Egypt in 1882, France expanded in Africa and Asia, Germany sought colonial status, Italy pursued Libya and East Africa, Russia pushed into Central Asia and Manchuria, Japan industrialized after the Meiji Restoration, and the United States entered overseas empire after the Spanish-American War.

Alliance blocs hardened as France and Russia concluded a military alliance in 1894, Britain and France formed the Entente Cordiale in 1904, and Britain and Russia settled Asian disputes in 1907. The Triple Entente was not originally built as an anti-German military alliance, but German naval expansion, Moroccan crises, the Anglo-German dreadnought race, and the instability of Austria-Hungary turned it into a counterweight to the Triple Alliance.

By 1914, Europe contained explosive pressures: militarism, mass mobilization plans, nationalism, imperial rivalry, domestic social tension, economic competition, and mistrust. The assassination of Archduke Francis Ferdinand at Sarajevo by a South Slav nationalist sparked the July Crisis, but the deeper causes lay in alliance mechanisms, fear of encirclement, Austro-Serbian conflict, Russian prestige, German risk-taking, French commitment to Russia, and Britain's concern for European balance and Belgian neutrality.

## World War I

[[World Wars (World War I)|World War I]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_War_I)) began in 1914 as a European crisis and became a global war. Germany sought a decisive victory before its enemies could fully mobilize, but the Schlieffen-style advance through Belgium failed to defeat France quickly. The Western Front hardened into trenches, while the Eastern Front remained more fluid but equally destructive. Prewar expectations of short war, offensive superiority, and national unity collided with machine guns, rapid-fire artillery, barbed wire, rail mobilization, and industrial slaughter.

The war of illusions destroyed political assumptions. Generals misjudged technology, socialists failed to stop patriotic mobilization, monarchies hoped war would strengthen old regimes but instead collapsed, liberals expected freedom but accepted censorship and command economies, and imperial planners discovered that global power depended on industrial depth, finance, shipping, colonial resources, and domestic endurance.

The war widened as Japan entered against Germany in 1914, occupying German Pacific islands and Tsingtao; the Ottoman Empire joined the Central Powers and opened Middle Eastern, Caucasian, and Dardanelles fronts; Italy left neutrality and joined the Allies in 1915; Bulgaria joined the Central Powers; Romania entered for the Allies and suffered defeat; and colonial campaigns drew Africa, Asia, and the Pacific into the conflict.

Stalemate produced total war. Belligerents searched for allies, new weapons, peripheral theatres, economic blockade, submarine warfare, aerial attack, chemical weapons, tanks, propaganda, and mobilization of whole societies. Britain used naval blockade; Germany turned to U-boats; the Ottoman alliance threatened Russia and the British empire; and the Middle East became a theatre where wartime promises to Arabs, Zionists, and imperial partners later collided.

Russia's collapse in 1917 transformed the war. Economic strain, casualties, inflation, food shortages, and political weakness produced the March Revolution, the abdication of the tsar, the Provisional Government, and then Bolshevik seizure of power. Vladimir Lenin promised peace, land, and bread, and the Treaty of Brest-Litovsk in 1918 removed Russia from the Allied side at huge territorial cost.

American entry in 1917 also transformed the war. German unrestricted submarine warfare, the Zimmermann Telegram, and Woodrow Wilson's liberal internationalist vision brought the United States into the conflict. American manpower and finance strengthened the Allies, while Wilson's Fourteen Points framed the struggle as a war for democracy, self-determination, open diplomacy, free trade, and a new League of Nations.

The final year was a four-way contest among German imperial strategy, Allied war aims, Wilsonian liberalism, and Leninist revolution. Germany's spring offensives in 1918 failed to win before American strength arrived. Allied counteroffensives, German exhaustion, blockade, domestic unrest, and the collapse of Austria-Hungary, Bulgaria, and the Ottoman Empire led to the Armistice of November 11, 1918.

## Peacemaking and the Versailles Order
Peacemaking after 1918 faced devastation, exhausted publics, revolutionary unrest, fallen empires, economic dislocation, nationalist claims, colonial expectations, and incompatible Allied goals. The Paris Peace Conference opened in 1919 with 27 nations present, but major decisions were made by the Big Three: Woodrow Wilson, David Lloyd George, and Georges Clemenceau. Italy and Japan mattered on specific issues but were less central to the European settlement.

Wilson's program made the League of Nations the symbol of a new diplomacy, but the League's Covenant relied mostly on consultation, moral pressure, economic sanctions, and voluntary military action. France wanted firm security against Germany, Britain wanted a stable balance without excessive commitments, and the United States ultimately refused to join the League when the U.S. Senate rejected the treaty.

The Treaty of Versailles imposed territorial losses, disarmament, occupation provisions, reparations liability, and war-guilt language on Germany. France sought a Rhineland buffer or guarantees, Britain balanced punishment with fears of destabilizing Germany, and Wilson tried to reconcile national self-determination with security and practical compromise. Germany denounced the settlement as a Diktat, helping feed revisionism.

Central and eastern Europe were reorganized from the wreckage of the German, Habsburg, Russian, and Ottoman empires. Poland reappeared but gained a corridor to the sea that left German and Kashubian populations inside its borders. Czechoslovakia gained defensible frontiers but included Sudeten Germans and Magyars. Romania expanded and acquired large minority populations. The Baltic states won independence. Yugoslavia united South Slavs but carried deep internal tensions. Hungary lost large territories under Trianon.

Self-determination proved difficult because populations were intermixed. Vilnius, Teschen, Upper Silesia, the Sudetenland, Transylvania, Macedonia, the Adriatic coast, and the Polish Corridor showed that ethnic maps, historical claims, strategic needs, economic assets, and great-power bargaining rarely matched. The settlement created new states but also new minorities and grievances.

The Russian question haunted the settlement. Bolshevik revolution removed Russia from the Allied coalition, repudiated debts, inspired Communist movements, and provoked Western intervention and fear. Allied efforts to support anti-Bolshevik forces were inconsistent and unpopular, while the Bolsheviks used both diplomacy and the Comintern to survive, encourage revolution, and exploit divisions among capitalist states.

Reparations and debts shaped the 1920s. The London schedule of 1921 set German obligations, while inflation and fiscal crisis destabilized Weimar Germany. France used pressure, including the Ruhr occupation of 1923, to enforce payment. The Dawes Plan in 1924 and Young Plan in 1929 internationalized the problem through American loans, adjusted payments, restored German financial independence, and linked European recovery to U.S. credit.

European security systems remained fragile. France built alliances with Poland, Czechoslovakia, Romania, and Yugoslavia, while the Little Entente tried to contain Hungarian revisionism and Habsburg restoration. Italy maneuvered in Albania, the Adriatic, and among defeated states. Germany under Gustav Stresemann pursued fulfillment, Locarno cooperation, League membership, and gradual revision, while refusing to accept permanent eastern borders.

The Locarno era briefly suggested reconciliation. The Locarno Treaties of 1925, Germany's admission to the League in 1926, the Kellogg-Briand Pact of 1928, and Briand's talk of European union reflected hopes for moral disarmament and diplomatic normalization. Yet disarmament failed, Germany still sought equality, France doubted security, Britain avoided hard commitments, and eastern Europe remained exposed.

## Interwar Breakdown
The Great Depression shattered the fragile economic and political basis of interwar diplomacy. The Wall Street crash, banking failures, deflation, unemployment, trade collapse, tariffs, currency disputes, and domestic radicalization undermined moderate governments. International conferences on debt, currency, trade, and disarmament failed as states turned inward and authoritarian movements gained strength.

Weimar Germany became especially vulnerable. Depression, Versailles resentment, fear of Communism, political fragmentation, emergency rule, paramilitary violence, and elite miscalculation allowed the Nazi movement to grow. Adolf Hitler became chancellor on January 30, 1933, then dismantled democratic institutions, outlawed opposition, coordinated society, rebuilt the armed forces, and made foreign revision central to his regime.

Nazi diplomacy challenged Versailles step by step. Germany withdrew from the League and Disarmament Conference in 1933, announced conscription in 1935, concluded the Anglo-German Naval Agreement, remilitarized the Rhineland in 1936, aligned with Fascist Italy and Japan, intervened in the Spanish Civil War, annexed Austria in 1938, and demanded the Sudetenland.

Appeasement grew from war-weariness, military unpreparedness, economic fear, anti-Communism, sympathy for some revisionist claims, and hope that limited concessions might satisfy Germany. The Munich Conference of 1938 handed the Sudetenland to Germany without Czech participation. Neville Chamberlain believed war had been averted, while Winston Churchill warned that Britain had suffered defeat without fighting.

Collective security failed in Manchuria, Ethiopia, Spain, Austria, Czechoslovakia, and China. Japan seized Manchuria in 1931 and later expanded war in China. Italy invaded Ethiopia in 1935. The League lacked enforcement power, Britain and France hesitated, the United States remained largely isolationist, and the Soviet Union was distrusted even when it joined collective-security rhetoric.

The road to war accelerated in 1939. Hitler occupied the rest of Czechoslovakia, showing that his aims exceeded self-determination for Germans. Britain and France guaranteed Poland. The Nazi-Soviet Pact of August 1939 shocked the world by joining ideological enemies in a temporary bargain that divided eastern Europe. Germany invaded Poland on September 1, 1939, and Britain and France declared war.

## World War II
[[World Wars (World War II)|World War II]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_War_II)) again began over east-central Europe but became a global conflict. Germany overran Poland, Denmark, Norway, the Low Countries, and France. Britain survived alone in western Europe after 1940, protected by the Royal Navy, air defense, imperial resources, and increasing U.S. aid. Italy joined Germany but became a burden in Greece, North Africa, and the Mediterranean.

The war became total on a scale beyond World War I. Civilians became direct targets of bombing, occupation, deportation, forced labour, massacre, and genocide. Nazi ideology produced a war of extermination against Jews, Slavs, Roma, and others. Japanese imperial warfare in China and the Pacific included brutal occupation, racial violence, forced labour, and atrocities. Stalinist rule brought deportations, terror, and coercion in areas under Soviet control.

Germany's invasion of the Soviet Union in 1941 transformed the European war. Operation Barbarossa sought Lebensraum, ideological destruction, and strategic self-sufficiency. Initial German advances were enormous, but Soviet manpower, space, industrial relocation, winter, and resistance prevented decisive victory. The Eastern Front became the main theatre of German losses.

The United States moved toward war through destroyers-for-bases, lend-lease, hemispheric defense, escort clashes in the Atlantic, and aid to Britain and then the Soviet Union. Franklin Roosevelt described the United States as the arsenal of democracy. Japan's attack on Pearl Harbor in December 1941 brought the United States fully into World War II and fused the European and Pacific struggles.

The Grand Alliance united Britain, the United States, the Soviet Union, China, and many others against the Axis, but it contained deep disagreements. Winston Churchill defended imperial and balance-of-power concerns; Roosevelt emphasized anti-fascism, open commerce, and postwar institutions; Joseph Stalin demanded security, territory, and influence in eastern Europe; Chiang Kai-shek sought recognition and support for China.

Allied conferences shaped wartime diplomacy. Casablanca emphasized unconditional surrender; Cairo addressed Japan's losses in Asia; Tehran confirmed the cross-Channel invasion; Yalta dealt with Poland, Germany, the United Nations, and Soviet entry into the Pacific war; and Potsdam revealed mounting tension over Germany, reparations, eastern Europe, and Japan. Decisions made under wartime pressures became Cold War disputes.

Military turning points included the Battle of Britain, the failure before Moscow, Pearl Harbor, Midway, El Alamein, Stalingrad, Allied landings in North Africa and Italy, D-Day in Normandy in 1944, Soviet offensives into eastern Europe, and the island-hopping campaign in the Pacific. Germany surrendered in May 1945, and Japan surrendered after Soviet entry into the Pacific war and U.S. atomic bombing of Hiroshima and Nagasaki in August 1945.

The war's settlement destroyed the European great-power system. Germany and Japan were occupied and demilitarized; Italy lost empire; Britain and France emerged victorious but weakened; the United States and Soviet Union became superpowers; eastern Europe fell under Soviet dominance; China moved toward civil-war resolution; colonial empires lost legitimacy; and the United Nations was created to preserve peace more effectively than the League.

## Cold War in Europe
The Cold War ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cold_War)) emerged from incompatible security aims, ideology, mistrust, wartime decisions, and power vacuums. Soviet control in eastern Europe, pressure on Iran and Turkey, civil war in Greece, and disputes over Germany convinced U.S. officials that cooperation had failed. The Truman Doctrine of 1947 and Marshall Plan made containment global and economic, while the Soviet bloc rejected Western integration.

Germany became the core issue. The Western zones moved toward economic recovery and political consolidation, while the Soviet zone became a Communist state. The Berlin blockade of 1948 and Western airlift dramatized the division. NATO formed in 1949, the Federal Republic of Germany appeared in May, the German Democratic Republic in October, and later the Warsaw Pact formalized the military division of Europe.

The Soviet bloc was not perfectly monolithic. Josip Broz Tito broke with [[Stalin]] in 1948, showing that national Communist movements could resist Moscow. Yet Soviet control remained strong in Poland, Czechoslovakia, Hungary, Romania, Bulgaria, and East Germany through party rule, security services, purges, economic coordination, and military pressure.

Early Cold War assumptions hardened after the Soviet atomic bomb and the Communist victory in China in 1949. The United States viewed Communism as expansionist and centrally directed; the Soviet Union viewed U.S. alliances, bases, nuclear weapons, and economic power as encirclement. Both sides universalized local conflicts by reading them through ideological competition.

## Decolonization and Asian Conflicts
Decolonization reshaped international relations after 1945. India and Pakistan became independent in 1947 amid partition violence and enduring conflict over Kashmir. Indonesia won independence from the Dutch by 1949. The Philippines gained independence peacefully. Britain fought Communist insurgency in Malaya, while France fought the Viet Minh in Indochina and later faced crisis in Algeria.

Nonalignment arose as many new states resisted choosing between U.S. and Soviet blocs. Jawaharlal Nehru made India a leading nonaligned state, while other Asian and African leaders sought aid, autonomy, and diplomatic space. Yet border disputes, internal divisions, development pressures, and superpower competition often drew new states into Cold War alignments.

China's revolution altered the Asian balance. The Communist victory of [[Mao Zedong]] in 1949 raised the question of who lost China in U.S. politics, but China's civil war reflected deep domestic dynamics. The People's Republic aligned initially with the Soviet Union, while the Nationalists retreated to Taiwan under U.S. protection. The Sino-Soviet relationship later fractured, opening new strategic possibilities.

The Korean War began when North Korea invaded South Korea on June 25, 1950. United Nations forces led by the United States defended South Korea, crossed north after Inchon, and then faced Chinese intervention. The war ended in an armistice in 1953, leaving Korea divided near the 38th parallel and militarizing U.S. commitments in Asia and Europe.

Indochina and Vietnam became the most prolonged Cold War conflict involving decolonization. France's defeat at Dien Bien Phu in 1954 led to Geneva arrangements dividing Vietnam. The United States gradually replaced France as the main anti-Communist supporter in South Vietnam, escalated under Lyndon Johnson, fought a costly war, and withdrew after domestic exhaustion and Vietnamization. The fall of Saigon in 1975 marked a major U.S. setback.

## Middle Eastern Crises
The Middle East ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Middle_East)) became a recurring centre of crisis because of decolonization, oil, Arab nationalism, Israel, Suez, superpower rivalry, religious identity, regional leadership struggles, and local wars. Israel's creation in 1948 and Arab defeat created refugee, security, and legitimacy problems that shaped decades of diplomacy.

The Suez Crisis of 1956 revealed the decline of old imperial powers. Gamal Abdel Nasser nationalized the Suez Canal after Western withdrawal of Aswan Dam support. Britain, France, and Israel attacked Egypt, but U.S. and Soviet pressure forced withdrawal. Nasser emerged as a symbol of Arab nationalism, while Britain and France learned that imperial action without U.S. support had become untenable.

The Arab-Israeli conflict escalated in the 1960s. Nasser demanded withdrawal of UN forces from Sinai and closed the Gulf of Aqaba. Israel struck first in the Six-Day War of 1967, defeating Egypt, Jordan, and Syria and occupying Sinai, Gaza, the West Bank, East Jerusalem, and the Golan Heights. The war transformed Israeli security, Palestinian politics, Arab strategy, and superpower diplomacy.

The 1973 war began with Egyptian and Syrian attacks on Yom Kippur. Early Arab successes gave way to Israeli recovery, but the war restored Arab diplomatic leverage and produced an oil embargo that shook the global economy. U.S. shuttle diplomacy under Henry Kissinger led to disengagement agreements, while later Egyptian-Israeli negotiations produced the Camp David Accords and the 1979 peace treaty.

Iran and Iraq became central in the late Cold War. The Iranian Revolution of 1979 overthrew the shah and produced an Islamic republic hostile to U.S. influence. The Iran-Iraq War of 1980-1988 caused enormous casualties, threatened Gulf oil traffic, and drew outside powers into naval protection and arms politics. Iraq used missiles and chemical weapons, including against Kurdish populations.

## Nuclear Rivalry, Crisis, and Detente
Nuclear weapons changed diplomacy by making direct superpower war potentially catastrophic. The United States began with nuclear monopoly, the Soviet Union tested an atomic bomb in 1949, hydrogen bombs followed, and delivery systems evolved from bombers to missiles and submarines. Deterrence, arms control, civil defense, alliance credibility, and crisis management became central concerns.

The Cuban missile crisis in 1962 brought the superpowers closest to nuclear confrontation. Soviet missiles in Cuba led John F. Kennedy to impose a naval quarantine and demand removal. Nikita Khrushchev accepted withdrawal in exchange for a U.S. pledge not to invade Cuba, while U.S. Jupiter missiles were later quietly removed from Turkey. The crisis encouraged a hotline, caution, and the 1963 Partial Test-Ban Treaty.

Arms control ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arms_control)) continued through the Outer Space Treaty of 1967, the Nuclear Non-Proliferation Treaty of 1968, the Strategic Arms Limitation Talks, SALT I, the Anti-Ballistic Missile Treaty, and SALT II. These agreements limited some categories and stabilized expectations but did not stop modernization, multiple warheads, submarine systems, tactical nuclear concerns, or competition in conventional forces.

Detente in the 1970s reflected strategic parity, U.S. fatigue after Vietnam, Soviet desire for recognition and technology, West German Ostpolitik, European interest in stability, and Chinese-American rapprochement. Richard Nixon and Henry Kissinger used triangular diplomacy with Moscow and Beijing. The Helsinki Accords of 1975 accepted European frontiers but also strengthened human-rights language that later aided dissidents.

Detente contained contradictions. The Soviet Union saw it as recognition of equality and freedom to support friendly movements abroad; many Americans expected restraint in Third World competition. Conflicts in Angola, the Horn of Africa, Afghanistan, Central America, and elsewhere renewed suspicion. SALT II foundered after the Soviet invasion of Afghanistan in 1979, which convinced Jimmy Carter to shift toward renewed containment.

## Regional Conflicts and Late Cold War Strain
South Asia saw persistent conflict among India, Pakistan, and China. China suppressed Tibet in 1950, border disputes with India erupted into the Sino-Indian War of 1962, and Pakistan's alliances with the West complicated U.S.-Indian relations. The Indo-Pakistani wars and Bangladesh's emergence in 1971 reflected the mix of nationalism, partition legacies, superpower ties, and regional rivalry.

China's role shifted dramatically after the Sino-Soviet split. Border clashes and ideological rivalry made Beijing hostile to Moscow. U.S.-Chinese rapprochement in the 1970s gave Washington leverage against the Soviet Union, while China's later war with Vietnam in 1979 showed the limits of using the China card and the complexity of Communist-state rivalries.

[[Afghanistan]] became a Soviet quagmire after the 1979 invasion. Moscow intervened to stabilize a friendly Communist regime but faced mujahideen resistance, Islamic mobilization, Pakistani sanctuaries, U.S. and Saudi support, and growing domestic unease. Stinger missiles supplied in the mid-1980s made Soviet air operations more costly. Withdrawal was negotiated under Mikhail Gorbachev and completed in 1989.

[[Central America]] preoccupied the United States in the 1980s. The Sandinista revolution in Nicaragua overthrew Anastasio Somoza Debayle in 1979 but developed close ties with Cuba and the Soviet bloc. The Reagan administration supported Contra forces and aided El Salvador's government against leftist insurgents, while debates over human rights, covert action, and fears of another Vietnam divided U.S. politics.

The Reagan era combined military buildup, ideological pressure, Strategic Defense Initiative, support for anti-Communist forces, and eventual negotiation. Ronald Reagan denounced the Soviet Union but later engaged Gorbachev in summits. Arms-control talks moved from confrontation to the 1987 Intermediate-Range Nuclear Forces Treaty, which eliminated an entire class of missiles.

## Gorbachev and the End of the Cold War
Gorbachev's dilemma arose from Soviet economic stagnation, technological backwardness, costly military competition, diplomatic encirclement, unpopular foreign commitments, and pressure for reform. His policies of perestroika and glasnost aimed to revive the Soviet system, but openness encouraged criticism, national movements, and demands for democracy in the Soviet Union and eastern Europe.

New thinking in Soviet foreign policy reduced ideological rigidity, accepted interdependence, and sought reduced confrontation with the West. Gorbachev signaled that Moscow would not automatically use force to preserve Communist governments in eastern Europe. This undermined the coercive foundation of the Soviet bloc and encouraged reformers in Poland, Hungary, Czechoslovakia, East Germany, Bulgaria, and Romania.

The year 1989 became an annus mirabilis. Poland legalized Solidarity and held partly free elections, Hungary opened its border with Austria, East Germans fled westward, demonstrations filled Leipzig and other cities, the Berlin Wall fell on November 9, 1989, Czechoslovakia experienced the Velvet Revolution, Bulgaria changed leadership, and Romania violently overthrew Nicolae Ceausescu.

German reunification ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/German_reunification)) moved faster than many governments expected. West German chancellor Helmut Kohl issued a 10-point plan, East Germans voted for unity, and negotiations had to satisfy the United States, Soviet Union, Britain, France, NATO, the European Community, and Germany's neighbours. The Two Plus Four process produced agreement, and Germany was reunified in 1990 within NATO.

The Soviet Union itself could not contain the forces released by reform. Lithuania and other Baltic states demanded independence, nationalist movements spread, Boris Yeltsin challenged the Communist centre, and a failed hard-line coup in 1991 accelerated collapse. The Soviet Union dissolved in December 1991, ending the bipolar structure that had dominated world politics since the late 1940s.

## Post-Cold War Settlements and Conflicts
The end of the Cold War did not end international conflict. It removed the ideological and military framework that had organized many disputes, but ethnic nationalism, state collapse, regional ambitions, economic transition, humanitarian crises, terrorism, and unresolved borders produced new challenges. The United States became the leading global power, yet its ability to shape outcomes remained limited by local realities and coalition politics.

The Persian Gulf War followed Iraq's invasion of Kuwait in 1990. The United States built a broad coalition, gained UN authorization, and expelled Iraqi forces in 1991. The war demonstrated post-Cold War U.S. military power and concern for Gulf oil and sovereignty, but it left Saddam Hussein in power and created continuing containment, sanctions, inspections, and humanitarian controversies.

The Middle East peace process gained new momentum after the Gulf War. U.S. prestige, weakened rejectionist states, reduced Soviet influence, and Israeli-Palestinian exhaustion led to the Madrid Conference in 1991. The Oslo process followed, with mutual recognition between Israel and the Palestine Liberation Organization in 1993, but settlements, security, Jerusalem, refugees, borders, and mutual distrust kept peace fragile.

South Africa moved from apartheid toward majority rule as the Communist threat receded and internal pressure grew. F.W. de Klerk released Nelson Mandela in 1990, legalized the African National Congress, repealed racial classification laws, and negotiated a transition. The first all-race democratic election in 1994 brought Mandela to the presidency and ended one of the century's major racial-political conflicts.

The Balkans exposed the limits of European and U.S. policy after Yugoslavia began to disintegrate. Slovenia, Croatia, Macedonia, and Bosnia and Herzegovina declared independence as Serbian nationalism under Slobodan Milosevic and competing national movements drove war. UN peacekeepers lacked power to impose peace, ethnic cleansing shocked observers, and NATO air power and U.S. diplomacy eventually helped produce the Dayton Agreement in 1995.

Northern Ireland moved toward settlement through negotiation among British, Irish, unionist, nationalist, and paramilitary-linked actors. The Good Friday Agreement of 1998 created power sharing, cross-border institutions, and consent principles. Decommissioning and mistrust remained difficult, but the agreement marked a major achievement in ending long-running violence.

East Asian transitions included Hong Kong's return to China in 1997 under the formula of one country, two systems, and new tension over Taiwan's democratic politics. China's missile exercises near Taiwan in 1996 and disputes over the one China policy showed that sovereignty questions remained volatile even as regional economies integrated.

Transnational terrorism became more visible before the century ended. Bombings of U.S. embassies in Kenya and Tanzania in 1998, attributed to a network led by Osama bin Laden, showed that nonstate actors could strike globally. U.S. missile strikes against suspected sites in Sudan and Afghanistan foreshadowed a new security agenda beyond traditional great-power rivalry.

## Major Themes
Power and ideology repeatedly interacted. Nationalism helped destroy empires in 1914-1918, fascism and Nazism drove expansion and war in the 1930s, liberal internationalism shaped Wilsonian and later U.S. projects, Communism organized Soviet and Chinese policy, anti-colonial nationalism reshaped Asia and Africa, and human rights gained force in the late Cold War and post-Cold War years.

Technology changed the scale and nature of diplomacy. Railways, dreadnoughts, machine guns, submarines, radio, aircraft, tanks, strategic bombing, atomic weapons, missiles, satellites, computers, television, and global communications altered military planning, public opinion, intelligence, deterrence, crisis timing, economic competition, and propaganda.

[[Economics]] were inseparable from diplomacy. Reparations, war debts, inflation, depression, trade barriers, oil shocks, development aid, Marshall Plan recovery, Soviet stagnation, Japanese and European growth, debt crises, and globalization all affected the power of states. The collapse of Soviet power was partly a diplomatic story, but also a story of economic and technological exhaustion.

Resource politics gave economic interdependence strategic force. Oil shaped Middle Eastern diplomacy, naval planning, industrial recovery, inflation, and the bargaining power of producers and consumers. Food, credit, technology transfer, sanctions, development loans, and access to markets also became instruments of influence. By the late 20th century, power was measured not only by armies and alliances but by energy security, financial resilience, export capacity, and the ability to survive shocks in an interconnected world economy.

International institutions grew despite repeated failure. The League of Nations could not stop aggression, but it introduced habits of collective discussion. The United Nations survived superpower rivalry and became important for legitimacy, peacekeeping, decolonization, sanctions, and development. NATO endured beyond the Cold War, while European integration turned old Franco-German rivalry into institutional cooperation.

Small and medium states were never merely passive. Serbia, Belgium, Poland, Czechoslovakia, Finland, Yugoslavia, Israel, Egypt, Vietnam, Cuba, Afghanistan, Nicaragua, Iraq, South Africa, and others shaped great-power choices. Local actors often used superpower rivalry to pursue their own goals, while great powers repeatedly misunderstood local nationalism, religion, class, and historical grievance.

- Otto von Bismarck ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Otto_von_Bismarck)): Built a post-1871 alliance system designed to preserve German security and European stability.
- Woodrow Wilson ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Woodrow_Wilson)): Linked U.S. intervention in World War I to liberal internationalism and the League of Nations.
- David Lloyd George ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Lloyd_George)): Represented British interests and public pressures at the peace conference.
- Georges Clemenceau ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Georges_Clemenceau)): Sought French security against renewed German power.
- Vladimir Lenin ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vladimir_Lenin)): Took Russia out of World War I and made Bolshevik revolution a global diplomatic factor.
- Gustav Stresemann ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gustav_Stresemann)): Pursued German revision through negotiation and Locarno diplomacy.
- Adolf Hitler ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adolf_Hitler)): Destroyed Weimar democracy, overturned Versailles, and drove Europe into World War II.
- Benito Mussolini ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Benito_Mussolini)): Used Fascist Italy's ambitions to challenge the interwar order and align with Germany.
- Franklin Roosevelt ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Franklin_Roosevelt)): Led the United States from aid to Britain into global war and postwar planning.
- Winston Churchill ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Winston_Churchill)): Defended Britain against Nazi Germany and shaped Allied wartime diplomacy.
- Joseph Stalin ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph_Stalin)): Led the Soviet Union through war and imposed postwar control in eastern Europe.
- Harry Truman ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harry_Truman)): Defined containment, the Truman Doctrine, and early Cold War strategy.
- [[Mao Zedong]]: Brought Communist China into the international system and later split with Moscow.
- Gamal Abdel Nasser ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gamal_Abdel_Nasser)): Made Egypt a centre of Arab nationalism and Cold War Middle Eastern politics.
- John F. Kennedy ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_F._Kennedy)): Managed the Cuban missile crisis and early 1960s Cold War tensions.
- Richard Nixon ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_Nixon)): Opened relations with China and pursued detente with the Soviet Union.
- Henry Kissinger ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henry_Kissinger)): Practiced triangular diplomacy and Middle Eastern shuttle diplomacy.
- Ronald Reagan ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ronald_Reagan)): Combined anti-Soviet pressure with late Cold War negotiation.
- Mikhail Gorbachev ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mikhail_Gorbachev)): Introduced reforms and foreign-policy changes that helped end the Cold War.
- Helmut Kohl ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Helmut_Kohl)): Led West Germany through rapid reunification.
- Nelson Mandela ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nelson_Mandela)) and F.W. de Klerk ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/F.W._de_Klerk)): Negotiated South Africa's transition from apartheid.
- Slobodan Milosevic ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slobodan_Milosevic)): Used Serbian nationalism during Yugoslavia's collapse.

## Interesting Facts
- The 20th century began with Europe dominating world politics and ended with Europe integrated, Germany reunified, and power distributed across a more global system.
- [[World Wars (World War I)|World War I]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_War_I)) destroyed the German, Habsburg, Russian, and Ottoman imperial orders.
- The League of Nations ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/League_of_Nations)) failed as a security system but introduced practices later developed by the United Nations.
- Appeasement was shaped by moral revulsion against war as well as strategic misjudgment.
- [[World Wars (World War II)|World War II]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_War_II)) transformed the United States and Soviet Union into superpowers while weakening the old colonial empires.
- Nuclear deterrence made superpower war less likely but made every major crisis potentially catastrophic.
- Decolonization produced many new states that shaped Cold War strategy rather than simply receiving it.
- The Sino-Soviet split showed that Communist ideology did not erase national rivalry.
- The Helsinki human-rights language became a tool for dissidents inside the Soviet bloc.
- The Berlin Wall fell in 1989 less than a year before German reunification.
- The Cold War ended without a direct U.S.-Soviet war, but many proxy and regional conflicts caused enormous damage.
