# Automation

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> Automation integrates programmed commands, sensors, and feedback control so machines can perform and regulate processes with minimal direct human intervention. From industrial robots and telephone switching to aircraft guidance and urban transit, it extends the speed, repeatability, and coordination of computer-controlled systems while transforming the organization of work and infrastructure.

## Introduction

Automation ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Automation)) is the application of machines to tasks once performed by human beings or increasingly to tasks that humans could not perform at all. It differs from simple mechanization, which merely replaces labour with machines, by emphasizing the integration of machines into a self-governing system.

The term arose in the automobile industry in the mid-1940s, but the concept has spread far beyond manufacturing. Automation is one of the most transformative technologies of modern life.

## Definition and Basic Idea

In general, automation is a technology that performs a process through programmed commands combined with automatic feedback control. Program instructions tell the system what to do, and feedback verifies whether the commanded action has actually been carried out correctly.

This combination makes possible operation without constant human intervention. As automation became more dependent on computers, the systems involved became more powerful, flexible, and complex.

## Feedback Control

![[attachments/automation_001.webp|420]]

*Closed-loop feedback control system diagram*

Feedback control follows a standard control-loop model. The key elements are the input or set point, the process being controlled, the output that is measured, the sensors that detect that output, and the controller and actuators that reduce any difference between desired and actual results.

A room-heating system is used as an example: the desired temperature is the set point, the heater is the process, the measured room temperature is the output, the bimetallic strip is the sensing element, and switches or other mechanisms act to correct the result. The same logic applies to far more complex systems.

## Program Control and Feedback Together
Programming and feedback control are closely related. Programmed commands establish the desired sequence of actions or set points, while feedback loops check whether each step has actually been achieved.

Some steps can be handled in open-loop fashion without verification, but wherever variation in materials, environment, or process quality matters, feedback becomes essential. Modern automation usually stores its program in computer memory, making systems easier to alter and reconfigure.

## Robotics

![[attachments/automation_002.webp|420]]

*Industrial welding robot*

One of the most visible offshoots of automation is robotics. An industrial robot, as defined by the Robotic Industries Association, is a reprogrammable, multifunctional manipulator able to move materials, parts, tools, or specialized devices through programmed motions.

Robotics includes both the mechanical manipulator and the computer system that controls it. It is therefore a specialized branch of automation rather than a wholly separate field.

## Robot Structure and Motion

Robot manipulators are built from rigid links joined by movable joints, which may be linear or rotary. The manipulator is usually divided into an arm-and-body section for moving through space and a wrist for orienting the tool or workpiece.

Different robot anatomies create different work envelopes, making them suitable for different industrial tasks. At the end of the wrist there may be a gripper or a tool such as a welding gun.

## Robot Programming

The robot's computer must be programmed to teach it the required movement sequence. One method is lead-through programming, in which the manipulator is physically guided or driven through the desired motions while the computer records them.

This shows a wider point: automation depends not only on machinery but on the formal encoding of action into repeatable, controllable sequences.

## Communications Applications

Some of the earliest practical applications of automation appeared in telephone switching. Modern switching systems are built around digital computers that monitor lines, store dialed numbers, establish connections, ring phones, supervise calls, disconnect them, and often perform billing and self-diagnostic functions.

Automation in communications also includes local area networks, communications satellites, and automatic mail-sorting systems. These applications show that automation is not confined to factory production.

## Transportation Applications

Transportation has also been deeply transformed by automation. Examples given include airline reservation systems, automatic pilots in aircraft and locomotives, and automated urban mass-transit systems such as BART, MARTA, and Washington's Metrorail.

Reservation systems monitor flights and seat availability in real time. Automatic pilots use gyroscopes, radio beacons, and related instruments to guide aircraft. Urban rail systems use automation to coordinate train movement, speed, and spacing with a high degree of regularity.

## Social and Technical Significance

Although the subject is mainly technical, automation clearly changes not only methods of work but the whole organization of modern life. Areas as different as manufacturing, communications, transport, and control systems have all been reshaped by the substitution of programmed and self-regulating processes for direct human action.

Advanced automated systems can outperform humans in speed, repeatability, precision, and complexity of coordination. This is why automation has become foundational to many later technologies, especially those tied to computer control.

## Conclusion

Automation ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Automation)) is the integration of machines, programming, and feedback into systems capable of operating with minimal human intervention. It is more than machine use; it is the construction of processes that govern themselves according to encoded instructions and measured results.

From production lines to robots, telephone exchanges, aircraft guidance, and urban transit, automation appears as one of the defining technologies of the modern world. Its importance lies in the way it extends human capability while also reorganizing work, control, and information.
