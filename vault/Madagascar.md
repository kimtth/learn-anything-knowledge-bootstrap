# Madagascar

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> Madagascar is a large island country off southeastern Africa whose plants and animals are exceptionally distinctive and whose people have strong historical links to Indonesia. Its geography, French colonial connection, central plateau, and development challenges distinguish it from nearby mainland Africa.

## Introduction

Madagascar is an island country off southeastern Africa and the world's fourth largest island. Though only about 250 miles from Africa, its people are mainly linked to [[Indonesia]], and its plants and animals differ strongly from nearby Africa. Colonial ties also connected the island to [[France]] and French-speaking western Africa, while recent development centered on the central plateau and Antananarivo.

## Land

Madagascar lies in the southwestern Indian Ocean, separated from Africa by the Mozambique Channel ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mozambique_Channel)), about 250 miles wide.

## Relief

Relief ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Relief)) divides Madagascar into a central plateau, an eastern coastal strip, and western low plateaus and plains. The plateau stands about 2,500 to 4,500 feet high, tilts westward, and includes high massifs such as Tsaratanana, Ankaratra, and Andringitra; Maromokotro reaches 9,436 feet, the island's highest point.

Eastern slopes drop sharply along the Great Cliff and the Betsimisaraka Escarpment, while the west descends in steps toward the Mozambique Channel ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mozambique_Channel)). The east has a narrow alluvial coast with lagoons linked by the Pangalanes Canal; the west has sedimentary hills, deltas, mangroves, coral reefs, and volcanic islands such as Nosy Be.

## Drainage

Short, steep eastern rivers such as the Mandrare and Mananara run through waterfalls and rapids to lagoons or the sea. Longer western rivers, including the Onilahy, Mangoky, Tsiribihina, and Betsiboka, carry fertile alluvium to plains, estuaries, and sandbank-filled mouths.

Volcanic lakes include Lake Itasy, while Alaotra survives on the eastern slope and Lake Tsimanampetsotsa is a saline lake without an outlet near Toliara.

## Soils

The central plateau and east coast rest mainly on crystalline rocks such as gneiss, granite, and quartz. Weathered gneiss forms red murrum, laterite, and fertile red earths, giving Madagascar the name Great Red Island; valleys and Lake Alaotra hold especially productive alluvial and sedimentary soils.

## Climate

Madagascar has a hot wet season from November to April and a cooler dry season from May to October. Southeast trade winds bring heavy rain to the east coast, while the plateau receives mist and drizzle and the west lies in a rain shadow, with the southwest almost desert.

Rainfall varies sharply, from nearly 150 inches near Maroantsetra to about 14 inches at Toliara. Cyclones from the Indian Ocean, especially from December to March, bring destructive eastern floods. Forest loss, erosion, prairie grasses, southern xerophytes, lemurs, tenrecs, chameleons, crocodiles, harmless snakes, rich fisheries, and the offshore coelacanth show the island's unusual natural life.

The panther chameleon is one of Madagascar's distinctive reptiles.

## Streaked Tenrec

Hemicentetes semispinosus, the streaked tenrec, represents Madagascar's unusual insectivores.

## Coelacanth

Latimeria chalumnae, the coelacanth, is a living-fossil fish found in offshore waters.

## People

Human settlement is relatively recent, about 1,300 years. Language and culture point strongly to Indonesian origins, but the route and pattern of settlement remain debated; some explanations stress direct voyaging, while others stress movement through India, Arabia, and Africa. Coastal evidence also shows Afro-Arab influence before 1000 CE.

More than nine-tenths of the population is Malagasy, divided into about 20 named groups. The largest are the Merina of the plateau, followed by the eastern Betsimisaraka and the Betsileo around Fianarantsoa; other groups include the Tsimihety, Sakalava, Antandroy, Tanala, Antaimoro, Bara, and several smaller peoples.

## Languages

Malagasy, the national language, is written in the Latin alphabet and is a standardized form of Merina, an Austronesian language. It includes Bantu words and Bantu phonetic and grammatical elements, while local varieties remain mutually intelligible. French is officially recognized and widely used in education, English ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/English_language)) has increased, and Comorian is spoken by immigrants from Comoros.

## Religion

Traditional ancestor worship is practiced by about two-fifths of the population and includes tombs, spirits, taboos, and belief in Zanahary or Andriamanitra. Nearly half the population is Christian, including Protestants and Roman Catholics, but Christian conversion often coexists with rites for the dead; a Sunni Muslim community lives in the northwest.

Settlement connects religion, farming, and social life. Cattle are raised widely, rice grows on plateau valley floors and irrigable slopes, and older Merina and Betsileo villages were hilltop settlements with ditches. Modern settlement is more scattered, while cities such as Antananarivo, Fianarantsoa, and Toamasina predate French rule and now contain mixed populations shaped by internal migration.

## Economy

Before 1972, state cooperatives, state farms, rural development programs, and consumer cooperatives shaped agriculture and trade. After the political changes of 1972-75, French economic control weakened, a military regime nationalized banks, insurance, trade, shipping, textiles, cotton, power, and irrigation agencies, and the economy declined through falling exports, inflation, debt, weak industry, and almost no foreign investment.

By 1982, bankruptcy forced Madagascar into IMF structural adjustment and renewed liberalization. Privatization proceeded slowly under Didier Ratsiraka, aid disputes affected Albert Zafy, and later agreements with the IMF and World Bank remained central; in 2004 about half of the country's debt was set to be written off.

Agriculture remains broad and varied. Rice dominates acreage, but imports are still needed; cassava, potatoes, yams, bananas, corn, fruits, coffee, beans, peanuts, vanilla, cacao, sisal, tobacco, cotton, and castor beans are also grown. Zebu cattle are widespread and carry ritual and wealth value, limiting government efforts to expand meat production and exports.

## Resources and Energy

Near Toliara and elsewhere, forest decline is linked to repeated burning, fuel needs, and charcoal use, even with reforestation efforts. Fisheries provide fish, shellfish, crustaceans, shrimp, and prawns, but illegal fishing and overfishing are serious; fish farming, especially on the western coast and in irrigated rice fields, offers an alternative.

Minerals include gold, gems, sapphires, chromite, ilmenite, iron ore, coal, nickel, cobalt, copper, and smaller deposits of many stones and ores. Hydroelectric stations provide more than two-thirds of electricity, while coal stations and mine or factory generators supply the rest; bituminous shales, oil, and natural gas have also been found.

## Manufacturing

Manufacturing ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manufacturing)) processes textiles, footwear, wood, paper pulp, fertilizer, oils, soap, sugar, tobacco products, beer, cement, foods, and beverages. Industry is concentrated around Antananarivo, Antsirabe, and Toamasina, while Merina jewelers work semiprecious stones in plateau towns.

## Finance

The ariary replaced the Malagasy franc in 2003; the Malagasy franc had replaced the CFA franc in 1963, and Madagascar left the Franc Zone in 1973. Exports include clothing, textiles, vanilla, cloves, fish, shellfish, and food products; imports include petroleum, chemicals, metals, machinery, vehicles, textiles, basic manufactures, foods, and live animals.

Trade with [[France]] fell after 1972, but France remained the main trading partner, with the [[United States of America (United States)|United States]], [[Germany]], [[Japan]], and [[Italy]] also important. Tourism grew after a government encouragement program in the late 1980s, although political conflict interrupted growth.

## Labour and Taxation

Sendika Kristianina Malagasy and other unions represent mainly office and industrial workers. Taxes come mostly from trade, domestic goods and services, and corporate income, but centralized collection, tax evasion, low revenue, and dependence on aid remain problems.

Transport serves mainly the plateau and east coast. Many roads are unpaved and become impassable in the wet season; railways connect Antananarivo with Toamasina, Fianarantsoa, Manakara, Antsirabe, and Ambatondrazaka. Main ports are Toamasina and Mahajanga, though cyclones have damaged Toamasina.

## Transport and Government

The harbour at Antsiranana is remote but among the world's finest natural harbours and includes the former French naval base. The Pangalanes Canal forms a waterway more than 370 miles long, airfields help cross the difficult landscape, and the main international airport is at Ivato near Antananarivo.

The constitutional framework moved from independence in 1960 to military rule in 1972-75, socialist reorganization in 1975, democratic transition in the early 1990s, and a new constitution in 2010. The current system is a unitary republic with a president, prime minister, National Assembly, and planned Senate; provinces, regions, and communes form local government.

## Justice

The Merina state had an elaborate legal system in the 19th century. Modern law is based on French codes and includes a High Constitutional Court, High Court of Justice, Supreme Court, Courts of Appeal, and tribunals.

## Security

The army did not play a direct political role until 1972. French troops left in 1973, and the naval base at Antsiranana was handed over in 1975; the armed forces include an army, navy, air force, paramilitary force, secret police, and local police under the ministry of the interior.

Health and welfare vary sharply by region. Rural areas often lack electricity, water, and safe drinking water; malaria deaths have fallen, but schistosomiasis, sexually transmitted diseases, poor access to doctors, urban concentration of hospitals, and limited social benefits remain important health problems.

## Housing

Housing ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Housing)) is usually rectangular with steep roofs, but materials vary: mud and wattle or matting in many rural areas, bamboo and palm thatch in eastern forests, upright planks in the south, and earth blocks on the plateau. Wealthier urban houses often follow a lofty Indonesian style with balconies, brick columns, and tiled roofs.

The style survives in the house of King Andrianampoinimerina at Ambohimanga and reached a high point in the Queen's Palace built by Jean Laborde in the 19th century, though the palace burned in 1995. Urban growth is expected to worsen overcrowding despite housing research and low-cost housing efforts.

## Education

[[Education]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Education)) includes primary and secondary schools, technical institutes, teacher-training colleges, and universities. Schooling is compulsory from ages 6 to 13; universities include Antananarivo (1961), Mahajanga (1977), and Fianarantsoa (1988). Literacy has risen greatly since the mid-1960s, but attendance is higher on the plateau than along the coast.

Malagasy culture combines Indonesian elements with Arabic, Islamic, Bantu, African, Christian, and Western influences. Rural councils of male elders, ceremonies, orators, musicians, lamba clothing, famadihana reburial rites, kabary speeches, seasonal festivals, and holidays such as June 26 Independence Day show how old and new customs coexist.

Music adapts Western instruments to Malagasy rhythms, while older instruments show Indonesian and African links. Mahafaly, Zafimaniry, Betsileo, and Merina traditions include wood carving, tomb posts, raffia cloth, straw hats, embroidery, printing, proverbs, written literature, and sorabe manuscripts in Arabic script.

Libraries, museums, archives, and natural science collections are centered in Antananarivo. UNESCO World Heritage sites include the Royal Hill of Ambohimanga, the Rainforests of the Atsinanana, and Tsingy de Bemaraha Strict Nature Reserve.

## Stadium

[[Sports]] include football, boxing, athletics, judo, women's basketball, and women's tennis. Dally Randriantefy and Natacha Randriantefy competed internationally, and Madagascar first appeared at the 1964 Tokyo Olympic Games.

## Tourism and Media

Madagascar's coast is almost 2,500 miles long, with beaches, lagoons, reefs, sandbars, warm waters, and rich marine life that attract visitors. Newspapers appear in French and Malagasy, Taratra is the national news agency, and a 1990 law guaranteed press freedom.

## History

Archaeology suggests settlement about 700 CE. Malagasy belongs to the Austronesian family but contains Bantu elements, reflecting a unique Asian-African cultural blend. Before the 16th century, internal migration peopled much of the island, while political life remained fragmented among small coastal states.

From 1500 to about 1650, European accounts described local states rather than a large empire. Afro-Arab trade shaped northern towns, Vohemar, and the southeast; between 1507 and 1513, Afro-Arabs from Malindi arrived near the Matitana River, and by the 1630s the Antemoro had a theocratic state with Malagasy texts written in Arabic script.

Between about 1650 and 1810, the Maroserana helped create Menabe and Boina, later the Sakalava empire, while the Betsimisaraka briefly united eastern coastal peoples under Ratsimilaho. In the central plateau, Andrianampoinimerina reunited Imerina around 1797 and left his son a goal of island-wide rule.

European contact began with Diogo Dias in 1500. Portuguese raids, the French Fort-Dauphin settlement from 1642 to 1674, pirates, slave links with the Mascarenes, and French trading posts prepared the setting for the kingdom of Madagascar. Radama I, allied with Sir Robert Farquhar, expanded Merina rule after 1817, cooperated against the slave trade, invited Europeans, and supported Christian and Latin-alphabet influence.

Ranavalona I reversed Europeanization after 1828, expelled missionaries, persecuted converts, and isolated Madagascar. From 1861, Radama II reopened the island but was overthrown; Rainilaiarivony modernized administration, law, education, provincial rule, and Protestant dominance before French pressure grew. French troops occupied Antananarivo on September 30, 1895, and annexation followed in 1896.

The colonial period brought conquest, abolition of slavery, exile of the queen, pacification, French administration, compulsory French schooling, roads, railways, health services, exports, and closer trade with France. Nationalist activity grew through the Vy Vato Sakelika in 1915 and Jean Ralaimongo in 1920; during [[World Wars (World War II)|World War II]], Madagascar passed from Vichy alignment through British and South African occupation to Free France.

## The French Union (194658)

In 1945, two Malagasy nationalists entered the French parliament, and the 1946 French Union made Madagascar an overseas territory. A major eastern insurrection began on March 30, 1947; the official death count was about 11,000, but many more died from famine, cold, and fear in forests.

Political life revived in the 1950s. The 1956 Overseas Territories Law created an elected executive, Philibert Tsiranana founded the Social Democratic Party, and a referendum on September 28, 1958 approved autonomy within the French Community. The autonomous Malagasy Republic was proclaimed on October 14, 1958.

## The First Republic

The opposition regrouped under the Congress Party for the Independence of Madagascar, with support from Protestant Merina dissidents and communists.

AKFM had strength in Antananarivo but won few legislative seats under the PSD electoral system. Tsiranana became president and helped secure independence on June 26, 1960; he kept strong French ties and anticommunist foreign relations, but unrest and the May 1972 revolution ended the First Republic when Gabriel Ramanantsoa took full powers.

## Transition

A plebiscite on October 8, 1972 confirmed Ramanantsoa, and Tsiranana resigned on October 11. French forces were removed, ties with communist states grew, the country left the Franc Zone, and rural committees were organized to sell produce to state companies.

In 1975, Richard Ratsimandrava took power but was assassinated after six days. A military directorate then named Didier Ratsiraka president and head of the Revolutionary Council, and a December 21, 1975 referendum approved a new Democratic Republic.

## The Second Republic

Ratsiraka took office on January 4, 1976, nationalized banks and industries, cultivated communist ties, declared Madagascar Marxist, created AREMA, and placed political life under the FNDR. Economic strain led to IMF negotiations by 1980-81 and a renewed rapprochement with France.

His second term brought criticism from the Council of Malagasy Churches, youth opposition through kung fu clubs, violent repression in 1985, privatization and subsidy cuts, and wider opposition. From 1989 to 1991, protests, constitutional demands, the Vital Forces alliance under Albert Zafy, shootings at the presidential palace, donor pressure, and a power-sharing agreement opened the way to a Third Republic.

## The Third Republic

Albert Zafy became the first president of the Third Republic in February 1993, but his rule was unstable because of fragmented parties, weak coalition support, plateau-coast tensions, dependence on foreign aid, and disputes over IMF and World Bank conditions. The National Assembly impeached him in July 1996, and the High Constitutional Court removed him in September.

Ratsiraka returned after the December 29, 1996 runoff, faced protests, and survived an impeachment attempt in 1998. Constitutional revisions strengthened the presidency and moved toward federalism, while IMF credits in 1999 improved finances.

The 2001 election caused a severe crisis between Ratsiraka and Marc Ravalomanana. Ravalomanana declared himself president in February 2002, the High Constitutional Court later named him winner, fighting ended by midsummer, and recognition from the [[United States of America (United States)|United States]] and [[France]] followed. He restored investor confidence, won debt relief in 2004, survived coup attempts, and won reelection in December 2006.

## Maureen Ann Covell

Andry Rajoelina, mayor of Antananarivo, challenged Ravalomanana in late 2008. Protests turned deadly in January 2009, the military shifted against Ravalomanana, and on March 17, 2009 power passed from Ravalomanana to the military and then to Rajoelina, a move widely condemned as a coup.

The crisis brought suspension from the African Union and SADC, aid cuts, exile politics, protests, and mediation in Maputo, Mozambique. Agreements among Rajoelina, Ravalomanana, Zafy, and Ratsiraka repeatedly broke down; a November 17, 2010 referendum approved a constitution criticized by the opposition and international community, and Rajoelina declared the Fourth Republic after promulgation in December 2010.

## The Fourth Republic

Political uncertainty continued after a September 2011 SADC agreement and a unity government. Elections were delayed until 2013, when candidacies linked to Ravalomanana, Rajoelina, and Ratsiraka were struck down; the first presidential round on October 25, 2013 led to a runoff between Richard Jean-Louis Robinson and Hery Martial Rakotoarimanana Rajaonarimampianina.

Rajaonarimampianina won with 53.5 percent of the vote and was inaugurated on January 25, 2014. His inauguration restored constitutional order, and the AU and SADC lifted Madagascar's suspensions.

## Interesting Facts

- Madagascar is closer to Africa than Indonesia, but Malagasy culture and language have strong Indonesian links.
- The island's isolation helped about 40 species of lemurs evolve there.
- Madagascar has been called the Great Red Island because of its red soils.
- The Pangalanes Canal runs more than 370 miles along eastern lagoons and swamps.
- By the beginning of the 21st century, about half of the world's sapphires were mined in Madagascar.
- UNESCO recognizes both cultural sites and natural reserves in Madagascar.
