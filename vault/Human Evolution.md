# Human Evolution

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> Human evolution is the long emergence of Homo sapiens from extinct primate lineages, with modern humans evolving in Africa alongside and after other hominins. Fossil and comparative evidence replaces the misleading image of a single missing link with branching relationships among humans, apes, and extinct relatives.

## Introduction

Human evolution ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Human_evolution)) is the process by which human beings developed on Earth from extinct primates. Zoologically, modern humans are Homo sapiens ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Human)), a culture-bearing, upright-walking species that lives on the ground and first evolved in [[Africa]] between about 100,000 and 200,000 years ago. Humans are the only living members of the human tribe, Hominini, but fossils show that many other hominins came before them and that Homo sapiens lived at the same time as Homo neanderthalensis ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neanderthal)), or Neanderthals.

Humans and extinct hominins are related, and humans are also related to living and extinct apes, but the exact pattern of relationship has long been debated. Charles Darwin's On the Origin of Species in 1859 and The Descent of Man ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Descent_of_Man%2C_and_Selection_in_Relation_to_Sex)) in 1871 opened the modern investigation, though Darwin did not simply claim that humans descended from modern apes. A more accurate view is that humans and apes share older common ancestors that branched into separate lineages.

The idea of a single missing link is misleading because evolution works through branching populations rather than a simple chain. The ancient primate ancestor of humans and apes has not been identified and may never be known with certainty. Fossil relationships are especially difficult even within the human lineage, because many forms lived in different places, overlapped in time, and combined primitive and derived features.

## Miocene apes and early hominins

The search for origins includes many Miocene apes and possible hominin relatives. Forms such as Kenyapithecus ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kenyapithecus)), Griphopithecus ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Griphopithecus)), Dryopithecus, Graecopithecus, Samburupithecus, Sahelanthropus ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sahelanthropus)), and Orrorin ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orrorin)) have been discussed as possible relatives of humans, African apes, or both. Kenyapithecus lived in Kenya, Griphopithecus in central Europe and Turkey, Dryopithecus in Europe, Graecopithecus in Greece, Samburupithecus in Kenya, Sahelanthropus in Chad, and Orrorin in Kenya.

Different models place these fossils differently. Some emphasize Eurasian forms and treat Graecopithecus as close to the human line or to great-ape ancestry, while others emphasize African Miocene species and connect Samburupithecus, Orrorin, Australopithecus, Paranthropus, and Homo in different ways. Molecular studies generally show that humans, chimpanzees, and bonobos are more closely related to one another than any are to gorillas, while orangutans are more distant.

Climate and habitat change shaped the setting for hominin evolution. During the Miocene, global climates became more seasonal and cooler north of the Equator. Evergreen forests in many ape habitats gave way to open woodland, shrubland, grassland, and mosaic environments with denser forests near rivers and lakes. These changing habitats created new pressures on movement, diet, social life, and survival.

## Australopithecus, Paranthropus, and Homo

![[attachments/human_evolution_002.png|420]]

*Human evolution timeline template image*

Early hominins included Australopithecus, Paranthropus, and early members of Homo. Their relationships remain uncertain, but fossils show a long period of diversity rather than a single straight line. Australopithecus anamensis may have been ancestral to several later forms, including A. afarensis, while A. afarensis may have led toward Paranthropus and perhaps later Homo.

The fossil known as Lucy, a 3.2-million-year-old A. afarensis, and the Laetoli evidence are important because they show early bipedalism. These hominins walked upright but still differed from modern humans in body size, brain size, skull shape, teeth, and probably aspects of behaviour. The early human story therefore combines upright walking with continuing anatomical diversity.

Teeth and jaws provide major evidence for diet and relationship. Paranthropus had robust skulls and large teeth suited to tough or hard foods, while gracile Australopithecus forms may have had more varied diets. Wear patterns suggest that A. afarensis stripped plant foods with the front teeth, P. robustus ate hard foods, and P. boisei chewed pods and fruits with hard coatings and tough seeds.

In Homo, teeth generally became smaller over time. Homo rudolfensis still had large rear teeth and a face closer to Australopithecus, while Homo ergaster approached the modern human pattern. These trends may relate to diet, food preparation, tools, or changing social behaviour, but the archaeological record does not prove a simple cause.

## Emergence of Homo sapiens

![[attachments/human_evolution_001.webp|420]]

*Female human evolution 2*

The emergence of Homo sapiens ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Human)) is difficult to trace because the relationships among Australopithecus, Kenyanthropus, Paranthropus, and early Homo remain unresolved. Early Homo forms show brain expansion, changes in face and teeth, and association with stone tools, but these changes did not all occur at once or in a single location. Human evolution was a branching process with experiments in anatomy, diet, movement, and culture.

Neanderthals were a Eurasian human form that lived at the same time as early modern humans. They were not simply a primitive stage before Homo sapiens, but a separate population or species with its own adaptations. Their existence shows that modern humans were not alone and that the genus Homo contained multiple human ways of living before only Homo sapiens survived.

The appearance of anatomically modern Homo sapiens ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Human)) in Africa between about 100,000 and 200,000 years ago did not instantly settle questions of behaviour, language, or culture. Fossils show anatomy, while tools, ornaments, sites, and art give indirect clues about thought and social life. The problem is to connect bones, brains, artifacts, and behaviour without assuming more than the evidence can show.

## Cognition, language, and symbolic behaviour

Debates about language origins are especially difficult because speech leaves little direct fossil evidence. Some investigators infer speech from internal skull features in H. habilis, but jaw shape and other traits complicate that claim. A so-called Broca cap inside a fossil braincase is not reliable proof of speech, because some chimpanzees have prominent Broca caps and still do not speak.

The vocal tract is also hard to judge from fossils because it consists largely of soft tissue. A lowered larynx, spacious pharynx, and mobile tongue help modern human speech, but absence of clear fossil evidence does not prove that ancestral hominins had no vocal language. If a lowered voice box were required in the strictest way, even Neanderthals would seem vocally limited, a conclusion that may underestimate them.

Tool use and art also cannot be treated as simple measures of speech. The theory of multiple intelligences suggests that tool behaviour, visual art, spatial ability, and verbal ability may depend on different neurological systems. Human children begin to use language before they become sophisticated tool users, so some form of speech could have preceded complex symbolic tools.

Late Paleolithic symbolic artifacts, including cave art, polished bone, antler, ivory, stone and shell objects, and shell beads from Morocco made about 82,000 years ago, show complex abstract thought. Sites such as Altamira point to rich visual expression, but art alone does not prove the exact character of speech. Historically, all human groups have rich language, religion, and social, political, and economic systems even without elaborate material culture.

Prehistoric Homo sapiens ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Human)) cannot be regarded as cognitively inferior to modern humans simply because their artifacts were different or fewer. Peoples with fewer material objects may face social intelligence demands as complex as those with advanced technology. The evidence supports complex thought and symbolism, while the exact timing and form of language competence remain uncertain.

## Key Events & Developments

- 1859: Charles Darwin published On the Origin of Species.
- 1871: [[Darwin]] published The Descent of Man ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Descent_of_Man%2C_and_Selection_in_Relation_to_Sex)), extending evolutionary thinking to humans.
- 16-14 million years ago: Kenyapithecus ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kenyapithecus)) and Griphopithecus ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Griphopithecus)) lived in Africa and Eurasia.
- 7-6 million years ago: Sahelanthropus ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sahelanthropus)) lived in Chad, near the possible beginning of the hominin line.
- 6 million years ago: Orrorin ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orrorin)) lived in central Kenya.
- 3.2 million years ago: Lucy, an A. afarensis individual, lived in eastern Africa.
- 100,000-200,000 years ago: Homo sapiens ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Human)) first evolved in Africa.
- 82,000 years ago: Shell beads in Morocco show early symbolic behaviour.

## Key People

- Charles Darwin ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_Darwin)): Naturalist whose works established the evolutionary framework for studying human origins.
- Lucy ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lucy)): Famous Australopithecus afarensis fossil showing early hominin bipedalism.
- Neanderthals ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neanderthals)): Eurasian members of Homo who lived alongside early modern humans.
- Homo sapiens ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homo_sapiens)): The only surviving hominin species and the focus of modern human evolution.

## Interesting Facts

- Human evolution ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Human_evolution)) is not a ladder but a branching pattern of related lineages.
- Modern humans and modern apes share older common ancestors, but humans did not descend from living apes.
- Paranthropus had robust skulls and teeth suited to tough or hard foods.
- The Broca cap is not reliable proof that a fossil hominin could speak.
- Shell beads from Morocco made about 82,000 years ago indicate abstract symbolic thought.
