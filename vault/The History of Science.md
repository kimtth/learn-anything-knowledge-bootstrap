# The History of Science

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> The history of science examines how disciplined, evidence-tested inquiry into nature emerged from early knowledge of seasons, celestial motions, materials, disease, and living things. The source follows non-Western, ancient, medieval, and modern traditions while emphasizing skepticism, explanation, changing methods, and scientific institutions.

## Introduction

![[attachments/history_science_001.webp|420]]

*Ptolemy Almagest manuscript page*

Science ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Science)) is knowledge of nature, but in a fuller sense it means disciplined inquiry into natural regularities, tested by evidence, skepticism, explanation, and method. Humans have always needed knowledge of the Sun, Moon, seasons, animals, plants, disease, and materials for survival.

Recognizing regularities is not enough. The mind may invent patterns where none exist, as in medieval beliefs that comets foretold upheaval. Science requires detached examination of data and skepticism toward premature generalization.

Scientific laws may describe regularity, but many people also seek causes. Modern science has sometimes changed what counts as explanation, as quantum mechanics shows by giving up some older forms of causation while retaining powerful prediction.

## Early and Non-Western Science

Astronomy was often the first science because calendars mattered for ritual, agriculture, government, and survival. Daily and annual solar motions, lunar cycles, eclipses, and seasons shaped early knowledge.

In [[China]], astronomy and astrology became part of government from the 2nd millennium BCE. The Chinese state needed accurate calendars, and officials kept long records of stars, eclipses, novae, and celestial events.

Chinese science emphasized practical order. Chemistry, alchemy, medicine, geology, geography, and technology flourished, supported by bureaucracy and by a worldview that saw the universe as an ordered organism.

In [[India]], astronomy served calendrical and religious purposes. Indian mathematics was highly developed, especially in geometry, algebra, and the numeral system later known in the West as Hindu-Arabic numerals.

The Maya developed astronomy and calendar systems independently in Central America. Solar and lunar eclipses, Venus cycles, and ritual timekeeping played major roles in Mayan society.

## Egypt and Mesopotamia

In [[Egypt]], cosmic order was guaranteed by gods, and astronomy focused mainly on calendrical calculation, especially the Nile flood. Egyptian religion answered many ultimate questions, so speculative natural inquiry remained limited.

Mesopotamia resembled China more closely in its need for technical control of harsh river environments. Irrigation, damming, flood control, temple administration, and astrology encouraged mathematics, astronomy, and record keeping.

Mesopotamian priestly institutions planned public works and studied heavenly signs, while practical needs supported arithmetic, geometry, and observations that later influenced Greek and Islamic science.

## Greek Medicine and Natural Philosophy

Greek science transformed earlier knowledge through natural philosophy, mathematics, proof, and rational explanation. Greeks pursued causes, structures, and principles beyond immediate practical use.

Greek medicine broke strongly with ritual healing. Hippocrates and his school insisted that disease was natural rather than supernatural, even for striking conditions such as epilepsy.

The Museum of Alexandria supported research in anatomy, medicine, mathematics, astronomy, and other fields. Scholars investigated the heart, blood vessels, nerves, brain, and organs.

Galen built a physiology from Alexandrian research and animal dissections. His system of natural, vital, and animal spirits moving through veins, arteries, and nerves dominated medicine for centuries, though practice remained eclectic.

## Rome, Christianity, and Medieval Science

Greek science reached high points with Euclid and [[Archimedes]], but Roman science was more practical and encyclopedic. Later Christianity preserved learning while also reshaping inquiry through theological priorities.

In medieval Europe, science survived through monasteries, schools, translations, Islamic scholarship, and universities. Natural knowledge often served theology, medicine, calendar calculation, and practical arts.

Islamic civilization preserved and extended Greek science, especially in astronomy, optics, medicine, mathematics, and philosophy. Translation movements and observatories helped transmit knowledge back to Europe.

## Copernicus and the Scientific Revolution

Nicolaus Copernicus published De revolutionibus in 1543, placing the Sun rather than Earth at the centre of the cosmos. This opened the scientific revolution and altered thought and material life.

Copernicus explained planetary retrograde motion by combining Earth's orbit with planetary motions. He also explained Mercury and Venus staying near the Sun by placing their orbits inside Earth's.

His system was simpler and more coherent than Ptolemy's but still retained epicycles and raised serious physical questions: why the rotating Earth did not fling objects away, and why moving Earth could not be felt.

## Tycho, Kepler, and Galileo

![[attachments/history_science_002.webp|420]]

*Portrait of Galileo with a telescope*

Tycho Brahe measured stellar and planetary positions with unprecedented accuracy. Though he rejected Earth's motion, he abandoned crystalline spheres and showed that comets and new stars lay beyond the Moon.

[[Galileo]]'s telescope attacked Aristotelian cosmology. He saw lunar mountains, Jupiter's satellites, sunspots, and countless stars in the Milky Way, showing that the heavens were not perfect, simple, or fully visible to unaided senses.

Johannes Kepler used Tycho's observations to show that Mars and other planets move in ellipses with the Sun at one focus. His work created a new celestial order but lacked a fully satisfactory physical cause.

Rene Descartes tried to replace Aristotle with matter, motion, and mechanical models. His vortex theory explained planetary motion through swirling subtle matter, but it eventually failed.

## Newtonian Synthesis

![[attachments/history_science_003.webp|420]]

*Title page of Newton's Principia*

Isaac Newton united terrestrial and celestial physics through mathematics, experiment, and mechanics. His method moved from phenomena to forces and then from forces back to other phenomena.

Newton's calculus ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Calculus)), developed independently of Leibniz's version, allowed forces and motions to be treated mathematically. His Principia of 1687 established the three laws of motion and universal gravitation.

Newton justified [[Copernicus]], Kepler, and Galileo while defeating Cartesian physics. Gravity explained planetary orbits, falling bodies, tides, and celestial mechanics under one framework.

Newton still linked space, order, and divine action. He considered absolute space essential and sometimes suggested that God preserved cosmic order where planetary perturbations might otherwise destabilize the system.

## Anatomy, Physiology, and Method

The same year as Copernicus's book, Andreas Vesalius published De humani corporis fabrica, correcting Galenic anatomy through direct observation and dissection.

William Harvey's 1628 work on the circulation of blood became the Principia of physiology. It showed that the heart pumps blood in a circuit, establishing anatomy and physiology as more exact sciences.

The scientific revolution spread a method of criticism, measurement, observation, experiment, and mathematical explanation across fields, not only astronomy and mechanics.

## Mechanics in the 18th Century

[[Mechanics - Energy, Forces, and Their Effects|Mechanics]] became the leading science and increasingly a branch of mathematics. Many physical problems were reduced to mathematical ones solvable by analysis.

Leonhard Euler developed the calculus of variations and mathematical physics, while d'Alembert and Lagrange helped turn mechanics into an axiomatic analytic system.

Newtonian mechanics was tested through the shape of Earth. Cartesians expected a pointed polar shape, while Newtonians predicted an oblate spheroid. Expeditions to Lapland and Peru confirmed Newton's prediction.

Pierre-Simon Laplace systematized celestial mechanics and showed that planetary perturbations are periodic, making the solar system stable without requiring divine correction.

## Chemistry and Gases

Chemistry changed through the study of gases. Joseph Black's work on magnesia alba showed that an air with specific properties could combine with solids and be recovered from them.

Chemists discovered many distinct gases with different properties: some burned, some extinguished flame, some killed animals, and some supported life.

Antoine-Laurent Lavoisier revolutionized chemistry through balance experiments. He rejected phlogiston theory and showed that combustion involves combination with oxygen.

The chemical revolution was a revolution in method as well as theory. Gravimetric analysis, precise weighing, classification of substances, and attention to composition made modern chemistry possible.

## Modern Science and Institutions

![[attachments/history_science_004.webp|420]]

*DNA double helix model*

Modern science grew through institutions: academies, universities, laboratories, journals, expeditions, observatories, museums, government bureaus, industry, and large-scale research projects.

The 18th and 19th centuries saw the rise of professional scientists, specialized disciplines, public funding, research communities, and tighter relations between science, technology, state power, and industry.

Modern science also expanded into life sciences, geology, evolution, genetics, molecular biology, social sciences, statistics, environmental science, and large-scale physics.

The history of science includes debates over gender, religion, empire, politics, economics, and technology. Scientific knowledge is powerful but is always produced in institutions and societies.

## Key Events & Developments

- 2nd millennium BCE: Chinese state astronomy and calendars became linked to government.
- 5th century BCE: Hippocratic medicine argued for natural causes of disease.
- 3rd century BCE: Alexandrian research advanced anatomy, mathematics, and astronomy.
- 1543: Copernicus published De revolutionibus; Vesalius published De fabrica.
- 1600: William Gilbert described Earth as a magnet.
- 1609-1610: Galileo used the telescope for astronomical discoveries.
- 1628: Harvey published on blood circulation.
- 1687: Newton published the Principia.
- 1750s: Joseph Black's gas studies transformed chemistry.
- 1798-1827: Laplace published Celestial Mechanics.
