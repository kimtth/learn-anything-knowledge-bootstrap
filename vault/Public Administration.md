# Public Administration

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> Public administration implements and often helps shape government policy through professional civil services, organizations, and management at central and local levels. The source traces bureaucracy from ancient empires through Prussian, French, British, American, Soviet, Chinese, and Japanese systems while weighing efficiency, hierarchy, expertise, accountability, and democratic responsiveness.

## Introduction

![[attachments/public_administration_001.webp|420]]

*Civil service commission seal*

Public administration ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Public_administration)) is the implementation of government policy and, in modern use, often includes helping determine government policies and programs. It involves planning, organizing, directing, coordinating, and controlling public operations. Every nation has public administration, whether its government is democratic, authoritarian, federal, unitary, ancient, or modern.

Administration occurs at central, intermediate, and local levels, and the relationships among these levels are a major problem of government. In most countries, public administration has become a profession carried out by trained officials. This professional body is usually called the civil service, although the term has different social meanings in different countries.

A civil servant ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Civil_service)) is normally a person directly employed in managing the internal affairs of the state. The role is distinguished from political, ministerial, military, judicial, or police work. Some countries also separate the home civil service from diplomatic service abroad, and many exclude local government or public corporations from the central civil service.

Specialized professional civil services provide technical knowledge rather than general administration. Engineers, scientists, legal experts, and other specialists may support government operations while still belonging to a broader administrative system. This shows that modern administration is both managerial and technical.

## History

![[attachments/public_administration_002.webp|420]]

*Public administration chart*

Public administration ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Public_administration)) has ancient origins. Egyptians and Greeks organized public affairs through offices whose holders were responsible for justice, law and order, and material plenty. The Romans developed a more complex imperial structure with separate administrative hierarchies for justice, military affairs, finance and taxation, foreign affairs, and internal affairs.

The Roman Empire created an empire-wide hierarchy of officials reporting upward to the emperor. This structure later influenced the Roman Catholic Church. In western Europe much of the system disappeared after the fall of Rome in the 5th century, but many practices continued in the Byzantine Empire, where civil-service rule helped produce the negative term Byzantinism.

Medieval [[Europe]] developed administration from royal households. Until the end of the 12th century, offices were often vague and duplicated. Better-defined household offices included butler, steward, chamberlain, and chancellor. During the 13th century, domestic household functions began separating from state governing functions.

The chancellor became the most important link between household offices and state administration because the role involved writing and sealing documents in the monarch's name. As royal government expanded, older household posts declined, became ceremonial, or disappeared. This shift helped create offices concerned with governing rather than personal service to a ruler.

## Prussia

The foundations of modern European bureaucracy were laid in Prussia in the late 17th and 18th centuries. The electors of Brandenburg, who became kings of Prussia from 1701, used centralized government to strengthen dynastic power and political stability. They suppressed city autonomy and weakened aristocratic feudal privileges.

Civil servants appointed by the Prussian central government administered provinces through Offices of War and Crown Lands. These offices combined crown-land management with military organization. Tax councillors supervised municipalities and communes, and all officials were responsible to the central government.

At first, Prussian civil servants were crown agents sent from [[Berlin]] to handle military matters such as recruiting, billeting, and supplying troops. Over time they extended supervision into civil affairs. By 1713, recognizable administrative units dealt with civil matters, and ordinances in 1722 and 1748 regulated recruitment.

The Prussian system emphasized central control, regular recruitment, promotion rules, and internal organization. Senior officials proposed candidates for high posts to the king, while the adjutant general proposed noncommissioned officers for lower administrative positions. This model helped define bureaucracy as disciplined, hierarchical, and career-based.

## France

The French Revolution of 1789 changed the status of the civil servant. Officials came to be seen as servants of the state rather than servants of the king. Even though monarchy and empire returned, the idea of the state as an impersonal public power remained important.

This depersonalization encouraged the growth of administrative law, which defined the organization, duties, and rights of public power. Napoleon I built a civil service marked by hierarchy, clear chains of command, rational organization, and universal rules drawn from Enlightenment thinking. Authority belonged to the office, not the person, though each official remained responsible for official acts.

France was divided into departements, arrondissements, and communes. In each unit, state civil servants helped maintain public order, health, and morality, all linked to the national Ministry of the Interior. Technical specialists were trained through the Ecole Polytechnique, and the Conseil d'Etat became central to administrative law and state advice.

## The British Empire

Britain developed modern administrative machinery partly through governing India and trying to prevent scandals in the East India Company. Robert Clive, governor of Bengal for the second time in 1764, introduced rules that barred company servants from private trading and accepting gifts from native traders.

Later governors strengthened these bans by raising salaries, using seniority for promotion, and reorganizing higher administration. After 1813, entrants to the Indian civil service studied Indian history, language, and law at Haileybury College and needed a certificate of good conduct before taking office.

Thomas Macaulay supported examination rather than patronage for recruitment. Rules from 1833 required multiple nominees for each vacancy to compete in examinations. Further reforms in 1853 helped make the Indian Civil Service a model for the modern British civil service.

The 1854 report on Britain's Permanent Civil Service, associated with Sir Charles Trevelyan, recommended abolishing patronage and using open competitive examination. It also supported a permanent career service organized by merit. Experience in India therefore directly shaped administrative reform in the United Kingdom.

## The United States

In the [[United States of America (United States)|United States]], patronage lasted longer than in Britain because many Americans opposed a permanent administrative caste and favoured elective office where possible. Andrew Jackson argued in 1829 that public duties were simple enough for intelligent citizens and that long tenure could be harmful.

Because election could not practically fill all lower administrative posts, the spoils system developed. Public office became a reward for political victory and party support. Over time, the system produced inefficiency, corruption, and partisanship, especially after the Civil War during rapid economic and social change.

In 1883, the federal government created the U.S. Civil Service Commission to control entry into federal office through competitive examination. At first its work covered mainly lower grades. During the first 20 years of the 20th century, the merit system expanded to about half of federal posts, and later control grew over lower, middle, and managerial positions.

After 1978, reforms changed the federal personnel system and divided functions that had once belonged to the commission. The American story shows a long tension between democratic suspicion of permanent officials and the practical need for skilled, nonpartisan administration.

## The Soviet Union

The Russian Revolution of 1917 abolished the tsarist civil service, but the new regime soon needed trained administrators for social engineering, economic planning, and world revolution. The Communist Party first feared that strong administration would weaken revolutionary spontaneity, then reluctantly used reliable former tsarist officials.

The new Soviet civil service was surrounded by party controls to ensure loyalty. As the party itself became bureaucratized and early revolutionary leaders were removed, special industrial academies trained party members with administrative talent. The First Five-Year Plan of 1928-32 improved civil servants' status and loosened some service conditions.

In 1935, the State Commission on the Civil Service was created under the Commissariat of Finance. It controlled personnel practice, administrative structure, job classification, staffing levels, and unnecessary functions. Unlike British or American commissions, it did not control recruitment, and it remained under close supervision of the Council of People's Commissars and party directives.

## China

The People's Republic of China shows the tension between revolutionary distrust of bureaucracy and the need for strong administration. China already had a long bureaucratic tradition when the Communist Party came to power in 1949. Within a decade, party doctrine criticized the administration for creating distance between elites and the masses.

Chinese cadres were divided by date of entry into the revolutionary movement and by a complex job-evaluation system. The civil service contained 24 grades, each with rank, salary, power, prestige, and privileges. These divisions created strong psychological barriers between high and low officials and between cadres and ordinary people.

During the Cultural Revolution of the 1960s and 1970s, these distinctions were attacked sharply. Yet they remained deeply rooted in the administrative structure. The Chinese case shows how revolutionary movements can try to reject bureaucracy while still depending on it.

## Japan

Before the 17th century, [[Japan]] under the shogunate was administered mainly by a military establishment of vassals and nobles. After the 1630s, a civil bureaucracy grew more important, but appointment depended on family rank and loyalty to feudal lords.

The arrival of Matthew C. Perry with four U.S. warships in 1853 ended more than two centuries of Japanese isolation and pushed bureaucracy away from feudal rank. Loyalty shifted toward the emperor. During the Meiji Restoration in the 1880s, Japan created a modern civil service based on job security, career paths, and open competition, with Tokyo University law graduates dominant.

After [[World Wars (World War II)|World War II]], Allied occupation authorities required a new view of public officials as servants of the people rather than servants of the emperor. The 1947 National Public Service Law created an independent National Personnel Authority to manage recruitment, promotion, employment conditions, performance standards, and job classification.

The postwar law placed nearly all civil servants, from labourers to the prime minister, within a regulated system. Officials were divided into regular service and special service. Regular service used competitive examination and tenure, while special service covered posts outside the ordinary career rules.

## Principles of Public Administration

Public administration ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Public_administration)) in the 20th century was often practical and normative rather than strongly theoretical. A general theory developed slowly, and strong interest in theory grew only after the spread of Max Weber's theory of bureaucracy in the mid-20th century. Even then, much bureaucratic theory focused on private organizations rather than political administration.

A central principle has been economy and efficiency: providing public services at minimum cost. Reformers often treated reorganization as a way to correct administrative problems. Other values, including responsiveness, justice, equal treatment, and citizen participation, became more important, but efficiency remained a major goal.

Many organizational principles came from the military and private business. They include grouping related activities together, matching responsibility with authority, ensuring unity of command, limiting the number of subordinates under one supervisor, and separating line activities from staff support. Management by exception also directed higher officials to handle only unusual or major issues.

Classical administrative thought stressed formal organization, clear hierarchy, and rational division of labour. Later critics argued that administration could not be separated cleanly from politics, values, discretion, and human relations. Public administrators not only execute orders; they often shape policy through expertise, interpretation, and practical control over implementation.

## Key Events & Developments

- The 5th century: The fall of the western Roman Empire weakened Roman administrative structures in western Europe.
- 1701: The electors of Brandenburg became kings of Prussia, strengthening centralized bureaucratic government.
- 1789: The French Revolution recast civil servants as servants of the state rather than the king.
- 1854: The British report on the Permanent Civil Service recommended open competitive examination and the end of patronage.
- 1883: The U.S. Civil Service Commission was established to support merit-based federal recruitment.
- 1947: [[Japan]]'s National Public Service Law created a new postwar civil-service system.

- Robert Clive ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Clive)): Introduced reforms in Bengal to curb East India Company corruption.

## Interesting Facts

- British civil-service reform was strongly influenced by administrative experience in India.
- Revolutionary governments in Russia and China criticized bureaucracy but still needed trained administrators to govern.
