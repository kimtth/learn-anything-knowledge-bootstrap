# Christianity

> [!abstract] Reference navigation

## Introduction

Christianity ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christianity)) is presented as the religion that grew from the life, teaching, death, and resurrection of Jesus of Nazareth, called the Christ or Anointed One of God. It is the largest and most geographically widespread of the world religions, with more than two billion believers and with major historical bodies in [[Roman Catholicism]], [[Eastern Orthodoxy]], and [[Protestantism]]. It also notes independent Eastern churches and many sects, so Christianity is not one simple institution but a world tradition with many forms.

The Christian faith ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christianity)) has two senses. It is the believer's act of trust in God through Christ, and it is also the content of what believers hold to be true. Christianity is therefore more than a set of ideas. It has produced worship, law, art, literature, customs, institutions, and ways of life passed down since the 1st century AD. The tradition is living because communities continue to interpret Christ, Scripture, worship, and moral life in new conditions.

A central question is what the essence of Christianity is. At the minimum, Christianity is the tradition that focuses on [[Jesus - The Christ and Christology (Jesus Christ)|Jesus Christ]]. Yet Christians have often disagreed about how to state that essence. Some have located it in doctrine, some in Scripture, some in sacramental life, some in personal faith, some in the church, and some in the moral or cultural life that Christianity has created.

The spread of Christian communities made the question of identity more difficult. After Eastern and Western churches separated, and after the 16th-century Protestant Reformation divided Western Christianity, no single group could easily claim to possess the whole Christian essence without dispute. Lutheran, Anglican, Presbyterian, Reformed, Anabaptist, Quaker, and other Protestant bodies all claimed to recover or express the essential gospel, often by returning to the Bible.

A largely phenomenological approach to Christian identity is most useful. It does not begin by deciding which church is most correct. Instead, it describes people and groups that call themselves Christian and explains how other Christians judge their claims. The case of Mormons, or the Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints, shows this difficulty: they use Christian language and center faith on Jesus, but many other Christians reject some of their teachings about God as outside traditional Christianity.

Modern views of Christianity are shaped by contact with secularism, Buddhism, Communism, other religions, and rival claims about truth and salvation. Christian identity is therefore both historical and contested. Christianity is a faith centered on Christ, but its boundaries have been argued through canon, creed, worship, institutional authority, cultural inheritance, and continuing debates about revelation and salvation.

## The history of Christianity

Christianity ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christianity)) began as a movement within Judaism. The earliest church belonged to a Jewish world marked by foreign domination, Hellenistic culture, Roman rule, and the hope that God would fulfill the promises made to Israel. Jewish faith held together monotheism, the special election of Israel, and a universal hope that God's salvation would finally concern all nations.

The early relation between Christians and Jews turned mainly on two questions. The first was whether Jesus was the Messiah promised in Scripture. The second was whether the Mosaic Law, including circumcision, food rules, and Sabbath observance, remained permanently binding on Gentile believers. These questions shaped the earliest Christian mission and helped explain why Christianity both continued and transformed Jewish expectations.

The primitive church was a community convinced that Jesus' resurrection had begun a new age. The earliest preaching linked Jesus with God's history with Israel and with the destiny of the human race. The coming of the Kingdom of God, Jesus' works of power, his death according to God's purpose, his resurrection, and the gift of the Holy Spirit formed a compact proclamation that called hearers to repentance, forgiveness, and entry into the community.

Early Christian teaching also took short confessional forms. Phrases such as Jesus is Lord, Jesus is the Son of God, and Peter's confession that Jesus is the Christ became ways to state the center of faith. Hymns and worship formulas also carried doctrine, as seen in passages used by Paul and in the heavenly songs of Revelation. The Gospels then gave narrative form to Jesus' life, teaching, death, and future work.

The formation of the New Testament canon was one of the most important early developments. The church gradually recognized certain apostolic writings as authoritative and rejected apocryphal gospels, acts, and apocalypses. The word canon meant a rule or criterion. Once established, the canon marked the authentic Gospel of the new covenant and implied that special apostolic revelation had ended with the death of the Apostles.

Creed and hierarchy had equal importance. At baptism, believers confessed faith in God the Father, Jesus Christ his Son, and the Holy Spirit in the church and resurrection. These baptismal questions grew into declaratory creeds, including the Apostles' Creed as known in Rome by about AD 200. Creed, canon, and episcopal authority gave scattered churches coherence and helped defend orthodoxy against rival teachings.

Church order also grew more centralized. By the late 3rd century the bishop of a provincial capital, the metropolitan, was consecrating his colleagues, and the bishops of [[Rome]], [[Alexandria]], and Antioch held authority beyond their provinces because each see was traced to an Apostle. Together with [[Jerusalem]] and Constantinople, founded in 330, these became the five patriarchates. The title papa, long an affectionate name for any beloved bishop, began to be reserved for the bishops of Rome from the 6th century and was almost exclusively theirs by the 9th century.

Early heresies forced the church to define itself. Gnosticism taught that secret knowledge freed the elect soul from a hostile or defective material world, often linked with a demiurge and a Docetic view that Jesus only seemed to have flesh. Marcion rejected the Old Testament and accepted only selected Christian writings. Opposition to these movements encouraged clearer teaching about creation, incarnation, Scripture, and salvation.

The Apologists of the 2nd century defended Christianity before Jewish and Greco-Roman critics. They answered accusations of cannibalism and immorality and explained Christian belief in God, Christ, resurrection, and moral life. By using Greek philosophical terms such as Logos, Mind, and Reason, writers influenced by Philo of Alexandria and Platonist vocabulary made Christianity intelligible to educated pagan society and began formal Christian theology.

Christians meanwhile faced waves of persecution. Cyprian of Carthage was martyred during the assault of 257-259, and the worst persecution began in February 303 under the co-emperors Diocletian and Galerius. After Galerius died in 311, the emperor Constantine won power claiming a vision of the cross, and in 313 he and Licinius issued the Edict of Milan, granting Christians full legal rights. Constantine had been proclaimed emperor at York in 306, convened the Council of Arles in 314, became sole emperor in 324, presided over the Council of Nicaea in 325, founded Constantinople in 330, and died in 337. By the end of the 4th century, Christianity had become the dominant faith of the empire.

Early worship centered on baptism, the Eucharist, and Sunday assembly. Christians replaced synagogue Sabbath meetings with worship on the Lord's Day, dropped circumcision as an initiation mark, and used baptism in the name of Father, Son, and Holy Spirit. Baptism included instruction, fasting, renunciation of evil, immersion in water, anointing, laying on of hands, and incorporation into the body of Christ.

The Eucharist marked continuing membership in the church. It was understood as fellowship with the risen Christ and as the meal of the messianic Kingdom. This practice connected with Jewish expectations of the Kingdom of God, Jesus' Last Supper, the appearances of the risen Christ, and the hope that believers would eat with the exalted Son of man in God's future reign.

After Constantine, Christian worship and public devotion changed greatly. The cult of saints, pilgrimage to shrines, veneration of relics, and elaboration of liturgy became major forms of religious life. Great centers such as [[Jerusalem]] and [[Rome]] became models for other churches. The Eucharistic prayer, called anaphora in Greek and canon in Latin, developed several regional forms while keeping a settled basic pattern by the 4th century.

Christian art and architecture grew steadily. Constantine's churches at Jerusalem and Rome encouraged later rulers, especially Justinian, to build churches with simple exteriors and rich interiors of marble, mosaic, angels, and saints. Surviving monuments such as Hagia Sophia in Constantinople and San Vitale at Ravenna show how worship, imperial power, and Christian visual theology were joined.

Imperial patronage drove this building. Constantine raised the Church of the Holy Sepulchre in [[Jerusalem]] in 335 and the Church of the Nativity at Bethlehem, while his mother, the empress Helena, journeyed to the Holy Land before AD 330 and was said to have discovered the holy cross, stimulating the cult of relics. Pilgrimage to such shrines, veneration of the saints, and the gathering of relics became central to medieval devotion, until [[Luther]] later attacked the practice by criticizing the relic collection of Frederick the Wise at Wittenberg on All Saints' Day in 1516.

Music and hymnody also became important. Greek liturgies used rich prose and poetry, while Latin style was more restrained. Ambrose of Milan, Prudentius, and Venantius Fortunatus wrote Latin hymns, and the later ascription of Roman chants to Gregory I helped form the idea of Gregorian chant. In the Greek East, Romanos Melodos created the kontakion, a long poetic homily used in worship.

Monasticism ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monasticism)) arose from the Christian ideal of perfection and from models such as the apostolic community in Jerusalem and Jesus' wilderness struggle. It emerged in the late 3rd century and became established by the 4th century, especially in Egypt and Syria. Early monks such as Anthony sought solitude in tombs, abandoned settlements, caves, and deserts to fight temptation and live wholly for God.

Monastic life took solitary and communal forms. Desert anchorites gathered for weekly prayer while living mostly alone. Pachomius, a former Roman soldier, created the first cenobitic or communal monasteries. Writings such as Athanasius's Life of St. Antony, the Rule of the Master, the rules of Basil, and the Rule of Benedict gave later Christians models of ascetic discipline, prayer, obedience, and community.

Ancient Christian theology was shaped by controversy and commentary. Athanasius, the Cappadocian Fathers, Cyril of Alexandria, and Theodoret wrote in the Arian and Christological controversies. Jerome translated the Latin Vulgate and strengthened Western biblical scholarship. [[Augustine]] became the greatest ancient Western theologian through works such as Confessions and The City of God, joining Platonic thought with Christian doctrine.

The great doctrinal disputes were fought over precise persons and dates. The Alexandrian presbyter Arius, who lived about 250 to 336, taught that the Son was God's first creature, and the Council of Nicaea in 325 answered with the term homoousios, of one substance with the Father. Athanasius, bishop of Alexandria from 328 to 373, defended this through long reaction, and the Council of Constantinople in 381 made Catholic Christianity the official religion of the empire and affirmed the divinity of the Holy Spirit. Apollinaris, who denied a human mind in Christ, was condemned in 381, and the dispute over Nestorius led the Council of Ephesus in 431, under Cyril of Alexandria, to defend the title Theotokos for Mary.

Political relations between East and West became more strained through disputes over Chalcedon, imperial power, language, and culture. Byzantine Christianity developed rich theology, art, and statecraft but did not share the West's centralized papal polity. Western Christianity, meanwhile, increasingly looked to the bishop of Rome, especially through the authority of Leo I and Gregory I.

The Christological settlement strained these ties further. The Council of Chalcedon in 451, prompted by Pope Leo I, who reigned from 440 to 461, defined Christ as one person in two natures, but it alienated Monophysite believers in Egypt and Syria. Byzantine emperors then spent roughly 250 years failing to win them back, through the Henotikon of the emperor Zeno in 482 and Justinian's condemnation of the Three Chapters at the Fifth Council in 553. In the West the medieval papacy was built up by Gregory VII, who reigned from 1073 to 1085, and Innocent III, who reigned from 1198 to 1216, while the popes held temporal sovereignty over parts of Italy from the 7th to the 19th century.

From the schism to the Reformation, the rise of papal authority in the West contrasted with the independent course of Byzantine Christianity in the East. Eastern churches developed autocephaly, meaning administrative independence for national churches. Byzantine missionaries brought Christianity to many Slavic peoples, while Byzantium also faced long pressure from Muslim powers during what Western history calls the Middle Ages.

The breach between East and West was marked by precise episodes. The Western addition of the word Filioque, that the Spirit proceeds from the Father and the Son, was introduced by the council of Toledo in 589 and adopted at [[Rome]] only in 1014. In 1054 Cardinal Humbert laid a bull of excommunication on the altar of Hagia Sophia, an act later remembered as the final rupture. Estrangement deepened with the sack of Constantinople by Western Christians in 1204 and the fall of the city to the Turks in 1453. The mutual excommunications of 1054 were finally lifted on December 7, 1965, by Pope Paul VI and the ecumenical patriarch Athenagoras I.

The Reformation began as an attempt to reform doctrine and church life from within Western Christianity, but it became a deep break. Reformers can be classified by their degree of protest against medieval theology, piety, and polity. Martin Luther and Anglican Reformers were relatively conservative, John Calvin and his followers were less conservative, and Anabaptists and related groups were the most radical.

Luther's break with Rome unfolded in dated steps. In 1520 he attacked the sacramental system in On the Babylonian Captivity of the Church, at the colloquy of Marburg in 1529 he rejected Zwingli's view of the Lord's Supper, and he approved the conciliatory Augsburg Confession in 1530. The Schmalkaldic Articles of 1537 turned fiercely against Rome, which replied at the Council of Trent from 1545 to 1563 by reaffirming traditional teaching on salvation, the sacraments, and the church. [[Calvin and Calvinism (Calvin)|Calvin]]'s Institutes of the Christian Religion, first issued in 1536 and completed in 1559, gave Reformed theology its classic form.

Despite their differences, Reformation movements shared several themes. They stressed the Bible ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bible)) over church tradition as religious authority, free grace in forgiveness, faith alone as the condition of acceptance with God, and a larger role for the laity in worship and church work. The Reformation did not originally aim to dissolve Christian culture, but it transformed both church and culture in a Europe already changed by nationalism, capitalism, exploration, science, and Renaissance learning.

Modern Christianity faced political revolution, scientific discovery, historical criticism, and new relations between church and state. [[Roman Catholicism]] in France, [[Eastern Orthodoxy]] in Russia, and Protestant churches in colonial settings could be identified with older regimes and attacked with them. At the same time, critical scholarship produced better editions of the Bible and early Christian texts and renewed interest in church history.

The 19th century is described as a great century of Christian missions, both Roman Catholic and Protestant. Critics of Christianity also provoked new apologists who reinterpreted faith in relation to modern philosophy and science. In the 20th century, Christianity faced totalitarianism, resurgent world religions, and indifference, but also renewed efforts to heal Christian divisions through the ecumenical movement.

## Contemporary Christianity

By the late 20th century, Christianity ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christianity)) had become the most widely disseminated religion on earth. Missionary activity had touched almost every nation, though Christians were minorities in many parts of Asia and Africa. The growth of large churches outside Europe challenged the older dominance of Western Christianity and forced all major traditions to think about new cultures, social needs, and forms of church life.

[[Roman Catholicism]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catholic_Church)) is described as the largest Christian body, outnumbering all other Christians combined. Its organization extends from local parish to diocese to papacy, with bishops acting in the name and authority of the pope while retaining administrative responsibility. Religious orders, congregations, and societies work alongside diocesan structures, subject to the pope but not simply identical with parish administration.

Roman Catholic polity rests on a theological claim, not just an administrative system. The church traces papal authority to [[Jesus - The Christ and Christology (Jesus Christ)|Jesus Christ]]'s grant of the power of the keys to Peter and his successors. Christ is the invisible head of the church, and the pope is the visible head. This belief shapes Catholic relations with other Christians and its claim to proclaim and protect the fullness of Christian truth.

Catholic life changed after Vatican II ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_Vatican_Council)) in 1962-1965. The Roman Catholic Church affirmed ties with separated brethren in Eastern Orthodoxy and Protestantism, even while maintaining its claim to be the true church of Christ. This created a more accommodating stance toward ecumenical dialogue, modern culture, and the personal character of Christian faith.

Earlier papal definitions had sharpened Catholic distinctiveness. Pope Pius IX proclaimed the Immaculate Conception of Mary in 1854, the First Vatican Council of 1869-1870 defined papal infallibility, and Pope Pius XII defined Mary's bodily Assumption into heaven in 1950. These dogmas, resting on the claim that the pope can teach saving truth without error, set Roman Catholicism apart from churches that recognize no such authority.

[[Protestantism]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Protestantism)) is harder to define because its diversity is immense. A formal definition treats it as non-Roman Western Christianity and groups most Protestantism into Lutheran, Anglican, Reformed, and Free Church families. It notes that a high-church Anglican or Lutheran may have more in common with an Orthodox theologian than with a Baptist, showing how complex Protestant identity is.

Lutheranism is the largest non-Roman Catholic denomination in the West. Lutheran churches in Germany, Scandinavia, and the Americas differ in polity but relate through national and international councils, especially the Lutheran World Federation. Their doctrine is set out in the Book of Concord and especially the Augsburg Confession. Lutherans kept much of the old liturgical tradition while changing doctrine and practice around Reformation convictions.

Lutheran mission societies in the 18th and 19th centuries gave Lutheranism an international character. North American Lutheranism gradually moved beyond its older German and Scandinavian membership toward a more cosmopolitan constituency. Lutheran churches joined the ecumenical movement and the World Council of Churches, though they have not often crossed denominational boundaries into full communion with other bodies.

Anglicanism includes the established Church of England and national Anglican churches around the world. Like Lutheranism, it retained much Roman Catholic liturgy and piety. The Oxford movement after the middle of the 19th century renewed claims about Anglicanism's Catholic character, restored ancient liturgical usage, and strengthened respect for tradition in Anglican theology.

Anglican identity is strongly linked to apostolic succession through bishops. Although Pope Leo XIII rejected Anglican orders in 1896, Anglicans continued to treat the historic episcopate as central to church unity. The Lambeth Quadrilateral made the historic episcopate one condition for full communion. Anglicanism also had to rethink its role as it moved beyond establishment in England and grew in Asia, Africa, and the New World.

Presbyterian and Reformed churches come from the reforming work of John Calvin and his associates. In Germany, France, and Switzerland they are often called Reformed; in Britain and the United States they are often called Presbyterian because of their polity. They separated more thoroughly than Lutherans or Anglicans from Roman Catholic liturgy, piety, and doctrine.

Reformed theology emphasizes the sole authority of the Bible ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bible)) with special rigor and tends to distrust symbolic and sacramental traditions from the Catholic centuries. It also developed a distinctive view of church and state, sometimes called theocratic, in which ministers proclaim God's revealed will to civil magistrates. Puritanism in England and America gave classic expression to this view, along with moral discipline and personal uprightness.

Free churches, Baptists, Congregationalists, Quakers, Pentecostals, and many later Protestant bodies show the continued variety of Protestant life. Congregationalism stressed the full authority of the local congregation under Christ and became especially important in North America after the Pilgrims arrived on the Mayflower in 1620. Even Pentecostal communities, originally shaped by free charismatic impulses, eventually developed regulated polity and ritual forms.

## Christian doctrine

[[Religious Doctrines and Dogmas (Doctrine and Dogma)|Doctrine]] means teaching, and Christian doctrine is the effort to state the message of the gospel and the content of faith responsibly. Doctrine includes both what is taught and the act of teaching it. Christians accept that God is transcendent and beyond full human comprehension, yet they also believe God has revealed enough to make truthful speech about God necessary.

Teaching occurs in several settings. Evangelism proclaims the gospel to those who have not heard it. Catechesis prepares converts for baptism. Liturgy teaches when the church gathers for worship. Ethics applies faith to daily life. Doctrine also becomes urgent when heresy, outside criticism, cultural change, or geographical expansion requires the church to clarify and restate its message.

Concern for orthodoxy appeared early. Already in apostolic times, Paul corrected misunderstandings about resurrection, and the First Letter of John opposed denial that Jesus Christ had come in the flesh. Ignatius of Antioch rejected Docetism because it denied the real humanity and suffering of Christ, which also undermined the Eucharist.

Irenaeus of Lyon and other early theologians opposed Marcion and the Gnostics by defending the unity of creation, redemption, and Scripture. The struggle against heresy was not only a negative boundary-making process. It helped the church state why the God of Israel, the Father of Jesus Christ, the creator of the world, the incarnation, and the resurrection all belonged together in one gospel.

[[Religious Doctrines and Dogmas (Doctrine and Dogma)|Dogma]] is the most authoritative form of Christian teaching. Dogma traces back to Jesus' authority, the apostolic commission to teach all nations, the promise of the Holy Spirit, and the apostolic charge to preserve the deposit of faith. By the 2nd century, bishops were treated as special guardians of apostolic teaching, and councils became a way to decide doctrine when serious disputes arose.

The first council in Christian memory is the meeting in [[Jerusalem]] described in Acts 15, where Apostles and elders decided conditions for Gentile admission to the church. Later ecumenical councils included Nicaea, Constantinople, Ephesus, Chalcedon, Constantinople II and III, and Nicaea II. The Roman Catholic Church recognizes 21 such councils, including Trent, Vatican I, and Vatican II.

The number of councils accepted varies by tradition. The Oriental Orthodox churches, comprising Copts, Ethiopians, Syrians, Indians, and Armenians, count only the first three, Nicaea in 325, Constantinople in 381, and Ephesus in 431, while the Eastern Orthodox add Chalcedon in 451, the second and third councils of Constantinople in 553 and 680-681, and the second council of Nicaea, which sanctioned the veneration of icons.

Most Protestant churches accept the first four councils as especially authoritative. The most widely accepted conciliar dogmas concern the Holy Trinity and the identity of Jesus Christ as the incarnate second person of the Trinity. Nicaea and Constantinople sharpened existing creeds rather than inventing Christianity, clarifying the baptismal confession of Father, Son, and Holy Spirit.

Athanasius of Alexandria argued that salvation itself was at stake in the Nicene dispute. If the Son and Spirit were not fully divine, then the persons invoked in baptism could not truly save. Basil of Caesarea defended the full divinity of the Spirit and connected dogma with liturgy, because the church worshiped Father, Son, and Holy Spirit together.

Medieval doctrine reached a high systematic form in Thomas Aquinas. His Summa theologiae begins with questions about human knowledge of God, reason and faith, and language about God. It then treats the Trinity, creation, human nature, moral life, the Incarnation, and sacraments. Aquinas used a disciplined method of objections, contrary authorities, his own response, and replies, making theology a rational and ordered inquiry.

Aquinas' authority grew after his death. He was canonized in 1323, declared a Doctor of the Church in 1567, and commended for Catholic study by Leo XIII in 1879. Neo-Thomism shaped Roman Catholic theology into the 20th century, and John Paul II again praised Aquinas in Fides et Ratio in 1998.

In [[Protestantism]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Protestantism)), doctrine is less centralized. Luther, Calvin, and Wesley remain major doctrinal mentors in their traditions, and confessions and catechisms create family resemblances among Lutherans, Reformed Christians, and Methodists. Yet modern individualism and Protestant accommodation to cultural change make it harder to speak of one common Protestant doctrinal mind.

Christian moral teaching also shows the problem of moving from broad gospel principles to concrete cases. Catholic encyclicals, Protestant assemblies, and the World Council of Churches have addressed labor, justice, peace, society, and preservation of creation. Christians may agree on a controlling moral vision but disagree in pastoral judgment about issues such as abortion or homosexual relations.

## God the Father

God the Father is understood through the tension between God's transcendence and God's self-disclosure. Christian mystics and theologians agree that God cannot be fully captured by concepts or images. Yet because God encounters human beings, theology must speak about that encounter, while admitting the limits of reason and language.

Rudolf Otto, a 20th-century theologian, described the experience of the holy as the numinous, the ineffable, and the overwhelming. He used the terms mysterium tremendum, the mystery that repels or inspires awe, and mysterium fascinosum, the mystery that attracts through beauty, blessing, and saving power. This experience of holiness connects with Christian ways of speaking about God.

Christian thought about the Father is also shaped by creation, providence, judgment, and covenant. God is not merely an abstract first principle. In Christian faith, God is the Lord of salvation history, creator of all things, and Father of Jesus Christ. This understanding allows Christians to speak of God personally while still affirming that divine reality exceeds ordinary human categories. The Father's relation to human freedom became a defining controversy when Augustine of Hippo, bishop from 396 to 430, insisted that salvation depends entirely on divine grace against Pelagius, who stressed human moral capacity; Pelagianism was condemned at the councils of Carthage in 418 and confirmed at Ephesus in 431, fixing grace at the centre of Western teaching about God.

## God the Son

![[attachments/christianity_001.webp|420]]

*Sixth-century icon of Christ Pantocrator from Sinai*

Faith in [[Jesus - The Christ and Christology (Jesus Christ)|Jesus Christ]] is connected closely with the Kingdom of God that he proclaimed. Late Jewish messianic hopes included the expectation of a Davidic king who would establish God's reign on earth and gather Israel. Political messianic hopes were strong under Hellenistic and Roman domination, especially in Galilee, where resistance movements expected divine power to overturn oppression.

Jesus disappointed expectations of a purely political Messiah. He proclaimed God's Kingdom but did not let himself be made an earthly revolutionary king. Christian faith then interpreted his death, resurrection, and exaltation as the decisive transformation of messianic hope. The Kingdom was present in his person and works, yet its fulfillment was still awaited in his return and final judgment.

[[Jesus - The Christ and Christology (Christology)|Christology]] is the effort to say who Jesus is. Early Christians confessed him as Lord, Son of God, Messiah, Word, and Savior. The Gospel of John emphasized the eternal Logos who became flesh, while other New Testament traditions stressed baptism, Spirit, obedience, suffering, resurrection, and exaltation. These different emphases later fed debates over incarnation and the relation of Christ's humanity and divinity.

Those debates produced firm conciliar definitions. The Council of Ephesus in 431 rejected Nestorius's division of Christ into two persons, the Council of Chalcedon in 451 defined him as one person in two natures, unconfused and undivided, and the third Council of Constantinople in 680-681 condemned monothelitism, the view that Christ had only one will. Earlier, the first Council of Constantinople in 381 had condemned Apollinaris, who denied that Christ had a rational human soul. Each ruling held that only a Savior fully God and fully human could redeem humanity.

The doctrine of salvation also depends on Christology. Anselm of Canterbury described Christ's atoning work as satisfaction for God's offended honor so that sinful human beings could return to God. In many parts of sub-Saharan Africa, Jesus has been received as healer, liberator from evil, protective ancestor or elder brother, Chief of Chiefs, and initiation master who introduces people to God's secrets. The Swedish theologian Gustaf Aulen later distinguished three classic types of atonement teaching: the dramatic victory over the powers of evil, the Latin theory of satisfaction shaped by Anselm, and the subjective model of moral influence associated with Abelard in the 12th century.

These varied images show that the Saviour and salvation are expressed through many cultural languages. The gospel changes vernacular speech, worship, and art, while local Christian expressions can spread beyond their places of origin. Liturgy and the arts are especially creative settings where Christ is interpreted as God and human, suffering servant and risen Lord, teacher and redeemer.

## God the Holy Spirit

![[attachments/christianity_002.webp|420]]

*Handling serpents at the Pentecostal Church of God. (Kentucky) by Russell Lee.*

The Holy Spirit is described as one of the most difficult themes in Christian theology. Unlike Father or Son, the Spirit does not evoke a concrete image as easily. In the Gospel of John, the Spirit is poured out after Christ's ascension as Paraclete or Counsellor, beginning a new time of salvation for the church left in the world.

The Spirit's free spontaneity is central. The Spirit blows where it wills, yet where it acts it creates divine authority and norm. Prophecy, knowledge, Scripture, office, sacraments, and church order are all connected with the Spirit. The laying on of hands symbolizes transmission of the Spirit and continuity of spiritual authority in bishops and priests.

The Spirit also creates charismatic persons. Prophets, healers, teachers, curers of souls, holy fools, and Pentecostal leaders appear throughout Christian history. Prophetic women ranged from Anna and the daughters of Philip to Hildegard of Bingen, Bridget of Sweden, [[Joan of Arc]], Reformation women, and holiness or Pentecostal pioneers such as Aimee Semple McPherson.

There is a recurring dialectic in the theology of the Spirit. The Spirit creates law and breaks law. Charismatic impulses may found new movements, but those movements often develop offices, rules, and ritual forms. This happened in the early church and again in Pentecostal communities, where free outpouring of the Spirit eventually needed biblical, congregational, or ritual structures. The Spirit's work in shaping Scripture also had a fixed endpoint: Athanasius of Alexandria, in his 39th Easter letter of 367, listed the canonical writings of apostolic origin, after which revelation in binding Scripture was regarded as closed. The Council of Constantinople in 381 then expanded the creed to confess the Holy Spirit as the Lord and Life-giver, who with the Father and the Son is worshipped and glorified.

## The Holy Trinity

![[attachments/christianity_003.webp|420]]

*ISR-2013-Jerusalem-Holy Sepulchre-dome*

The classic doctrine of the Trinity gathers Christian beliefs about God into one central form. Its foundation is older than the doctrine itself: early Christians experienced God as Creator and Father, as Jesus Christ living among them and risen from death, and as the Holy Spirit giving new life and power. The doctrine arose from the need to reconcile this threefold encounter with faith in one God.

The question was difficult because Christianity ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christianity)) inherited Jewish monotheism and rejected pagan polytheism. Early answers differed, especially according to how Christians understood Jesus. The Gospel of John made Christ's divinity central through the Logos. The Gospel of Mark could be read as emphasizing Jesus' baptism and anointing by the Spirit. The Holy Spirit was often harder to picture as a personal figure and so received less early speculation.

Neoplatonic themes helped theologians think about the relation between divine unity and threefold manifestation. The Logos tradition supplied language for eternal divine Word, creation, and restoration. Speculative theology asked how the transcendent God, beyond all being, could be revealed as Father, Son, and Spirit without dividing the one God into three gods.

[[Augustine]] shaped Western Trinitarian thought by linking the Trinity with anthropology. Since humans are made in God's image, Augustine looked for traces of the Trinity in human knowing, self-consciousness, and contemplation. This did not explain away the mystery, but it gave Western theology and metaphysics a model for thinking about unity, relation, memory, understanding, and will.

Joachim of Fiore developed a Trinitarian view that divided salvation history into successive periods of Father, Son, and Holy Spirit. This model influenced later revolutionary and utopian thought because it suggested that history could move toward a new age of the Spirit. It was controversial because it could turn Trinitarian theology into a program for historical acceleration.

The final dogmatic formula, often linked with the Athanasian Creed, is one substance and three persons. This formulation goes back to Tertullian and holds together two claims: God is one, and God is revealed as Father, Son, and Holy Spirit. The doctrine protects the reality of salvation and redemption rather than solving the mystery in a fully rational way. The so-called Athanasian Creed reached its final wording about 500, while the decisive term homoousios, meaning of one substance, had been adopted at the Council of Nicaea in 325 to safeguard the Son's equality with the Father. Athanasius of Alexandria argued that salvation itself was at stake unless all three persons invoked at baptism were fully divine, a conviction that shaped the church's worship and creeds.

## Anthropology

Christian anthropology begins with the belief that human beings are created in the image of God. This links God and humanity through a mysterious relation. Humans are creatures who depend completely on God for their existence, yet they also have a special place in creation because God reflects the divine image in them and calls them into partnership.

Two directions appear in early Christian interpretation. One direction stresses creaturely solidarity: humans share dependence on God with all other creatures. This solidarity was sometimes overshadowed by the emphasis on human superiority and dominion, but it survived in figures such as Francis of Assisi, whose Canticle of the Sun praised God with all creatures.

The second direction stresses the human being's special vocation. Humanity is given a unique relation to God and a role in ruling or caring for creation. This special position is not simple pride, because humans remain dependent creatures. It becomes meaningful only when joined to responsibility before God and to the restoration of God's image through salvation.

Christian anthropology is also corporeal. Christianity does not aim at escape of the spirit from the body. Because of incarnation and resurrection, redemption concerns the whole human being: body, soul, and mind. Vladimir Solovyov expressed this by asking what moral victory would mean if death in the physical body were not overcome.

Another theme is progressive human perfection. Traditional anthropology often pictured humanity as complete in a finished world, with salvation as restoration of a lost image. Some theologians, however, read the New Testament as pointing toward growth in individuals and in humanity as a whole. The promises of resurrection, glory, and sharing in divine life give Christian hope a dynamic future.

The church's social stance changed over time. Earlier models could identify church order with fixed hierarchy and discipline. With John XXIII and Vatican II ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_Vatican_Council)) in 1962-1965, the Roman Catholic Church adopted a more accommodating stance toward modern society, ecumenism, and the personal character of Christian faith. This shift affected Christian anthropology because it rethought human freedom, dignity, and historical responsibility.

## Organization

Early church discipline focused on holiness. Problems included relation to pagan culture, sexual conduct, murder and property crimes, and false teaching. Early Christians distinguished mortal from nonmortal sins, with apostasy after baptism treated as especially serious because it seemed to reject salvation after receiving it.

The problem of second repentance strengthened episcopal authority. Pope Calixtus, who reigned in the early 3rd century, allowed bishops to decide exclusion from and readmission to the congregation. Though this faced fierce opposition from movements such as Montanism, it helped concentrate penitential discipline in bishops' hands and strengthened the monarchical episcopate.

Church organization took many later forms. Catholic and Orthodox traditions developed strong sacramental and episcopal structures. Protestant groups often emphasized the priesthood of all believers, freedom of proclamation, and the authority of the local congregation. Congregationalism was especially strong in New England after the Pilgrims reached Plymouth in 1620. Episcopal structures survived unevenly into the Reformation: the Swedish state church kept its bishops through a resolution of the imperial Diet of Vasteras in 1527, whereas in the German Lutheran territories the line of apostolic succession was broken, since the Catholic prince-bishops of the 16th century refused to surrender their temporal sovereignty by joining the Reformation.

Even movements that begin with free charisma usually create order. Pentecostalism shows this pattern. Pentecostal communities could not remain in constant unregulated spiritual impulse. They formed biblical, congregational, or ritual structures, confirming the wider Christian pattern that the Spirit both creates law and breaks law.

## Liturgy

Liturgy ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liturgy)) is one of the main places where Christian faith becomes visible. The early Eucharist was a fellowship meal with the risen Christ and a sign of the promised messianic banquet. It joined memory of Jesus' Last Supper, faith in his resurrection, and hope for the Kingdom of God.

The Eucharistic meal helped Christians experience the continuing presence of the Resurrected One. The community saw in the meal a continuation of the appearances of the risen Christ and a confirmation of salvation. Because of this, many liturgical forms developed to deepen the meaning of the meal, the sacrifice, the thanksgiving, and the unity of the church.

Christian tradition also grew through Scripture. The church joined the story of Jesus' life, passion, death, and resurrection to the Hebrew Scriptures, which it interpreted as the Old Testament. The New Testament became the literary form of church tradition. This created a tension between spirit and letter: the Spirit creates tradition, but can also renew or break tradition when written forms become lifeless.

Sacramental understandings differ across traditions. The Roman Catholic Church fixed the number of sacraments at seven: baptism, confirmation, Eucharist, penance, holy orders, matrimony, and anointing of the sick. The Orthodox Church also fixed seven mysteries. Classical Protestant churches usually accepted only baptism and Eucharist as sacraments, though Luther allowed penance a special place.

The forms of worship were standardized over time. The Roman Church changed from Greek to Latin in its services about AD 370, and the canon of the Latin mass had nearly its lasting shape by the 6th century. After the Council of Trent, the Roman see fixed the words and gestures of the rites in a series of official books: the Breviary in 1568, the Missal in 1570, the Pontifical in 1596, and the Ritual in 1614. A parallel catechetical effort produced the Catechismus ad Parochos in 1566 for the instruction of parish priests.

Other holy acts are treated differently in different traditions. Foot washing, which replaces the Lord's Supper in the Gospel of John, did not become a universal sacrament but remains in some churches and on some occasions. Orthodox baptism includes triple immersion, renunciation of Satan facing west, turning east toward Christ, and chrismation as the seal of the gift of the Holy Spirit.

## Monasticism

Monasticism ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monasticism)) expresses the Christian search for perfection through ascetic discipline, prayer, and love of God and neighbor. Its inspiration is traced to Jesus in the wilderness and the apostolic community in Jerusalem. By the 4th century, it had become a major institution, especially through Egyptian and Syrian ascetics.

Anthony represents the solitary ideal. His withdrawal into the desert, struggle against demons, and life of prayer made him a model for Christian perfection. Athanasius's Life of St. Antony spread this ideal widely. The desert became a place where Christians imagined the deepest battle against the flesh, the devil, and worldly distraction. Athanasius carried the Pachomian rule westward during his banishment to Trier in 340 to 346, helping to spread communal monasticism in the Latin world.

Pachomius represents communal monasticism. His cenobitic monasteries organized monks into common life, work, prayer, and obedience. Later rules by Basil, the Rule of the Master, and the Rule of Benedict gave monasticism stable forms that shaped Christian spirituality, education, hospitality, manuscript culture, and reform movements for centuries.

Dates and places anchor this growth. Pachomius, who lived about 290 to 346, founded his first communal cloister at Tabennisi in Egypt in 323, joining houses of 30 to 40 monks each under a superior. Basil, bishop of Caesarea from 370 to 379, rejected the purely hermit ideal in favor of communities bound to service. Monasticism reached the West through John Cassian of Marseille, who lived about 360 to 435, and Benedict of Nursia, about 480 to 547, whose balanced Rule became predominant. Because monks copied manuscripts, monasteries such as that of Cassiodorus, who lived about 490 to 585 and had Cicero and Quintilian copied, preserved classical learning.

Monastic literature also became a major part of Christian tradition. The Desert Fathers, John Climacus's Heavenly Ladder, John Moschus's Spiritual Meadow, hagiography, and monastic rules taught readers how to understand holiness. They were not modern biographies but works of devotion designed to form desire, discipline, and religious imagination.

## Eschatology

The last things were first in urgency for early Christians. They believed that God's promises had been fulfilled in Jesus Christ, but not yet completed. They awaited Christ's Second Coming, the resurrection of the dead, the Last Judgment, and the full Kingdom of God.

Early Christian eschatology inherited two Jewish patterns. One expected an earthly messianic kingdom centered in Jerusalem, ruled by a Davidic Messiah. The other expected a heavenly kingdom inaugurated by the Son of man, where the elected from all times would share resurrection life. Early Christian hope combined these patterns rather than keeping them separate.

Under persecution, Christians developed expectations of reigning with the returning Lord. Paul's letters and Revelation speak of living believers meeting Christ, dead believers rising, and the faithful sharing Christ's reign before the general resurrection and judgment. Revelation condensed this hope into the idea of a thousand-year reign, later called millennialism.

Eschatological expectation continued after the Reformation in radical and sectarian movements. Thomas Muntzer linked apocalyptic hope with the poor and with revolt during the German Peasants' War of 1524-1525. The Munster movement under Jan of Leiden, Fifth Monarchy Men in England, Adventist currents, and revival movements all show how expectation of Christ's imminent return could create new groups.

Such expectation reached far back into the Middle Ages. [[Charlemagne]] accepted imperial coronation on Christmas Day in 800 partly amid eschatological hopes tied to the legend of the Last Emperor, the year 1000 stirred the Peace of God movement in France, and the knights of the First Crusade of 1095-1099, some of whom massacred Jews in Germany, were moved by apocalyptic expectation. Joachim of Fiore later built a millennialist philosophy of history that influenced radical reform.

A secularized form of eschatology appears in social utopia. Christian visions of a future perfect society influenced works such as Johann Valentin Andreae's Christianopolis, [[Francis bacon|Francis Bacon]]'s New Atlantis, Henri de Saint-Simon's New Christianity, and Etienne Cabet's Voyage to Icaria. Later socialism and even Marxist utopian hope preserved transformed elements of Christian end-time expectation.

Christian ideas of life after death include immediate bliss or perdition, sleep of the soul until the Last Judgment, resurrection, purgatory, and eternal life. Tensions exist between individual judgment after death and the general Last Judgment. Roman Catholic purgatory gives an interim state where correction remains possible, while other traditions reject or reinterpret that idea.

The hope of eternal life affected Christian views of personality, marriage, and friendship. Emanuel Swedenborg's ideas influenced romantic philosophy of religion and interpretations of marriage and friendship by Schelling and Schleiermacher. The Western concept of personality was deepened by the Christian claim that persons have eternal value before God.

## Christian philosophy

Whether there is such a thing as Christian philosophy is debatable. Christianity is not primarily a system of ideas but a way of salvation. Yet as it became a historical tradition, it absorbed philosophical assumptions, created new arguments, and responded to criticism from both inside and outside the church.

Greek philosophy strongly shaped early Christian thought. As Christianity moved from its Jewish beginning into the Greco-Roman world, it had to speak in terms educated people could understand. Middle Platonism, Neoplatonism, Epicureanism, and Stoicism formed a common intellectual background, especially through ideas of hierarchy, spiritual reality, eternal values, and return to the divine source.

Neoplatonic themes gave Christian thinkers language for the relation of the spiritual and physical, the reality of goodness, truth, and beauty, and the soul's movement toward God. The Logos doctrine in John allowed Christians to connect Christ with divine reason, creation, and restoration. The Apologists used these tools to defend Christianity and to build theology.

Several classic arguments about God are debated. The design argument saw the order of the world as evidence of an intelligent creator. David Hume challenged this in Dialogues Concerning Natural Religion in 1779, arguing that order might arise without design and that the world's imperfections do not justify belief in an infinitely good and powerful creator.

Charles Darwin's theory of natural selection made Hume's challenge stronger by explaining biological adaptation without direct design. In the 20th century, thinkers such as Frederick R. Tennant and Richard Swinburne reformulated design arguments using probability, human intelligence, moral experience, religious experience, and the fine-tuning of the universe. These arguments remained debated and contested.

The cosmological argument appears in forms such as Aquinas' first-cause argument and argument from contingency. The ontological argument, associated with Anselm, claims that the idea of God as unsurpassably perfect implies existence. [[Descartes and Cartesianism (Descartes)|Descartes]] reformulated it, while Kant, Gassendi, Bertrand Russell, and others criticized the assumption that existence is a predicate. Anselm, archbishop of Canterbury from 1033 to 1109, also gave classic form to the theology of atonement in Cur Deus homo, Why God Became Man, arguing that only the God-man could satisfy the debt of human sin. Medieval Christian philosophy reached its summit in the Scholasticism of Thomas Aquinas, who lived from about 1224 to 1274 and sought a synthesis of faith and reason after the recovery of [[Aristotle and Aristotelianism|Aristotle]].

In the 20th century, philosophers such as Charles Hartshorne, Norman Malcolm, and Alvin Plantinga defended new forms of Anselm's argument. These debates exemplify how Christian philosophy interacts with general philosophy: it borrows tools, faces objections, revises arguments, and keeps returning to questions about God, world, reason, and faith.

## Christian mysticism

Mysticism is the sense of contact with the divine or transcendent, often understood as union with God. It is a major force in Christian history and modern religion. Scholars have studied it psychologically, comparatively, philosophically, theologically, hermeneutically, and deconstructively, especially through attention to mystical texts.

Theoretical debates ask whether mysticism is the core of personal religion or one element among many. Some scholars seek a common core of mystical experience across religions, while others argue that experience and interpretation cannot be separated and that Christian mysticism is shaped by Christian doctrine, Scripture, worship, and church life. Walter T. Stace and Robert C. Zaehner represent different approaches to typology and comparison.

Eastern Christian mysticism developed from the 2nd century onward by joining early Christian themes with Greek philosophical categories. The unknowability of God, the Logos, contemplation of God, deification, and sharing in the divine nature all became important. Clement of Alexandria and especially Origen gave early speculative shape to mystical theology.

Deification, or theiosis, became a major Eastern theme. It meant not that humans become God by nature, but that they share in divine life through Christ and the Spirit. The language drew on biblical texts about becoming children of God and sharing divine nature, and it later became important in monastic, liturgical, and contemplative traditions.

Western Catholic mysticism begins especially with [[Augustine]], bishop of Hippo. In Confessions he describes experiences of touching or attaining God, and in his commentary on Genesis he classifies visions as corporeal, spiritual or imaginative, and intellectual. He did not teach full personal union with God in this life, but he shaped Western thought about contemplation, the vision of God, and the restoration of the image of the Trinity.

Gregory I transmitted Augustinian themes to the medieval West, including the active and contemplative lives and the sacramental dimension of mysticism. Later, Johannes Scotus Erigena translated and interpreted Eastern mystical writings such as those of Pseudo-Dionysius, creating a speculative negative mysticism that emphasized God's unknowability.

The 12th century brought new mystical schools connected with monastic reform. Bernard of Clairvaux among the Cistercians and Richard of Saint-Victor among the canons developed systematic accounts of love, contemplation, and ascent to God. Later Christian mysticism included many forms: Christ-mysticism, Trinitarian contemplation, negative theology, affective devotion, and visions.

Mysticism was never isolated from church and society. It related to morality, doctrine, liturgy, gender, text, and authority. Debates continue over whether mystical knowing has cognitive value, how ineffable experience can be written, and how mystical union relates to ethical life. It shows that Christian mysticism is both personal and deeply tied to the tradition's symbols.

## Christianity and culture

Christianity is a producer of culture ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Culture)), not only a set of beliefs. It shaped literature, music, architecture, painting, education, political thought, social reform, and myths. Medieval legends, saints' lives, liturgical poetry, monastic rules, and theological disputes all became part of a broad Christian civilization.

Christianity ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christianity)) also interacted with science and modern intellectual life. Some modern scientific discoveries challenged cherished doctrines of creation, but many Christian thinkers responded by reinterpreting doctrine rather than abandoning faith. Historical criticism attacked traditional assumptions but also gave churches better access to the Bible, Fathers, Reformers, and the history of doctrine.

Christian social thought developed through charity, discipline, reform, and institutional action. Protestant bodies often acted through assemblies and agencies; Catholics through papal encyclicals and episcopal teaching; ecumenical bodies through common statements. The World Council of Churches, founded in 1948, promoted concepts such as responsible society, justice, peace, and preservation of creation.

Christian culture can also be criticized from within. Radical reformers, mystics, social utopians, abolitionists, missionaries, ecumenists, and modern theologians all challenged inherited structures. Christianity therefore appears as a tradition of continuity and reform, preserving Scripture, worship, and doctrine while repeatedly producing movements that question existing church life.

## Church and Society

Christianity and society have never had one fixed relationship. Churches have sometimes supported political authority, sometimes resisted it, and sometimes tried to stand apart from it. Christian debates over church and state, war and peace, property and poverty, marriage and sexuality, minorities, and social welfare show that doctrine becomes practical when believers organize communities and respond to public life.

Care for the poor, sick, widowed, orphaned, imprisoned, and displaced became one of Christianity's most durable social forms. Monasteries, hospitals, schools, charities, missions, deaconal agencies, Catholic orders, Protestant societies, and ecumenical organizations all made welfare part of Christian witness. This activity could reinforce social order, challenge injustice, or accompany empire, but it also made compassion, discipline, and service central tests of Christian life.

## Missions and ecumenism

Christian mission is grounded in the command to make disciples of all nations and in the conviction that Christ's salvation concerns the whole world. Missions extended from early expansion to the 19th century, when Roman Catholic and Protestant missions reached many parts of Africa, Asia, and the Pacific. Mission societies, Bible translation, schools, hospitals, and local churches changed both Christian communities and receiving cultures.

Early medieval missions had already spread the faith across Europe. The Goth Ulfilas converted his people to Arian Christianity about 340 to 350 and translated the Bible into Gothic; the Franks under Clovis accepted Catholic Christianity between 496 and 508; and Ansgar, who lived from 801 to 865, opened the mission to Scandinavia, which reached Iceland by 996. The mission to the Poles began about 966, and the brothers Cyril and Methodius created a Slavonic alphabet in the 9th century, so that by 1050 most of Europe lay within Christian influence apart from Muslim Spain.

Mission history also created new theological problems. Christianity spread through European empires but was not identical with them. In former colonies, Protestantism and Catholicism could be seen as parts of foreign rule, yet local believers also made Christianity their own. The growth of African and Asian churches challenged the older idea that Christianity was mainly Western.

Ecumenism seeks healing among divided Christians. Modern ecumenical efforts grew from Protestant and Anglican beginnings into wider participation by Orthodox churches and, after Vatican II, Roman Catholicism. Earlier hopes of reunion suggested that Christians once shared a common essence and might recover it by stripping away later disputes and focusing on essentials.

Ecumenism also faces real doctrinal and institutional barriers. Roman Catholics maintain papal authority and the identity of the Catholic Church; Orthodox churches preserve autocephaly and ancient liturgical traditions; Protestants differ over Scripture, sacraments, ministry, and church order. Yet the movement continues because Christians share baptismal language, Scripture, Christ-centered faith, and concern for witness in the world.

## Christianity and other religions

A final theme is Christian relations with other world religions. Christianity historically made strong claims about revelation, salvation, and the uniqueness of Christ. These claims made contact with other religions difficult, especially when Christians feared syncretism or believed other traditions were rivals to the gospel.

Modern pluralism forced new questions. Christians encountered Buddhism, Islam, Hinduism, secularism, Communism, and many Indigenous traditions not only as objects of mission but as living systems with their own truth claims and moral worlds. Modern theology of religions asks whether salvation is found only inside explicit Christian faith, through Christ beyond visible church boundaries, or in many religious paths.

John Hick is noted in connection with theology of religions and pluralistic interpretation. Such approaches try to explain how different faiths may relate to ultimate reality, while more traditional Christian views defend the uniqueness of Christ and the church's mission. The tension between witness and dialogue remains part of contemporary Christianity.

## Belief, Institutions, and Global Diversity

Christianity ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christianity)) began as a Jewish movement centred on Jesus of Nazareth and the conviction among his followers that he was the Messiah and had been raised from death. Early Christians interpreted Jesus’ life, death, and resurrection through Jewish scripture while also carrying their message into Greek- and Latin-speaking cities of the Roman Empire. This movement gradually became distinct from Judaism as disagreements over law, identity, mission to non-Jews, and authority developed.

Central Christian beliefs concern God, Jesus Christ, salvation, sin, forgiveness, resurrection, and the hope of a renewed creation. Most major traditions confess the Trinity: one God understood as Father, Son, and Holy Spirit. Christians differ over how to explain the relationship between divine grace, human freedom, scripture, tradition, sacraments, moral action, and church authority. These differences are theological, but they have also shaped political alliances, education, art, family life, and social organization.

The early church developed bishops, councils, creeds, worship practices, and a canon of scripture as it responded to persecution, internal disagreement, and imperial patronage. When Christianity became linked to the Roman Empire, church and state gained a complex relationship: imperial support gave the church security and reach, but it also entangled religious authority with political power. The division between Greek-speaking eastern churches and Latin-speaking western churches became formal in 1054, producing the enduring distinction between Eastern Orthodoxy and Roman Catholicism.

The Protestant Reformation of the sixteenth century challenged papal authority and debated scripture, justification, sacraments, clerical life, and church governance. Lutheran, Reformed, Anglican, Anabaptist, and later evangelical traditions developed alongside Catholic renewal. Reformation conflicts led to war and persecution but also encouraged literacy, translation, congregational participation, and new forms of political thought. No single Protestant model replaced older Christianity; the result was a more plural Christian world.

Christianity became global through migration, trade, empire, missionary work, local conversion, and indigenous adaptation. Churches in Africa, Asia, Latin America, the Pacific, Europe, and North America developed distinctive worship, leadership, theology, and social priorities. Modern Christians debate poverty, peace, gender, sexuality, racial justice, science, ecology, religious freedom, and relations with other faiths. Christianity is therefore best understood not as one fixed culture but as a worldwide family of traditions connected by shared texts, practices, and arguments.

## Key Events & Developments

- 1st century AD: Jesus of Nazareth's life, death, and resurrection become the center of Christian faith.
- 2nd century: Apologists, anti-Gnostic writers, canon formation, and baptismal creeds strengthen Christian identity.
- AD 200: Roman baptismal confession resembles the later Apostles' Creed.
- 4th century: Constantine, councils, public churches, saints' cults, and monasticism reshape Christian life.
- 325 and 381: Nicaea and Constantinople clarify the divinity of the Son and Holy Spirit.
- 367: Athanasius of Alexandria lists the canonical New Testament writings in his 39th Easter letter.
- 451: Chalcedon becomes central for later Christological debates.
- 843: The end of iconoclasm in the Byzantine world renews figurative Christian art.
- 1054: The East-West schism hardens separation between Eastern and Western Christianity.
- 1517 onward: The Protestant Reformation divides Western Christianity and creates new confessional families.
- 1545-1563: The Council of Trent defines the Catholic Reformation response and fixes the wording of the rites.
- 1568-1614: The Roman see issues the Breviary, Missal, Pontifical, and Ritual as official liturgical books.
- 1620: The Mayflower migration helps make North America a major setting for Congregational and Protestant development.
- 19th century: Roman Catholic and Protestant missions expand Christianity worldwide.
- 1948: The World Council of Churches is founded as a major ecumenical body.
- 1962-1965: Vatican II ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_Vatican_Council)) changes Roman Catholic relations with modernity, other Christians, and the wider world.
