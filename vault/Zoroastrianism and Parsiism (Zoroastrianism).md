## Introduction

Zoroastrianism ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zoroastrianism)) is the ancient pre-Islamic religion of [[Iran]]. It survives in small Iranian communities and more strongly in [[India]], where descendants of Zoroastrian Persian immigrants are known as Parsis or Parsees. In India the religion is also called Parsiism. It was founded by the Iranian prophet and reformer Zoroaster in the 6th century BC and contains both monotheistic and dualistic features.

The religion had wide historical influence. It likely affected [[Judaism]], [[Christianity]], and [[Muhammad and the Religion of Islam (Islam)|Islam]], and ancient Greek and later Christian traditions imagined Zoroaster as a master of dualism, astrology, magic, or biblical prophecy. Modern study of Zoroastrianism ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zoroastrianism)) has also helped reconstruct parts of early Indo-European religion.

The religion worships one supreme god while preserving traces of an older polytheistic world. Its dualism is not an equal contest between two eternal powers. Good is certain to win, but evil temporarily limits divine rule. Human beings freely join the struggle with body and soul. Because good and evil are not the same as spirit and matter, fasting and celibacy are generally rejected except in purification rites. Daily life still keeps a strong ritual concern with purity and death-related defilement.

## History

*Zoroastrian Fire Temple, Yazd 03*

Pre-Zoroastrian Iranian religion is known indirectly through later Iranian sources and comparison with Indian religion. The Iranians and northern Indians probably came from common Indo-Iranian ancestors, and the Avesta and Vedas share fire worship, sacred drink, and gods related to Mithra, Varuna, and Indra. A treaty from about 1380 BC also names Aryan gods.

A major Iranian religious shift raised the ahuras and degraded the daevas into demons. Zoroaster, or Zarathushtra, was a priest of Ahura Mazda, the Wise Lord. His hymns, the Gathas, cannot be securely placed in a known historical setting, but he probably lived in eastern Iran before Cyrus II unified Iran. His protector Vishtaspa cannot be confidently identified with the father of Darius I.

The Achaemenid kings worshipped Ahura Mazda, though their exact link with Zoroaster is uncertain. Darius I, who seized power in 522 BC, restored cult places destroyed by the Magian usurper Gaumata. Xerxes later replaced some daeva worship with Ahura Mazda's cult, and from Artaxerxes II onward inscriptions also mention Mithra and Anahita.

After Alexander's conquest, Iranian religion was partly submerged by Hellenism. It slowly reappeared in mixed Greek-Iranian forms, such as Zeus Oromazdes and Apollo Mithra in Commagene. A Zoroastrian calendar is attested at Nisa before the Christian era, suggesting some official recognition. In Persis, however, evidence for fire worship disappears for a time before the rise of the Sasanians.

Under the Sasanian dynasty, Zoroastrianism became more organized. Ardashir I, a hereditary priest from Staxr, rose with strong religious zeal. Later tradition credits Tansar with collecting sacred texts, while Karter built hierarchy, restored Mazdean religion in conquered lands, and persecuted rival religions and heresies.

Sasanian religious politics also involved conflict with rival movements. Mani, founder of Manichaeism, died in prison under Bahram I. Under Qobad, the reformer Mazdak promoted communal property and social equality, threatening nobles and clergy. Qobad first supported him, was overthrown, regained power, and then suppressed the Mazdakites. Khosrow I continued this suppression and strengthened both state and Mazdean church.

## Post-Islamic Iranian Zoroastrianism

[[Muhammad and the Religion of Islam (Islam)|Islam]] defeated the last Sasanian forces at al-Qadisiyah in 635. Although Islam theoretically tolerated the old religion, conversions by persuasion and force became widespread. Zoroastrian resistance sometimes caused persecution, and survival became concentrated in small communities, especially around Yazd and Kerman.

Iranian Zoroastrians, called Gabars by Muslims, produced books to preserve the essentials of the religion during danger. The exact course of decline is unclear, but the ancient majority religion became a persecuted minority tradition after the Islamic conquest.

## The Parsis in India

From the 10th century, groups of Zoroastrians migrated to [[India]] and found refuge in Gujarat. Their connection with Iranian Zoroastrians was almost broken until 1477, when letter exchange began again and continued until 1768.

Under British rule, the Parsis moved from modest agriculture into commerce and industry and became a prosperous modern community centred in [[Bombay (Mumbai)|Bombay]]. They adopted Gujarati language and local dress, later British customs, girls' education, and the end of child marriage, and helped poorer Iranian Zoroastrians.

Parsis adapted their practice to Indian surroundings. They reduced features offensive to Hindus, such as blood sacrifice, and accepted some interest in astrology and theosophy. When Christian missionaries attacked their dualism, Parsis increasingly stressed the monotheistic side of their doctrine.

## Sources

The only texts confidently attributed to Zoroaster are the Gathas, hymns written in a special dialect and various metres. They are embedded in the Yasna, a major Avesta division recited by priests in a sacrifice ceremony. Other Avesta divisions include the Visp-rat, the Videvdat or Vendidad, the Siroza, the Yashts, the Hadhoxt Nask, and the Khurda Avesta.

The Avesta was compiled in stages and completed under the Sasanians, but only part survives. Pahlavi works later preserved doctrine, including the Denkart, Bundahishn, Menok-i Khrat, Book of Artay Viraf, writings of Zatspram and Manushchihr, and Mardan-Farrukh's defense of Mazdean religion.

## God

Zoroaster's message belongs to the religion of the ahuras, opposed to the cult of the daevas. He placed Ahura Mazda, the Wise Lord, at the centre and described the divine side as ordered against evil. The moral opposition between Asha, truth or right order, and Druj, falsehood, is fundamental. The Twin Spirits choose between them: the Bounteous Spirit sides with Asha, while the Destructive Spirit, Angra Mainyu, sides with Druj.

This conflict reaches into human life and the material world. The good side includes the Good Mind, Dominion, Aramaiti, Integrity, Immortality, fire, cattle, earth, water, plants, and faithful people. Because divine powers also appear as human faculties, followers can participate in the struggle through thoughts, words, deeds, and loyalty to Ahura Mazda.

Later theology changed Zoroaster's system. Older gods such as Mithra, Anahita, Verethraghna, and Vayu returned in the Later Avesta. The Bounteous Spirit was absorbed into Ahura Mazda, while Ahura Mazda and Ahriman came to appear as opposing principles. By the 4th century BC, Greek testimony already described Magian belief in two principles.

## Cosmogony

The Bundahishn presents Ormazd and Ahriman as separated by the void. Ahriman's envious attack begins the process of creation. Because Ahura Mazda could no longer easily be father of both opposing spirits, Zurvanism explained Ormazd and Ahriman as sons of Zurvan, or Time. Orthodox Mazdeans rejected this as heresy, but Zurvanism was widely accepted, especially in Sasanian times.

Orthodox texts still preserve traces of Zurvanite speculation. Ormazd creates the material world from Infinite Light through a bright, white, round form of fire. Gayomart, the Primal Man, is also spherical, like the sky. Mazdean groupings of Time, Space, Religion, and Ormazd seem adapted from Zurvanite groupings of Zurvan, Light, Wisdom, and Power.

## Cosmology

Ormazd creates the world as a battlefield where Ahriman can be defeated. The struggle lasts 9,000 years and is divided into great periods. Ormazd first defeats Ahriman with the sacred Ahuna Vairya prayer. Later Ahriman enters the material world, kills the Primal Bull and Gayomart, and unintentionally helps generate plants, animals, metals, and the first human couple through the purified remains of his victims.

Human beings are a microcosm of the larger creation. They are mortal because of Ahriman's attack, but they have immortal parts, including life, religion, knowledge, soul, and fravashi. After death, the soul meets its own religion, daena, as a beautiful young woman if the person lived justly or as a hideous figure if not.

The soul is judged by Mithra, Sraosha, and Rashnu and must cross the Bridge of the Requiter. Good souls reach paradise through good thoughts, words, and deeds; bad souls fall into hell; and balanced souls remain in an intermediate place.

## Eschatology

Zoroaster expected saviours who would renew the world. Later tradition developed the idea that three sons of Zoroaster would be born at thousand-year intervals from his preserved semen. The last saviour, Saoshyans or Astvat-ereta, brings final justice.

Pahlavi texts describe the end as a return to the original state. People gradually give up meat, milk, and plants until they live only on water. In the final battle, Ormazd's warriors defeat Ahriman's forces. Molten metal burns the wicked but feels like warm milk to the just; after three days, all humanity enjoys happiness and hell is sealed forever.

## Cultic Places

Although Herodotus claimed that Persians had no temples, archaeological remains show terraces, towers, square rooms, and chahartaq buildings. Permanent fire altars are clear from the Sasanian period and appear on coins.

Three great fires were linked with social orders. The Farnbag fire belonged to priests, the Gushnasp fire to warriors and royal-religious unity, and the Burzen-Mihr fire to farmers. Fires were also classified as Aduran, village fires, and Varhran, provincial or royal fires.

## Priesthood

The Magians became connected with Zoroaster's teaching by at least the 4th century BC and controlled religion at the Achaemenid court. Under the Sasanians a hierarchy developed, including the magupat, or chief of magi, and the higher title magupatan magupat, chief magus of the chief magi.

The ehrpat was originally a religious teacher and fire caretaker. In modern usage, herbad or ervad means a lower priest who assists in major ceremonies. Above him is the mobed, and above all is the dastur, a bishop-like figure who directs important temples. Priesthood is hereditary, but priests still require investiture rites.

## Ceremonies

Young Parsis are initiated at age seven in India and at age 10 in Persia. They receive the sadre, a sacred shirt, and the kusti, a girdle, which they wear throughout life. Purification ranges from simple ablution to a bath and the complex bareshnum ritual, which lasts several days and includes the protective gaze of a dog.

The chief ceremony is the Yasna, a sacrifice of haoma before the sacred fire with recitations from the Avesta and offerings such as bread and milk. The sacred fire must burn continually and be fed at least five times a day. Prayers are also recited five times daily. Confession, penance, and the resolve not to sin again help cleanse faults.

Death rites protect purity from corpse pollution. A dog is brought before the corpse, fire is kept near the body, and the corpse is taken by day to the Tower of Silence. Bodies are exposed, stripped by vultures, dried by the sun, and later placed in a central well. The fourth morning matters because the soul reaches judgment.

## Festivals

Zoroastrian festivals combine worship with joy, because the religion treats happiness as a duty. The main Parsi festivals are the six seasonal Gahanbars and the days at the year's end when the dead are remembered.

Each day and each month is dedicated to a deity, and when the day name matches the month name it becomes that deity's feast. Noruz, the New Year and spring festival, honours Rapithwin, the personification of noon and summer. Mehragan, the feast of Mithra, was traditionally an autumn festival with importance similar to Noruz.

## Ethics

Mazdean ethics centre on preserving life and fighting evil. To preserve life, people should work through cattle raising and agriculture and should procreate. To fight evil, they must oppose demons and beings linked to death. Good is related to light and life; evil is related to darkness and nonlife.

This life-affirming ethic is not universal respect for all life. Harmful animals may be destroyed because they belong to the evil side. The tradition also developed strong endogamy, including close-kin marriage, partly to preserve family privilege.

A person's future life depends on the balance of good and evil thoughts, words, and deeds. Human weakness is recognized, so faults can be removed through confession and through transferred merits gained by prayers and ceremonies for the dead.

## Iconography

There is no independent Zoroastrian art in the usual sense. Iranian art in the Achaemenid, Arsacid, and Sasanian periods was mainly royal. In the Achaemenid period, Ahura Mazda appears as a winged disk above the king.

Later rulers used Greek models for divine images, and Artaxerxes II introduced statues of Anahita. In the Sasanian period, deities usually appear in investiture scenes, such as Ormazd and Anahita at Naqsh-e Rostam or Ormazd and Mithra at Taq-e Bostan. The bull-man figure may have royal meaning and is linked in Pahlavi with Gopatshah, king of Gopat.

Zoroastrian ideas also relate to other religions. Parallels with Israelite religion may include creation, light and darkness, saviour expectation, judgment, resurrection, and final renewal. Scholars have also compared Iranian ideas with Greek thought, including Anaximander and Heracleitus.

## Conclusion

Zoroastrianism ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zoroastrianism)) is strongly moral, but it is not purely ethical in a simple modern sense. Daily practice surrounds the believer with rules about purity, death pollution, demons, confession, rites, destiny, and ritual protection. The follower acts freely, yet life can feel governed by constant threats of defilement and by the power of fate.

This tension sometimes connects with Zurvanism and fatalism. Still, the broader religion remains based on a moral view of life: good and evil are real, human choice matters, and the final order of the world belongs to good.

## Key People

- Zoroaster ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zoroaster)): Prophet and reformer who centred the faith on Ahura Mazda, truth, moral choice, and the struggle against evil.
- Ahura Mazda ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ahura_Mazda)): Wise Lord and supreme god of Zoroastrian worship.
- Darius I ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Darius_I)): Achaemenid king who used Auramazda as an imperial divine focus.
- Karter: Sasanian priest who built hierarchy, advanced orthodoxy, and persecuted rival religions.
- Tansar ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tansar)): Priest remembered in later tradition for collecting sacred texts and fixing the canon.
- Mani: Founder of Manichaeism, a rival movement suppressed under Sasanian Zoroastrianism.
- Mazdak ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mazdak)): Social and religious reformer whose egalitarian movement was crushed.
- Khosrow I ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Khosrow_I)): Sasanian ruler credited with strengthening state and Mazdean church after suppressing Mazdakism.
- Saoshyans ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saoshyant)): Final saviour figure who brings resurrection, judgment, and renewal.
