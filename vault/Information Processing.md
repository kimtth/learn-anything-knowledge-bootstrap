## Introduction

Information processing is the acquisition, recording, organization, retrieval, display, and dissemination of information. In recent use the term often refers to computer-based operations, but information also includes facts, opinions, art, gestures, physical responses, and genetic codes. Organized information becomes knowledge; applied knowledge becomes expertise; and deeper analytic or experiential insight can become wisdom.

Information phenomena became major objects of study in the 20th century across philosophy, physics, biology, linguistics, information science, computer science, communications engineering, management science, and the social sciences. Commercially, information services became a new global industry, while manufacturing and service industries increasingly depended on information handling.

## Recording and Storage

![[attachments/information_processing_002.webp|420]]

*Bottom-up processing of sublimation*

Recorded information exists in analog and digital forms. Paper, film, magnetic audio tape, magnetic videotape, magnetic characters, bar codes, and special marks can preserve text, image, sound, and machine-readable data. Photography greatly improved the accurate capture of visual information, while electromagnetic media in the 20th century expanded direct recording of speech, music, video, and alphanumeric signals.

Digital representation ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Digital_data)) stores information as binary signals in devices with two states, conventionally represented by 0 and 1. Text, numbers, images, sound, and video can be digitized, compressed, transmitted, indexed, searched, and recombined. Large nondocument data streams are collected automatically in astronomy, environmental monitoring, scientific modeling, experimentation, and national security.

Document capture can be manual or automatic. Keyboarding, touch screens, digital tablets, and pen computers enter data directly, while editing and formatting software reduce some errors and support page design. Document imaging became important because many paper documents contain drawings or images and cannot be efficiently converted by keyboard transcription alone.

Optical and electronic storage expanded access and capacity. Optical discs and jukeboxes could store millions of pages of text or terabytes of image data, and magneto-optical rewritable discs suited multimedia information combining text, image, and sound. These technologies helped digital libraries and software distribution move beyond traditional paper and microform storage.

## Databases and Retrieval

Databases became a new information resource after computers digitized text in the early 1960s for Index Medicus and NASA's Scientific and Technical Aerospace Reports. By the late 1960s, bibliographic and numeric databases existed outside traditional libraries and archives, often controlled by database vendors.

Online database services grew through advances in computer storage, telecommunications, time-sharing, automated indexing, and searching. Libraries and archives adopted computerized catalogs, union catalogs, library networks, and automation programs. After 1990, communications networks accelerated the virtual library, making public-domain resources, catalogs, texts, journals, encyclopaedias, scientific data, software archives, and institutional information accessible through the Internet.

Indexing refines access beyond broad subject headings by assigning words or phrases that identify important topics, names, or concepts. Abstracting condenses full documents into short summaries, though it always loses some information and may introduce bias. Together, indexed abstracts help users keep track of primary information sources.

Electronic document description borrows principles from bibliographic cataloging but faces new problems. In globally distributed databases, a document name may need to include a server location and a logical address because files can move or disappear. Physical traits such as format and size are less stable or meaningful for electronic documents than for analog ones.

## Content Analysis

Text analysis uses indexing, linguistic analysis, and database methods to identify meaning and support retrieval. Natural-language information is difficult because words, syntax, context, and intention are complex, yet digital representation allows documents to be searched, compared, summarized, and linked.

Image analysis relies mainly on image processing and pattern recognition. Image processing enhances, compresses, analyzes, and reconstructs images. Pattern recognition reduces information by measuring objects, extracting defining features, and assigning visual or logical patterns to classes. Applications include astronomy, medicine, industrial robotics, and satellite remote sensing.

Speech analysis converts sound elements into alphanumeric equivalents so speech can be indexed and processed like text. Speech recognition classifies spectral representations and matches digital spectrographs against stored templates. The task is difficult because speech sounds overlap, vary by speaker, and require heavy storage and computation. Synthetic speech reverses the process by converting stored sound elements into analog signals.

## Display and Output

Video display converts stored data into visual form. Television sets were useful for menu-oriented systems such as videotex but had limited text resolution. Computer terminals became the main interface for human viewing, first through cathode-ray tubes and later through flat-panel displays using liquid crystal, light-emitting diode, plasma, or electroluminescent media.

Advanced displays support scrolling, paging, zooming, multiple windows, colours, graphics, video, and sometimes touch commands. Resolution determines how much information a screen can show, from character-addressable displays to high-resolution bit-addressable screens required for graphics, video, engineering workstations, and information-systems design.

Print remained dominant because paper is convenient and portable. Since the 1960s, large computers and high-speed printers automated volume publishing, while microcomputer-based desktop publishing let small organizations format text and graphics interactively and output page images to electronic printing or photocomposition devices.

Printers are divided into impact and nonimpact types. Impact printers strike paper through inked ribbons with character hammers or dot-forming pins. Nonimpact printers include laser printers, LED printers, ion-deposition printers, and ink-jet printers. Microfilm and microfiche were important storage and reproduction media for decades but were largely replaced by optical disc technology in the 1990s.

Voice output uses synthetic speech for information requests, repetitive instruction, voice mail, toys, and other settings where spoken response is natural or efficient. Because human speech is the most effective everyday communication form, speech technologies became increasingly attractive even when technically demanding.

## Dissemination History

Handwritten records required laborious copying by scribes, monks, and other specialists. Mechanical reproduction began with Chinese block printing during the T'ang dynasty, from 618 to 907. Movable type was invented in China in the mid-11th century and used more extensively in Korean bookmaking during the 15th century, though ideographic writing required many type forms.

European movable type is associated with Johannes Gutenberg in 1440-1450. Within 30 years, movable-type printing spread across Europe. Ottmar Mergenthaler's 1886 Linotype machine automated line casting, and Monotype further mechanized composition. Mechanical typesetting dominated until electronic and optical methods displaced it after the 1960s.

Graphic reproduction used woodcuts, metal engravings, etched stone plates, lithography, and offset lithography. Aloys Senefelder's late-18th-century lithography enabled planographic image transfer, while later photographic and electronic processes expanded the speed, scale, and fidelity of information dissemination.

## Core Equations

Engineering and technology topics often combine efficiency, signal flow, and computational cost:

$$\eta=\frac{\text{useful output}}{\text{input}}$$

Efficiency compares useful output with total input, so losses appear as the gap between the two.

$$SNR=10\log_{10}\left(\frac{P_s}{P_n}\right)$$

Signal-to-noise ratio compares signal power with noise power on a logarithmic decibel scale.

$$T(n)=O(n\log n)$$

Big-O notation describes how running time grows with input size, here at a rate typical of efficient divide-and-combine algorithms.

## Key Events & Developments

- Digital text: Computers digitized text for major abstracting journals in the early 1960s.
- Online databases: Bibliographic and numeric databases became new resources by the late 1960s.
- Virtual libraries: After 1990, networks and the Internet accelerated distributed access to information.
- Chinese block printing: Mechanical reproduction developed during the T'ang dynasty.
- Movable type: Johannes Gutenberg's character-oriented printing spread through Europe after 1440-1450.
- Linotype: Ottmar Mergenthaler introduced keyboard-driven line casting in 1886.
