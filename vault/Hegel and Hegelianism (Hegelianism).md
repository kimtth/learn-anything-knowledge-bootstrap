# Hegel and Hegelianism (Hegelianism)

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> Hegelianism comprises the diverse movements and interpretations that developed from Hegel's attempt to unite logic, metaphysics, nature, law, history, and culture. Its successors disputed how the dialectical relation of history, thought, and totality should be preserved, criticized, or transformed.

Hegelianism ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Georg_Wilhelm_Friedrich_Hegel)) refers to the philosophical movements that grew out of the work of Georg Wilhelm Friedrich Hegel. that this tradition is not simply a school of thought in the narrow sense, but a broad family of interpretations that took Hegel’s ideas in different directions. At its core, Hegelianism treats history and logic as connected, and it sees truth as something whole rather than as a collection of isolated facts.
Hegelianism emerged as a response to the ambition of Hegel’s own system. His philosophy tried to unify logic, metaphysics, nature, law, history, and culture into one comprehensive structure. Later thinkers then argued over what should be preserved, rejected, or transformed in that inheritance.

## The Hegelian Heritage

The Hegelian system was a major attempt to solve all major philosophical problems at once. Hegel held that reality is best understood as a totality, or Absolute Spirit, developing itself through history and thought. This idea gave later Hegelian thinkers a powerful model for connecting philosophy with culture, politics, and social life.

At the same time, this totalizing ambition created deep tensions. Many critics believed that Hegel’s system absorbed the particularity of individuals, institutions, and historical experiences into one grand scheme. That criticism became a major source of later debates within and beyond German philosophy.

## Early Reactions and the Left, Centre, and Right

The first major divisions within Hegelianism appeared early. Some thinkers, especially on the right and centre, tried to preserve or reform Hegel’s system. Others, on the left, used his ideas for more radical conclusions about history, religion, and society. Figures such as David Friedrich Strauss, Bruno Bauer, and Friedrich Schelling appear as important participants in this early struggle over the meaning of Hegel.

The discussion of Strauss is especially important because his radical critique of biblical and theological assumptions pushed Hegelian thought in a more critical direction. This produced a lively intellectual battle over whether Hegelian philosophy could remain compatible with historical criticism and modern ideas of religion.

## Criticism and Revision
Major criticisms of Hegelianism came from thinkers such as Friedrich Adolf Trendelenburg, who challenged Hegel’s logic and dialectic. Trendelenburg argued that Hegel’s movement from being to nothing to becoming rested on hidden assumptions and that his use of contradiction was not properly justified. These criticisms helped push later Hegelian thought toward more careful analysis of logic and method.
These disputes connect to the rise of later movements, including existentialism, neo-Hegelianism, and Marxism. In particular, it notes that thinkers such as Søren Kierkegaard attacked Hegel’s effort to reconcile all oppositions, arguing that human existence contains irreducible tensions that cannot be dissolved by abstract reasoning.

## Global Influence
Hegelianism spread far beyond Germany. It influenced philosophers and political thinkers in France, Italy, Russia, Poland, and the United States. In some places, Hegelian ideas became tied to social reform or political activity; in others they were adapted to theological, historical, or cultural debates. This international spread helped make Hegelianism one of the most important intellectual movements of the 19th century.

It also notes that in the 20th century, neo-Hegelianism reappeared in different forms, especially in Britain, Italy, and the United States. Thinkers such as F.H. Bradley, Benedetto Croce, and Giovanni Gentile revived or reshaped Hegelian themes in response to positivism and modern philosophy.

## Key Events & Developments

- 19th century: Hegelianism developed as a broad movement after Hegel’s death.
- 1830s: Early debates emerged between right, centre, and left Hegelians.
- 1840s: Critiques by Trendelenburg and Kierkegaard deepened the movement’s internal conflict.
- 20th century: Neo-Hegelianism reappeared in Britain, Italy, and the United States.
