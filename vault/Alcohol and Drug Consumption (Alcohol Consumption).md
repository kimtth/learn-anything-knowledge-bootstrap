# Alcohol and Drug Consumption (Alcohol Consumption)

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> Alcohol consumption concerns the physiological effects, social patterns, cultural meanings, and regulation of drinking beverages containing ethyl alcohol. Its consequences range from intoxication and dependence to public-health and policy concerns.

## Introduction

Alcohol consumption ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alcoholic_beverage)) is the drinking of beverages containing ethyl alcohol ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethanol)). Such beverages are consumed largely for their physiological and psychological effects, but drinking also takes place within specific social settings and sometimes in religious practices. Because alcohol affects both the body and behaviour, governments often regulate its use. The main alcoholic beverages are beer, wine, and spirits ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liquor)). Beer usually contains between 4 and 5 percent alcohol, though some varieties range from 2 to 8 percent. Natural wines commonly contain 8 to 12 percent alcohol, with some somewhat higher. Spirits such as vodka, rum, and whiskey generally contain 40 to 50 percent alcohol.

A standard drink in many bars contains roughly 0.5 to 0.7 fluid ounce of absolute alcohol. Because of that, a shot of vodka, a glass of wine, and a bottle of beer may be equally intoxicating if each contains a similar amount of alcohol. Alcohol can be studied both as a chemical agent acting on the individual and as a social practice shaped by local customs, religion, law, and public policy.

## Ingestion

![[attachments/alcohol_consumption_001.webp|420]]

*Alcohol dehydrogenase enzyme structure*

When an alcoholic drink is swallowed, it is diluted by gastric juices in the stomach. A small amount of alcohol enters the bloodstream directly through the stomach wall, but most passes through the pyloric junction into the small intestine, where absorption is rapid. Before it reaches the intestine, however, up to half of the alcohol may be degraded in the stomach itself. In general, a lower proportion is degraded in a young woman's stomach than in a young man's, because the gastric secretions of young women tend to contain lower levels of the enzyme alcohol dehydrogenase (ADH), which helps break down alcohol before absorption.

The rate of absorption is affected by several conditions. Strong alcohol taken on an empty stomach may cause a pyloric spasm that slows movement into the intestine and changes the absorption pattern. Food in the stomach, especially fatty food, tends to delay absorption. Carbonated alcohol or alcohol consumed with carbonated beverages is usually absorbed faster than noncarbonated drinks. Emotional state may also matter. Once absorbed, alcohol is distributed through the body according to the water content of tissues and organs, appearing in higher concentration in blood and brain than in fat or muscle. The same amount of alcohol therefore tends to produce higher blood levels in women than in men because of average differences in body composition and metabolism.

## Intoxication

Alcohol is a drug that affects the central nervous system. It is commonly classified as a depressant, along with barbiturates, minor tranquilizers, and general anesthetics. Its effects are paradoxical. In some conditions alcohol seems to excite behaviour, while in others it acts as a sedative. At high concentrations it becomes increasingly depressive, leading to stupor, deep sedation, and coma. The early excitement phase includes exhilaration, loss of restraint, talkativeness, mood shifts, and anger. Much of this apparent stimulation actually comes from alcohol's suppression of inhibitory brain centres rather than from direct energizing action.

Physical signs of intoxication include slurred speech, unsteady walking, altered sensory perception, and loss of fine motor control. These effects result mainly from alcohol's action on the brain systems that organize muscular coordination and judgment. The most important immediate effects are on higher mental functions such as thinking, learning, memory, and decision making. Controlled experiments have shown that many supposed benefits of drinking, such as better dancing, sounder sleep, less sexual inhibition, or greater creativity, depend more on suggestion and self-perception than on real improvement. In objective terms, alcohol usually reduces rather than enhances performance.

## Effects of Alcohol on the Brain
Alcohol affects judgment before the drinker necessarily feels severely impaired. It interferes with the higher functions of the brain, and what seems like confidence or ease may really be a weakening of restraint and critical self-control. Experiments also suggest that memory and learning partly depend on the mental state in which they occur. Material learned while under the influence of alcohol may sometimes be recalled better in a similar state. This finding illustrates how strongly alcohol alters the conditions of mental processing, though it does not make alcohol useful for learning.

## Europe

In [[Europe]], patterns of drinking differ greatly. In both [[France]] and [[Italy]], wine consumption is high, but attitudes and customs differ in important ways. French parents tend to show strong emotional attitudes either in favour of or against their children's drinking, whereas Italian parents more often introduce children to wine with little emotional drama. Italian standards for respectable drinking are generally lower, and intoxication is more strongly disapproved. In [[France]], drunkenness may be treated with humour or even seen among men as a sign of virility. These differences are thought to help explain why alcoholism historically caused much more mortality and illness in [[France]].

Among the Scandinavian countries, the main pattern is not daily drinking with meals but heavy drinking on weekends or special occasions. This helps explain why alcohol-related problems may be relatively high even when total per capita consumption is lower. These countries also have strong temperance movements and large abstaining populations. In England and [[Ireland]], the pub remains a major social centre of drinking, and beer is the most popular drink. This preference for beer and the social importance of the pub are also found in places heavily shaped by British settlement, including [[Australia]], [[New Zealand]], and much of [[Canada]].

## Latin America

There has been less systematic research on drinking patterns in Latin America, [[Africa]], and Oceania than in [[Europe]] and the [[United States of America (United States)|United States]]. The available evidence suggests that people in Latin America drink significantly less on average than people in [[Europe]], [[North America]], or Oceania. One study found that in 1998 total per capita alcohol consumption in Latin America was 40 percent lower than in eastern [[Europe]] and [[North America]] and 50 percent lower than in [[Europe]] as a whole.

Local patterns vary sharply. A study in [[Chile]] found one middle-class population whose habits resembled some European ones: moderate drinking, often at home and with meals, and disapproval of drunkenness. A larger working-class group customarily drank outside the home, in male company, on weekends or paydays, and sought intoxication as a sign of virility and friendship. A third group described as indigenous showed a similar pattern. Favoured drinks included pisco, a strong local brandy, and wine. Drinking accompanied both secular and religious events such as births, baptisms, marriages, and funerals, though women were expected to drink in a much more limited way.

## Japan

In [[Japan]], heavy drinking and drunkenness have traditionally been permitted within clearly delimited social situations and have had a socially integrative role. The traditional drink is sake, often called rice wine but more properly a brewed drink comparable in process to beer, typically containing 14 to 17 percent alcohol. Another common beverage is shochu, a distilled product containing about 25 percent alcohol. Numerous customs and rituals involving sake have been tied to religious and social occasions.

At the same time, that [[Japan]] has a long history of both heavy drinking and concern about its consequences. Abstinence was practiced by some followers of [[Buddha and Buddhism (Buddhism)|Buddhism]] and by some respected Japanese thinkers. In the late 19th century, modernization brought a temperance movement influenced partly by the Woman's Christian Temperance Union and the Salvation Army. After [[World Wars (World War II)|World War II]], growing American influence helped increase the popularity of beer and imported beverages, especially whiskey.

## United States

Drinking patterns in the [[United States of America (United States)|United States]] have been studied more systematically than those in any other country. The evidence shows no single national model but rather many patterns shaped by immigrant traditions, native populations, economic circumstances, politics, and historical change. Still, some broad changes can be identified. After Prohibition and [[World Wars (World War II)|World War II]], the proportion of abstainers declined, especially among women. By the early 1970s, about 77 percent of adult men and 60 percent of adult women were drinkers, and these figures later stabilized.

Underage drinking appears to have decreased in some settings, though heavy drinking on college campuses, especially binge drinking, remained a serious problem. Abstention generally rises with age. There were also strong differences among social and ethnic groups. Whereas about 30 percent of whites abstained, nearly 50 percent of African Americans and Hispanics, and about 65 percent of Asians and Pacific Islanders, abstained. Rural populations also contained larger proportions of abstainers than urban ones, partly because of stronger ties to religious institutions and different educational and economic patterns.

## Abstinence and Religion

Religious orientation is presented as one of the strongest influences on abstinence. Groups such as Mormons, Christian Scientists, Seventh-day Adventists, Jehovah's Witnesses, and Baptists often made abstinence a mark of committed membership. In some European countries, abstinence movements also drew support from labour organizations influenced by socialism and from groups such as the Order of Good Templars. The larger proportion of abstainers in the [[United States of America (United States)|United States]] compared with some European countries reflects the greater strength of religiously motivated abstinence.

## Scientific Orientation and Public Policy

![[attachments/alcohol_consumption_002.webp|420]]

*Chemical structure of ethanol*

Modern thinking about alcohol has changed greatly. In earlier periods, research and education were often dominated by the ideology of the temperance movement. More recently, there has been a shift toward objective information from scientific and academic sources. In the [[United States of America (United States)|United States]], one major symbol of this change was the founding of the National Institute on Alcohol Abuse and Alcoholism in 1970. Internationally, the International Bureau Against Alcoholism, founded in 1907, became in 1964 the International Council on Alcohol and Alcoholism and later the International Council on Alcohol and Addictions, reflecting a move away from total condemnation of drinking toward a more analytical view of alcohol-related harm.

The most effective methods of reducing per capita alcohol consumption and abuse are increased taxation, limits on availability and advertising, and random breath-analyzer tests supported by quick and certain penalties. By contrast, preventive educational efforts in schools had not fulfilled expectations. This conclusion reinforces the larger theme that alcohol consumption is shaped not only by personal desire but also by biology, social custom, and political structure.

## Key Events & Developments

- 1907: The International Bureau Against Alcoholism is founded.
- 1964: It becomes the International Council on Alcohol and Alcoholism.
- 1970: The National Institute on Alcohol Abuse and Alcoholism is founded in the [[United States of America (United States)|United States]].
- Late 20th century: Scientific approaches replace temperance-era assumptions in policy and research.
- Early 21st century: Taxation, limited availability, and breath testing are identified as effective control measures.
