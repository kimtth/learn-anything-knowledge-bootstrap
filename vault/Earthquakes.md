## Introduction

Earthquakes ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Earthquake)) are sudden shaking events of the ground caused by seismic waves moving through the Earth's rocks. They happen when energy stored in the crust is released quickly, usually because rock masses that have been pushing against one another break and slip. Most earthquakes happen along faults, where rock blocks move in relation to each other, and many of the largest faults lie near the edges of the Earth's tectonic plates. Before modern seismology developed in the early 20th century, people knew earthquakes mostly through damage and fear, not through clear scientific explanation.

Earthquakes are common on a global scale. Tens of thousands are felt each year without instruments, and a much smaller number are strong enough to cause serious damage when they happen near populated places. Very large earthquakes are less frequent, but they can bring deaths, destruction, fires, landslides, and tsunamis. Earthquakes are not rare accidents in nature; they are a regular part of how the Earth releases built-up stress, and they have shaped human life for a long time throughout history.

## Nature and Distribution

The nature of earthquakes is clear from the way the main earthquake belts of the world follow the margins of tectonic plates, so the global pattern is not random. The greatest concentration is found in the Circum-Pacific Belt ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ring_of_Fire)), which runs around much of the Pacific Ocean and includes places such as [[Japan]], [[New Zealand]], Alaska, and the western coasts of the Americas. Another major belt is the Alpide Belt, which stretches from the Mediterranean region through Asia and joins the Pacific belt in the East Indies. Smaller but important zones of activity also follow ocean ridges and the rift valleys of East Africa.

The causes of earthquakes follow a similar logic. Earthquakes arise where the rocks of the crust are under strong stress, and the release of that stress can come from elastic strain, gravity, chemical change, or even the movement of large bodies. The most important cause is elastic strain, because rocks can store force for a long time before breaking. In that sense, an earthquake is both a local event and a sign of large-scale movement in the Earth system as a whole.

## Tectonics

![[attachments/earthquakes_002.webp|420]]

*Etna Awakens on its Side*

Tectonic earthquakes are explained by the elastic rebound theory, which says that stress builds up in rock until the rock can no longer hold it. At that point it breaks, and the fault slips quickly. The famous San Andreas Fault rupture of 1906 helped shape this theory after the great [[San Francisco]] earthquake. In that event, the ground moved for hundreds of kilometres, showing that an earthquake can involve a long rupture path rather than a single point of breakage.

During rupture, rock masses are thrown in opposite directions and then rebound toward a less strained position. The movement does not always happen smoothly; it can slow, stop, and start again. These irregular changes help create the earthquake vibrations that travel as seismic waves. The point where rupture begins is the focus, or hypocentre, and the point directly above it at the surface is the epicentre. Fault barriers and rough patches called asperities can stop or slow rupture, so real earthquakes are often more complex than a simple model would suggest.

## Volcanism

Some earthquakes are linked to volcanic activity and are called volcanic earthquakes. In these cases the disturbance may still come from a sudden slip of rock near the volcano, but heat and pressure from rising magma or trapped gas may also help to build the stress. Volcanoes and earthquakes often appear in the same regions, especially around the Pacific and along oceanic ridges, but this does not always mean one directly causes the other in a simple way.

Even when an earthquake seems to occur below a volcanic structure, the deeper cause is often shared tectonic movement rather than a direct volcanic trigger. That is why the presence of volcanoes does not by itself explain most major shallow earthquakes. The relationship is real but indirect: both phenomena can be expressions of the same large Earth processes. In this sense, volcanism is an important setting for earthquakes, but not the only setting and not always the main cause.

## Tsunamis

A tsunami is a long-wavelength sea wave that can follow an undersea earthquake or a submarine landslide. The word comes from Japanese and means harbour wave. These waves are sometimes called tidal waves, but that name is misleading, because the Moon and Sun do not create them. A tsunami happens when a large body of water is suddenly lifted or lowered, usually because the seabed is shifted by a fault rupture or by a landslide triggered by an earthquake beneath the ocean floor.

Tsunamis can travel very fast in deep water while remaining low in height, so ships may not notice them at first. When the waves reach shallow water, their height grows and the damage can become enormous. Major historical examples include the deadly 2004 Indian Ocean tsunami after the Sumatra earthquake. Volcanic eruptions near shorelines, such as Thera and Krakatoa, can also create tsunamis, showing that earthquake-related sea waves are part of a larger coastal hazard system.

## Seiches

Seiches are rhythmic water movements in bays and lakes that can be caused by earthquakes or tsunamis. They may last for hours or even a day or two, and they can be noticed far from the original quake. The great [[Lisbon]] earthquake of 1755 caused oscillations in waters as far away as Scotland and [[Sweden]]. Similar effects were seen in lakes in Texas after the 1964 Alaska earthquake.

A related effect happens when seismic waves pass through seawater after being refracted through the seafloor. If the waves are strong enough, a ship may feel as if it has struck a submerged object, and this is called a seaquake. These examples show that an earthquake can disturb water in more than one way. The ground may shake directly, but the motion can also travel through lakes, bays, and seas in forms that people notice long after the first rupture has passed far away.

## Frequency

Earthquake frequency follows an empirical pattern in which small earthquakes are much more common than large ones. A logarithmic relation links the number of earthquakes and magnitude, showing that the frequency rises sharply as magnitude drops. A one-unit drop in magnitude can mean about ten times as many earthquakes, but the increase in number does not balance the huge loss in energy for smaller events. That is why the largest earthquakes matter most for total energy release.

Earthquakes with magnitude above 4.0 number about 50,000 each year. This number helps explain why seismology is both a study of rare catastrophes and a study of everyday planetary activity. Even though most earthquakes are small, they provide the background against which the great events stand out, and they help scientists understand the full frequency pattern of seismicity.

## Seismic Waves

Earthquake energy travels outward as seismic waves. The main body waves are P waves and S waves, and the main surface waves are Love waves and Rayleigh waves. P waves move fastest and can travel through both solids and liquids. S waves are slower and can move only through solids. Love and Rayleigh waves travel along the surface, and they often cause much of the shaking people actually feel near the ground.

These waves behave differently because of the materials they pass through. Rock layering, soil type, density, and rigidity all change the way waves move, reflect, refract, and scatter. Strong surface shaking can also lead to slumping and liquefaction in loose soil. Seismic waves therefore do more than carry energy away from the focus; they also reveal the internal structure of the Earth and the weakness of the ground at a site during strong shaking.

## Strong-Motion Recordings

Strong-motion recordings are measurements of the actual ground movement close to a quake source. They show acceleration, velocity, and displacement, and they help researchers understand how shaking changes over time. Near the fault, the motion often begins with P waves, followed by a stronger pulse linked to S waves and fault slip. Later, surface waves may dominate, especially farther from the rupture. The pattern is not exactly the same in every earthquake, but it is regular enough to be useful for engineering and research in damaged urban areas.
These recordings are important because they let scientists compare theory with real motion. Modern instruments, including accelerographs, can capture powerful local shaking without being overwhelmed. In cities such as [[Los Angeles]], Tokyo, and [[Mexico City]], the data can be sent quickly to computers and displayed on maps. This makes strong-motion records a practical tool, not just a scientific one, because they help communities respond faster and design safer buildings after each event.

## Seismic Hazard Maps

Seismic hazard maps are built to show where strong shaking is most likely. Good maps should consider both earthquake size and earthquake frequency, use historical seismicity, and include major tectonic trends, acceleration attenuation curves, and intensity reports. They should also be simple, gradual, and free of sharp artificial breaks. In practice, this means the map should help planners see risk without pretending to be more exact than the data allow.

The purpose of hazard mapping is to guide safe planning, especially in areas where strong earthquakes are known to occur. The anticipated shaking may be expressed as peak acceleration or peak velocity, which gives engineers a practical basis for design. Hazard maps also point toward resistant structures, because they only become useful when builders and public agencies use them to shape construction and safety rules for real places.

Earthquake-resistant design is meant to reduce, not eliminate, damage. Many countries require ordinary buildings to meet local codes, while larger or more critical buildings may need more detailed structural analysis. The goal is especially to protect people during moderate but common earthquakes and to keep major structures standing during the strongest shaking. That is a realistic engineering aim for an active planet.

Good design depends on seismological knowledge as well as engineering skill. Builders need information from strong-motion records, intensity reports, and estimates of how often earthquakes occur. Rapid post-earthquake response can also reduce risk, because fast action after the shaking can prevent further injury and damage. In this sense, resistant structures are only one part of a wider safety strategy that joins design, planning, and response into one system.

## Probing the Interior

Seismic waves are not only useful for studying earthquakes; they also help scientists understand the Earth's deep interior. P and S waves, surface wave dispersion, and the vibrations of the whole Earth all provide data for seismological models. By comparing observed travel times and wave shapes with computed models, scientists can infer the structure of the crust, mantle, and core. The result is a picture of the planet built from its own vibrations.

This work has revealed three-dimensional structure in the crust and lithosphere, especially beneath subduction zones, at the base of the mantle, and in the inner core. The answer is still not unique, but the models become better as more data are added. In this sense, the exploration of the Earth's interior is a continuing scientific process rather than a finished task built from evidence.

## Seismology Beyond Earth

Seismology extends beyond Earth, with data from the Moon and Mars. Spacecraft have carried instruments that recorded seismic waves, and lunar stations detected thousands of moonquakes each year during the Apollo era. Because the Moon has a very quiet surface compared with Earth, the instruments could work at high magnification, and multiple stations allowed scientists to locate the source of the waves by using P and S arrival times much as they do on Earth.

These extraterrestrial measurements show that seismic methods are not limited to our planet. Lunar seismic activity includes impacts and other internal events, and similar techniques can also be used for Mars. The broader lesson is that seismic phenomena help scientists study the structure and behavior of rocky worlds wherever they are found, which makes seismology a subject larger than one planet alone.

## Major Historical Earthquakes

Several famous earthquakes stand out among the major historical events. These include the 1755 [[Lisbon]] earthquake, the 1906 [[San Francisco]] earthquake, the 1964 Alaska earthquake, the 1995 Kobe earthquake, the 2004 Sumatra earthquake and tsunami, and the 2010 Haiti earthquake. Together they show how earthquake history is also human history marked by disaster and recovery.

These events matter because they helped shape science, engineering, and public awareness. Some led to new ideas about fault rupture, some showed the danger of tsunamis, and some exposed the weakness of buildings and response systems. In this way, the history of major earthquakes is not only a record of destruction. It is also a record of learning, because each great event pushed people to understand the Earth more carefully and to build more safely after the shock.

## Key Events & Developments

- ancient times: Earthquakes are interpreted through myth and religious explanation.
- 19th and 20th centuries: Seismology and plate tectonics turn earthquakes into a scientific field.
- modern period: Monitoring, building codes, and warning systems become central to earthquake response.
