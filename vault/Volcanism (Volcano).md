## Introduction

A volcano is a vent in the crust of [[Earth - Its Properties, Composition, and Structure (Earth)|Earth]] or another planet or satellite from which molten rock, hot rock fragments, and hot gases erupt. The word can also refer to the landform built around such a vent by repeated eruptions of lava and volcanic debris. Eruptions show immense natural power, but they can cause disastrous loss of life and property, especially in densely populated regions.

Volcanic eruptions often begin when gas-rich magma accumulates in reservoirs near the surface. Warning signs may include steam and gas from small vents, swarms of small earthquakes, or movement of dense viscous magma through surrounding rock. In some eruptions, fluid lava rises and flows steadily or fountains upward. In others, trapped gases tear magma apart and blast ash, bombs, and hot fragments into the air.

One of the most feared hazards is the pyroclastic flow ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pyroclastic_flow)), also called a nuee ardente ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pyroclastic_flow)), a fast-moving mixture of hot gas and incandescent particles that can sweep down a volcano's flanks and incinerate everything in its path. Ash falling on snow or glaciers can melt ice and create destructive mudflows called lahars.

Volcanic landforms differ because eruptions differ. Mauna Loa in Hawaii is a broad, gently sloping shield volcano built by fluid lava. Mount Fuji in [[Japan]] is a steep stratovolcano built from alternating layers of ash and lava. These shapes record long histories of repeated volcanic activity.

## Explosions and Eruption Types

![[attachments/volcanism_volcano_001.webp|420]]

*Tavurvur volcano edit*

Large volcanic explosions occur when gases expand rapidly. This can happen through sudden depressurization of a gas-charged magma body, disturbance of a shallow hydrothermal system, or rapid mixing of magma with groundwater. Explosive eruptions eject particles classified by size: volcanic dust, ash, cinders or scoriae, blocks, and bombs. Blocks are older rock torn from a vent; bombs are hot, soft lava fragments that may twist or crack while flying.

The most destructive blasts can occur when part of a volcanic cone fails, as at Mount St. Helens in the [[United States of America (United States)|United States]] on May 18, 1980. If the blast cloud becomes dense and fluidized, it behaves like a pyroclastic flow and can devastate hundreds of square kilometres.

Eruptions are often grouped into ideal types. Icelandic eruptions pour basaltic lava from long fissures and may build lava plateaus. Hawaiian eruptions release fluid lava from summit and radial fissures, building shield volcanoes. Strombolian eruptions produce repeated moderate bursts of incandescent lava. Vulcanian eruptions produce ash-laden explosive clouds. Pelean eruptions generate pyroclastic flows. Plinian eruptions are intensely violent, with sustained jets of gas and fragmented magma rising into the stratosphere.

Explosiveness depends on dissolved gas, magma viscosity, decompression rate, and the number of nucleation sites where bubbles can form. Magmas at convergent plate margins often contain much gas and are viscous, so gas remains trapped until pressure is high enough to fragment the magma. Rapid ascent, as at Mount Pinatubo in 1991, helps preserve gas and increase explosiveness.

## Pinatubo and Mauna Loa

![[attachments/volcanism_volcano_002.webp|420]]

*Volcán de Ollagüe, Bolivia, 2016-02-03, DD 80-88 PAN*

Mount Pinatubo in the [[Philippines]] became a classic modern example of explosive volcanism in 1991. Before then, it was not listed in world-volcano catalogs and had no written record of eruptions. Earthquake swarms began in March 1991, steam and sulfur vents opened in April, and sulfur dioxide emissions increased sharply in May. Scientists from the Philippine Institute of Volcanology and Seismology and the U.S. Geological Survey monitored the crisis and recommended evacuations.

Pinatubo erupted violently in June 1991, sending ash and gas high into the atmosphere. Its eruption showed the importance of monitoring earthquakes, gas emissions, deformation, and steam explosions. It also showed how long-dormant volcanoes can be extremely dangerous, especially near dense populations and major facilities such as Clark Air Base.

Mauna Loa in Hawaii illustrates effusive volcanism. In 1984, after increased seismicity, an eruption began along the summit caldera and then migrated down the northeast rift zone. Curtains and fountains of lava fed flows from new vents. The eruption produced dramatic lava streams rather than a huge ash cloud, reflecting Mauna Loa's basaltic magma and shield-volcano setting.

These two eruptions show the contrast between gas-rich, viscous, explosive volcanism and fluid, basaltic, effusive volcanism. Many real eruptions combine types, and a single volcano can change behaviour during an eruption sequence.

## Volcanic Landforms

![[attachments/volcanism_volcano_003.webp|420]]

*Pahoeoe fountain edit2*

Shield volcanoes are large dome-shaped mountains built by many fluid lava flows. Their slopes are gentle, often less than six degrees. Large shields such as Mauna Loa can form over about 1 million years through repeated eruptions from summits and rift zones.

Submarine volcanoes form under the sea, often as cone-shaped seamounts. Some old island volcanoes are eroded flat at sea level and then sink as the crust cools, becoming flat-topped guyots. Many active submarine volcanoes occur at shallow depths, while deep submarine eruptions are harder to detect because water pressure suppresses explosive boiling. Loihi, southeast of Hawaii, is a young submarine volcano in the Hawaiian chain.

Calderas are large circular or oval depressions, usually more than one kilometre across, formed by collapse after large volumes of magma are expelled. They are larger than ordinary craters and may contain lakes. Crater Lake in Oregon lies within a volcanic caldera, while Kilauea is called a shield volcano even though it has a summit caldera.

Complex volcanoes form when eruptive habits or vent locations change. A stratovolcano may develop an explosion crater later filled by a lava dome, or new cones may grow on a caldera rim. Pyroclastic cones, lava domes, lava plateaus, shield volcanoes, stratovolcanoes, and calderas all reflect differences in eruption volume, interval, chemistry, vent geometry, eruption style, and environment.

## Tectonics and Geothermal Energy

Most volcanoes occur in belts related to [[Plate Tectonics|plate tectonics]]. The Ring of Fire around the [[Pacific Ocean]] contains many active volcanoes associated with island arcs and mountain chains. Volcanoes commonly occur at subduction zones, where one plate sinks beneath another, and at rift zones, where plates move apart. They are generally absent from transform boundaries such as the San Andreas Fault.

Subduction volcanoes, such as those in [[Japan]], the Aleutian Islands, the Cascade Range, and parts of [[Central America]], tend to be explosive and often build stratovolcanoes. Rift-related volcanoes, especially along ocean-floor spreading centres, usually erupt basalt and tend to be more effusive. There are exceptions, but the tectonic setting strongly affects magma type and eruption style.

Some volcanoes form within plates above hot spots. The Hawaiian Islands and Emperor Seamounts record movement of the Pacific Plate over a long-lived volcanic centre active for at least 80 million years. A major bend between the Emperor and Hawaiian chains formed about 45 million years ago, marking a shift in Pacific Plate motion.

Volcanism is closely linked to geothermal energy. Earth's internal heat is enormous, but useful geothermal power is available only where heat, water, and permeability allow extraction. Hot springs and geysers reveal natural heat flow. In geysers such as Old Faithful in Yellowstone National Park, deep water heats under pressure, flashes into steam as pressure falls, and forces hot water and steam into the air before the system recharges.

## Key Events & Developments

- AD 79: Mount Vesuvius erupted in a Plinian event that buried Pompeii and killed Pliny the Elder.
- 1902: Mount Pelee on Martinique produced a destructive Pelean eruption.
- May 18, 1980: Mount St. Helens erupted after flank failure and a directed blast.
- 1984: Mauna Loa erupted with lava fountains and flows along its summit and northeast rift.
- 1991: Mount Pinatubo erupted explosively after earthquakes, steam vents, and rising sulfur dioxide emissions.
- 45 million years ago: A bend in the Emperor-Hawaiian volcanic chain recorded a change in Pacific Plate movement.
- 80 million years ago: The Hawaiian hot spot had already begun producing the volcanic chain recorded by seamount ages.

## Interesting Facts

- A volcano can mean either the vent itself or the landform built around the vent.
- Pyroclastic flows are among the most dangerous products of explosive eruptions.
- Volcanic bombs are hot lava fragments shaped while flying through the air.
- Stromboli has been called the “lighthouse of the Mediterranean” because of frequent small eruptions.
- Old Faithful erupts after underground water heats, flashes into steam, and recharges.
- Most active volcanoes cluster near plate boundaries rather than being randomly distributed.
- The Hawaiian chain records the motion of the Pacific Plate over a hot spot.
