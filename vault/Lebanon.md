# Lebanon

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> Lebanon is a small eastern Mediterranean state whose coastal trade cities, mountain geography, and religious diversity shaped a distinctive but fragile political order. French mandate formation, confessional power sharing, Palestinian displacement, and regional pressure contributed to civil war and enduring political challenges.

## Introduction

Lebanon is a small state on the eastern Mediterranean coast, with [[Beirut]] as its capital. Its coastal cities, including Tyre, Sidon, and Byblos, were old centers of trade and culture, but the modern state took shape only in 1920 under a French mandate, became a republic in 1926, and gained independence in 1943.

Lebanese confessionalism, the sharing of political power among religious communities, shaped the modern state but also made politics fragile. Mountain terrain gave shelter to many religious and ethnic groups, while Lebanon's position between [[Israel]], Arab neighbors, and Palestinian refugees created pressures that helped lead to the civil war beginning in 1975.

## Land

![[attachments/lebanon_003.webp|420]]

*View from Sidon's Sea Castle, Sidon, Lebanon*

Lebanon is bordered by [[Syria]] to the north and east, [[Israel]] to the south, and the Mediterranean Sea to the west. Its narrow territory makes geography central to its history, economy, settlement, and defense.

## Relief

![[attachments/lebanon_002.webp|420]]

*Bsharri (Becharre, Bsharre) village, Kadisha Valley, Lebanon*

Relief in Lebanon is varied within short distances. Four main regions appear from west to east: a narrow coastal plain, the Lebanon Mountains, Al-Biqa valley, and the Anti-Lebanon and Hermon ranges.

The Lebanon Mountains rise steeply from the coast and reach Qurnat al-Sawda at 10,131 feet, where famous cedars grow. Al-Biqa, about 110 miles long, is a fertile rift valley between mountain systems and becomes rougher toward Mount Hermon.

## Drainage

Most Lebanese rivers are winter torrents from the western mountain slopes. The main exception is the Litani River, which rises near Baalbek, flows south through Al-Biqa, and reaches the Mediterranean near historic Tyre.

## Soils

[[Soils]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soil)) differ sharply by region. Mountain limestone gives thin poor topsoil, while lower and middle slopes have been terraced and cultivated; coastal and northern mountain reddish clay soils hold moisture but also suffer erosion.

## Climate

Lebanon has a Mediterranean climate with hot, dry summers and mild, humid winters. Winter storms bring most rain, from 30 to 40 inches on the coast to more than 50 inches at higher elevations, while Al-Biqa is drier.

Ancient and medieval forests, including cedar, oak, pine, cypress, fir, juniper, and carob, were heavily grazed, burned, and cut. Few large wild animals remain, but smaller mammals and many migratory birds still use mountains, marshes, and coastal areas.

## People

Lebanon has a mixed society formed from Phoenician, Greek, Armenian, Arab, Kurdish, and other elements. Arabic ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arabic)) is the official language, while Armenian, Kurdish, French, English, and Syriac also appear in communities, schools, and churches.

## Religion

Religion ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Religion)) is central to Lebanon's social and political structure. Since the 7th century, the mountains served as refuge for Christian and Muslim communities, including Maronites, Greek Orthodox Christians, Shiites, Sunnis, and Druze.

Because religion and government are formally linked, population balance is politically sensitive, and no official census has been taken since 1932. Settlement patterns also changed after the civil war, as mixed villages and cities realigned along confessional lines and many displaced people moved into poor suburbs.

## Economy

Before the civil war, Lebanon was a regional commercial center with low government intervention, favorable tax conditions, strong services, tourism, and remittances from workers abroad. Imports exceeded exports, but invisible earnings helped cover deficits.

The civil war first showed the economy's resilience, but after the mid-1980s the Lebanese pound fell sharply and infrastructure destruction deepened. Postwar reconstruction led by Rafiq al-Hariri revived parts of [[Beirut]], but borrowing, debt, austerity, and poverty remained serious problems.

Agriculture used scarce arable land intensively because water and climate allowed fruit, vegetables, and other crops. Rural production was important, yet the country's economy depended more heavily on trade, services, finance, and reconstruction.

## Manufacturing

Manufacturing ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manufacturing)) included food products, cement, bricks, ceramics, wood products, and textiles. Civil war damaged many industries, destroyed [[Beirut]]'s industrial belt, and the Israeli occupation of the south brought competing goods that weakened Lebanese producers.

## Finance

Banking and insurance grew during the first decade of civil war because capital flowed from Lebanese abroad and commercial banks held high liquidity. By 1983, these inflows declined, the Lebanese pound weakened, and postwar governments relied on European bonds and treasury bills for reconstruction.

Later shocks damaged financial recovery. The assassination of Rafiq al-Hariri in 2005 and the 2006 war between [[Israel]] and Hezbollah caused marked economic decline and renewed damage to infrastructure.

## Trade

[[Beirut]]'s port and airport, free-exchange system, favorable interest rates, and banking secrecy law made Lebanon a center of trade and services before 1975. Civil war obscured trade through smuggling, covert aid to armed groups, and illegal drug production.

Exports included vegetable products, textiles, and nonprecious metals, mainly to Middle Eastern markets. Imports came largely from western Europe and included consumer goods, machinery, transport equipment, petroleum products, and food; remittances and loans helped cover the deficit.

## Services

Services produced most national income before the war, especially through banking, tourism, transport, and trade. Historic sites, mountain and seaside resorts, nightlife, hotels, festivals, and natural scenery made tourism a year-round industry.

Civil war damaged the service sector ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tertiary_sector)) deeply and pushed skilled workers to emigrate. Trade unions gained some benefits before and after the war, but conflict split labor organizations and weakened their normal functions until postwar civil society revived them.

## Transportation

Lebanon's position made it a crossroads between East and West. Highways linked Europe, the Arab countries, and the East; ports served the coast; and Beirut International Airport had been one of the busiest in the Middle East before the civil war.

War damage required broad transport reconstruction after 1990. Roads and the airport were renovated, but railway lines along the coast, through Al-Biqa, and across the mountains remained out of service, and the 2006 Israel-Hezbollah war damaged transport facilities again.

## Justice

Lebanese law is largely modeled on French concepts, with courts of first instance, appeal, cassation, a Court of Justice for state security, and a Council of State for administrative affairs. Religious courts handle personal-status issues such as marriage, inheritance, and property.

The Taif Accord led to a Constitutional Council for electoral disputes and constitutional review. Despite a developed legal system and many lawyers, some disputes still move outside courts through feud and vendetta.

## Security

Lebanon's armed forces include an army, air force, navy, gendarmerie, and police. During the civil war the army nearly disintegrated as splinter groups joined rival factions, and later reconstruction relied first on help from the [[United States of America (United States)|United States]] and then from [[Syria]].

Public health and welfare services were concentrated in cities, while religious, communal, and nonprofit groups provided many services. The National Social Security Fund covered sickness, maternity, labor accidents, occupational disease, family benefits, and termination-of-service benefits, though it was not fully implemented.

## Housing

The 1960s Popular Housing Law aimed to rehabilitate substandard housing, but shortages worsened as villagers moved to [[Beirut]]. Civil war destroyed homes, evacuated villages, violated property rights, and led the government to create a Housing Bank for loans.

## Education

Lebanon has one of the Middle East's higher literacy rates and an education system reaching all levels. Public schools expanded, but private schools remained favored, and civil war reduced the share of students in public schools from more than two-fifths in the early 1970s to about one-third afterward.

Major institutions include the American University of Beirut, founded in 1866, Universite Saint-Joseph, founded in 1875, Lebanese University, founded in 1951, and Beirut Arab University, founded in 1960. Social inequality and government inaction helped make the 1975 conflict more likely and continued after the war.

## Phoenicia

Human settlement in the area of modern Lebanon goes back to the Paleolithic and Neolithic periods. Byblos became an early center, with pottery and metallurgy visible by the 4th millennium BCE and urban settlement around 3050-2850 BCE.

The Phoenicians, probably close to the Canaanites, appeared around 3000 BCE, though their original homeland remains uncertain. Byblos had strong sea links with [[Egypt]], was destroyed around 2150 BCE, and later rebuilt during a period of close Egyptian contact.

## Phoenician

Phoenician government was usually based on kingship, limited by powerful merchant families and councils of elders. Under Nebuchadrezzar II, Tyre replaced monarchy with short-term suffetes, while Persian rule later linked Sidon, Tyre, and Aradus in a limited federation.

## Colonies

By the 2nd millennium BCE, Phoenician settlements spread along the Levant coast. Ugarit held temples to Baal and Dagon and produced an archive with an early alphabetic script, one of the major Phoenician contributions to Western civilization.

The Sea Peoples ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sea_Peoples)) destroyed several coastal cities in the late 13th century BCE, after which Tyre became the leading Phoenician city. Colonization expanded through links with Solomon of Israel, North African sites such as Utica, and later Carthage.

## Commerce

Phoenician commerce grew under Egypt's 18th dynasty because Phoenicia lay at the meeting point of sea and land routes. The alphabet helped trade, and Egyptian, Homeric, biblical, and Classical traditions remembered Phoenicians as merchants, craftsmen, traders, pirates, and slave dealers.

Exports included cedar and pine, linen, Tyrian purple cloth, embroideries, metalwork, glass, wine, salt, and dried fish. Imports included papyrus, ivory, ebony, silk, amber, spices, incense, horses, metals, jewels, and precious stones, while transit trade linked Egypt, Babylonia, and the Mediterranean.

## Roman and Byzantine Rule

Under Roman and Byzantine administration, Syria Phoenice included coastal Phoenicia and inland territories. In the early 5th century CE, reorganization created Phoenice Prima and Phoenice Secunda, connecting the coast, Mount Lebanon, and cities such as Emesa, Heliopolis, [[Damascus]], and Palmyra.

The native Phoenician language disappeared, while Aramaic, Latin, and Greek successively held cultural or administrative importance. Heliopolis became famous for Roman temples, and Berytus for a Roman law school destroyed in the mid-6th century by earthquakes, tidal wave, and fire.

## Medieval Communities

In the 7th century CE, Lebanon's population began taking its later form. Maronites settled in the northern mountains, absorbed Mardaites and local peasants, shifted gradually from Syriac to Arabic in daily life, and kept Syriac for liturgy.

Druze communities developed in the south from the 11th century, while Shiite Muslims settled on mountain fringes and in Al-Biqa, and coastal towns became mainly Sunni Muslim. Under Umayyad and later dynasties, Lebanon was tied to Damascus, Egypt, Byzantium, and the Crusader states.

## Nineteenth-Century Mount Lebanon

![[attachments/lebanon_001.webp|420]]

*Beirut Corniche, Beirut, Lebanon*

Mission schools and community schools multiplied in the 19th century. The Syrian Protestant College was founded in 1866, the Jesuit Universite Saint-Joseph in 1875, and a literate professional class grew, especially among Christians.

The rise of Christian communities changed the balance of Mount Lebanon. Bashir II, who ruled from 1788 to 1840, allied with Ibrahim Pasha during Egyptian occupation, but Anglo-Ottoman intervention and a popular rising ended Egyptian rule and deposed him.

Conflict between Maronites and Druze worsened after 1842, with French support for Maronites, British support for part of the Druze, and Ottoman pressure for direct control. The 1860 massacre of Maronites led to French intervention and the Organic Regulation of 1861, which gave Mount Lebanon autonomy under a Christian governor.

## Independence

After independence, Lebanon kept parliamentary democracy but struggled to apply the unwritten National Pact of 1943. President Bishara al-Khuri cooperated with Riad al-Sulh, but corruption, a manipulated 1947 election, and a second presidential term made Khuri unpopular.

A failed 1949 coup by the Syrian Social Nationalist Party was followed by the assassination of Riad al-Sulh in 1951. A general strike forced Khuri to resign in 1952, and Camille Chamoun succeeded him.

## The 1958 Crisis

Camille Chamoun angered Gamal Abdel Nasser by refusing to break relations with Britain and France during the 1956 Suez War. After [[Syria]] and [[Egypt]] formed the United Arab Republic in 1958, Muslim opposition demanded association with Pan-Arab unity.

Armed insurrection spread from Tripoli, but army commander Fuad Chehab refused to attack because the mixed army might split. [[United States of America (United States)|United States]] Marines landed near Beirut after the Iraqi coup of 1958, and Parliament later chose Chehab as compromise president, with Rashid Karami as premier.

Chehabism sought compromise, national unity, and administrative reform from 1958 to 1976 through Chehab and Charles Helou. Yet Palestinian armed activity, Israeli attacks, and sectarian tensions increasingly strained the system before full civil war.
