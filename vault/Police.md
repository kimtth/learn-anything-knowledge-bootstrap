## Introduction

![[attachments/police_001.webp|420]]

*London Metropolitan Police museum*

Police ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Police)) are officers who represent the civil authority of government. They maintain public order and safety, enforce law, prevent and investigate crime, and often carry out licensing or regulatory duties. Scholars note that this common definition is incomplete because police work involves more than law enforcement and because police are defined by their special means: the legal power to impose urgent, coercive, and nonnegotiable solutions.

Egon Bittner's definition explains policing as the legal competence to use force or the threat of force to resolve harmful situations quickly. A police officer may keep people away from a fire, stop violence, or secure a crime scene, not mainly by negotiation but by lawful authority. This means policing can be done by public police, private security, military bodies, intelligence agencies, and regulatory organizations.

Public constabulary forces remain the most visible model because they patrol public spaces, wear uniforms, and represent state authority. Yet in countries such as [[Australia]], [[Canada]], the [[United Kingdom]], and the [[United States of America (United States)|United States]], private security agents may outnumber public police. Since the September 11 attacks in 2001, security and intelligence agencies have also become more central to counterterrorism.

## Resolving Problematic Situations

Problematic situations have two main features: they may cause harm, and they must be handled urgently before that harm develops. Police power is designed for these cases. It allows a fast, conclusive response, but it also creates the need for public accountability, legal limits, and public trust.

Modern policing crosses old boundaries between public and private, criminal and political, local and national. Public police departments, private security companies, military units, customs services, border forces, and intelligence agencies may all perform tasks that resemble policing, especially when surveillance, investigation, or coercive authority is involved.

## Police and Society

![[attachments/police_002.webp|420]]

*Police car*

The relationship between police and society varies across history, place, and political order. Police may be servants of law, defenders of public order, protectors of ruling power, emergency responders, regulators, or crime fighters. Their legitimacy depends not only on their legal powers but also on how the public experiences their conduct.

## The History of Policing in the West

Policing ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Police)) is much older than modern police forces. Ancient communities used mediation, elite authority, hired guards, and communal duties to settle disputes and protect order. The word police comes from the Greek polis, or city, reflecting the urban roots of early policing.

Ancient policing had three lasting features. It did not always depend on force; mediation was often the first method of resolving disputes. Legal responsibility and actual enforcement were often separated, with elites directing low-status workers. Police-like bodies also performed many tasks unrelated to crime, from firefighting to sanitation.

Egypt created one of the first known policing organizations around 3000 BC, with officials responsible for justice and security in administrative districts. Greek city-states, Rome, medieval towns, and early modern European states later developed varied watch, guard, military, and administrative systems that gradually led toward specialized police.

## The Crisis of Policing

In the 1960s and 1970s, policing in the United States entered crisis. Crime rose despite larger police budgets, and hundreds of riots and demonstrations exposed deep hostility between police and minority communities, especially African Americans. Televised beatings and arrests during civil rights and antiwar protests damaged police legitimacy.

President Lyndon Johnson's 1967 national commission found that racism lay behind the urban crisis and warned that the United States was moving toward two separate and unequal societies. It also concluded that police tactics, including unwarranted deadly force, often made riots worse. Fear of crime rose even faster than crime and pushed many citizens to avoid public spaces or leave cities.

Britain faced similar tensions in minority neighbourhoods. The Scarman Report of 1981, written after rioting in Brixton, argued that police had become too distant from local communities and needed more sensitivity to cultural pluralism. Yet it still defended police as a necessary public institution.

## Compstat

Compstat ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CompStat)), or computerized statistics, spread in the late 20th century as a system for mapping crime by time, day, and location. It helped departments identify patterns that had been hidden and allocate resources more effectively. Many administrators treated it as a new model for 21st-century policing.

Compstat ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CompStat)) can support different strategies. A department may use it for aggressive crime fighting, community policing, or a mixed approach. Its central value is organizational: it connects data, accountability, deployment, and repeated review of police performance.

## Police and Counterterrorism

The September 11 attacks in 2001 changed counterterrorism policing. Police had fought terrorism before, but large international attacks showed that ordinary methods were not enough. Departments had to work more closely with national security agencies and shift resources toward surveillance, intelligence, and suspected terrorist networks.

European police had already faced terrorist groups between about 1960 and 1980, including ETA, the Red Army Faction, the Red Brigades, and the Front de Liberation du Quebec in Canada. Police success often depended on infiltrating networks with human sources and informants, methods also used against organized crime.

Counterterrorism sometimes brought emergency powers, longer detention, work bans on sympathizers, and other measures that raised civil-liberty concerns. The problem for democratic policing is to prevent violence without turning security work into political repression.

## National Police Organizations

Police systems range from highly centralized national forces to extremely decentralized local systems. Continental Europe usually favours centralized police, while the United States represents the decentralized extreme. Canada, Britain, Australia, Germany, Belgium, and the Netherlands occupy different intermediate positions.

Centralized systems may have one national force, a few national forces, regional forces under federal authority, or local forces supported by a strong national agency. Many European systems also include domestic security arms and municipal police that handle local bylaws and traffic.

## Centralized Police Forces

[[France]] has two main national agencies: the National Police in cities and the Gendarmerie Nationale in rural areas and small towns. Italy has the State Police, the Carabinieri, and the Financial Police. Spain has the National Police and Civil Guard, the latter militarized and important in rural policing, national security, and crowd control.

[[Germany]] and the [[Netherlands]] illustrate regional centralization under federal authority. Germany has federal criminal and border agencies, but state police form the basic structure. The Netherlands uses regional police forces under national coordination and also has the Royal Dutch Constabulary. Belgium combines federal and local police in a model closer to Anglo-American decentralization.

## Decentralized Police Forces

The [[United States of America (United States)|United States]] has tens of thousands of police bodies: federal agencies such as the FBI, Drug Enforcement Administration, Secret Service, and Postal Inspection Service; state police and investigative agencies; county sheriffs; city police; township police; campus police; and special district forces. Many are very small.

Supporters of decentralization argue that local problems need local remedies and that concentrated national police power could threaten liberty. Critics argue that consolidation would reduce costs and improve efficiency. Many small American municipalities therefore choose either to keep separate departments or join regional agencies.

## International Police Organizations

Interpol began in 1923 in Vienna as the International Criminal Police Commission, was taken over by the Nazis in 1938, ceased functioning during World War II, and was reconstituted in Brussels in 1946. Its headquarters later moved to Paris and then Lyon, and its constitution was ratified in 1956.

Each member country has a national bureau linked to Interpol's General Secretariat. These bureaus exchange criminal information, support searches and arrests requested by other countries, and use databases of fingerprints, DNA records, photographs, and other identifying information. Interpol focuses especially on trafficking, organized crime, financial crime, and international terrorism.

Interpol is limited because it rests on cooperation among police forces rather than a binding state convention. It must operate within national law, depends on extradition treaties, and relies heavily on member countries' intelligence capacity. Poorer countries with weak databases cannot contribute as much.

## Interpol and Europol

By the early 21st century, Interpol was no longer the only international policing organization. The European Union developed police cooperation through laws and conventions on terrorism, drug trafficking, trafficking in human beings, money laundering, and organized crime.

European cooperation grew from the TREVI counterterrorism group in 1975, the Schengen agreements of 1985 and 1990, and the creation of Europol in 1992 as the European Drugs Unit. Europol, based in The Hague, gathers and analyzes intelligence but remains far from ordinary field policing.

## Police Work and Law Enforcement

Routine police work differs by society, department, and administrator. Most officers are assigned to uniformed patrol, on foot or in vehicles, but studies show that only a small part of patrol time involves arrests or formal criminal-law action. Much work involves emergency service, order maintenance, disputes, and general assistance.

Police are accountable partly through courts. In France and similar legal systems, investigating magistrates formally direct inquiries, though police often work with great independence. In Anglo-American systems, courts review legality through warrants, evidence rules, due process, and civil or criminal cases against police misconduct.

## Crowd Policing

Political protest and crowd events are difficult because groups may have different goals. Some want to be heard, while others want disruption or confrontation. Events around international meetings, such as Vancouver in 1997, Seattle in 1999, and Quebec, Gothenburg, and Genoa in 2001, show how global protest can create policing challenges.

Riots often arise from long frustration over racial, ethnic, or social grievances and are triggered by a single event. The Los Angeles riots of 1992 followed the acquittal of officers charged after beating an African American motorist, while French riots in 2005 followed the deaths of two youths allegedly running from police.

Crowds may be policed by military forces, paramilitary forces, militarized police units, or ordinary police. Strategies range from escalated force to negotiated management. Democratic systems increasingly favour negotiation and limited force, while dictatorships may rely on military power and intimidation.

## High Policing

High policing protects national security and the higher interests of the state. In Anglo-Saxon countries it is performed by national police and specialized intelligence agencies such as the FBI and Secret Service in the United States, MI5 in the United Kingdom, and security intelligence services in Australia, Canada, and New Zealand.

The main tool of high policing is [[Intelligence and Counterintelligence (Intelligence)|intelligence]], gathered through human sources, informers, electronic surveillance, and eavesdropping. The East German Stasi relied on vast informer networks, while the U.S. PATRIOT Act of 2001 expanded domestic surveillance powers after September 11.

Because high-policing agencies receive broad legal powers and operate secretly, they can abuse power even in democratic states. The tension is permanent: national security may require hidden investigation, but democracy requires legality, oversight, and protection of political freedom.

## Police Work as an Occupation

Public policing was long a low-status occupation, underpaid and associated with coercion rather than education. Some forces hired former criminals or violent men, and low pay encouraged corruption, especially in early 20th-century urban police forces in the United States.

In Western-style democracies and [[Japan]], 20th-century reforms professionalized policing, improved pay, and increased public respect. Anglo-Saxon systems usually recruit officers at the lowest level and promote through performance and exams. Continental European systems often have more differentiated entry routes and formal education for higher ranks.

## Police Technology

Police technology includes the scientific methods, equipment, and systems used to prevent crime, investigate offences, and identify offenders. Ancient Egyptians used detailed descriptions of suspects, and Babylonians used fingerprints in clay. Modern police technology became a distinct field in the 1960s.

Technology differs by wealth, politics, and setting. Urban police need different tools from rural police, and democratic states face stronger legal limits than authoritarian states. Some poor but undemocratic countries still invest heavily in police technology because it supports control.

## Mobility

Mobility is basic to policing. Foot patrol created close contact through beats and watches, while mounted patrol served larger rural areas and crowd-control needs. Bicycle patrol spread in Europe in the late 19th century and returned later as a flexible tool for parks, campuses, housing complexes, and crowded urban spaces.

Automobiles transformed policing in the early 20th century. Akron, Ohio, claimed an electric police patrol wagon in 1899, New York City introduced motorcycle patrol in 1905, and French mobile brigades used gasoline cars by 1910. Cars expanded patrol areas, shortened response times, and made radio dispatch more important.

Aircraft also became police tools. Helicopters with spotlights and forward-looking infrared devices help ground units at night. Fixed-wing aircraft support border patrols, drug surveillance, personnel transport, and highway traffic control.

## Communication

Early police [[Communication|communication]] relied on oral orders, written instructions, bells, rattles, and the hue and cry. The Metropolitan Police Act of 1829 formalized English policing, but face-to-face command became inefficient as cities expanded.

Telegraphs, telephones, street call boxes, radio dispatch, emergency numbers, and computerized dispatch transformed response. London created a 999 emergency system in 1937, while later systems such as 911 connected public calls, patrol cars, radio networks, and centralized records.

## Computerization

Police adopted computer databases early. The U.S. National Crime Information Center, created in 1967, let agencies search records for warrants, stolen vehicles, fingerprints, sex-offender registries, mug shots, and phonetically similar names. State systems added misdemeanour warrants, driving records, and vehicle ownership data.

Europe created the Schengen Information System for criminals, missing persons, stolen property, and other law-enforcement data. Europol and Interpol maintain computerized databases, while computer-assisted dispatch systems record call volume, response time, call type, and outcomes. Enhanced 911 identifies callers' phone numbers and often locations.

## Explosives and Surveillance

Police use explosives sparingly, mainly to breach barricades, distract suspects, deliver tear gas or capsicum, or suppress riots through flash-bangs and projectiles. Bomb-disposal robots let police handle suspected explosives from a safe distance after evacuation.

Surveillance includes electronic eavesdropping, telephone registers, covert microphones, radio transmitters, binoculars, telescopes, telephoto cameras, video, CCTV, night-vision devices, and thermal imaging. These tools can gather evidence and leads but also raise privacy and civil-liberty questions.

## Photography

Police used [[Photography (Photography, History of)|photography]] in Brussels as early as the 1840s to track criminals. Mug shots became essential identification tools. Digital photographs later allowed images to be transmitted rapidly across departments and countries through electronic networks.

## Anthropometry

Anthropometry was developed in the late 19th century by Alphonse Bertillon of the Paris police. The Bertillon system measured body parts, especially the head and face, to create a detailed portrait parle. It helped identify repeat offenders before fingerprinting became dominant.

## Fingerprinting

Fingerprinting replaced anthropometry as the main identification method. Although ancient Babylonians and Chinese used fingerprints, modern systems grew from Francis Galton's work, Juan Vucetich's classification system in Argentina, and Edward Henry's system adopted by Scotland Yard in 1901.

Fingerprint science, or dactyloscopy, studies ridges, furrows, loops, whorls, arches, and minutiae. Prints may be visible in blood, dirt, or soft material, or latent through sweat and oils. Dusting, chemicals, and automated fingerprint identification systems made it possible to search millions of records and confirm matches through expert analysis.

## DNA Fingerprinting

DNA fingerprinting, developed in the 1980s, compares human DNA from crime scenes with samples from suspects or databases. It became one of the most reliable identification tools, helping convict offenders and free wrongly convicted prisoners. CODIS, developed by the U.S. Department of Justice and FBI, links computer technology with forensic DNA records.

## Biometrics

Biometrics identifies people through unique biological markers such as fingerprints, retinal or iris patterns, handprints, and facial structure. Facecams linked to offender databases and CCTV appeared in Britain in the 1990s and in some U.S. cities and airports in the early 21st century.

Supporters argue that biometric data in passports or licenses can improve security, especially after September 11. Critics answer that such systems may infringe civil liberties and would not necessarily identify unknown attackers, since only a few suspects may already be on watch lists.

## Crime-Scene Investigation and Forensic Sciences

The first police crime laboratory was founded in Lyon in 1910 by Edmond Locard. Locard's exchange principle states that criminals cannot leave a scene without leaving trace evidence. Crime-scene investigators collect physical evidence, which laboratories analyze through forensic science.

## Toxicology

Toxicology was systematized by Matthieu Orfila. Toxicologists examine blood and tissues for drugs or poisons, estimate quantities, judge whether a substance was fatal, and help determine when it entered the body.

## Serology and Document Examination

Serology studies blood and other human fluids. Karl Landsteiner discovered ABO blood groups in 1901, Leone Lattes developed dried-bloodstain typing in 1915, and the Rh system appeared in 1939-40. DNA analysis later made body-fluid identification far more precise.

Questioned-document examiners study handwriting, typewriting, document age, alterations, ink, paper, and the order of preparation. Handwriting analysis assumes adult writing develops individual habits, while forged signatures may reveal the forger's habits, memory errors, tracing marks, or drawing patterns.

## Key Events & Developments

- 3000 BC: Egypt created one of the earliest known organized policing systems.
- 1829: The Metropolitan Police Act formalized full-time paid policing in England.
- 1923: Interpol's predecessor was founded in Vienna.
- 1967: The U.S. National Crime Information Center began computerized police records.
- 1981: The Scarman Report criticized distant policing after Brixton rioting.
- 1992: Europol began as the European Drugs Unit.
- 2001: September 11 reshaped counterterrorism and high policing.
