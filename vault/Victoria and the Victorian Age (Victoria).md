# Victoria and the Victorian Age (Victoria)

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> Queen Victoria ruled Britain from 1837 to 1901 and became the figure around whom the Victorian age was named. The source follows her lineage, accession, partnership with Albert, widowhood, relations with Disraeli and Gladstone, and the changing constitutional monarchy, party politics, and imperial culture of her reign.

## Introduction

![[attachments/victoria_001.webp|420]]

*Portrait of Queen Victoria*

Queen Victoria, in full Alexandrina Victoria, was born on May 24, 1819, at Kensington Palace in [[London]] and died on January 22, 1901, at Osborne on the Isle of Wight. She ruled the United Kingdom of Great Britain and Ireland from 1837 to 1901 and was empress of [[India]] from 1876 to 1901.

Victoria was the last monarch of the House of Hanover and gave her name to the Victorian Age ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Victorian_era)). During her reign the monarchy changed from an uncertain political power into the modern ceremonial institution that helped stabilize the crown. Her long reign, strict sense of duty, and public image restored dignity and popularity to a monarchy that had seemed less secure when she came to the throne.

The queen was a paradoxical figure. She ruled during the Victorian Age ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Victorian_era)), which praised motherhood, family, reform, technology, and social improvement, yet she disliked pregnancy, resisted innovation, opposed democracy and female suffrage, and presided over the crown’s move toward symbolic influence.

## Lineage and Early Life

Victoria became important to the succession after Princess Charlotte, daughter of the prince regent, died in 1817. Several sons of George III then married to produce legitimate heirs. Edward, duke of Kent, fathered Victoria, who became third in line after George IV came to the throne in 1820.

The young princess grew up mainly at Kensington Palace with her mother, the Duchess of Kent, her half sister Feodore, and her governess Louise Lehzen. Her uncle Leopold, later king of the Belgians, acted as an important father figure.

Her childhood was shaped by the Kensington system ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kensington_System)), controlled by the duchess’s adviser Sir John Conroy. He isolated Victoria from other royal relatives and hoped to make her dependent on him. Victoria’s strong will and Lehzen’s support helped her survive this system, but it also taught her caution, resentment, and a habit of remembering injuries.

## Accession to the Throne

In the early hours of June 20, 1837, Victoria learned that William IV had died. The Privy Council was impressed by the young queen’s poise, small figure, and clear voice. Because Hanover followed Salic law, which barred female succession, the British and Hanoverian crowns separated.

As queen, Victoria quickly asserted independence. She moved her mother to distant rooms in Buckingham Palace, pensioned off Conroy, and kept only Lehzen close. She enjoyed court ceremony, freedom, late nights, and her new authority, but this early period was politically risky.

Lord Melbourne became her main guide and political friend. He encouraged her confidence and Whig sympathies, helping create two crises in 1839: the Hastings affair, when Victoria wrongly suspected Lady Flora Hastings of pregnancy, and the bedchamber crisis, when she refused Sir Robert Peel’s request to replace Whig ladies.

## The Albertine Monarchy

![[attachments/victoria_003.webp|420]]

Victoria married her cousin Prince Albert of Saxe-Coburg-Gotha on February 10, 1840, after proposing to him in October 1839. Their marriage was emotionally intense and politically important. At first she resisted giving Albert a role in government, but she gradually depended on him for personal decisions, papers, ministers, and political judgment.

The couple had nine children, including Victoria, princess royal, and the future Edward VII. Their marriages connected many European royal families, but Victoria saw pregnancy and childbirth as the painful “shadow-side” of marriage.

Albert slowly became the queen’s private secretary and, in his own phrase, her permanent minister. He helped resolve the relationship with Peel, replaced Lehzen in Victoria’s loyalty, rationalized royal finances, and shaped the residences at Osborne and Balmoral. These homes expressed a new monarchy that valued privacy, domesticity, and a grand version of middle-class family life.

Victoria and Albert supported the repeal of the Corn Laws in 1846, but they were more absorbed by royal homes, foreign policy, and constitutional influence than by Irish famine or working-class suffering. Victoria supported repression of the Chartists and saw revolution as destructive and morally wrong.

The Great Exhibition ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Exhibition)) of 1851 was the high point of the Albertine monarchy. Organized by Albert and housed in the Crystal Palace, it displayed British wealth, technology, and industry. Its profits helped fund the South Kensington museums and colleges, and Victoria treated its success as proof of Albert’s genius.

Albert taught Victoria the need to stand above party, although both had Conservative sympathies. The unsettled party politics between 1846 and 1868 gave the crown room to influence coalitions and ministers, but Victoria’s political role was still secondary. Albert also insisted that the sovereign had a special role in foreign affairs and should not simply nod at ministers’ decisions.

The royal couple clashed with Lord Palmerston, whose independent foreign policy angered them. After he approved Louis Napoleon’s coup in 1851 without consulting the queen, he was dismissed, though he soon returned to high office. During the Crimean War of 1854-56, royal popularity revived as Victoria supported wounded soldiers, helped relief work, visited hospitals, and created the Victoria Cross.

Albert died on December 14, 1861. His influence remained decisive: he had trained Victoria in hard work, paperwork, political intervention, and private intelligence. The monarchy gained less formal prerogative but more subtle influence.

## Widowhood

After Albert’s death, Victoria entered deep grief and long mourning. For years she suffered despair and withdrew from public ceremony, spending months at Balmoral and Osborne. Ministers found this absence inconvenient, and public sympathy slowly turned into impatience, but she refused to be moved.

She still wanted political influence and tried to act according to what she believed was Albert’s point of view. This was increasingly difficult after the Reform Bill of 1867, wider electorates, stronger parties, and changing cabinet politics reduced the monarch’s role as maker of ministries.

Victoria’s grief damaged her relationship with the Prince of Wales, whom she partly blamed for Albert’s death after Albert had visited him during a family scandal. She refused him responsibility and resented the popularity of the prince and princess, leaving a breach that never fully healed.

## Disraeli, Gladstone, and Party Politics

Benjamin Disraeli won Victoria’s affection by entering into her grief, flattering her, writing lively letters, and making royal business feel easier. William Ewart Gladstone, by contrast, irritated her with long papers, moral seriousness, pressure to resume ceremony, and sympathy for Irish reform. Her emotional and political preferences helped make her a strong Conservative partisan.

Victoria and Disraeli shared a romantic interest in empire and the East. She admired his purchase of shares in the Suez Canal in 1875, his assertive foreign policy, and his addition of Empress of India to her title in 1876. During the Russo-Turkish crisis, she pressed for a harder line against [[Russia]], while Disraeli had to restrain her.

The Congress of Berlin in 1878 delighted Victoria because it reduced Russian influence in the Balkans and gave Britain control of [[Cyprus]]. When Gladstone returned after the Conservative defeat of 1880, she openly disliked him, distrusted his attacks on Lord Beaconsfield, and opposed his policies, including the withdrawal from Kandahar in [[Afghanistan]].

Victoria believed Gladstone’s government encouraged destructive democracy. Yet she did mediate between the two houses during the struggle that produced the third parliamentary Reform Act in 1884. In 1886, she tried and failed to prevent another Gladstone ministry by encouraging an anti-Radical coalition; Irish Home Rule, not the queen, eventually defeated him.

## Last Years

The Salisbury administration from 1895 to 1902 suited Victoria better than Gladstone’s ministries. Its connection with Joseph Chamberlain and imperial expansion reminded her of Disraeli’s world. The South African War of 1899-1902 dominated her final years and brought her back into public activity through troop inspections, medal ceremonies, and hospital visits.

By the end of her reign, Victoria had become popular again. The jubilees of 1887 and 1897 showed that middle-class and poorer subjects could imagine her as a motherly national figure. Lord Salisbury believed she had an extraordinary sense of what her people, especially the middle class, would think.

She remained distant from many late Victorian movements. She disliked democracy, rejected female suffrage, noticed individual workers more than the working class, and used railroads and telegraphs without welcoming technological change.

Victoria worked to the end, reading boxes and signing papers with habits Albert had taught her. She lived among mementos of Albert and remained passionate, strong-willed, commanding, and frightening to rule-breakers.

Her main achievement was not the political power she tried to preserve, but the ceremonial and symbolic role she often neglected. By restoring respectability, continuity, and emotional value to the crown, she helped create the democratic monarchy she resisted. She died after a short illness and was buried beside Albert at Frogmore near Windsor.

## Key Events & Developments

- 1819: Victoria was born at Kensington Palace.
- 1837: She became queen after the death of William IV.
- 1839: The Hastings affair and bedchamber crisis exposed early political partisanship.
- 1840: Victoria married Prince Albert.
- 1846: The repeal of the Corn Laws showed Albert’s and Victoria’s sympathy with Peel.
- 1851: The Great Exhibition ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Exhibition)) celebrated British industry and Albert’s influence.
- 1854-56: The Crimean War revived royalist feeling and led to the Victoria Cross.
- 1861: Albert died, beginning Victoria’s widowhood and partial retirement.
- 1876: Victoria became Empress of India.
- 1884: She helped mediate the third parliamentary Reform Act compromise.
- 1887 and 1897: The jubilees displayed her renewed popularity.
- 1901: Victoria died after the longest English reign to that date.

## Key People

- Queen Victoria ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Queen_Victoria)): Monarch whose long reign restored dignity and popularity to the British crown.
- Prince Albert: Husband and political partner who shaped royal work, domestic life, and constitutional influence.
- Lord Melbourne ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lord_Melbourne)): Early prime minister and mentor who encouraged Victoria’s Whig partisanship.
- Sir Robert Peel ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sir_Robert_Peel)): Conservative leader involved in the bedchamber crisis and later respected by Victoria and Albert.
- Benjamin Disraeli ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Benjamin_Disraeli)): Conservative leader who flattered Victoria, shared her imperial imagination, and became her favourite minister.
- William Ewart Gladstone ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Ewart_Gladstone)): Liberal leader whom Victoria distrusted because of his moral politics, Irish policy, and democratic reforms.
- Lord Palmerston ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lord_Palmerston)): Foreign secretary and prime minister whose independent diplomacy angered Victoria and Albert.
- Lord Salisbury ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lord_Salisbury)): Prime minister of Victoria’s final years, when imperial politics and public ceremony revived her role.
- Edward VII ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edward_VII)): Victoria’s son and successor, long denied real responsibility by his mother.

## Interesting Facts

- Victoria learned she might become queen at age 10 and reportedly answered, “I will be good.”
- The Kensington system ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kensington_System)) tried to isolate Victoria so Conroy could control her future reign.
- Her marriage linked many European royal families through the marriages of her nine children.
- Victoria disliked pregnancy and childbirth even though she ruled an age that idealized motherhood.
- She loved Balmoral and Osborne, and railways and telegraphs made her long absences possible.
- The Great Exhibition ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Exhibition)) profits helped create the South Kensington museum and college complex.
- She resisted [[Democracy|democracy]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Democracy)) but preserved monarchy by making it respectable and emotionally powerful.
