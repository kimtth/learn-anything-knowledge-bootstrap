# The History of Technology

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> The history of technology traces systematic techniques for changing the environment through tools, materials, power, food production, manufacturing, building, transport, communication, and war. The source distinguishes invention from adoption by showing how social need, resources, capital, skill, institutions, and cultural acceptance govern technological change.

## Introduction

![[attachments/history_technology_001.webp|420]]

*Neolithic stone tools*

Technology ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Technology)) is the development of systematic techniques for making and doing things. The word joins Greek techne, meaning art or craft, with logos, meaning word or discourse, and originally meant discussion of the arts.

By the 20th century, technology included [[Tools|tools]], machines, processes, means, activities, and ideas by which humans change or manipulate their environment. It is increasingly hard to separate technology from scientific inquiry.

The history of technology can be traced through materials, power sources, food production, manufacturing, building, transport, communications, military techniques, and social conditions.

Technological change depends on social need, social resources, and a receptive social ethos. A society must feel a need, possess materials, capital, skill, and labour, and accept experiment or new practice. Military pressure, markets, advertising, resource scarcity, and curiosity can stimulate invention, but many inventions fail when the surrounding society cannot support or adopt them.

This framework explains why invention is not the same as adoption. A device may be clever yet remain unused if it lacks capital, skilled makers, available materials, transport, demand, or cultural acceptance. Conversely, an urgent need can turn a modest improvement into a major force when markets, institutions, and labour systems are ready to spread it.

## Prehistory and the Neolithic Revolution

The Neolithic Revolution began near the end of the last ice age, about 15,000 to 20,000 years ago, when some communities shifted from hunting and gathering to settled life based on agriculture and animal husbandry.

This transition increased [[Population|population]], community size, craft specialization, and social complexity. It led from Old Stone Age techniques to New Stone Age settlements and eventually to urban civilizations around 3000 BCE.

Stone gave technological unity to prehistory because stone tools survived in abundance. Flint and other fine-grained stones were shaped by flaking, polishing, hafting, and edge making into scrapers, cutters, spearheads, harpoons, and arrowheads.

Other materials such as [[Forestry and Wood Production (Wood)|wood]], bone, fur, leaves, grasses, clay, textiles, and later metals entered use. Pottery, brick, spinning, weaving, and metalworking marked major advances.

## Fire, Power, Tools, and Transport

The control of fire was prehistoric technology's major contribution to power. It offered heat, protection, cooking, and later metallurgical possibilities, though direct mechanical power remained mostly human and animal.

Domesticated animals supplied draft power, while the sail began harnessing wind for boats by the end of prehistory. Tools and weapons included spears, spear-throwers, bows, arrows, slings, boomerangs, blowguns, traps, snares, and nets.

Late Neolithic peoples also developed early rotary mechanisms: the potter's wheel, bow drill, pole lathe, and wheel. These devices turned intermittent hand motion into controlled rotation for shaping clay, drilling, turning workpieces, and moving vehicles, foreshadowing later machine principles.

Prehistoric technology also shows the importance of local adaptation. Communities did not all invent the same tools in the same order; hunters, fishers, farmers, and pastoralists selected spears, nets, traps, canoes, slings, bows, pottery, textiles, or metalwork according to their environments. The long timescale makes small improvements look slow, but they accumulated into new social possibilities.

Early manufacturing ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manufacturing)) began with grinding grain, baking clay, spinning, weaving, dyeing, fermenting, distilling, and soft-metal working. Trade moved manufactured objects such as flint arrowheads across wide areas.

## Urban Revolution

Around 3000 BCE, a new urban phase began in favourable river regions. Agricultural surplus, larger populations, specialized craftsmen, metallurgy, trade, writing, and social hierarchy produced the first civilizations.

Metallurgy began with native metals such as gold and copper and advanced when people learned to reduce ores such as malachite. This encouraged mining, trade, smelting, casting, and a specialized metalworking class.

Bronze required alloying copper with tin or related metals and made stronger tools and weapons than soft copper. Because tin and many metals were scarce in river-valley civilizations, bronze technology encouraged long-distance trade, mining expeditions, and the spread of technical knowledge along Mediterranean and Near Eastern routes.

Systematic irrigation supported Egypt and Mesopotamia. Nile basin irrigation stored predictable floodwaters, while Sumerian engineers channelled less predictable Tigris-Euphrates waters through canals, dikes, embankments, aqueducts, and water-lifting devices such as the shadoof.

Urban manufacturing produced pottery, wines, oils, cosmetics, textiles, metal goods, bricks, and writing materials. Administration and engineering were closely linked.

## Ancient Building and Classical Technology

![[attachments/history_technology_002.webp|420]]

*Ancient Roman aqueduct*

Mesopotamia built ziggurats from sun-dried and baked bricks, while Egypt used stone for pyramids and temples. Egyptian construction used rollers, ramps, levers, skilled masons, and priest-architects with mathematical and astronomical knowledge.

Slave labour supported large works but may have discouraged mechanical innovation, helping explain why ancient civilizations achieved great construction but limited machine development.

Ancient construction depended on organization as much as devices. Surveying, ramps, rollers, levers, brickmaking, quarrying, transport, food supply, and skilled supervision had to be coordinated over years. The pyramids, ziggurats, roads, aqueducts, and temples were therefore technologies of command, measurement, labour, and logistics as well as stone, brick, and mortar.

Greco-Roman technology is paradoxical. Greek and Roman achievements in philosophy, politics, literature, law, and science were enormous, but their technological innovation was often more incremental than revolutionary.

The major classical material advance was iron. Smelting required high heat, charcoal furnaces, bellows, bloom extraction, and hammering into wrought iron. Iron tools improved agriculture and weapons.

Roman building technology used fired brick, tile, cement setting under water, arches, vaults, domes, amphitheatres, aqueducts, tunnels, bridges, walls, lighthouses, and roads. Roads were especially military tools for controlling empire.

Greek and Roman transport used sailing ships, oared warships, cargo ships, and Roman roads. Military success depended more on organization and discipline than on technological superiority.

## Medieval Innovation and Transmission

Medieval technology raises the problem of innovation. Some inventions may have diffused westward from older civilizations, while others arose from European social needs. Missing records make origins difficult to prove.

Byzantium preserved Hellenic traditions and mediated contacts between Western Europe and Islamic, Indian, and Chinese technologies. Hagia Sophia influenced architecture, though Byzantium's direct technological contribution was limited.

The [[Islamic World|Islamic world]] transmitted Greek, Persian, Indian, and Chinese knowledge to Europe through Spain, Sicily, the Holy Land, and trade. It contributed to science, instruments, mathematics, medicine, and technology.

[[India]] was known for ironworking and textile techniques, while [[China]] developed hydraulic engineering, iron casting, porcelain, brass, paper, silk working, the magnetic compass, and mechanical clocks.

Chinese technology illustrates both invention and social filtering. Hydraulic engineering, cast iron, porcelain, paper, brass, silk, the compass, and water-powered clocks appeared early, but bureaucratic stability could restrain disruptive innovation. Technologies moved west through trade, travelers, Islamic intermediaries, and imitation, often long before Europeans understood how to reproduce them.

Chinese inventions moved westward unevenly. Westerners admired porcelain for centuries before making comparable products, while papermaking, the compass, silk techniques, and other technologies influenced Europe.

## Medieval Power, Architecture, and War

Medieval Europe greatly expanded waterpower. Water mills ground grain and later fulling cloth, sawing wood, crushing seeds, and driving machinery. Overshot wheels and gearing increased power where water head allowed.

The windmill appeared in Europe by the late 12th century. Post mills and later tower mills flourished in dry areas, lowlands, fens, and polders where waterpower was limited.

Gothic architecture used cross-rib vaults, flying buttresses, and large stained-glass windows to build tall, light-filled masonry structures. Castle technology evolved from motte-and-bailey timber strongholds to masonry fortresses, then became vulnerable to artillery.

Gunpowder, known earlier in East Asia, appeared in western Europe in the 13th century. Cannon evolved from wrought-iron strapped barrels to bronze casting and transformed fortifications and warfare.

## Medieval Transport and Communication

![[attachments/history_technology_003.webp|420]]

*Gutenberg-style printing press*

Medieval inland transport remained limited, but sea transport advanced decisively. Square sails, lateen sails, sternpost rudders, compasses, charts, astrolabes, better barrels, ropes, anchors, and hull construction produced reliable oceangoing ships.

These nautical changes linked several technical systems at once: hull design, steering, sail handling, navigation, storage, and port trade. Their combined effect mattered more than any single device, because sailors could carry cargo farther while estimating direction and position with greater confidence.

These improvements made later voyages of discovery possible by giving mariners confidence to sail beyond coasts, familiar routes, and seasonal habits.

Mechanical clocks appeared in the Middle Ages, including weight-driven clocks with verge escapements. Spring-driven clocks later made more compact devices possible, and the fusee helped compensate for weakening spring force.

The mechanical clock was important because it turned [[Time|time]] into a regular public measure. Weight, gear, verge, escapement, spring, and fusee systems made motion itself an object of design. Clocks served monasteries, towns, commerce, astronomy, and civic discipline, while also encouraging confidence that complex motion could be controlled mechanically.

Movable metal-type [[Printing, Typography, and Photoengraving (Printing)|printing]], developed by Johannes Gutenberg at Mainz by about 1455, transformed communication. It drew on block printing, typecasting, ink making, and paper production and made books far more available.

## Renaissance and Early Modern Technology

The Renaissance ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Renaissance)) combined admiration for antiquity with a new progressive attitude. [[Leonardo da Vinci]]'s notebooks included designs for submarines, flying machines, helicopters, gears, and fluid flow, though many lacked contemporary social need or resources.

The scientific revolution challenged [[Aristotle and Aristotelianism|Aristotle]], Ptolemy, and Galen. Observation and experiment created new models of nature and increasingly linked science to practical applications.

European expansion brought new commodities: tea, sugar, tobacco, cotton, cocoa, and plantation products. These created industries in refining, processing, clay pipes, textiles, and trade.

British agriculture changed through enclosure, investment, drainage, machinery such as Jethro Tull's seed drill, livestock breeding, new crops, and crop rotation. This agricultural revolution was a prelude and part of industrialization.

Construction used more brick, tile, glass, cast iron decoration, land drainage, Dutch dikes, English fen reclamation, and Vauban-style star-shaped fortifications adapted to artillery.

Chemistry emerged from alchemy, dyes, cosmetics, pharmacy, laboratory equipment, and later the work of Boyle, Lavoisier, and Dalton. The period created an environment for the invention of invention: organized need, capital, raw materials, experiment, and commercial support.

## Industrial Revolution

![[attachments/history_technology_004.webp|420]]

*Watt steam engine*

The Industrial Revolution ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Industrial_Revolution)) describes the extraordinary acceleration of technological growth from about 1750 to 1900. It began in Britain and spread to Europe, North America, and eventually much of the world.

Its British beginning depended on a prepared environment. Commercial capital, coal, iron, skilled mechanics, patent incentives, agricultural improvement, colonial markets, transport links, and a willingness to solve practical bottlenecks had accumulated before the classic factory age. Industrialization was therefore a rapid acceleration of older pressures rather than a sudden isolated break.

Windmills improved through spring sails, patent sails, fantails, and automatic control, but wind declined as steam power expanded.

James Watt's separate condenser, patented in 1769, transformed the Newcomen engine. Steam engines then improved for more than a century and became the characteristic power source of British industrialization.

Electricity developed from work by Benjamin Franklin, Alessandro Volta, and Michael Faraday. Faraday's 1831 work on electromagnetism enabled generators and motors, while dynamos made large-scale electricity commercially possible.

Thomas Edison and Joseph Swan developed carbon-filament lamps, making electric light a competitor to gas lighting. Alternating-current systems later made long-distance, high-voltage transmission practical.

## Petroleum, Metals, Textiles, and Chemicals

Petroleum became important after Edwin Drake struck oil in Pennsylvania in 1859. At first kerosene for lamps was the main product, but gasoline became valuable when internal-combustion engines needed light fuel.

Petroleum changed because refining became a chemical technology. Early distillation separated fractions, but thermal cracking in 1913 and catalytic cracking in 1936 let refiners break heavy molecules into lighter, more valuable fuels. This turned crude oil from a lamp-fuel source into the basis of gasoline, petrochemicals, plastics, synthetic materials, and global energy politics.

Rudolf Diesel developed the heavy-oil engine from patents beginning in 1892. Internal combustion made lighter mobile engines possible for vehicles and later aircraft.

Metallurgy shifted from charcoal to coal and coke, greatly increasing iron and steel production. Coke smelting, crucible steel, puddling, rolling, and reverberatory furnaces supplied iron for bridges, fireproof factories, machines, and steam engines.

The British cotton textile industry moved from cottage production to mechanized, power-driven factory work. Spinning jennies, frames, mules, power looms, and steam power transformed productivity and became models for other nations.

Cotton's importance lay in a linked factory system. Spinning machines, power looms, carding machines, steam engines, canals, coal, iron, wage labour, capital investment, and global raw cotton all reinforced one another. The industry became a template for industrialization because it joined machine design with disciplined production, urban factories, and export markets.

The chemical industry grew to serve textiles, especially bleaching. Sulfuric acid, chlorine bleaches, bleaching powder, the Leblanc soda process, and later organic chemistry and aniline dyes made chemistry industrial.

## Agriculture, Civil Engineering, and Transport

Nineteenth-century agriculture used mechanization, threshing machines, steam traction, reapers, combines, barbed wire, canning, food packing, and refrigeration. The United States advanced quickly because labour was scarce.

Civil engineering used gunpowder, dynamite, steam diggers, compressed air, hydraulic tools, tunneling shields, cranes, lifts, and locks to reduce manual labour and build large infrastructure.

Transportation shifted from steam to internal combustion and electricity, though steam turbines continued in large ships such as the Mauretania and Queen Elizabeth.

The automobile became a mass product through Henry Ford's assembly line. The Model T, first produced this way in 1913, reached nearly two million yearly production by 1923.

The airplane was entirely a 20th-century product, transforming transport, war, communication, and global distance.

## Communications and Electronics

The 20th-century communications revolution grew from [[Electronics|electronics]]. Photography was already known, but cinema became widely available after World War I.

On December 12, 1901, Guglielmo Marconi transmitted the first wireless message across the Atlantic. Early radio equipment improved quickly through better transmitters, receivers, and coded signals.

The thermionic valve, derived from the Edison effect and developed by John Ambrose Fleming, rectified high-frequency signals and made radio, amplification, and electronics possible.

Later [[Electronics|electronics]], telecommunications, television, computers, and digital systems transformed production, administration, science, war, entertainment, and daily life.

Electronics also shifted technology from heavy mechanisms toward control and information. Valves, amplifiers, radio receivers, transmitters, sensors, switching circuits, and later digital logic made signals manipulable at high speed. This prepared automation, telecommunications, radar, computing, and forms of production in which instructions and feedback became as important as engines.

## Twentieth-Century Power, Materials, and Computers

The 20th century greatly expanded electric power generation and transmission. Larger steam plants, higher pressures, higher voltages, hydroelectric dams, and long-distance alternating-current systems lowered costs and spread electricity.

Nuclear energy began with fission bombs but soon promised electricity through controlled atomic piles heating steam turbines. Nuclear power raised hopes but also waste, safety, and maintenance problems. Fusion remained an attractive but unsolved controlled energy source.

Petroleum refining advanced through thermal cracking in 1913 and catalytic cracking in 1936, increasing gasoline yield and launching petrochemicals, plastics, synthetic materials, and near-total use of crude oil fractions.

Plastics began with Parkesine and celluloid, then expanded after Leo Baekeland's Bakelite in 1909. Polymer chemistry produced materials with tailored properties for electrical goods, film, household products, industry, and later composites.

The space age encouraged new materials: glass fibre, carbon fibre, high-temperature ceramics, plastics, copper conductors, tin plating, lead shielding, silver photography, and advanced alloys.

[[Computers]] transformed engineering after World War II. Electronic digital computers use binary impulses at high speed, with punched cards, magnetic tape, and later memory systems for storage and input. The theory reached back to Charles Babbage's 1830s ideas.

## Technology and Society

Modern critics warned of technological change overwhelming individuality and tradition. J. Robert Oppenheimer reflected anxiously on the atomic bomb and accelerating change.

Jacques Ellul argued that humans now live in a technological milieu rather than nature, where means dominate ends and politics or economics become situated within technology.

Such criticism is common in advanced societies, while developing countries often seek technology's benefits: productivity, food, health, power, transport, and higher living standards.

Major modern problems include population growth, food supply, energy, resource depletion, pollution, nuclear waste, rainforest destruction, mining damage, acid rain, carbon dioxide, greenhouse effects, pesticides, and ecological balance.

Technology ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Technology)) can worsen these problems but also offers tools for agriculture, new food sources, ocean farming, renewable energy, pollution control, medicine, and environmental monitoring.

The modern dilemma is therefore not simply pro- or anti-technology. The same technical capacity that multiplies production can intensify waste, risk, and dependence, while the same engineering skill can also monitor damage, conserve resources, redesign processes, and support public health.

## Core Equations

Engineering and technology topics often combine efficiency, signal flow, and computational cost:

$$\eta=\frac{\text{useful output}}{\text{input}}$$

Efficiency compares useful output with total input, so losses appear as the gap between the two.

$$SNR=10\log_{10}\left(\frac{P_s}{P_n}\right)$$

Signal-to-noise ratio compares signal power with noise power on a logarithmic decibel scale.

$$T(n)=O(n\log n)$$

Big-O notation describes how running time grows with input size, here at a rate typical of efficient divide-and-combine algorithms.

## Interesting Facts

- [[Printing, Typography, and Photoengraving (Printing)|Printing]] depended on paper, ink, typecasting, and block-printing experience, not one isolated invention.
- Windmills may have developed independently in medieval Europe despite eastern precedents.
- Gasoline was once an unwanted petroleum fraction before internal-combustion engines made it valuable.
- Bakelite was especially useful because it did not conduct electricity.
- Technology criticism is often strongest in societies already transformed by technology.
