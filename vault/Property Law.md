# Property Law

## Introduction

![[attachments/property_law_001.webp|420]]

*Property deed document*

Property law ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Property_law)) consists of principles, policies, and rules for resolving disputes over property and structuring property transactions. It concerns relationships among people with respect to things, whether tangible, such as land, factories, or rings, or intangible, such as stocks, bonds, and bank accounts.

Because it governs allocation, use, and transfer of wealth, property law reflects a society's economy. Because it controls wealth between spouses and across generations, it reflects family structure. Because it deals with economic power and family order, it also reflects politics.

The main Western systems are the Anglo-American system derived from English common law and the civil-law system built on Roman law. Modern comparison also includes socialist and postcommunist systems such as Russia, Romania, and China, where private ownership has been restricted, reintroduced, or combined with public ownership.

## Definition and Basic Themes

![[attachments/property_law_002.webp|420]]

*Land registry building*

Property is often defined as a person's rights with respect to a thing. This is difficult because property is not just the thing itself but a set of legal relations: rights to possess, privileges to use, powers to transfer, and immunities against interference.

The English word property comes from Latin proprietas, connected with what is one's own or peculiar to a person or thing, as opposed to common or another's. Western property thought often contrasts individual ownership with communal or alien claims.

A persistent Western tendency is to agglomerate rights in one legal person, preferably the current possessor. Western systems tend to give that person the right to possess, privilege to use, power to convey, and power to exclude others.

This tendency is cultural rather than universal: non-Western systems may divide use, control, ritual claims, kinship claims, and political authority in ways that do not fit the Western model of a single owner.

## Rome and England

In classical Roman law, ownership was called dominium or proprietas. Roman law tended to identify the legal possessor as owner and to protect possession procedurally. It also limited divisions of ownership, such as usufruct, servitudes, and common ownership.

Roman ownership looked absolute, though it had limits. For example, full recovery of a thing was not always available as a modern owner might expect. Still, Roman law strongly shaped later civil-law ideas of unified private ownership.

Roman law also resisted permanent co-ownership: the maxim that no one should be forced into common property supported division when owners could not remain together.

In medieval England, feudal rights and procedures blurred property and obligation. By the late 12th century, royal courts helped transform free tenants into landowners in a modern sense, while lords' rights became increasingly limited to payments.

English law also developed fee tail, common recovery, copyhold ownership, and other devices that concentrated power in the current possessor. Even before the abolition of forms of action in the 19th century, English law moved toward agglomerated ownership.

## Theories of Property

Samuel von Pufendorf argued that property began with occupation, or physical seizure, but required state sanction to become legal right. The state could not create legitimate property without some foundation in possession.

John Locke offered a labour theory. A person gains a right to a thing by mixing labour with it. For Locke, property is a natural right that should be protected by the state and is central to the social contract.

Jeremy Bentham gave a utilitarian theory. Property is an expectation of protection created by law and settled practice. Because stable expectations support happiness, legislators should rarely interfere with property unless the resulting gain in welfare is greater than the loss.

[[John Stuart Mill]] connected property with liberty, while Georg Wilhelm Friedrich Hegel connected it with the expression of human will. Karl Marx and Friedrich Engels attacked property as a tool of class domination, arguing that capitalist property allowed expropriation of proletarian labour.

## Liberalism, Marxism, and Codification

The Napoleonic Civil Code of 1804 gave systematic expression to private law and was reluctant to recognize interests other than the owner's. Later civil-law jurists, especially German pandectists, developed the Roman agglomerative tendency into a strong modern ownership concept.

The German Civil Code, effective in 1900, embodied many of these ideas and influenced other systems. Anglo-American law also became more absolutist in property matters through reforms such as the English Settled Land Acts and Married Women's Property Acts.

Marxist theory had little direct effect on property law until the Russian Revolution. After socialist revolutions, private property in land and productive assets was restricted or abolished, but postcommunist reforms later reintroduced complex mixtures of private, public, and hybrid ownership.

## Objects of Property

Western systems treat some resources differently from ordinary private property. Water and land near water often remain subject to strong public control. In the eastern United States, stream water rights are tied to riparian landownership; in the western United States, they often depend on prior appropriation.

[[Minerals and Rocks (Mineral)|Minerals]] may be state-owned or privately owned depending on the system. Continental systems often subject minerals to state ownership or control. England historically reserved gold, silver, and lead to the crown. The United States generally allows private mineral ownership but regulates it heavily.

The human body, living or dead, is generally not treated as private property in the West. This creates legal difficulties for blood, organs, donated tissue, testamentary dispositions, and medical uses, because such transfers look property-like but do not fit ordinary ownership categories.

## Group Ownership

Western law treats individual ownership as typical, but it recognizes group ownership. Co-owners may each have an undivided interest in the whole thing, as when siblings inherit property together.

In Anglo-American law, major forms are joint tenancy and tenancy in common. In both, each tenant has the right to possess the whole. Joint tenancy includes survivorship, so surviving tenants take a deceased tenant's share. Tenancy in common passes the share to heirs or devisees.

If co-owners cannot agree on use or possession, courts may order partition. If physical division is impractical, the property may be sold and proceeds divided. These rules balance shared ownership with the Western reluctance to force people into permanent co-ownership.

## Trusts

The trust is a major Anglo-American division of ownership. Legal title belongs to the trustee, who has possession, use, and power to convey. Beneficial ownership belongs to the beneficiary, who receives the benefits.

The trustee owes a fiduciary duty to manage the property for the beneficiary, not for personal gain. If the trustee violates this duty, courts may require repayment, impose remedies, or remove the trustee.

Trusts ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trust_%28law%29)) developed from the separation of common-law and equity courts. Even after those courts merged, legal and equitable ownership remained conceptually distinct. Trusts are used in family settlements, charitable gifts, estate planning, and asset management.

## Criminal, Regulatory, and Private Protection

Theft is nearly universal: if one person takes another's property without permission and intends permanent deprivation, the law punishes it. Anglo-American law historically distinguished larceny, burglary, robbery, and embezzlement; civil-law codes do not always make the same distinctions.

Land cannot normally be stolen, but wrongful entry can be punished as criminal trespass, and deliberate damage can be punished criminally. Modern environmental regulation can also punish injury to land, water, or air.

Regulatory law expanded in the 20th century, especially for land use and environmental harms. It limits one owner's privilege of use to protect neighbouring owners, the public, and shared resources. Some jurisdictions allow affected persons to sue violators directly; others rely on administrative enforcement.

Private-law protection includes actions to recover possession, establish ownership, or obtain damages. Roman distinctions between in rem and in personam actions and between possessory and petitory actions influenced both civil-law and Anglo-American systems.

## Takings and Expropriation

Governments may take or restrict property for public purposes. In the United States, constitutional law developed the idea of regulatory taking, especially after Pennsylvania Coal Co. v. Mahon in 1922, when regulation can go so far that compensation is required.

French law generally requires compensation only when government permanently deprives an owner of possession. German law, influenced by post-World War II constitutional protection of property, developed doctrines that parallel some American concerns.

Expropriation, adverse possession, and prescription show how property rights can be created or extinguished over time. Limitation periods may bar actions, extinguish rights, or transfer title to adverse possessors depending on the system.

## Sales, Gifts, and Wills

A sale of movable property is both contract and conveyance. Anglo-American and French law often allow the contract itself to transfer title unless parties agree otherwise. German law, following Roman ideas, requires a separate transfer act, though practical outcomes may converge.

Gifts differ sharply. Anglo-American law normally does not enforce a promise to make a gift because there is no consideration; delivery, intent, and acceptance are central. Civil law may enforce a notarized gift promise if formalities are met and family inheritance rights are protected.

A will transfers property at death and is revocable before death. Western systems agree that wills are ambulatory: they create rights only at death and affect property then owned by the testator. Formalities differ, with civil-law systems often accepting handwritten holograph wills and Anglo-American systems emphasizing witnessed formal wills.

## Postcommunist and Socialist Property Systems

After communism fell in much of the world, private ownership expanded, but not in a single pattern. Postcommunist systems may combine private ownership, state ownership, hybrid forms, restrictions on foreigners, and different rules for land, buildings, enterprises, and natural resources.

In [[Russia]], the 1993 constitution and 1995 Civil Code recognized private land ownership, but land law took years to clarify. The Land Code of 2001 and farmland-sale laws of 2002 shaped transfer rules. Land transactions require registration, and non-Russians may lease but not own land.

Russia also recognizes trust management, but it is contractual and does not transfer legal ownership as Anglo-American trusts do. Russian land-use servitudes resemble easements, allowing uses such as access or pipelines over another's land.

In Romania, postcommunist land law is based on the 1864 Civil Code and later constitutional rules. Land ownership is restricted to Romanians, though Romanian companies partly or wholly owned by foreigners may own land. Public property belongs to the state and cannot be sold, while private property can be sold.

Agricultural land in Romania receives special treatment, including family purchase limits and possible preemptive rights for neighbours or leaseholders. Transfers require state registration, and landlord-tenant rules combine contract and real-property elements.

In [[China]], private law was long modeled on Soviet law, but market reforms led to a 2007 property rights law. The law recognizes a hybrid socialist-market system in which public ownership remains dominant while diverse forms of ownership develop side by side.

Chinese land cannot be privately owned because all land is public, either state or collective. Only land-use rights and buildings or other landed assets can be transferred, and they must generally move together. Gifts are treated as contracts under China's 1999 contract law, which differs sharply from common-law gift doctrine.

## Key Events & Developments

- 1285: England's De donis conditionalibus allowed fee tail inheritance limits.
- 1804: The Napoleonic Civil Code expressed a strong ownership model.
- 1847: [[Marx and Marxism (Marx)|Marx]] and Engels attacked private property in The Communist Manifesto.
- 1900: The German Civil Code took effect.
- 1922: Pennsylvania Coal Co. v. Mahon shaped U.S. regulatory takings law.
- 2007: China adopted its first property rights law.
