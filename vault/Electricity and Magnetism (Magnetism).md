# Electricity and Magnetism (Magnetism)

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> Magnetism arises from moving charges and magnetic moments, producing fields and forces that interact with electric phenomena. Its principles support motors, generators, materials science, and electromagnetic technology.

## Introduction

Magnetism ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnetism)) is a physical phenomenon connected with moving electric charge. The motion may be an electric current in a wire, charged particles moving through space, or an electron moving in an atomic orbit. Magnetism also comes from the spin of elementary particles, especially the electron.

## Fundamentals

![[attachments/electricity_and_magnetism_magnetism_001.webp|420]]

*Solar Magnetic Field Lines*

The central idea in magnetism is the magnetic field and its action on matter. A magnetic field can be detected because it exerts a force on moving charges. This force is at right angles to both the field and the velocity of the charge, so it bends the path of the charge without changing its speed.

## Diamagnetism

![[attachments/electricity_and_magnetism_magnetism_002.webp|420]]

*Iron filings showing magnetic field lines around a magnet*

In diamagnetism, a magnetic field changes the motion of electrons in atomic orbits. Each electron orbit precesses around the direction of B and gains a small additional angular momentum. The induced magnetic moment opposes the applied magnetic field.

## Paramagnetism

Paramagnetism occurs mainly in substances whose atoms, ions, or molecules have permanent magnetic dipole moments. The magnetization depends on the balance between magnetic energy, which favours alignment with the field, and thermal energy, which disorders the dipoles.

## Ferromagnetism

Ferromagnetism occurs when permanent atomic magnetic dipoles are spontaneously oriented parallel to one another even without an external field. This is surprising because two side-by-side dipoles with moments in the same direction would normally repel magnetically. A different quantum mechanical interaction is needed to explain the alignment.

## Remanence

Remanence is the retention of magnetization when the external magnetic field H is zero. The remaining field is called residual flux density. In a hysteresis loop, this point is marked after the material has first been driven toward magnetic saturation.

## Antiferromagnetism

In antiferromagnetism, exchange interactions between adjacent atomic dipoles have the opposite sign from those in ferromagnets. Adjacent dipoles tend to line up antiparallel rather than parallel. At high temperatures, the material behaves as a paramagnet.

## Ferrimagnetism

Ferrimagnetism is found in ferrites, including lodestone or magnetite, Fe3O4. Ferrites and related materials have many properties like ferromagnets, including spontaneous magnetization and remanence. However, unlike ferromagnetic metals, they have low electrical conductivity.

## Key Events & Developments

- In the early 19th century, Jean-Baptiste Biot and Felix Savart gave the law used to calculate magnetic fields from steady currents.
- In 1845, [[Faraday]] classified substances as diamagnetic or paramagnetic by observing their forces in inhomogeneous magnetic fields.
- In 1895, Pierre Curie measured the temperature dependence of paramagnetic susceptibility and showed the relation now called Curie's law.
- In 1936, Louis-Eugene-Felix Neel explained antiferromagnetic behaviour through antiparallel ordering below the Neel temperature.
- In 1948, Neel explained ferrimagnetism as unequal opposing sublattices produced by basically antiferromagnetic exchange forces.
- The development of MRI applies magnetic dipole energy and resonance to measure hydrogen concentration in body tissues and produce detailed images.

## Interesting Facts

- A magnetic field bends a moving charged particle without changing its speed because the magnetic force is perpendicular to the particle's velocity.
- The total magnetic flux through any closed surface is zero, so magnetic field lines close on themselves rather than beginning or ending on isolated magnetic charge.
- A three-millimetre-thick NdFeB magnet can produce a field comparable to a copper loop carrying several thousand amperes.
- The Earth's geographic North Pole acts magnetically like a south pole because compass field lines point toward it.
- In an MRI scanner, a solenoid commonly provides a magnetic field of one to three teslas, and the radio-wave frequency is typically 40 to 130 megahertz.
- A Helmholtz coil can create a highly uniform magnetic field and can be adjusted to cancel the Earth's magnetic field in a test region.
- Superconductors show extremely strong diamagnetism because the induced magnetic moment is governed by the size of the whole sample rather than by individual atoms.
- Hard magnetic materials need a broad hysteresis loop for permanent magnets, while soft magnetic materials need a thin loop for transformer use.

## Core Equations

Magnetic forces act on moving charges and currents:

$$\vec{F}=q\vec{v}\times\vec{B}$$

This gives a compact mathematical form for magnetic forces act on moving charges and currents.

A current loop has magnetic moment:

$$\vec{\mu}=I\vec{A}$$

This gives a compact mathematical form for a current loop has magnetic moment.
