# Biological Growth and Development (Growth)

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> Biological growth is the regulated increase of cell size and number that builds an organism’s patterned form across its life history. ## Introduction

Biological growth is the increase in cell size and number during an organism's life history. Growth is planned rather than random, with patterns that help determine the eventual size and shape of the organism. Growth is therefore more than accumulation. It is a regulated biological process that reflects genetic instructions, developmental timing, and environmental conditions.

Growth also has an evolutionary dimension. The way a body grows can affect how it adapts to its environment and, over long periods, how new body forms arise. Growth is therefore a central part of both development and evolution.

## Growth in Cells and Organs

Growth depends on mitosis and on the enlargement of cells. Chromosomes are duplicated and distributed precisely to daughter cells, allowing organized increase in cell number. Growth may be localized, as at plant shoot tips, or spread through the body, as in embryos.

Different parts may grow at different rates, producing the characteristic shape of an adult organism. This patterned growth is what gives bodies their final form, because changes in rate and location matter as much as total size. In some organisms growth is not patterned at all. Slime molds are an example of forms that do not develop a regular adult shape in the same way plants and animals do. That contrast helps define what regular growth means.

## Tumours and Abnormal Growth

When growth is not properly regulated, tumours may result. Tumours are masses of cells with abnormal division rates and abnormal organization, and malignant tumours can spread to other parts of the body. Growth is therefore a medical issue as well as a developmental one.

Harmless and dangerous abnormal growths differ. Some tumours may remain localized or relatively slow, while malignant tumours invade other tissues and become life-threatening. This distinction is important because it shows that not every growth abnormality has the same consequence.

A larger point is that orderly growth and disordered growth must be understood together. One makes normal development possible, while the other reveals what happens when developmental control fails.

## Regeneration and Compensation

*Diagram of the stages of mitosis*

Some organisms can regenerate lost parts. Examples from salamanders, lizards, humans, and plants show that regenerative capacity differs greatly among species and tissues. Regeneration often requires cells to lose specialized features, divide more actively, and then respecialize into the missing structure.

Compensatory growth occurs when one organ increases in size after the loss of another paired organ. The kidney is a central example, and this can occur through hypertrophy, hyperplasia, or both. The remaining organ responds to increased demand by enlarging and working harder.

Growth can be corrective as well as developmental. An organism may use growth not only to build itself in the first place but also to repair or rebalance after injury.

## Environmental Factors

![[attachments/biological_growth_002.webp|420]]

*Diagram of phototropism in plant growth*

Temperature, pressure, light, and chemical conditions all influence growth. Cold slows biochemical reactions, high hydrostatic pressure can interfere with cell division, and wind can shape plants by repeated mechanical stress. Growth is therefore sensitive to physical as well as genetic conditions.

Light is especially important because it drives photosynthesis in plants and helps regulate flowering, stem elongation, and other developmental responses. In animals, light can influence seasonal reproduction and other rhythms through specialized sensory systems. Light is one of the most powerful external regulators of development.

Growth cannot be explained by internal programming alone. Organisms respond continuously to their surroundings, and the same species may grow differently under different physical conditions.

## Seasonal and Chemical Control
Growth is sensitive to changing seasons and chemical conditions. Plants may stop growing in winter and resume in spring, while animals may enter dormancy or shift energy use depending on temperature and food supply. These seasonal cycles show that growth is often linked to survival strategy.

Chemical factors such as nutrients and internal signals also matter. Growth depends on the availability of resources and on the physiological state of the organism. Chemical conditions can either support or limit development, depending on what the organism needs and what the environment supplies.

These factors tie together the main theme: growth is a regulated process that is responsive to both inside and outside factors. It is never just a simple increase in size.

## Conclusion

Biological growth is a regulated, responsive process that shapes life from the cell outward. Growth is controlled by genetics but modified by environment, pathology, and the organism's own developmental needs. The result is a picture of growth as one of the basic organizing processes of life.

## Key Events & Developments

- Cells increase in number through mitosis and in size through enlargement.
- Normal growth produces patterned adult form.
- Uncontrolled growth produces tumours, including malignant ones that can spread.
- Regeneration restores lost tissue in some animals and plants.
- Seasonal and environmental changes regulate growth rates in both plants and animals.
