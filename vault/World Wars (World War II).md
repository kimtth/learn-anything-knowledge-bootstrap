# World Wars (World War II)

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> World War II was the 1939-45 global conflict between the Axis and Allies, the largest and bloodiest war in history. The source traces its origins in unresolved post-1918 tensions and campaigns across Europe, Africa, Asia, and the Pacific, while explaining its transformative effects on Soviet power, China, the United States, and the international order.

## Introduction

World War II ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_War_II)), also called the Second World War ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_War_II)), was fought from 1939 to 1945 and involved almost every part of the world. The main Axis powers ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Axis_powers)) were [[Germany]], [[Italy]], and [[Japan]]. The main Allies were [[France]], Great Britain, the [[United States of America (United States)|United States]], the Soviet Union, and, to a lesser degree, [[China]]. The war continued many unresolved conflicts left by World War I after an uneasy 20-year pause.

The war was the largest and bloodiest conflict in history, with about 40,000,000 to 50,000,000 deaths. Like World War I, it became a major watershed in 20th-century geopolitics. It extended Soviet power into eastern Europe, helped create the conditions for communist victory in [[China]], and shifted world power away from western Europe toward the [[United States of America (United States)|United States]] and the Soviet Union.

The European war began when Adolf Hitler decided to invade [[Poland]]. Poland had guarantees from [[France]] and Great Britain, but Hitler first neutralized the danger of Soviet resistance by signing the German-Soviet Nonaggression Pact ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Molotov%E2%80%93Ribbentrop_Pact)) on August 23-24, 1939. A secret protocol divided Poland between Germany and the U.S.S.R.. Germany invaded on September 1, 1939. Britain and France declared war on September 3, and World War II had begun.

## Forces, Blitzkrieg, and the Technology of War

In 1939, the Allies had greater combined population, industry, and military manpower than Germany, but the Wehrmacht had better organization, training, and doctrine. German infantry divisions had stronger firepower than French, British, or Polish divisions. More important, Germany had panzer divisions, while the Allies had many tanks but no equivalent armored divisions.

Blitzkrieg ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blitzkrieg)), or lightning war, depended on tanks massed in divisions, motorized infantry, artillery, and close air support. Following the ideas of Heinz Guderian, German armor punched through enemy lines, isolated enemy formations, and left infantry to surround and capture them. Dive bombers attacked supply and communication lines and spread confusion behind the front. The Luftwaffe was designed mainly to support the army and had modern aircraft, standardized production, and operational readiness.

The interwar years had changed war technology. Many leaders after World War I believed defense had proved superior, leading to fortifications such as the Maginot Line ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maginot_Line)) and Siegfried Line. Yet tanks and aircraft had already shown by 1918 that mass, surprise, and mobility could break trench systems. Germany learned this lesson more fully than France and Britain, which were slower to create armored formations and new doctrines.

## The German Conquest of Poland, Scandinavia, and France

The invasion of [[Poland]] showed the power of German mobility and air-ground coordination. Soviet forces entered eastern Poland under the secret agreement with Germany. Poland was defeated quickly, and western Europe entered a period of limited land action before Germany widened the war.

In 1940, Germany moved into northern and western Europe. The attack on [[France]] and the Low Countries avoided a direct assault on the Maginot Line ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maginot_Line)). Fedor von Bock's Army Group B attacked through the Netherlands and Belgium, drawing Allied forces north. Gerd von Rundstedt's Army Group A, with strong armored forces under Paul Ludwig von Kleist and Hermann Hoth, moved through the Ardennes toward the weak hinge near Sedan.

The German breakthrough split Allied forces. The British Expeditionary Force and other Allied troops were driven toward Dunkirk. On May 24, 1940, Hitler's halt order stopped German armor near Dunkirk, partly because Hermann Goring claimed the Luftwaffe could destroy the trapped forces and partly because Hitler still imagined Britain might accept peace. The pause helped make the evacuation possible. By June 4, about 198,000 British and 140,000 French and Belgian troops had escaped from Dunkirk, though heavy equipment was left behind.

Germany then renewed its offensive south of the Somme and Aisne. Erwin Rommel's armor broke through toward Rouen, and Guderian's forces drove deep after crossings on the Aisne. France collapsed, and Germany took more than 1,000,000 prisoners in three weeks, at a cost of about 60,000 casualties.

## The Battle of Britain

After France fell, Great Britain remained Germany's main enemy in the west. Hitler ordered preparations for an invasion, Operation Sea Lion, on July 16, 1940. Such an invasion required German control of the air over the English Channel. Goring issued the Eagle Day directive, aiming to destroy British air power.

The Battle of Britain ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Britain)) was fought mainly in August and September 1940, with the later Blitz continuing into the winter of 1940-41. The British had about 600 frontline fighters. Germany had about 1,300 bombers and dive bombers, 900 single-engine fighters, and 300 twin-engine fighters. The Royal Air Force Fighter Command had important advantages: radar early warning, effective control systems, clear defensive purpose, and excellent fighters such as the Spitfire and Hurricane.

The Luftwaffe shifted between attacks on shipping, airfields, aircraft production, and cities. Its bombers lacked the capacity to deliver permanent strategic destruction, and daylight raids were vulnerable. The RAF victory prevented invasion, kept Britain in the war, and created the conditions for Germany's eventual defeat.

## East Africa

The East African campaign removed the Italian threat to the Suez Canal from the south and to Kenya from the north. In August 1940, Italian forces overran British Somaliland. Archibald Wavell then used reinforcements from India and support from Haile Selassie, the former Ethiopian emperor, to organize a counteroffensive.

Orde Wingate and Ethiopian exiles crossed from [[Sudan]] into Ethiopia. William Platt advanced into Eritrea, while Alan Cunningham advanced from Kenya into Italian Somaliland. The Italian defense at Keren was strong, but after it fell on March 26, 1941, the road to Asmara, Massawa, and Ethiopia opened. Addis Ababa fell, and Italian East Africa was destroyed as a strategic threat.

## German Strategy, 1939-1942

[[Hitler|Hitler's]] strategy joined power politics to racist ideology. His program aimed first to dominate central Europe, then to build a continental empire including European Soviet territory, and later to make Germany a world power equal to the British Empire, [[Japan]], and the [[United States of America (United States)|United States]]. Conquest in the east was tied to settlement, exploitation, and the extermination of Jews.

Hitler hoped for short campaigns that would secure Germany's rear before the great eastern war. He wanted to defeat France, avoid a premature world war, and eventually attack the Soviet Union. Once Britain refused peace and Germany attacked the U.S.S.R., the war became much larger than the quick campaigns he had expected.

## Operation Barbarossa and the Eastern Front, 1941-1942

Operation Barbarossa, the German invasion of the Soviet Union, began in June 1941. Germany won huge early victories, but the campaign began to fail when it could not destroy Soviet power before winter. German forces advanced toward Leningrad, [[Moscow]], and [[Ukraine]], but the size of the Soviet state, the resistance of the Red Army, and German logistical strain prevented a final decision.

In the winter of 1941-42, the Soviets counterattacked. Hitler forbade major withdrawals and ordered German troops to hold strongpoints such as Novgorod, Rzhev, Vyazma, Bryansk, Orel, Kursk, Kharkov, and Taganrog. This rigid defense helped prevent a rout, but it drained the Luftwaffe and reduced many German divisions to a fraction of their strength.

The invasion pushed the Soviet Union toward cooperation with Britain and the United States. The Anglo-Soviet agreement of July 12, 1941 pledged mutual help and no separate peace. British and Soviet forces occupied [[Iran]] in August 1941 to prevent German use of the country and to secure supply routes.

## Japan, China, and the Road to Pearl Harbor

[[Japan]] had been expanding in [[China]] since the invasion of Manchuria in 1931. In 1937 and 1938, Japanese forces advanced through northern and central China, taking [[Nanking (Nanjing)|Nanking]], Suchow, the Wu-han cities, [[Canton (Guangzhou)|Canton]], and other coastal centers. The Nationalist government moved to [[Chungking (Chongqing)|Chungking]], while the Chinese Communists developed guerrilla resistance from north-central China.

By 1941, 38 of Japan's 51 infantry divisions, about 750,000 men, were tied down in China and Manchuria. The war in Europe opened chances for Japanese expansion into Southeast Asia, especially after France and the Netherlands were weakened. Japan wanted resources, above all oil, and considered the Dutch East Indies, Malaya, and other western Pacific areas essential.

Negotiations with the [[United States of America (United States)|United States]] failed because Japan would not accept withdrawal from China and the United States would not accept Japanese domination. Japanese planners therefore prepared coordinated attacks across the Pacific, aiming to destroy or disable Allied forces, seize Southeast Asian resources, and build a fortified defensive perimeter.

## Pearl Harbor and the Japanese Offensive, 1941-1942

![[attachments/world_wars_world_war_ii_003.webp|420]]

*World War II woman aircraft worker, Vega Aircraft Corporation, Burbank, California 1942*

The attack on Pearl Harbor followed Yamamoto Isoroku's plan. Nagumo Chuichi's carrier force reached a point north of Hawaii undetected and launched about 360 aircraft on December 7, 1941. All eight U.S. battleships in the harbor were hit, more than 180 aircraft were destroyed, and more than 2,330 troops were killed.

The attack failed in one crucial way: the three U.S. aircraft carriers of the Pacific Fleet were at sea and survived. Oil storage and shore installations also escaped serious damage. The attack unified American opinion, ended support for neutrality, and led the U.S. Congress to declare war on [[Japan]] on December 8.

Japan struck simultaneously across the Pacific and Southeast Asia. Bombers hit the [[Philippines]], destroying much of U.S. air power there. Japanese forces advanced into Malaya, Borneo, Celebes, Sumatra, and the Dutch East Indies. The unified ABDACOM command under Wavell tried to defend the region but was formed too late and with too little strength.

Singapore, Britain's major Pacific base, fell on February 15, 1942, when A.E. Percival surrendered a 90,000-strong garrison after Japanese forces attacked from the landward side. Java fell in March after the Allied naval defeat in the Battle of the Java Sea. By early 1942, Japan had achieved a vast expansion.

## Coral Sea, Midway, and Guadalcanal

Japan next tried to cut Allied communications with [[Australia]] by moving toward Port Moresby, New Caledonia, Fiji, and Samoa. U.S. intelligence detected the Port Moresby plan, leading to the Battle of the Coral Sea in May 1942. The Japanese sank the U.S. carrier Lexington and damaged Yorktown, but the carrier Shokaku was crippled and Japanese aircraft losses forced cancellation of the Port Moresby operation.

At Midway in June 1942, U.S. codebreaking again gave warning of Japanese intentions. Japan sought a decisive naval battle, but U.S. carrier aircraft destroyed the core of Japan's carrier striking power. Midway shifted the naval balance and ended Japan's ability to expand with the same confidence.

The Allied offensive at Guadalcanal began on August 7, 1942, after Japan started building an airfield there. U.S. Marines seized the airfield and nearby positions, while a long series of land, sea, and air battles followed. Japan repeatedly tried to reinforce the island but lost ships, aircraft, and troops. By January 1943, Allied control of Guadalcanal marked the first major offensive success against Japan.

## China, Burma, and India, 1942-1943

The war in Burma mattered because the Burma Road was the main land supply route to Chiang Kai-shek's Nationalist government in China. Joseph W. Stilwell became Chiang's chief of staff, and Chinese armies crossed into Burma. Japanese forces proved stronger. They took Lashio on April 29, 1942, cutting the Burma Road and isolating China except by air.

British, Indian, and Chinese forces retreated toward [[India]] and China. By the end of 1942, Burma was in Japanese hands, China was isolated, and India was exposed to possible invasion. In 1943, command was reorganized under Louis Mountbatten's South East Asia Command, with Stilwell as deputy and William Slim commanding the British-Indian 14th Army.

## North Africa, El-Alamein, and Torch

In [[North Africa]], Erwin Rommel and the Axis threatened Egypt and the Suez Canal. After Claude Auchinleck checked Rommel near El-Alamein, Bernard Law Montgomery took command of the British 8th Army. Rommel attacked at Alam al-Halfa in late August 1942, but deep minefields, fuel shortage, air attacks, and British defenses stopped him.

Montgomery opened the Second Battle of el-Alamein on the night of October 23-24, 1942. The British had major advantages in men, tanks, and aircraft, while Axis supply lines across the Mediterranean were under heavy attack. The battle forced Rommel into retreat and began the removal of Axis power from Egypt and Libya.

The Allied invasion of northwest Africa, Operation Torch, began on November 8, 1942, under Dwight D. Eisenhower. Landings took place near Casablanca, Oran, and Algiers. The political situation was difficult because French North Africa remained loyal to Vichy France. Jean Darlan reached an agreement with the Allies, and Henri Giraud became military commander.

## Tunisia, November 1942May 1943

Axis troops quickly entered Tunisia after the Allied landings. Kenneth Anderson's Allied 1st Army tried to reach Tunis and Bizerte, but strong German and Italian reinforcements checked the advance. Jurgen von Arnim took command of Axis defense in northern Tunisia.

Rommel, retreating from Libya to the Mareth Line, joined the Tunisian struggle from the south. In February 1943, Axis forces attacked U.S. troops near Faid, Gafsa, and the Kasserine Pass, exposing American inexperience. Allied command and tactics improved afterward. With Montgomery advancing from the east and Allied forces pressing from the west, Axis forces in Tunisia were trapped. The campaign ended in May 1943 with a large Axis surrender.

## German-occupied Europe

German rule in occupied Europe followed Hitler's racial and imperial goals. Western and northern areas such as the [[Netherlands]] and [[Norway]] were expected eventually to be incorporated more closely into the Reich. Areas under military administration, such as [[France]] and Serbia, were to be part of a German-dominated bloc. [[Poland]] and the Soviet Union were treated as colonial zones for settlement and exploitation.

The SS gained broad powers across German-dominated Europe. Fritz Sauckel, the labor commissioner, forced millions into German war industry; eventually about 7,500,000 people became forced or slave laborers. The central crime was the Final Solution ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Final_Solution)), Hitler's policy of physically exterminating Jews wherever German rule or influence reached. The mass killing grew alongside the war against the Soviet Union and reflected Nazi claims that Communism and Jews were biologically linked enemies.

## Casablanca and Trident, JanuaryMay 1943

At the Casablanca Conference in January 1943, [[Franklin D. Roosevelt]] and Winston Churchill agreed that Sicily should be attacked after victory in North Africa. They also agreed on Pacific and Burma operations. Politically, the conference is remembered for Roosevelt's public demand for the unconditional surrender of Germany, Italy, and Japan.

At the Trident Conference in [[Washington, D.C. (Washington)|Washington, D.C.]], in May 1943, Allied leaders confirmed the Sicilian operation and set May 1, 1944 as the target date for a cross-Channel invasion of France. The question of whether Sicily should be followed by invasion of Italy remained unsettled, but the outline of future Allied strategy was becoming clearer.

## The Eastern Front, FebruarySeptember 1943

After the Soviet victories around Stalingrad and the winter counteroffensive, Germany struck back in February 1943, retaking Kharkov and Belgorod and restoring a front on the Donets River. Hitler also allowed a withdrawal to a straighter line near Smolensk and Orel. The remaining Soviet bulge around Kursk tempted Hitler into one more great offensive.

The Battle of Kursk began on July 5, 1943. Germany committed large infantry and armored forces, but the Red Army had improved in numbers, equipment, and tactical skill. Soviet commanders waited for the German attack, absorbed it in prepared defenses, and then counterattacked. Kursk ended Germany's last major strategic offensive in the east and gave the Soviet Union the initiative.

## The Southwest and South Pacific, JuneOctober 1943

![[attachments/world_wars_world_war_ii_002.webp|420]]

*Crew of a Sherman-tank south of Vaucelles*

Allied Pacific strategy in 1943 aimed to reduce the Japanese base at Rabaul by encirclement. Douglas MacArthur's forces advanced along New Guinea, while U.S. forces moved through the Solomon Islands. Landings on Woodlark, Kiriwina, New Georgia, and Rendova supported this pressure.

The fighting in the Solomons caused heavy naval losses and strong Japanese counteraction, but Allied reinforcements secured the New Georgia group and extended operations to Vella Lavella. In New Guinea, Allied troops captured Salamaua, Lae, and Finschhafen. Air attacks destroyed more than 200 Japanese aircraft at Wewak. Japan responded by planning a last defense line from western New Guinea through the Carolines to the Marianas.

## The Quadrant Conference (Quebec I)

The success in Sicily and the fall of Mussolini made American leaders more willing to support a campaign in [[Italy]]. At the Quadrant Conference in Quebec in August 1943, Roosevelt, Churchill, and their advisers debated the timing and scale of Overlord, the planned invasion of France from Britain.

The Allies agreed that the Overlord assault force should be 25 percent larger than first planned, that it should be supported by a landing in southern France, and that a U.S. officer should command it. They also approved Pacific plans against the Gilbert and Marshall islands and organized a new Southeast Asia theater under British command.

## The Allied Invasion of Italy and German Strategy from 1943

The Allies landed in Calabria on September 3, 1943, and Italy secretly accepted surrender terms the same day. The armistice was announced on September 8. Allied landings at Salerno began on September 9, but German resistance was strong. Germany quickly occupied much of Italy and turned the peninsula into a difficult defensive front.

From late 1942 onward, German strategy became mainly defensive. Hitler tried to hold the huge area under German control against Soviet attack in the east and Anglo-American attack in the south and west. His refusal to permit timely withdrawals trapped armies in hopeless positions. The policy could delay Allied advances, but it consumed German manpower and made strategic recovery impossible.

## The Eastern Front, October 1943April 1944

By October 1943, the Red Army had bridgeheads across the Dnieper River. N.F. Vatutin advanced on [[Kiev]], while Ivan Konev pushed from the Kremenchug bridgehead. Kiev fell on November 6, 1943, and Soviet forces reached farther west into Zhitomir and Korosten.

In early 1944, Soviet forces crossed the prewar Polish frontier and encircled German divisions near Korsun. Georgy Zhukov, Rodion Malinovsky, and Konev drove through Ukraine, pushing toward Tarnopol, the southern Bug River, and the Dniester. The Germans lost the initiative and were forced back across broad sections of the eastern front.

## The Pacific Advance, Rabaul, New Guinea, and the Central Pacific

The Allies isolated Rabaul rather than directly storming it. New Zealand troops took the Treasury Islands, U.S. forces landed at Empress Augusta Bay on Bougainville, and other U.S. troops landed at Arawe and Cape Gloucester on New Britain. Captured air bases blocked Japanese movement and made Rabaul less useful.

In western New Guinea, U.S. and Australian troops advanced through Saidor, Sio, Hollandia, Aitape, and Biak. Hollandia became a major base for the Southwest Pacific. Some Japanese garrisons, such as Wewak, held out long after they had been bypassed.

In the central Pacific, Chester Nimitz's forces invaded the Gilbert Islands in November 1943. Makin fell easily, but Tarawa cost the U.S. Marines about 1,000 killed and 2,300 wounded. The Marshalls followed, with Kwajalein and Enewetak captured in early 1944. U.S. carrier attacks on Truk destroyed aircraft and shipping, allowing the Allies to bypass that major Japanese base.

The next central Pacific objective was the Mariana Islands. Their capture would provide bases for heavy air raids on Japan. Japan prepared Operation A, relying on land-based aircraft and carrier forces, but its air strength had already been badly weakened.

## Normandy and the Liberation of France

D-Day, the Allied invasion of Normandy, began on June 6, 1944, under Dwight D. Eisenhower. U.S., British, Canadian, and other Allied forces landed across the Normandy beaches. The operation opened the long-awaited western front and forced Germany to fight major land campaigns in both east and west.

The Allies gradually broke out from Normandy. German counteraction failed, and the front in France collapsed. [[Paris]] was liberated, and Allied forces pushed toward the German frontier. The western campaign, combined with Soviet pressure in the east, made Germany's strategic position hopeless.

## The Eastern Front, JuneDecember 1944

The Soviet summer offensive of 1944 destroyed much of German power in the east. Soviet forces advanced through Belorussia, into [[Poland]], and toward the Baltic. Germany's Army Group Centre was shattered, and the Red Army moved close to the borders of the Reich.

The eastern advance also reshaped the politics of postwar Europe. Soviet armies entered territories that would later fall under Soviet influence. Military victory and political control moved together as the Red Army pushed west.

## Burma and China, October 1944May 1945

In Burma, Allied command changes and improved forces reversed earlier defeats. A.C. Wedemeyer replaced Stilwell in China-related command duties, while William Slim's 14th Army fought the Japanese in Burma. Allied operations reopened land communication with China and pushed Japanese forces back.

In [[China]], Japanese pressure continued, but Japan's wider position was worsening. The loss of Pacific bases, shipping, and air strength made it harder to support distant campaigns. Burma's recovery also reduced Japan's ability to threaten India.

## The Pacific, 1944-1945

In the Marianas, U.S. forces landed on Saipan on June 15, 1944. The battle was fierce and costly, but the island's capture gave the United States an air base within range of Japan. The accompanying naval victory in the Battle of the Philippine Sea destroyed much of Japan's remaining carrier air power.

The return to the [[Philippines]] began at Leyte in October 1944. Douglas MacArthur fulfilled his promise to return, and the Battle of Leyte Gulf became a massive naval battle that further crippled the Japanese fleet. U.S. forces then advanced through the Philippines.

The campaigns for Iwo Jima and Okinawa in 1945 showed how costly an invasion closer to Japan could be. U.S. Marines landed on Iwo Jima and fought for Mount Suribachi. On Okinawa, Japanese defenses and kamikaze attacks inflicted heavy losses. Simon Bolivar Buckner, Jr. was killed during the Okinawa campaign.

## Allied Conferences, Yalta, and Potsdam

Allied leaders used conferences to coordinate strategy and shape the postwar order. Roosevelt, [[Churchill]], and [[Stalin]] met at Tehran, where Stalin was reassured that Overlord would happen in 1944 and promised Soviet action against Japan later. The conference also addressed Iran and Soviet territorial aims.

At the Yalta Conference in February 1945, the leaders dealt especially with Poland and eastern Europe. Soviet military occupation gave Stalin great leverage. The conference is remembered for decisions and tensions over postwar political arrangements.

At Potsdam in 1945, Harry S. Truman, [[Churchill]] and later Clement Attlee, and [[Stalin]] met after Germany's defeat. The conference addressed occupation, reparations, borders, and the ongoing war with Japan. It also occurred as the United States possessed the atomic bomb.

## The Defeat of Germany

By 1945, Germany was attacked from east and west. Soviet forces advanced toward [[Berlin]], while western Allied forces crossed the Rhine and pushed into Germany. Hitler's refusal to withdraw or surrender left German forces to collapse piecemeal.

[[Berlin]] fell after a Soviet assault, and Hitler died as the city was being taken. Germany surrendered in May 1945. The war in Europe ended with the destruction of Nazi power, the occupation of Germany, and Soviet control over much of eastern Europe.

## The Defeat of Japan

![[attachments/world_wars_world_war_ii_001.webp|420]]

*Mamoru Shigemitsu signs the Instrument of Surrender, officially ending the Second World War*

The United States used long-range bombers from the Marianas to attack Japanese cities. Japan's navy and merchant shipping had been devastated, and its empire was cut apart by Allied advances. Yet Japanese leaders did not immediately surrender.

On August 6, 1945, the U.S. bomber Enola Gay dropped an atomic bomb on Hiroshima. A second atomic bomb was dropped on Nagasaki on August 9. The Soviet Union also entered the war against Japan, attacking Japanese forces in Manchuria. Japan surrendered, and Douglas MacArthur later received the formal surrender. World War II ended in 1945.

## Europe

The war left [[Europe]] devastated and politically transformed. Western European states no longer held the same world power they had before the war. Germany was defeated and occupied, and eastern Europe came under strong Soviet influence as a result of Red Army advances and postwar settlements.

The conflict also exposed the full criminal nature of Nazi rule, including forced labor, occupation terror, and the Final Solution ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Final_Solution)). The postwar order in Europe was shaped by occupation zones, new borders, displaced populations, and the growing division between Soviet-controlled eastern Europe and the western Allies.

## The Far East

In the Far East, Japan's defeat ended its imperial expansion and changed the balance of power in Asia. China had endured years of war, occupation, and internal division. The weakening of Japan and the wartime strengthening of Chinese Communist forces helped prepare the later communist victory in China.

The Pacific war also confirmed the rise of the [[United States of America (United States)|United States]] as a dominant military and naval power. Bases, fleets, aircraft carriers, amphibious operations, and long-range bombing had shaped the campaign. By the end of the war, the old European imperial presence in Asia had been weakened, while American and Soviet power had expanded.

## Costs and Devastation

The human cost of World War II ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_War_II)) is difficult to measure precisely because armies collapsed, borders changed, civilians fled, and many governments could not keep reliable records. Even cautious estimates show that civilian deaths were at least as important as battlefield losses. Bombing, siege, famine, disease, forced labour, genocide, reprisals, and the sinking of ships all made the war a catastrophe for noncombatants as well as soldiers. The greatest losses fell on eastern Europe, the Soviet Union, [[Poland]], Yugoslavia, and [[China]], where occupation, racial policy, scorched-earth warfare, and civil disorder overlapped.

The physical destruction was also vast. German occupation drained western and eastern Europe of food, labour, raw materials, transport, and industrial output, while resistance and reprisals deepened local devastation. Britain suffered aerial bombardment and later rocket attacks; German cities were devastated by Allied bombing and the final invasion; and transport systems, housing, farms, factories, ports, and power networks were damaged across much of the continent. In Asia, Japanese occupation, bombing, famine, refugee movements, and the reconquest of occupied territories shattered cities and rural economies. Hiroshima and Nagasaki added the new problem of atomic destruction and radiation to the older burdens of modern war.
