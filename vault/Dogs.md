## Introduction

The dog, Canis lupus familiaris, is a domestic mammal in the family Canidae and a subspecies of the gray wolf. For more than 12,000 years, dogs have lived with humans as hunters, guards, herders, beasts of burden, companions, objects of reverence, and sometimes food animals.

Human selection turned the dog from a wolflike ancestor into more than 400 breeds. Dogs are valued differently around the world: in much of Europe and North America they are protected and admired as companions, while in other regions they may be used mainly as guards, workers, or food. In ancient [[Egypt]], dogs could be treated as sacred and buried with rulers.

## Ancestry and Domestication

*YellowLabradorLooking new*

The ancestry of dogs reaches back to ancient canids. About 60 million years ago, a small mammal called Miacis lived in parts of Asia and became ancestral to later canids. By 30 to 40 million years ago, Cynodictis had appeared, and the Eurasian branch Tomarctus later led toward wolves, dogs, and foxes.

The exact domestication of dogs remains debated. Some genetic evidence points to China, some to Europe, and some to a small gray wolf strain from what is now India. Other research suggests dogs and modern wolves are separate lineages from a common ancestor, and some African dogs may include jackal ancestry.

Domestication probably began when wild canids scavenged near human camps and became useful hunting partners. Humans then bred animals with stronger sight, smell, speed, strength, guarding instinct, or companionship traits. Ancient images from Europe, the Middle East, and North America show dogs hunting with people, guarding tombs, and resembling modern canines.

## Origin of breeds

Dog breeds developed as humans selected animals for specific tasks. Sight hounds were admired in the Middle East, while mastiff-like dogs in Europe protected homes and travellers. With agriculture, herding and guarding dogs became important for flocks.

Small companion breeds were developed for noble families, such as the Pekingese in China and the Chihuahua. Terriers were bred mainly in England to kill rodents in barns and stables. Pointing and retrieving breeds helped hunters locate and recover game. Many breeds are ancient, while others developed as recently as the 1800s.

## Physical traits and functions

Dogs vary greatly in size and appearance, from Great Danes to poodles, but all share the same basic canine anatomy. They have 78 chromosomes, or 39 pairs, and a normal adult rectal temperature of about 100 to 102.5 °F.

The skeleton normally has 319 bones, though docked or naturally short-tailed dogs have fewer. External differences in legs, skulls, ears, tails, and coats reflect breed selection for work, climate, and appearance.

## Teeth and digestion

Dogs have two sets of teeth. Puppies develop 28 deciduous teeth by six to eight weeks, and these are replaced by 42 adult teeth by six to seven months. Incisors nip, canines tear, and premolars and molars shear and crush food.

The digestive system reflects carnivore ancestry. Dogs usually gulp food rather than chew it thoroughly. Food passes through the esophagus to the stomach, while most digestion and absorption occur in the small intestine with help from the pancreas and liver. The liver produces bile, metabolizes nutrients, removes toxins, and makes clotting factors.

## Senses and coats

The dog’s sense of smell is far stronger than that of humans. Dogs track missing people, detect buried substances, identify drugs and explosives, and distinguish individual human scents after long periods. Breeds such as bloodhounds and German shepherds have especially strong scenting ability, while short-nosed breeds are less suited to tracking.

Dogs also have acute hearing, detecting sounds beyond the human range and filtering unwanted noise. Their taste is weaker than that of humans, and their colour vision is limited. Their eyesight is less important than smell, though movement detection helps many breeds.

Coats may be short, medium, or long, and may be fine, coarse, dense, or sparse. Colour and pattern are important in many breeds. Shedding is seasonal and influenced by daylight and climate. Grooming protects the skin, maintains the coat, and lets owners notice health problems.

## Reproduction and development

Sexual maturity usually occurs between six months and one year, though large breeds mature later than small breeds. Females normally cycle about twice a year, except the basenji, which usually cycles annually.

The female heat cycle lasts about 18 to 21 days and includes proestrus, estrus, diestrus, and anestrus. Estrus is the receptive fertile period. If no pregnancy occurs, diestrus lasts 60 to 90 days before the next resting period.

Puppies are born blind and deaf and depend completely on the dam. Their eyes and ear canals open around 10 to 14 days. Early development includes critical periods in which contact with littermates, the mother, people, and the wider environment shapes later social behaviour.

## Behaviour and communication

Dogs are social animals and usually prefer people or other dogs to isolation. They retain pack-related instincts from wolf ancestry, including territory marking, greeting rituals, dominance and submission, play, and care for young.

Vocal communication includes barking, growling, howling, yipping, and breed-specific sounds. Owners often learn to distinguish warning, fear, play, pain, and excitement. Hounds were bred to howl on game trails, northern breeds often howl rather than bark, and the basenji yodels instead of barking.

Breed background strongly affects behaviour. Hounds can be independent and scent-focused, bird dogs often seek human interaction, herding dogs are alert and task-oriented, guarding breeds protect territory, terriers are lively and confrontational, and Newfoundlands are known for lifesaving instincts.

## Dogs as pets, selection, and training

Keeping a dog as a pet is a major responsibility because the animal depends on the owner for care throughout life. A good match depends on lifestyle, breed traits, size, temperament, grooming needs, exercise, and health risks.

When choosing a puppy or adult dog, buyers should look for friendliness, curiosity, clear eyes, clean ears, firm gums, healthy skin, a clean coat, and good body condition. Purebred dogs offer predictable appearance and temperament but may carry inherited problems. Mixed-breed dogs also can have genetic issues, though these may be harder to predict.

Training works best when commands are clear, consistent, and enforceable. Dogs should be praised for correct behaviour, and punishment, if used, must be immediate and linked to the act. Breed history matters: a guard dog, retriever, hound, or herding dog will not learn in exactly the same way because each was bred for different instincts.

## Maintenance and ailments

Dog care includes balanced food, grooming, ear cleaning, nail trimming, brushing, vaccination, parasite control, and regular veterinary attention. Puppies receive a series of vaccines beginning around six weeks of age, and adult dogs need continued preventive care.

Fleas and ticks cause irritation and disease, while internal parasites are especially dangerous for puppies. Dogs can also suffer from cancer, respiratory disease, allergies, arthritis, heart disease, and breed-linked conditions.

Large breeds are at risk of gastric dilatation volvulus, a medical emergency in which the stomach twists and fills with gas. Hip dysplasia is another major problem, especially in large breeds, involving abnormal hip-joint development and inherited risk.

## Breed groups

Hounds are hunting dogs divided broadly into scent hounds and sight hounds. Beagles, basset hounds, foxhounds, coonhounds, bloodhounds, Afghan hounds, Salukis, borzois, greyhounds, dachshunds, and Rhodesian ridgebacks show the range of the group.

Terriers were bred to kill vermin, dig into burrows, and work around barns and stables. Many are small, wiry-coated, active, vocal, and bold. Larger terriers include the Airedale, Irish, Kerry blue, and soft-coated wheaten.

Working dogs were bred for guarding, guiding, protecting, pulling, hauling, and rescuing. The group includes Akitas, boxers, Doberman pinschers, Great Danes, mastiffs, Rottweilers, Newfoundlands, Saint Bernards, Siberian huskies, Samoyeds, and others.

Herding dogs are intelligent, agile, and responsive to signals. They move flocks by barking, circling, eye contact, nipping, hand signals, or whistles. Collies may show herding instinct even as puppies.

Toy breeds were developed as small companions, often for aristocratic households. Pekingese, Japanese Chin, English toy spaniels, toy poodles, miniature pinschers, Chinese cresteds, Shih tzus, and others show how affection, portability, and status shaped breeding.

The Non-Sporting group is a mixed category for breeds that do not fit neatly elsewhere, such as bulldogs, bichon frises, Dalmatians, poodles in some systems, and other diverse breeds.

## Wild canids

The domestic dog is closely related to the wolf. Wolves and dogs share play, dominance, submission, scent marking, maternal care, and the ability to mate. Wolves mature more slowly, cycle annually, and are more deeply shaped by pack life.

Coyotes are lighter, rangier, and highly adaptable canids of North and Central America. They often live in smaller groups than wolves and have survived long human efforts to eliminate them.

Foxes differ from dogs and wolves in having yellow eyes with elliptical pupils. They are usually small, monogamous, and not pack animals. Red foxes, gray foxes, Arctic foxes, fennecs, and large-eared African foxes show the group’s variety.

Jackals are fleet-footed hunters and scavengers native from southeastern Europe through southern Asia, India, and Africa. The golden, black-backed, side-striped, and Abyssinian jackals are the main varieties.

Other wild canids include the South American maned wolf, the Guiana bush dog, the Japanese raccoon dog, the Australian dingo, and the dhole of Asia. These species show how canids adapted to different climates, prey, social systems, and landscapes.

## Key Events & Developments

- more than 12,000 years ago: Dogs began living with humans as hunting companions and protectors.
- 7,000 to 9,000 years ago: Dogs became useful as herders and guardians when livestock were domesticated.
- Bronze Age: Images show dogs hunting with humans and guarding tombs.
- 1800s: Some modern breeds were developed or refined.
- 1950s and 1960s: Studies of dog behaviour clarified the role of instinct and early social contact.
