# Iran

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> Iran is a Southwest Asian country whose mountains, plateau, Persian language, Shiite Islam, and long state tradition have shaped a distinctive society. Its history spans ancient empires, Safavid consolidation, Qajar and Pahlavi modernization, the 1979 revolution, and the Islamic Republic's regional role.

## Introduction

Iran ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iran)) is a mountainous, arid, ethnically diverse country of southwestern Asia, historically known as Persia and long important because of its imperial heritage, strategic position, and petroleum resources. Much of the country is a central desert plateau enclosed by mountains, with most people living around the plateau's edges, in oases, or in cities such as Tehran, Esfahan, Shiraz, Mashhad, Tabriz, and Qom.

Iranian civilization ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_Iran)) reaches back to the Achaemenian period, beginning in 550 BC, and later absorbed influences from Hellenistic Seleucids, Parthians, Sasanids, Muslim Arabs, Turkic dynasties, Mongols, Timurids, Safavids, Qajars, Pahlavis, and the Islamic Republic. The Arab conquest of the 7th century brought Islam and Arabic influence, while later Persian literary and political revivals kept Iranian identity distinct.

## Land and Environment

![[attachments/iran_003.webp|420]]

*Mount Damavand in northern Iran*

Iran's boundaries touch Azerbaijan, Armenia, Turkmenistan, the Caspian Sea, Pakistan, Afghanistan, the Persian Gulf, the Gulf of Oman, Turkey, and Iraq. About one-third of its boundary is seacoast, and Iran controls several islands in the Persian Gulf.

Relief is dominated by mountain systems around a high interior basin. The Zagros Mountains stretch from the northwest toward the Persian Gulf and Baluchistan, forming rugged parallel ridges and fertile valleys. The Elburz Mountains run along the Caspian Sea, where Mount Damavand rises as Iran's highest peak. Volcanic belts, active faults, and frequent earthquakes make seismic hazard a major feature of the landscape.

The interior plateau contains the Kavir and Lut deserts, salt wastes, sand, gravel, and basins where water often evaporates before reaching the sea. Drainage generally flows down mountain slopes to the Caspian Sea, Persian Gulf, Gulf of Oman, or inland marshes. The Karun is Iran's only navigable major river, while the Sefid and Zayandeh rivers are important for irrigation and regional life.

[[Climate and Weather (Climate)|Climate]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Climate)) ranges from subtropical to subpolar, shaped by elevation, latitude, winds, seas, and mountains. The Caspian coast is humid and fertile, with forests, swamps, and rice paddies. Much of the rest of Iran is dry, with winter as the main rainy season, hot summers, and sharp regional contrasts between places such as Abadan, Tabriz, and the Sistan region.

Flora and fauna vary with water and altitude. Caspian forests contain oak, beech, elm, walnut, ash, and hornbeam, while Zagros scrub oak, steppe shrubs, desert plants, oases, reeds, and date palms occupy drier regions. Wildlife includes leopards, bears, hyenas, gazelles, mouflons, wild asses, cheetahs, reptiles, amphibians, Caspian sturgeon, Persian Gulf marine life, and protected species in sanctuaries and national parks.

## People, Language, and Religion

Iran's population is culturally diverse. Persian speakers form the predominant group, but the country also includes Azerbaijanis, Kurds, Lurs, Bakhtyari, Baloch, Qashqai, Turkmen, Arabs, Armenians, Assyrians, Jews, Brahuis, and others. Persians, Kurds, Lurs, and Bakhtyari are linked to Indo-European-speaking peoples, while Turkic-speaking groups arrived mainly from Central Asia beginning in the 11th century.

Languages come mainly from Indo-European, Turkic, and Afro-Asiatic families. Persian, or Farsi, is the official and dominant language of literature, journalism, administration, and science. Kurdish, Luri, Armenian, Azerbaijanian, Turkmen, Arabic, and other languages are also spoken. Arabic is especially important because of Islam and the Quran, and Persian contains many Arabic words and constructions.

Twelver Shiism is Iran's official state religion and the faith of most Iranians. Its central beliefs include devotion to the imams and expectation of the return of the hidden 12th imam, Muhammad al-Mahdi al-Hujjah. Since the 1979 revolution, the Shiite clergy have been the dominant political and social force.

Shiite religious authority is scholarly rather than priestly. Mullahs, hojatoleslams, mujtahids, ayatollahs, grand ayatollahs, and marja-e taqlid figures gain status through learning, recognition, and popular following rather than formal ordination. Sayyids, regarded as descendants of the family of [[Muhammad and the Religion of Islam (Muhammad)|Muhammad]], have traditionally held special prestige.

Religious minorities include Sunni Muslims, Christians, Jews, Zoroastrians, and Bahais. Christians are mainly Armenians and Assyrians. Zoroastrians are concentrated in Yazd, Kerman, and Tehran. Christians, Jews, and Zoroastrians are recognized minorities under the 1979 constitution, while Bahais have faced especially severe persecution, and many Jews and Bahais emigrated after the revolution.

## Settlement and Society

Settlement patterns reflect topography and water. Deserts, salt flats, mountain gorges, and seasonal rivers encouraged insularity, tribalism, and concentration around the plateau's edge and oases. Traditional dwellings include Turkmen yurts, Bakhtyari black tents, and Baloch huts, while modern cities retain old cores of narrow streets alongside broad avenues, ring roads, and new districts.

Demographic change accelerated in the late 20th century. Iran's birth boom after the revolution later slowed, while life expectancy and literacy rose. Rural-to-urban migration increased from the 1960s, and the revolution encouraged the emigration of educated, secularized Iranians and religious minorities. Iran also absorbed many refugees from Afghanistan and Iraq.

[[Education]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Education)) is compulsory for children and highly valued. The University of Tehran was founded in 1934, and later universities, technical schools, and teachers' colleges expanded higher education. After the revolution, schools and universities were required to promote Islamic values, many secular professors left or were dismissed, and women's university admission rose dramatically by the end of the 20th century.

Women in Iran stand at the centre of social change. Pahlavi modernization, revolutionary Islamization, university expansion, family law, dress rules, employment, and political activism all changed women's public roles. The Islamic Republic restricted some rights and enforced religious norms, yet female education, professional ambition, urban life, and reform movements created continuing pressure for participation and legal change. Gender therefore links household life to the state, the mosque, the university, and the street.

Health, housing, and welfare improved in some areas after World War II, with smallpox eradicated and malaria reduced, but urban growth produced housing shortages, harsh conditions for the urban poor, and pressure on services. Major cities have piped water and modern facilities, while smaller towns and villages rely more on wells, qanats, springs, rivers, and simpler heating.

## Economy

Iran's economy is shaped by oil, state control, sanctions, demographic pressure, and efforts at self-sufficiency. After the revolution, goals included economic independence, full employment, and a comfortable standard of living, but isolation from international markets, U.S. sanctions, limited foreign investment, inflation, unemployment, and dependence on petroleum exports created persistent obstacles.

Agriculture ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agriculture)) uses only part of Iran's arable land because of poor soil and water scarcity. Less than one-third of cultivated land is irrigated, and many farms are too small to be efficient. Wheat, rice, fruits, nuts, livestock, poultry, and dairy are important, but agricultural decline and rural hardship helped drive migration to cities and made Iran a food importer by the late 20th century.

Petroleum and natural gas are Iran's most important resources. The National Iranian Oil Company produces oil for export and domestic use, with Khark Island as a major terminal. Refineries at Abadan, Esfahan, Shiraz, Lavan Island, Tehran, and Tabriz supply fuels. Iran also has vast natural gas reserves, including the South Pars field, and major trunklines serving large cities and industrial areas.

Energy and nuclear development became internationally contentious. Iran established the Atomic Energy Organization in 1973, suspended the shah's nuclear program after the revolution, and later completed a Bushehr reactor with Russian help. Revelations in 2002 about uranium enrichment raised suspicions about weapons ambitions, though Iran maintained peaceful intentions, and international sanctions followed from 2006.

Manufacturing and trade include automobiles, appliances, telecommunications equipment, machinery, steel, paper, rubber, textiles, pharmaceuticals, food products, carpets, and traditional crafts. Petroleum exports generally create a favourable trade balance, while imports include food, machinery, transport equipment, and basic manufactures. Germany, Japan, and the United Kingdom were important trading partners in the period described.

Finance and labour changed after 1979, when private banks and insurance companies were nationalized and Islamic banking practices replaced interest with service charges or commissions. Labour unions are weak or state-controlled, strikes are often suppressed, and employment is divided among agriculture, manufacturing, construction, services, transportation, communications, and finance.

## Government, Law, and Security

The Islamic Republic is rooted in the 1979 constitution and the doctrine of velayat-e faqih, or guardianship of the jurist. The rahbar, or leader, holds sweeping authority, while elections, the Majles, presidency, judiciary, Council of Guardians, and other institutions operate within religious oversight.

The judiciary applies Shariah-based law. After the revolution, monarchical law codes were revised, Islamic penal provisions were introduced, and punishments such as qisas and corporal penalties entered the legal system. A special court was created to try clergy accused of crimes.

Political life has been tightly controlled. The Islamic Republic Party was dissolved in 1987, while opposition parties such as the Tudeh Party and Mojahedin-e Khalq were outlawed or repressed. Elections occur, but candidate approval by the Council of Guardians and clerical institutions has limited political competition, especially after conservative resurgence in the early 21st century.

Security forces include the regular army, Revolutionary Guards, police, and rural gendarmerie structures. The monarchy's large armed forces collapsed during the revolution, but the military was rebuilt during the Iran-Iraq War. The Revolutionary Guards became the most politically reliable and ideologically committed force, central to external conflict, internal security, and revolutionary defense.

## Culture and Arts

Iranian culture combines pre-Islamic, Islamic, Persian, regional, and modern elements. Noruz, the Persian New Year, remains a major festival, while Shiite holidays, the birthday of the 12th imam, food traditions, bazaars, and family gatherings shape social life. Rice, lamb, vegetables, yogurt, saffron, lime, mint, turmeric, rosewater, pomegranates, and walnuts are common features of Persian cuisine.

Crafts include carpets, cloth, metalwork, turquoise work, pearls, wood inlay, carved wood, ceramics, tiles, mosaics, pottery, stonework, and felt. Carpet centres such as Kashan, Kerman, Khorasan, Esfahan, Shiraz, Tabriz, and Qom are known for distinctive designs and quality, and carpets remain important to rural incomes and exports.

Architecture ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Architecture)) has deep pre-Islamic and Islamic roots. Chogha Zanbil, Persepolis, Armenian monastic ensembles, Seljuq, Il-Khanid, and Safavid mosques, madrasahs, shrines, and palaces all contributed to Iranian architecture. Esfahan became especially celebrated under the Safavids, while Pahlavi-era architecture combined Western imitation with efforts to revive indigenous forms, including the Azadi Tower.

[[The Art of Literature (Literature)|Literature]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Literature)) is one of Iran's greatest cultural achievements. Persian literature emerged in its current form in the 9th century and includes masters such as Ferdowsi, Nezami, Hafez, Jami, and Rumi. Modern writers such as Sadeq Hedayat, Jalal Al-e Ahmad, Sadeq-e Chubak, Ahmad Shamlu, and Forough Farrokhzad used literature for introspection, dissent, and personal protest.

Cinema became a major art of social commentary. After the revolution, ideological filmmaking gave way to more varied works about war, childhood, nature, family, gender, and social tension. Directors such as Bahram Bayzai, Abbas Kiarostami, Mohsen Makhmalbaf, Dariyush Mehrjui, Rakhshan Bani Etemad, and Tahmineh Milani gained international recognition.

## History to the Safavids

![[attachments/iran_004.webp|420]]

*Ruins of Persepolis in Iran*

The Islamic conquest of the 7th century broke Sasanian power and brought Iran into the expanding Muslim world. Under the Abbasids, Baghdad absorbed Persian administrative, literary, and intellectual influences, while the shuubiyyah movement celebrated non-Arab Muslim peoples and helped prepare a Persian cultural revival.

Iranian dynasties reappeared under the Saffarids, Samanids, Ghaznavids, Buyids, Seljuqs, Khwarezm-Shahs, Il-Khans, Timurids, and Turkmen. Yaqub ibn Layth of the Saffarids promoted Persian verse and military autonomy. The Samanids encouraged Persian literature and Sunni legitimacy. The Buyids captured Baghdad and made the Abbasid caliph politically dependent while preserving the caliphate's symbolic role.

The Seljuqs restored Sunni political order under Turkic sultans and theorists such as al-Mawardi and al-Ghazali. The Ismaili movement under Hasan-e Sabbah built mountain strongholds such as Alamut and used assassination to challenge Seljuq authority. Mongol invasions destroyed old centers, ended the Abbasid caliphate in Baghdad in 1258, and established the Il-Khanid regime in Iran.

Timur devastated much of Iran in the late 14th century, and Timurid rule remained unstable. Yet Persian art, literature, and court culture continued under regional dynasties. Shiraz, Herat, Tabriz, and other cities helped maintain cultural life despite repeated political fragmentation.

## Safavid, Afsharid, Zand, and Qajar Iran

![[attachments/iran_002.webp|420]]

*Iranian Tiles 1*

The Safavids transformed Iran after Shah Ismail I took power in 1501. They made Twelver Shiism central to state identity, relied first on Kizilbash tribal support, and battled the Sunni Ottoman Empire. Defeat at Chaldiran slowed expansion, but the dynasty consolidated a distinct Iranian Shiite monarchy.

Shah Abbas I strengthened Safavid Iran by moving the capital to Esfahan, promoting trade, developing the silk monopoly, encouraging carpets, relocating Armenian merchants to New Jolfa, using English help against the Portuguese at Hormuz in 1622, replacing unreliable tribal forces with slave soldiers, and building a powerful court and bureaucracy.

Nadir Shah rose after Safavid decline and was proclaimed ruler in 1736. His Indian campaign of 1738-1739 captured Delhi and enormous treasure, temporarily solving fiscal problems. He tried to moderate Persian-Ottoman religious hostility and build a navy, but his later cruelty led to his murder in 1747. The Zand dynasty followed in southern Iran before the Qajars unified the country.

The Qajar dynasty ruled from 1796 to 1925. It faced British and Russian rivalry, economic dependence, territorial losses in the Caucasus through the treaties of Golestan in 1813 and Turkmanchay in 1828, and a semicolonial position as Iran supplied raw materials and imported industrial goods. The 1907 Anglo-Russian Convention divided Iran into spheres of influence.

## Pahlavi Rule and Revolution

Reza Khan rose from the Persian Cossack Brigade, staged a coup in 1921, built a loyal army, restored political order, and became Reza Shah Pahlavi after deposing Ahmad Shah in 1925. He modernized education, law, administration, and infrastructure, reduced clerical authority, banned veiling, raised the marriage age, expanded secular schools, founded the University of Tehran, and suppressed unions, parties, and the press.

[[World Wars (World War II)|World War II]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_War_II)) ended Reza Shah's rule after his ties to Germany and refusal to remove Germans gave Britain and the Soviet Union a pretext for invasion in 1941. He abdicated in favour of Mohammad Reza Shah Pahlavi, whose early reign occurred under occupation, inflation, political pluralism, parties, unions, clerical revival, and oil disputes.

The Pahlavi state later became more authoritarian. SAVAK symbolized surveillance and fear. The White Revolution, ratified in 1963, redistributed land, created literacy and health corps, reduced tribal autonomy, expanded women's rights, and used oil revenue for industrial development. Rapid modernization, corruption, repression, urban migration, inequality, and clerical opposition created explosive tensions.

The Iranian Revolution unfolded in 1978-1979 after protests over attacks on Ayatollah Ruhollah Khomeini, economic frustration, state violence, and cycles of Shiite martyrdom. The shah left Iran in January 1979, Khomeini returned from exile, the monarchy collapsed, and the Islamic Republic replaced it.

## Islamic Republic and Recent Issues

The Iran-Iraq War began in September 1980, when Saddam Hussein invaded Khuzestan, using a border dispute as pretext. Iran's weakened military resisted unexpectedly well, while internal bombings, repression, Revolutionary Guard power, and wartime mobilization consolidated the new regime. The war lasted until 1988 and devastated both countries.

Postwar reconstruction under Ali Akbar Hashemi Rafsanjani emphasized economic liberalization, privatization, and limited rapprochement, but conservatives resisted reform and foreign investment. Intellectuals such as Abdolkarim Soroush argued for reconciling Islam and democracy, while satellite dishes, the Internet, exile media, and human-rights campaigns widened public debate.

Mohammad Khatami won the presidency in 1997 and was reelected in 2001, reflecting reformist hopes. Conservative institutions, judiciary actions, candidate disqualifications, and repression limited change. Conservative victories in the 2004 Majles elections and the rise of Mahmoud Ahmadinejad in 2005 marked a return of hard-line politics.

Iran-U.S. relations worsened after the September 11, 2001 attacks despite Iranian condemnation of terrorism. George W. Bush labeled Iran part of the axis of evil in 2002, the U.S. invasion of Iraq increased regional instability, and Iran's nuclear program became a major international issue after hidden facilities were revealed in 2002.
