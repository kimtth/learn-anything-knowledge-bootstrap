# Pacific Islands

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> The Pacific Islands are the island societies of Melanesia, Micronesia, and Polynesia, linked by oceanic geography but varied in landforms, cultures, economies, and colonial histories. The account follows settlement, European exploration, missionaries, colonial labor systems, World War II, and decolonization.

## Introduction

![[attachments/pacific_islands_001.png|420]]

Pacific Islands are the island geographic region of the Pacific Ocean, conventionally divided into Melanesia, Micronesia, and Polynesia. The term excludes Australia, the Indonesian, Philippine, and Japanese archipelagoes, Japanese island arcs, the Aleutians, and isolated eastern Pacific islands such as Juan Fernandez. In broad usage, Oceania can include more of these areas, but the narrower Pacific Islands region covers more than 300,000 square miles of land and millions of square miles of ocean.

## Melanesia, Micronesia, and Polynesia

Melanesia, north and east of Australia and south of the Equator, includes New Guinea, the Bismarck Archipelago, Solomon Islands, Vanuatu, New Caledonia, and Fiji. Micronesia, north of the Equator and east of the Philippines, includes Palau, Guam, the Northern Marianas, the Federated States of Micronesia, Nauru, the Marshall Islands, and Kiribati. Polynesia lies largely within the triangle formed by Hawaii, New Zealand, and Easter Island.

## Physical Geography

![[attachments/pacific_islands_004.webp|420]]

*Pacific island atoll*

The main islands span the Equator obliquely and are divided by the Andesite Line. Continental islands southwest of the line are generally larger, higher, faulted, folded, mineral-rich, and agriculturally fertile. Oceanic islands farther east are mainly basaltic volcanic islands or coral islands. New Guinea and New Zealand make up about nine-tenths of the region's land area.

## Economy and Transport

Many Pacific Island economies depend on tourism ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tourism)), remittances, aid, fishing, minerals, copra, cocoa, coffee, tea, spices, and imported fuel. Imports commonly exceed exports, and external trade is mostly with Japan, France, the United States, Australia, and New Zealand rather than within the region. Air transport and interisland shipping are essential, while extensive roads are limited to larger islands. Tourism is especially developed in French Polynesia, Guam, Hawaii, Fiji, and New Zealand.

## Early Settlement

![[attachments/pacific_islands_003.webp|420]]

*Polynesian navigation canoe*

The prehistory of the Pacific Islands extends back at least 33,000 years through archaeological remains in the Bismarck Archipelago, and migration may have begun more than 40,000 years ago. By the 2nd millennium CE, settlers had reached every habitable island. Written historical documents are mostly European and must be balanced with anthropology and oral traditions, which preserve island perspectives imperfectly but indispensably.

## European Exploration

Ferdinand Magellan's voyage of 1519-1522 opened European awareness of the Pacific. Spanish explorers such as Alvaro de Mendana de Neira, Pedro Fernandez de Quiros, and Luis Vaez de Torres searched for lands, riches, and Christian converts. Dutch navigators Jakob Le Maire and Abel Janszoon Tasman later explored Tonga, New Zealand, Tasmania, Fiji, and New Britain, though their commercial hopes were limited.

## Cook and Enlightenment Exploration

James Cook completed much of the main European exploration of Oceania. His first voyage observed the transit of Venus at Tahiti and charted New Zealand and eastern Australia. His second disproved the great southern continent while charting many islands. His third explored the North Pacific and reached Hawaii, where he was killed in 1779. Cook's exact mapping and the account of Joseph Banks influenced later settlement and colonial expansion.

## Itinerants and Missionaries

After 1788, Oceania supplied the Australian settlement with pigs, sandalwood, pearl shell, beche-de-mer, seals, and other goods. Castaways, beachcombers, and traders altered island politics through firearms and alliances. Christian missions began major social transformations: the London Missionary Society reached Tahiti in 1797, converted Pomare II, and later spread through Polynesian teachers, while Methodists and others expanded mission work.

## Colonial Rule and Labour

European powers gradually imposed colonial rule, often first through consuls, traders, missionaries, and naval enforcement. France annexed Tahiti; Germany and the United States divided Samoa in 1899; Britain accepted Fiji in 1874 and regulated British nationals through the Western Pacific Order in Council of 1877. Labour recruiting, especially for Queensland plantations, caused violence and missionary protest, particularly in the New Hebrides and Solomons.

## World War II and Decolonization

The Pacific Islands were deeply affected by the Great Depression, world commodity markets, Japanese expansion, and World War II. Japan controlled much of northern Oceania and advanced into New Guinea and the Solomons. After the war, United Nations trusteeship replaced mandates, and colonial powers increasingly accepted independence or self-government as goals. Western Samoa gained stronger local authority in 1947, American Samoa shifted to civilian rule in 1951, and French territories received greater autonomy in 1956.

## Key Events & Developments

- 33,000 years ago: Archaeological evidence appears in the Bismarck Archipelago.
- 1519-1522: Magellan's expedition crossed the Pacific.
- 1768-1779: James Cook's voyages mapped much of Oceania.
- 1797: The London Missionary Society began work in Tahiti.
- 1874: Britain accepted control of Fiji.
- 1899: Samoa was divided between Germany and the United States.
- 1947: Western Samoa's Legislative Council gained a Samoan majority.

## Key People

- Ferdinand Magellan ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ferdinand_Magellan)): Led the first European expedition to cross the Pacific.
- Luis Vaez de Torres ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Luis_Vaez_de_Torres)): Charted southeastern New Guinea and the strait later named for him.
- Abel Janszoon Tasman ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abel_Janszoon_Tasman)): Explored New Zealand, Tasmania, Tonga, Fiji, and New Britain.
- James Cook ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Cook)): Produced highly accurate Pacific exploration and mapping.
- Joseph Banks ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph_Banks)): Naturalist whose accounts influenced Australian settlement.
- Pomare II ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pomare_II)): Tahitian chief whose conversion aided missionary expansion.

## Interesting Facts

- New Guinea and New Zealand account for about nine-tenths of Pacific Island land.
- The Andesite Line separates many western continental islands from central and eastern oceanic islands.
- Tourism and remittances often only partly offset trade deficits.
- European records are important but often distort island societies they only partly understood.
