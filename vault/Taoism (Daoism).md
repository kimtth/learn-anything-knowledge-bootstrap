# Taoism (Daoism)

## Introduction

![[attachments/taoism_001.webp|420]]

*Portrait painting of Laozi*

Daoism ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taoism)), also spelled Taoism, is an indigenous Chinese religio-philosophical tradition that has shaped Chinese life for more than 2,000 years. It emphasizes accepting, yielding, joyful, carefree, spontaneous, and natural ways of living, balancing the moral duty and social purpose associated with Confucianism.

Daoism includes the ideas of the Daodejing, Zhuangzi, Liezi, and related writings; the religious worship of the Dao; and communities that identify as Daoist. It has influenced Chinese culture far beyond formal religious institutions and spread through Chinese influence to Vietnam, Korea, Japan, and other Asian societies.

Older scholarship separated Daoist philosophy ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taoist_philosophy)) from later Daoist religion, but this division is now seen as too simple. Philosophical texts, ritual worship, longevity practices, alchemy, meditation, liturgy, and popular religion developed together in changing forms.

## Cosmology

![[attachments/taoism_003.png|420]]

*Daoist taijitu diagram*

The Dao is nameless, formless, and beyond ordinary categories. Names in ancient Chinese thought placed things within a ranked universe, but the Dao precedes and exceeds such ranking. Heaven, earth, and the Ten Thousand Things arise from it.

Daoist cosmology speaks of Nameless and Named, Nothing and Something, not as absolute opposites but as interdependent modes of the constant Dao. Nothing means indeterminacy and empty potential, not mere nonexistence. It is the fertile void that allows all forms and powers to appear.

The Daoist sage empties the mind of fixed notions and distracting passions so that the Dao can act without obstruction. The guiding quality is ziran, the self-so or what-is-so-of-itself. The Dao governs heaven, and Dao itself follows spontaneity.

## Wuwei and De

De is the power or efficacy of the Dao in human life, often translated as virtue. Daoist virtue differs from Confucian virtue because it does not consciously claim goodness. It is latent, quiet, and effective without display.

Wuwei means nonassertive action, not laziness or total inaction. It is action so perfectly in accord with things that it leaves no self-conscious trace. The Dao never acts by force, yet nothing is left undone.

The Daoist ideal is to return to pu, the Uncarved Block: original simplicity before society shapes people into instruments of rule, knowledge, and moral display. The sage rules best by not carving, not forcing, and not disrupting natural transformation.

Daoist social thought often praises primitivism, a small agrarian community living close to natural rhythms. Willful intervention, ambition, technical excess, and rigid government are seen as threats to harmony.

## Early History

Daoism in the Qin and Han periods developed partly around courts, where local traditions, ritual specialists, historians, wonder workers, and seekers of immortality competed for imperial favour. Emperors seeking power and longevity became patrons of esoteric arts.

Eastern coastal specialists told of islands inhabited by Immortals, inspiring expeditions and cults of astral divinities. Li Shaojun advised Emperor Wudi to sacrifice to the furnace, summon spirits, turn cinnabar into gold, eat and drink from golden vessels, and approach the Immortals of Penglai.

These traditions joined alchemy, immortality, sacrificial ritual, medicine, cosmic geography, and imperial cult. Bronze mirrors and stone inscriptions show airborne Immortals, cults of particular perfected beings, and worship of Laozi as a divine figure.

## Laozi as God

One major development was the elevation of Laozi from sage to god. Texts such as the Book of the Transformations of Laozi present him as cosmic, omnipresent, omnipotent, source of life, adviser of ancient rulers, and recurring saviour figure.

In late Han messianic settings, Laozi calls followers to recite the Daodejing, meditate on his divine attributes inside the body, and join him against the failing Han order. This shows how scripture, body meditation, politics, and salvation became linked.

## Celestial Masters and Maoshan

![[attachments/taoism_002.webp|420]]

*Taoist temple in China*

The Way of the Celestial Masters created organized Daoist community, ritual authority, registers, confession, healing, and moral discipline. It became a major foundation for later Daoist religion.

The Maoshan revelations of the 4th century formed a brilliant synthesis of Celestial Masters traditions and southeastern aristocratic Daoism. Yang Xi received visions from perfected beings of the Shangqing, or Supreme Purity, heaven between 364 and 370 CE.

These revelations gave the Xu family scriptures, hagiographies, meditation methods, and apocalyptic hope. They taught that demonic forces of the Six Heavens would cleanse the world through disaster, while the good would take refuge in luminous caverns under sacred mountains and prepare for a new Daoist order.

## Literature and Sects

Daoism produced extensive scriptural, hagiographic, ritual, alchemical, moral, and biographical literature. Later sects such as Quanzhen preserved biographies of masters, collections of sayings, missionary tracts, inscriptions, and accounts such as a patriarch's 1220-24 journey to Central Asia at the summons of Genghis Khan.

Daoist texts also developed liturgy ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liturgy)), ordination, talismans, sacred geography, revelations, ritual manuals, and techniques of meditation and longevity. Inscriptions provide evidence for Daoist temples, patrons, and local institutions across centuries.

## Alchemy and Inner Transformation

Daoist alchemy began with external laboratory practices using minerals, metals, elixirs, cinnabar, and gold in the search for immortality. Over time, especially by the Song period, interest shifted strongly toward internal alchemy, or neidan.

Neidan used the language of furnaces, elixirs, essences, and refinement to describe processes inside the body. Earlier external alchemy was increasingly reinterpreted as symbolic inner practice, and tangible chemical elixirs came to be viewed by some as mistaken or deceptive.

Funeral liturgy also transformed alchemical imagery. The liandu, or salvation by smelting, used written talismans to make an elixir of immortality for the deceased.

## Syncretism and Folk Daoism

Daoists built syntheses with [[Buddha and Buddhism (Buddhism)|Buddhism]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buddhism)) and Confucianism. The Pure and Luminous Way of Loyalty and Filial Obedience, inspired by revelations to He Zhengong in 1131, taught Confucian virtues as essential to salvation and appealed to conservative officials and intellectuals.

The Three Religions movement blended Daoist, Buddhist, and Confucian teachings. Popular moral tracts known as books on goodness circulated widely from the Ming dynasty onward.

Folk religion, or shenjiao, is the broad unorganized religious undercurrent of Chinese culture. Its gods come from many traditions and are involved in daily blessings, dangers, household rites, community festivals, and special occasions.

Daoist priests distinguished great jiao rites from smaller popular rites performed by ritual specialists such as fashi. Still, Daoism and folk religion deeply overlapped through exorcism, offerings, talismans, festivals, local gods, and practical ritual needs.

## Influence on Chinese Culture

Daoism contributed to [[Medicine|medicine]], health practices, pharmacology, longevity techniques, dietetics, meditation, and body cultivation. The Huangdineijing presents itself as teachings addressed to the Yellow Emperor, and later pharmacopoeias grew partly from mineral, plant, and animal experiments.

Daoist curiosity about nature reflected direct observation and experience rather than reliance on tradition alone. Zhuangzi praised skill learned through deep attunement, using artisans such as wheelwrights, butchers, sword makers, carvers, animal tamers, and musicians as images of effortless mastery.

Daoists admired intuitive skill but often distrusted technology and artificial interference. New discoveries in China were often framed as recoveries of what ancient masters really meant, reflecting a culture of rediscovery rather than open innovation.

Daoism shaped Chinese arts, poetry, landscape feeling, martial practices, healing, ritual theatre, festivals, popular morality, religious geography, and ideas of rulership. It also gave Chinese culture a language for spontaneity, emptiness, noncoercion, transformation, and harmony with the natural world.

## Key Events & Developments

- 206 BCE-220 CE: Han-period Daoism developed court cults, immortality practices, and early organized traditions.
- 165 CE: Inscriptions honoured Immortals and Laozi as a divine figure.
- 364-370 CE: Yang Xi received the Maoshan, or Shangqing, revelations.
- 1131: Revelations to He Zhengong inspired the Pure and Luminous Way.
- 1220-1224: A Quanzhen patriarch travelled to Central Asia at Genghis Khan's summons.
- 1368-1644: Ming-era books on goodness helped spread Three Religions moral teaching.

## Key People

- Laozi ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laozi)): Traditional sage of the Daodejing and later a major Daoist deity.
- Zhuangzi: Daoist thinker associated with spontaneity, transformation, and anti-formal wisdom.
- Li Shaojun ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Li_Shaojun)): Han wonder worker who linked alchemy, longevity, and imperial ritual.
- Emperor Wudi ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emperor_Wudi)): Han ruler who patronized immortality and esoteric practices.
- Ge Hong ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ge_Hong)): Daoist writer linked to alchemy, immortality, and religious synthesis.
- Yang Xi: Visionary who received the Maoshan revelations.
- Xu Mi ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Xu_Mi)) and Xu Hui ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Xu_Hui)): Aristocratic recipients of Shangqing revelations.
- [[Genghis Khan]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Genghis_Khan)): Summoned a Quanzhen Daoist patriarch to Central Asia.

## Interesting Facts

- Dao is called a byname because its true reality is beyond naming.
- Wuwei means action without forcing, not simple idleness.
- Pu, the Uncarved Block, represents original simplicity before social carving.
- Cinnabar linked alchemy, gold, longevity, and immortality cults.
- Daoist gods and folk gods often overlap in daily Chinese religious practice.
