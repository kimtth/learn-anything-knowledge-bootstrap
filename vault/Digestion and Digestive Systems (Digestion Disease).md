# Digestion and Digestive Systems (Digestion Disease)

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> Digestive diseases disrupt the organs and processes that break down food, absorb nutrients, and eliminate waste. Their causes include infection, inflammation, inherited disorders, and environmental factors.

## Introduction

Many diseases affect the human digestive tract, from the mouth to the pancreas and liver, showing how infection, inflammation, obstruction, cancer, and poor diet can damage each part of the system. Visible signs also appear in the mouth and oral cavity, where changes in the lips, mucosa, and tongue can reflect wider disease in the body. Regular prevention matters because many disorders are helped by a balanced diet, moderate alcohol use, and early medical care.

## Pharyngitis

Inflammation in the throat and nearby tissue is common, especially in young people, and it may come from streptococcal or staphylococcal bacteria or from viruses. Throat disease is also linked with congenital defects and with the salivary glands, since cleft lip, faulty tooth and jaw position, missing glands, or weak nerve control of the tongue and pharynx can make chewing and swallowing less effective. When saliva is reduced, speech becomes dry and thick, and the mouth loses an important natural protection.

## Esophagus

The esophagus is vulnerable because it only transports food and liquid; it does not change them chemically, and it must cope with material that may be rough, acidic, or harmful. Disease here includes injury from reflux, caustic substances, foreign bodies, infection, and degenerative disorders, so problems often involve both the lining and the muscle wall. In practice, esophageal disease often becomes a chain of linked troubles rather than a single isolated event, and swallowing difficulty is one of the earliest warnings.

## Strictures

When inflammation heals, scar tissue can contract and narrow the esophagus, creating a stricture that blocks the passage of food. The narrowing may develop slowly after repeated injury, and in severe cases it can nearly close the lumen. Diagnosis is usually straightforward because the tightened segment can be seen with imaging or an esophagoscope, and the pattern of fibrous scarring explains why the problem often worsens over time.

Dysphagia means difficulty swallowing, and it can arise from obstruction, muscle failure, nerve damage, or disease of the throat and esophagus. Older adults are especially likely to have a tumor or other structural cause, while strokes and other nervous system diseases can interrupt the swallowing reflex and disturb coordination in the pharynx and upper esophageal sphincter. A person may feel food sticking high in the throat even when the real blockage is lower down, so the symptom needs careful interpretation; a lump-in-the-throat feeling may also appear with reflux or stress.

## Pain

Pain from the esophagus can be confusing because it may be felt in the chest, back, or arms and may resemble heart pain. Spasm, transient blockage by a pill or other object, or irritation of the lining can produce episodes that radiate along the same nerve pathways as cardiac pain, which is why the symptom is often alarming. Esophageal spasm is not only uncomfortable but also easy to mistake for another disease.

## Motility

Disorders of movement in the esophagus often appear during stress or after rapid eating, when the muscle system must coordinate very precisely. Achalasia is the key example: the lower sphincter does not relax normally, food and liquid collect above it, and the esophagus slowly stretches while the nerve network in the wall fails to work properly. Chagas disease can destroy ganglion cells directly through parasites, and treatment aims to weaken the overly tight sphincter by dilation or surgery, even though this may weaken the anti-reflux barrier; the central issue is a failure of peristaltic control.

## Esophageal Diverticula

Pouches in the esophageal wall can form where muscle layers are weak, and they may trap food or saliva, especially near the neck. Some diverticula become large enough to press on the esophagus and cause true obstruction, while others are discovered after vomiting or after instrument use reveals a tear or rupture. Boerhaave syndrome and Mallory-Weiss lesions show how violent retching can produce sudden injury, pain, and bleeding, so these conditions combine outpouching, tearing, and emergency repair.

## Esophageal Cancer

Esophageal tumors may be benign or malignant, but the dangerous ones are usually squamous cancers or adenocarcinomas. They commonly present first as difficulty swallowing solid food and later produce pain, hoarseness, or near blockage of the esophageal channel. Risk is shaped by age, sex, smoking, alcohol, Barrett esophagus, and some blood or nutritional disorders, and early diagnosis is difficult because symptoms often appear late.

## Indigestion

Indigestion, or dyspepsia, is a broad pattern of discomfort rather than one single disease, and it can be triggered by stress, smoking, alcohol, coffee, certain foods, or poor eating habits. The symptom list is wide: abdominal discomfort, belching, nausea, vomiting, flatulence, constipation, diarrhea, heartburn, and loss of appetite can all appear together. Problems in stomach movement or nerve control may lead to dangerous retention and dehydration, so indigestion can range from mild annoyance to severe illness.

## Gastritis

Gastritis is inflammation of the stomach lining, often acute after contaminated food, alcohol abuse, or gastroenteritis, but sometimes chronic because of aspirin, NSAIDs, or H. pylori infection. Another form is gastric atrophy, in which the lining thins and the secreting cells are lost over many years; this can be linked to iron deficiency or pernicious anemia. The stomach is a tissue that can be irritated, eroded, and gradually weakened, so chronic damage and acid-related injury are both central themes.

## Stomach Cancer

Stomach cancer is common in many parts of the world and is more frequent in older people and in men. It often develops silently, first showing as loss of appetite, mild weight loss, anemia from slow blood loss, or vague pain, and it may later cause blockage or spread to the liver and lymph nodes. Other gastric tumors and polyps also exist, but the main warning is that early stomach cancer is usually subtle and easily overlooked.

## Duodenum

The duodenum is affected by disease in nearby organs, especially the pancreas and biliary tract, and it can be blocked or compressed by tumors, rings of pancreas, or parasites. Disease also extends into the small intestine more broadly, where motility problems can cause distension, cramping pain, and obstruction, and where traveler’s diarrhea, intestinal infection, and irritable bowel symptoms may appear after contaminated food or stressful travel. Together these disorders show that the upper intestine is vulnerable both to structural disease and to rapid changes in movement and absorption.

## Malabsorption

Malabsorption occurs when the small intestine cannot move broken-down nutrients into the body effectively, whether because enzymes are missing, the lining is damaged, motility is disturbed, bacteria overgrow, or bile and lymph pathways are blocked. Malabsorption can also stem from congenital malformations such as Meckel diverticulum, which can contain stomach-type tissue and bleed or ulcerate, as well as from multiple diverticula that may cause bacterial overgrowth and nutrient loss. Celiac disease and tropical sprue show how immune reaction or chronic tropical illness can destroy the intestinal surface and create severe malabsorptive diarrhea, making this one of the clearest examples of nutrition failure caused by bowel disease.

## Roundworms

Roundworms such as Ascaris lumbricoides can cause obstruction when they become numerous, and their migration through the body may bring fever, lung inflammation, bile-duct inflammation, and pancreatitis. They also interfere with the absorption of fat and protein, which can produce diarrhea and weakness. Treatment depends on anthelmintic drugs, but severe blockage may still require surgery, so the disease combines infection, migration, and mechanical obstruction.

## Hookworm

Hookworms enter through the skin, usually the feet, then travel through the liver and lungs before attaching to the small intestine. They feed on the host and can cause marked chronic iron-deficiency anemia, which may be corrected with iron replacement and worm-killing drugs. This is a classic example of a parasite that harms by slowly draining nutrients, so blood loss and malnutrition are the main clinical effects.

## Pinworms

Pinworms live mainly in the cecum, and the adult female migrates at night to the anus to lay eggs on the skin around it. The result is anal itching, household spread through the fecal-oral route, and a disease that can affect entire families. Pinworm disease is usually not dramatic, yet the itching and easy transmission make it persistent and annoying.

## Tapeworms

Tapeworms are acquired most often from undercooked beef, pork, or fish, although other species may also harm humans. The worms may cause little trouble until they become large enough to obstruct the intestine, but fish tapeworms can also consume vitamin B12 and produce severe anemia. Their life cycle depends on animal hosts and contaminated food, so the disease involves food preparation, parasite maturity, and nutrient theft.

## Appendicitis

Appendicitis is inflammation of the appendix, usually from infection or blockage, and it is a common cause of abdominal pain in younger people. Antibiotics and changing disease patterns can leave more late abscesses, and parasitic worms may play a part in some cases. Chronic inflammations of the small intestine, including tuberculosis and Crohn disease, are also discussed here because they produce vague fever, loose stools, weight loss, cramping pain, and a need for biopsy, drugs, or surgery; these are all examples of long-lasting inflammation that narrows and injures the bowel.

## Constipation

Constipation is slow passage of waste through the large intestine, and it can result from habits, poor muscle support, brain or metabolic disease, or drugs. A related disorder is megacolon: congenital Hirschsprung disease removes ganglion cells from a distal segment so peristalsis cannot occur, while acquired megacolon may develop when a child repeatedly withholds stool and damages the bowel’s nerve supply. Both forms show that constipation can become a structural and neurologic disorder, not just a temporary inconvenience.

## Diarrhea

Diarrhea is the rapid passage of loose stool through the large intestine, and it may follow inflammation, cancer, vascular disease, bacterial or viral infection, or parasites. Lactase deficiency is a common example, because undigested lactose draws water into the bowel and causes cramping and watery stools after milk is taken. Intestinal gas is another problem, since swallowed air and digestive by-products can collect in the colon and cause pain that is mistaken for disease in nearby organs.

## Colonic Diverticula

Colonic diverticula are small pouches in the wall of the large intestine, most often associated with aging, high pressure in the colon, and a low-fiber diet. They may bleed when hard stools rub against exposed vessels, or they may become inflamed when debris and bacteria are trapped in the pouch. Diverticulosis is a common modern disorder in which pressure, diet, and wall weakness combine to create pouches, bleeding, and diverticulitis.

## Abscesses

Abscesses around the anus are common complications of bowel disease and skin contamination, especially where hygiene is poor or diabetes makes infection easier. Bacterial infections of the colon and nearby bowel also occur, as Shigella, Salmonella, cytomegalic virus, and amoebic infection can inflame the lining, cause bleeding, and produce dangerous fluid loss. Entamoeba histolytica is especially important because it can undermine the mucosa, create ulcers, and spread to the liver, so infection here is both local and systemic.

## Colitis

Ulcerative colitis is the best-known chronic inflammation of the colon in the Western world, and it can range from mild mucus and blood in the stool to severe tissue destruction, toxemia, and perforation. Antibiotic-associated colitis also appears when the normal flora are disrupted and harmful bacteria take over, while Crohn disease causes deeper, patchier injury in the digestive tract and is often harder to distinguish by symptoms alone. Colitis is not one disease but a family of inflammatory patterns with different depths, causes, and risks.

## Colon Cancer

Colon cancer is more common than stomach cancer in the Western world, and it is linked to age, high-fat low-fiber diets, family history, polyps, and ulcerative colitis. It may cause blood in the stool, obstruction, pain, or even perforation, but it can also remain hidden until it has spread to other organs. Surgery, colostomy, and reconstruction are practical treatments, and screening and early detection matter because the disease can advance before symptoms become obvious.

## Polyps

Polyps are benign growths on the inner wall of the colon, and in familial polyposis they may cover the bowel in huge numbers and almost inevitably lead to cancer if the colon is not removed. A villous adenoma is another dangerous form because it can recur and turn malignant. Anal disorders also appear, such as fissures, fistulas, and hemorrhoids, which may bleed, itch, or hurt and are often linked with chronic bowel disease or difficult defecation.

## Liver

The liver can be injured by viruses, drugs, pollutants, genetic disorders, and systemic disease, and damage may begin in the cells, the bile channels, or the blood vessels. Acute hepatitis may resolve, but persistent injury can destroy large areas of tissue and set the stage for cirrhosis or cancer. The liver is therefore an organ where inflammation, toxic injury, and failure of repair can all lead to long-term disease.

## Cirrhosis

Cirrhosis is the end result of many chronic liver injuries, in which scar tissue replaces normal structure and the liver becomes distorted and less effective. Alcohol abuse is a major cause in the Western world, but chronic viral hepatitis, autoimmune bile-duct disease, congenital disorders, and long-term obstruction of the biliary system can also produce it. Cirrhosis leads to later complications such as portal hypertension and cancer, making slow scarring the central mechanism.

## Ascites

Ascites is the buildup of fluid in the abdominal cavity, and in cirrhosis it results from portal hypertension, low blood albumin, and kidney retention of sodium and water. The fluid may become massive enough to affect breathing and appetite, so treatment begins with drainage, salt restriction, and diuretic drugs. If these measures fail, shunts may be used, which shows that ascites is both a symptom of liver failure and a treatment problem in its own right.

## Tumours

Liver tumors may be primary or metastatic, and many of the most serious ones arise in people who already have cirrhosis or chronic hepatitis. Benign tumors and cysts can cause pain, heaviness, or rupture, while abscesses may develop from infection spreading from the biliary tract, appendix, pelvic organs, or intestinal parasites. Tumor growth ties to structural damage and spread from elsewhere, so the liver is both a target and a hub for disease.

## Gallstones

Gallstones are the most common disease of the biliary tract, and they may be made mainly of pigment, cholesterol, or both. Pigment stones are associated with hemolysis and certain infections, while cholesterol stones are favored by obesity, diabetes, high-fat diets, pregnancy, female hormones, and poor bile flow. A chemical imbalance in bile can turn into crystal formation, pain, and obstruction.

## Jaundice

Jaundice is the yellowing of skin and eyes that appears when bilirubin in the blood rises too high, and it can come from overproduction, liver-cell damage, or blockage of bile flow. Jaundice has unconjugated, hepatocellular, and cholestatic forms, and each one changes urine color, stool color, and the risk of complications such as kernicterus in newborns. This makes jaundice a key sign that bilirubin handling has broken down at one of several points.

## Pancreatitis

Pancreatitis is inflammation of the pancreas, and it may be acute, repeated, or chronic. Alcohol and gallstones are major causes, but direct injury, drugs, infection, heredity, high blood fats, and duct defects can also trigger it. The gland may begin to digest itself when activated enzymes leak into the tissue, and the result can be severe abdominal and back pain, systemic complications, and dangerous self-destruction, so the core problem is enzyme activation gone wrong.

## Pancreatic Cancer

Pancreatic cancer usually starts in the duct system, and it has become more common, especially in older people and in those with smoking, high-fat diets, diabetes, or chronic pancreatitis. Pain in the upper abdomen, weight loss, obstructive jaundice, and spread to nearby tissue are common signs, while CT scan and biopsy help confirm the diagnosis. Treatment is limited and often palliative, which shows why early recognition is critical even though cure is difficult.

## Core Equations

Health and behavioural sciences often quantify risk, dose, change over time, and population variation:

$$C(t)=C_0e^{-kt}$$

This models concentration falling exponentially over time, often used for clearance, decay, or elimination processes.

$$z=\frac{x-\mu}{\sigma}$$

A z-score tells how many standard deviations a value lies above or below the mean.

$$\text{risk}=\frac{\text{cases}}{\text{population}}$$

This ratio turns the preceding idea into a measurable comparison, with the numerator as the quantity of interest and the denominator as the scale used for comparison.

## Key Events & Developments

- early medicine: Digestive illness is explained through diet, humors, and observation of symptoms.
- modern period: Gastroenterology and pathology make digestive disease a distinct medical field.
- contemporary medicine: Diet, infection control, and diagnostic testing shape treatment.
