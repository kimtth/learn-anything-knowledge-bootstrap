# Photosynthesis

## Introduction

![[attachments/photosynthesis_001.png|420]]

*Photosynthesis diagram*

Photosynthesis ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Photosynthesis)) is the process by which green plants and certain other organisms transform light energy into chemical energy. In green plants, light energy is captured and used to convert water, carbon dioxide, and minerals into oxygen and energy-rich organic compounds. It is essential for food, atmospheric oxygen, organic matter, and the fossil fuels that power industrial society.

If photosynthesis stopped, most food and organic matter would disappear and Earth's atmosphere would eventually lose most gaseous oxygen. Fossil fuels represent excess photosynthetic production accumulated over millions of years, but modern civilization is returning that carbon dioxide to the atmosphere in only a few centuries, affecting climate.

## Light and Dark Stages

![[attachments/photosynthesis_002.png|420]]

*Chloroplast diagram*

Photosynthesis involves many enzyme-catalyzed reactions summarized by simple equations. The light stage captures light energy and drives electron transfers that produce ATP and NADPH. The dark stage uses ATP and NADPH to reduce carbon dioxide into organic carbon compounds, a process called carbon fixation.

## Van Niel and Oxygen Source

Comparisons between green plants and photosynthetic sulfur bacteria clarified the mechanism. Cornelis van Niel proposed in the 1930s that oxygen released by green plants comes from water, not carbon dioxide. Experiments using oxygen isotope 18O confirmed by 1940 that photosynthetic oxygen gas derives from water.

## Products of Photosynthesis

Carbohydrates are the main direct products of photosynthesis, though plants usually store glucose as starch or combine it with fructose as sucrose. Photosynthesis also supports synthesis of amino acids, proteins, lipids, pigments, and many green-cell components. Minerals supply nitrogen, phosphorus, sulfur, and other elements needed for these compounds.

## Water and Minerals

Water is required directly in photosynthesis and indirectly through transpiration. Stomata admit carbon dioxide but lose water vapour; closing them conserves water while limiting carbon dioxide and raising leaf temperature, which can increase photorespiration. Essential minerals include nitrogen, sulfate, phosphate, iron, magnesium, calcium, potassium, manganese, copper, and chloride.

## Internal Regulation and Efficiency

Each plant species has regulatory mechanisms that adjust enzyme activity to environmental conditions and plant needs. Carbon dioxide enrichment may initially raise photosynthesis, but if the plant cannot use the extra sucrose, the rate falls. Photosynthetic energy efficiency compares light energy absorbed with chemical energy stored in oxygen and organic products.

## Lamellae, Lipids, and Pigments

Photosynthetic membranes, or lamellae, contain lipids, proteins, pigments, and coenzymes. Polar lipids form bilayers that insulate charges across membranes. Chlorophyll a and chlorophyll b are bound to proteins, with most chlorophyll-protein complexes harvesting light and transferring energy to special chlorophyll a molecules involved directly in energy conversion.

## Proteins, Cytochromes, and Quinones

Lamellar proteins include enzymes, cofactors, cytochromes, and electron carriers. Manganese helps split water and form oxygen; iron and copper proteins move electrons. Ferredoxin transfers electrons to nitrate, sulfate, and carbon dioxide reduction systems. Plastocyanin carries electrons through thylakoid spaces, while plastoquinones diffuse through membranes carrying electrons and hydrogen atoms.

## Light Reactions

When chlorophyll absorbs light, electrons rise to excited states. In photosynthesis, energy is conserved by transferring an electron from special chlorophyll a molecules, P680 or P700, to electron acceptors. If electron transfer is blocked, energy may be emitted as red fluorescence. Photosystems I and II each contain light-harvesting and core complexes, reaction centres, electron donors, electron acceptors, and chlorophyll-protein structures.

## ATP and Quantum Requirements

The light reactions generate ATP ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adenosine_triphosphate)) and NADPH for carbon reduction. Each light reaction requires roughly one photon per electron transferred, so oxygen formation through two light reactions requires at least eight quanta, with actual requirements nearer nine or ten because cyclic photophosphorylation also forms ATP.

## Calvin-Benson Cycle

The Calvin-Benson cycle ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Calvin_cycle)) reduces carbon dioxide through four stages: carboxylation, reduction, isomerization-condensation-dismutation, and phosphorylation. Rubisco adds carbon dioxide to RuBP, producing PGA. ATP and NADPH convert PGA to Gal3P. One Gal3P leaves as net product, while five regenerate RuBP through enzyme-controlled steps.

## Regulation and Products of Carbon Reduction

Calvin-Benson enzymes are turned off in the dark to avoid wasteful reversal of glycolysis-related steps. In light, enzyme activity changes with reduced ferredoxin, acids, phosphate, magnesium, and other chloroplast conditions. Gal3P is exported to the cytosol for sucrose synthesis, amino acid carbon skeletons, lipids, pigments, and other compounds, or stored as starch in chloroplasts.

## Photorespiration and C4 Plants

Photorespiration occurs under high light, heat, and water limitation when oxygen competes with carbon dioxide at Rubisco. Oxygenation of RuBP produces PGA and phosphoglycollate, wasting fixed carbon as carbon dioxide. C4 plants such as sugarcane and corn reduce photorespiration by separating functions between mesophyll and bundle-sheath cells and first fixing carbon dioxide into compounds derived from PEP.

## Key Events & Developments

- 1930s: Cornelis van Niel proposed that photosynthetic oxygen comes from water.
- 1940: Heavy-isotope experiments confirmed water as the oxygen source.
- Photosystems I and II: Light reactions were separated into interacting photochemical systems.
- Calvin-Benson cycle ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Calvin_cycle)): Carbon fixation was explained through cyclic enzymatic steps.
- C4 pathway: Some plants were shown to suppress photorespiration through specialized anatomy and biochemistry.

## Key People

- Cornelis van Niel: Explained the hydrogen-transfer logic of photosynthesis.
- Hans Lambers ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hans_Lambers)): Editor credited in the source treatment.
- Plant biochemists: Used isotopes, fluorescence, membrane studies, and enzyme analysis to map photosynthetic mechanisms.

## Interesting Facts

- Photosynthesis ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Photosynthesis)) supplies most atmospheric oxygen.
- Rubisco catalyzes both carbon fixation and the competing oxygenation reaction.
- Chlorophyll fluorescence appears when normal electron transfer is blocked.
- C4 crops include corn and sugarcane.

## Core Equations

Photosynthesis is commonly summarized by the net reaction:

$$\mathrm{6CO_2 + 6H_2O + light \rightarrow C_6H_{12}O_6 + 6O_2}$$

This net photosynthesis equation shows carbon dioxide and water converted into sugar and oxygen using light energy.

Light absorption is quantized by photon energy:

$$E=h\nu$$

This relation says that photon energy is proportional to frequency; higher-frequency radiation carries more energy per photon.
