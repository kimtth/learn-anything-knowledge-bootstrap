# Food Processing (Food Preservation)

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> Food preservation keeps food safe and edible after harvest or slaughter by controlling microbial, enzymatic, chemical, and physical spoilage. Ancient drying and fermentation now coexist with canning, pasteurization, freezing, irradiation, additives, and packaging that support food safety and distribution.

## Introduction

Food preservation ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Food_preservation)) is the set of methods used to keep food safe and suitable for eating after harvest or slaughter. that this practice is ancient, with early methods such as drying, refrigeration, and fermentation still important today. Modern preservation relies on canning, pasteurization, freezing, irradiation, and chemical additives, often supported by improved packaging.

The main problem addressed by preservation is spoilage ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Decomposition)), which occurs when food becomes unfit to eat because of microorganisms, insects, enzymes, or physical and chemical changes. Foods from plants and animals begin to deteriorate quickly after harvest or slaughter, so preserving them is essential for nutrition, safety, and long-distance distribution.

## Microbial Spoilage

Bacteria, yeasts, and molds are the chief causes of microbial spoilage and foodborne illness. Bacteria grow rapidly under favourable conditions, and their growth is affected by nutrients, moisture, pH, oxygen, and temperature. Water activity is a key measure for microbial growth, because most bacteria need relatively high water availability.

Fungi also play an important role. Yeasts and molds can grow in acidic conditions and may spoil fruits, nuts, and other foods. Controlling contamination requires attention to sources such as soil, air, sewage, feed, hides, plant surfaces, and processing equipment.

## Chemical and Enzymatic Changes

Spoilage is not only microbial. how enzymes naturally present in foods can break down tissues and cause off-flavours, texture changes, and nutrient loss. It also explains chemical deterioration such as autoxidation ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Autoxidation)), in which fats become rancid through free-radical reactions, and the Maillard reaction ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maillard_reaction)), which causes nonenzymatic browning in stored foods.

These reactions affect flavour, appearance, and nutritional quality. They are especially important in foods such as oils, nuts, snacks, and processed products.

## Preservation Methods

Several major preservation technologies are in wide use. Refrigeration slows spoilage by keeping foods below 4 C, while freezing preserves nutritional quality by reducing chemical change at subzero temperatures. The size of ice crystals affects texture, which is why quick freezing is valuable in many foods.

Canning is described as a major heat-based method. It was developed through the work of Nicolas Appert, Peter Durand, and later industrial improvements. The process relies on sterilization to destroy harmful microbes such as Clostridium botulinum, though acidity influences the required heat treatment.

## Pasteurization and Blanching

Pasteurization uses relatively mild heat to destroy pathogens, inactivate enzymes, and reduce spoilage organisms. This method causes less damage to flavour and nutrients than full sterilization. It gives examples for fruit juices and milk, including high-temperature short-time treatments.

Blanching is mainly used before freezing, drying, or canning. It cleans food, reduces microbial load, removes trapped gases, and inactivates enzymes that lead to deterioration during storage.

## Dehydration, Irradiation, and Packaging

Drying or dehydration removes water and thereby limits microbial growth and enzyme activity. that dried foods can keep well, but quality depends on drying conditions because excessive heat may reduce nutritional value. It also points out that packaging is essential because it controls moisture, oxygen, and light exposure.

Irradiation is another modern method. that controlled doses of ionizing radiation can reduce spoilage and kill harmful organisms, while preserving many food qualities. It also notes that the radiation levels used are too low to induce radioactivity in food.

## Storage and Quality

Proper storage is essential to preserve quality after processing. Temperature control, humidity, and atmospheric gases all affect shelf life. that foods with high water content need careful humidity control, while fruits and vegetables may benefit from controlled atmosphere storage. It also remarks that poor storage can reduce vitamin content and worsen colour, flavour, and texture.

## Key Events & Developments

- Preservation methods date back to prehistoric times.
- Drying, refrigeration, and fermentation are among the oldest techniques.
- Canning became an industry in the early 19th century.
- Quick freezing developed in the 20th century.
- Irradiation and modern packaging expanded preservation options in the modern era.

## Interesting Facts
