# Indian Ocean

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> The Indian Ocean is the smallest and youngest of the three major oceans, extending between Africa, Asia, Australia, and Antarctica. Its complex boundaries, depths, circulation, and connection to surrounding lands make it a major physical and historical setting for the regions on its shores.

## Introduction

The Indian Ocean ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_Ocean)) is the smallest, youngest, and most physically complex of the three major oceans. It covers about one-fifth of ocean area, stretches more than 6,200 miles between southern Africa and Australia, and spans about 28,360,000 square miles without marginal seas. Its average depth is 12,990 feet, and its deepest point is the Sunda Deep ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sunda_Trench)), at 24,442 feet.

Boundaries are clearer in some directions than others. The ocean is bordered by Iran, Pakistan, India, and Bangladesh to the north; the Malay Peninsula, Sunda Islands, and Australia to the east; Antarctica to the south; and Africa and the Arabian Peninsula to the west. The Atlantic boundary is commonly drawn from Cape Agulhas south along the 20 E meridian; Pacific and southern limits are more disputed, especially near Tasmania, Indonesia, and Antarctica.

## Origin and Basin Structure

![[attachments/indian_ocean_003.webp|420]]

*Indian Ocean in 1912*

Origin is tied to the breakup of Gondwana about 150 million years ago, India's northeastward movement beginning about 125 million years ago, its collision with Eurasia about 50 million years ago, and Australia's separation from Antarctica about 53 million years ago. By 36 million years ago, the Indian Ocean had roughly its present configuration, though most of its basin is less than 80 million years old.

Oceanic ridges form an inverted Y on the ocean floor. The Carlsberg Ridge begins in the Arabian Sea, continues into the Mid-Indian Ridge, and branches near Madagascar into the Southwest Indian and Southeast Indian ridges. The Ninetyeast Ridge ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ninety_East_Ridge)), discovered in 1962, is an aseismic ridge running along the 90 E meridian for about 2,800 miles, the longest and straightest ridge of its kind.

Basins and shelves reflect complicated ridge topography. Major basins include the Arabian, Somali, Mascarene, Madagascar, Mozambique, Agulhas, Crozet, Central Indian, Wharton, and South Australia basins. The continental shelf averages about 75 miles wide, reaching about 190 miles off Mumbai and northwestern Australia. Submarine canyons carved by the Ganges, Indus, and Zambezi carry sediment far beyond the shelf.

Trenches are few compared with other oceans. The Java Trench is narrow, volcanic, seismically active, and more than 2,800 miles long, continuing as the Sunda Trench past Sumatra and toward the Andaman and Nicobar Islands. A 2004 undersea earthquake of magnitude 9.1 along the Sumatra section generated devastating tsunamis across the ocean.

Bottom deposits are dominated by immense terrigenous sediment loads, especially from rivers of the Indian subcontinent. The Ganges-Brahmaputra cone has sediments more than 7 miles thick and extends southward across the Bay of Bengal. Brown and red clays dominate much deep sea, while calcareous, siliceous, and diatomaceous oozes occur in highly productive equatorial and Antarctic convergence zones.

## Coasts and Islands

Coasts include estuaries, deltas, salt marshes, mangrove swamps, cliffs, coral reefs, barrier islands, lagoons, beaches, and dunes. The Hugli estuary near Kolkata, the Indus delta, and the Ganges delta are major coastal systems. The Sundarbans, in the lower Ganges delta, contain the world's largest mangrove forests.

Islands are relatively few. [[Madagascar]], the world's fourth largest island, along with Sri Lanka, Socotra, Seychelles, and the Maldives, represents continental fragments. Volcanic islands and island groups include Christmas, Cocos, Prince Edward, Saint-Paul, Amsterdam, Amirante, Andaman and Nicobar, Chagos, Crozet, Kerguelen, Sunda, Comoros, Lakshadweep, Mauritius, and Reunion. The Andamans and Sundas are island arc-trench systems.

## Climate and Hydrology

[[Climate and Weather (Climate)|Climate]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Climate)) is organized into four broad latitudinal zones. North of 10 S, the monsoon zone has semiannual wind reversal: the southwest monsoon from about May to October brings wet conditions to South Asia, while the northeast monsoon from November to April affects southern Indonesia and northern Australia. The southwest monsoon is regular but highly variable in onset and intensity.

Southern zones include the trade-winds belt between 10 and 30 S, the subtropical and temperate belt between 30 and 45 S, and the subantarctic-Antarctic belt from 45 S to Antarctica. Southeasterly trades, westerlies, cyclones, rainfall gradients, cold Antarctic air, and snow in the far south create strong contrasts across the ocean.

Surface circulation is wind-driven and uniquely shaped by monsoon reversal. In the north, gyres in the Arabian Sea and Bay of Bengal change direction seasonally. During the southwest monsoon, the North Equatorial Current reverses into the strong east-flowing Monsoon Current, while the Somali Current strengthens along East Africa. South of about 10 S, a steadier subtropical gyre includes the South Equatorial, Mozambique, Agulhas, South Indian, and West Australian currents.

Deep circulation is sluggish and layered. High-salinity intermediate water enters through the Arabian Sea from the Persian Gulf and Red Sea, spreading below fresher surface water. Antarctic Intermediate Water, North Atlantic Deep Water, and Antarctic Bottom Water occupy deeper layers. Unlike the Atlantic and Pacific, the Indian Ocean has no separate source of bottom water.

Upwelling is seasonal because of the monsoon. During the southwest monsoon it occurs off Somalia, Arabia, and south of Java; during the northeast monsoon it occurs along western India and in mid-ocean near 5 S. Upwelling lowers surface temperatures and brings nutrients upward, supporting biological productivity and fisheries.

Surface salinity ranges roughly from 32 to 37 parts per thousand. The Arabian Sea is saltier because evaporation is high, while the Bay of Bengal is fresher because of huge river runoff. Ice forms only in the far south during Antarctic winter, and icebergs may occur as far north as 40 S.

Tides include diurnal, semidiurnal, and mixed forms, though semidiurnal tides are most widespread. Open-ocean islands have small ranges, such as Port Louis at about 1.6 feet. The largest ranges occur in the Arabian Sea, including Bhavanagar in the Gulf of Khambat at about 38 feet.

## Resources and Economy

Mineral resources are led by oil, especially in the Persian Gulf, the world's largest oil-producing region. Offshore petroleum and natural gas exploration also occurs in the Arabian Sea, Bay of Bengal, northwestern Australia, the Andaman Sea, southern Africa, and southwestern Madagascar. Manganese nodules are abundant, but commercial extraction remains difficult; nearshore sands contain ilmenite, tin, monazite, zircon, and chromite.

Biological resources are rich in tropical and temperate waters. Coral reefs support invertebrates and reef fishes, while mangroves stabilize coasts and serve as nurseries. Marine life includes copepods, mollusks, jellyfish, squid, flying fish, anchovies, lantern fish, tuna, sailfish, sharks, sea turtles, dugongs, whales, dolphins, seals, albatrosses, frigate birds, and penguins in cooler zones.

Fisheries benefit from nutrient-rich upwelling in the northern Arabian Sea and along South Africa. Shrimp is the most important commercial coastal species, with India accounting for the largest catch. Croakers, snappers, skates, grunts, sardines, mackerel, anchovies, tuna, and billfish are also taken. Deep-sea resources are underused except for tuna, and exclusive economic zones let small states such as the Maldives sell fishing rights.

Trade and transport are dominated by raw materials, manufactured imports, petroleum, iron ore, coal, rubber, tea, seafood, and tourism. Dhows once carried goods across the western Indian Ocean using monsoon winds, but most cargo now moves by powered ships, container vessels, bulk carriers, and tankers. Major ports include Durban, Maputo, Djibouti, Aden, Karachi, Mumbai, Chennai, Kolkata, Colombo, Melbourne, Port Adelaide Enfield, and Port Hedland.

Tanker routes carry Persian Gulf oil across the northern Indian Ocean to the Strait of Malacca and southward around the Cape of Good Hope. The Suez route became less important for the largest tankers because of draft limits, and the biggest tankers bound for Japan must use the Lombok Strait rather than Malacca or Singapore.

## Human Impact and Exploration

Environmental damage began with colonial deforestation, cultivation, hunting, exotic species introductions, and guano mining. Modern threats include nearshore domestic and industrial waste and oil pollution from routine tanker operations or major spills. Risks are serious where oil routes overlap with productive plankton zones, such as East Africa, the Arabian Sea, and approaches to the Strait of Malacca.

Early exploration may have begun with Egyptian voyages to Punt as early as about 2300 BC, and perhaps earlier. Egyptians also built a Suez isthmus canal in the 12th dynasty that operated, with interruptions, until AD 775. Arab and Persian pilots used monsoon winds and left sailing instructions from the 9th to 15th centuries for routes from East Africa to China.

[[The History of European Overseas Exploration and Empires (European Exploration)|European exploration]] followed older Arab and Indian Ocean navigation. Afanasy Nikitin reached India in 1469 on an Indian trading vessel. Vasco da Gama crossed the ocean with an Arabian pilot after rounding Africa in 1497. Juan Sebastian del Cano, Abel Tasman, James Cook, Russian voyagers, Antarctic explorers, and later scientific expeditions extended mapping and knowledge.

Scientific study became systematic with the Challenger expedition beginning in 1872. The International Geophysical Year of 1957-1958, the International Indian Ocean Expedition of 1960-1965, and Deep Sea Drilling Project work from 1968 to 1983 advanced understanding of monsoons, marine geology, geophysics, continental shelves, deep-ocean minerals, and resource potential.
