## Introduction

![[attachments/psychological_testing_001.webp|420]]

*Likert scale questionnaire*

Psychological testing ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Psychological_testing)), also called psychometrics, is the systematic use of tests to measure behaviour, abilities, problems, and likely future performance. A test is any planned way of eliciting responses that can be related to behaviour in other settings. An aptitude test predicts later performance, such as success in school, while an achievement test measures present academic or vocational skill.

Tests are used in guidance offices, mental-health clinics, psychiatric hospitals, industry, government, and research. Ability and personality tests can help detect or diagnose troublesome behaviour. Employers and public agencies use tests to select workers, while researchers use them to turn abstract ideas such as intelligence into measurable variables.

Psychological measurement is often indirect. A person can feel temperature directly, but no one can directly observe another person’s hunger, pain, intelligence, or introversion. These qualities must be inferred from observable signs, such as answers, choices, actions, facial expressions, or performance on tasks. A thermometer illustrates the same logic: the observer reads an instrument rather than directly touching the property itself.

The central problem is that many psychological attributes are constructs. They are not visible objects, but inferred traits or states that help explain behaviour. A test must therefore connect responses to an attribute in a defensible way. The more indirect the inference, the more carefully the test must be designed, scored, and interpreted.

Measurement also depends on reliability and validity. A useful test should give reasonably consistent results when the same trait is measured under comparable conditions, and it should measure what it claims to measure. Without these qualities, numerical scores can look exact while giving weak or misleading information about a person.

## Psychophysical and Psychometric Scaling

![[attachments/psychological_testing_002.png|420]]

*Raven matrices score chart*

Early measurement in psychology drew from psychophysics, the study of relations between physical stimuli and sensory experience. Johann Friedrich Herbart helped prepare the idea that mental events could be treated quantitatively. Ernst Heinrich Weber later observed that the smallest noticeable difference in stimulus intensity is proportional to the original intensity.

Weber's law ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weber%E2%80%93Fechner_law)) means that a small change may be noticed when a stimulus is weak, but a larger change is needed when the original stimulus is strong. A person might notice a slight change in a 10-gram weight, but need a bigger change to notice a difference from a 100-gram weight. More technically, subjective intensity varies with the logarithm of objective stimulus intensity.

Traditional psychophysical scaling uses ordered physical stimuli, such as weights, sounds, or lights, and relates them to judgments made by subjects. In the method of average error, a subject adjusts a variable stimulus until it seems equal to a standard stimulus. The average of repeated judgments gives a measure of the perceived equality point.

These methods were used to study visual illusions, touch intensities, auditory pitch, and similar experiences. They showed that psychological experience can be measured, but not simply by copying physical units. The mind’s response to a stimulus may follow a regular pattern while still differing from the physical scale.

Psychometric scaling extended this tradition beyond direct physical stimuli. It can place attitudes, preferences, personality characteristics, leadership potential, initiative, or judgments of artistic quality on a linear scale. In such cases there may be no physical quantity like weight or loudness, but the aim is still to order people, objects, or traits in a meaningful way.

Psychological scales may ask people to rate themselves or others. They may also scale objects, occupations, social institutions, foods, or other targets. The value of a scale depends on whether the responses form a stable pattern and whether the numerical positions help predict or explain behaviour outside the test situation.

Aptitude, achievement, personality, and attitude tests all depend on this broader measurement logic. They translate selected behaviours into scores, but the score is only useful when tied to evidence about interpretation. A high score may predict school success, job performance, symptom patterns, or social preference only if the test has been checked against relevant outcomes.

Because testing can affect education, employment, diagnosis, and public decisions, test construction requires careful item writing, sampling of the target behaviour, scoring rules, statistical checking, and interpretation. A test that is too narrow may miss important abilities, while one that is poorly standardized may confuse personal differences with testing conditions.

## Key Events & Developments

- The psychophysical tradition: Researchers began linking controlled physical stimuli with sensory judgments, creating a model for psychological measurement.
- Weber's work: Ernst Heinrich Weber showed that just-noticeable differences depend on the starting intensity of a stimulus.
- Modern psychometrics: Psychological scaling expanded measurement from sensation to attitudes, abilities, personality traits, and preferences.

## Key People

- Johann Friedrich Herbart ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Johann_Friedrich_Herbart)): Helped prepare the quantitative treatment of mental phenomena in early psychology.
- Ernst Heinrich Weber ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ernst_Heinrich_Weber)): Formulated the relation later known as Weber's law ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weber%E2%80%93Fechner_law)), a foundation for psychophysical scaling.

## Interesting Facts

- Psychological tests measure behaviour directly but infer hidden traits such as intelligence, introversion, or aptitude.
- Weber's law ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weber%E2%80%93Fechner_law)) explains why the same physical change may be easy to notice in a weak stimulus but hard to notice in a strong one.
- Psychometric scales can measure not only people but also preferences, objects, institutions, and judged qualities.
