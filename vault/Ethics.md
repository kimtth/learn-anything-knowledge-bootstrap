# Ethics

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> Ethics, or moral philosophy, studies what people ought to do and how judgments of right, wrong, value, virtue, and justice should be made. It connects practical decisions with philosophical accounts of human flourishing and social responsibility.

## Introduction

Ethics ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethics)), also called moral philosophy ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethics)), is the study of what people ought to do, what counts as good or bad, right or wrong, and how moral judgments should be made. Ethics is a branch of philosophy that deals with practical decision-making rather than with simple factual description. It asks questions such as whether happiness, virtue, justice, or knowledge should be the central aim of life, and it examines both everyday moral choices and large social issues such as war, poverty, cloning, and the treatment of animals.
Ethics is closely linked to many other fields, including politics, law, religion, biology, and economics, but it remains distinct because it is not only about facts. It asks what should be done, not merely what is the case. This makes ethics both a philosophical and a practical discipline. Its central concerns are the nature of value, the standards by which actions are judged, and the relation between moral beliefs and human motivation.

## Origins of Moral Thinking
Moral reflection is ancient and widespread. The earliest surviving ethics-like texts are lists of precepts prepared for boys of the ruling class of [[Egypt]] some 3,000 years before the Christian era, urging rulers to judge impartially, to make their people prosperous, and to share bread with the hungry. These writings often connect morality with a good reputation, stating that honesty is the best policy, yet they do not develop systematic principles or analyze conflicts between duties, so they are not yet philosophical ethics.

In [[India]], by contrast, ethics was philosophical from the start. The Vedas ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vedas)), dating from about 1500 to 1200 BCE and described as the world's oldest philosophical literature, linked moral order to the structure of the universe through the principle of rita, the word from which the Western notion of right is derived. Indian thought set four proper goals of life—prosperity, satisfaction of desires, moral duty, and spiritual liberation—and praised honesty, charity, nonviolence, modesty, and purity of heart while condemning falsehood, cruelty, theft, and injury to living things.

In [[China]], the two greatest ancient moral philosophers thought very differently. Laozi (flourished c. 6th century BCE), whose aphoristic Daodejing teaches the Dao, or Way, urged simplicity, sincerity, and nonviolence and said nearly 600 years before Jesus that one should recompense injury with kindness. [[Confucius and Confucianism (Confucianism)|Confucius]] (Kongfuzi; 551-479 BCE) was the more down-to-earth reformer; the province where he served as minister of justice became renowned for honesty and care of the poor, and his practical teachings had a far greater influence on China than Laozi's.

## Ancient Greece and the Birth of Western Ethics

![[attachments/ethics_001.jpg|420]]

*Plato and Aristotle in Raphael's School of Athens*
Ancient Greece is the birthplace of Western philosophical ethics. Earlier Greek poetry of the 7th and 6th centuries BCE contained moral precepts, and the Greeks honoured the so-called seven sages, but real theory began with thinkers such as Pythagoras (c. 580-c. 500 BCE), whose school advocated vegetarianism and the rebirth of the soul, and the Sophists, professional teachers of rhetoric in the 5th century. By stressing persuasion, convention, and the social making of values, the Sophists challenged older assumptions about fixed moral truth and provoked the philosophers who opposed them.

[[Socrates]] (c. 470-399 BCE), who said that the unexamined life is not worth living, is presented as one of the greatest teachers of ethics, not for giving rules but for teaching a method of inquiry. He questioned those who claimed to know what justice or virtue was and exposed their vague opinions, holding that virtue can be known and that people act badly only from ignorance. Because his questioning threatened conventional belief, his enemies had him put to death on a charge of corrupting the youth of [[Athens]], yet his influence passed powerfully to [[Plato and Platonism (Plato)|Plato]].

[[Plato and Platonism (Plato)|Plato]] developed the Socratic idea that goodness is objective and knowable, writing in dialogue form. In the Republic he answered the challenge of the legendary ring of Gyges, which makes its wearer invisible and seems to remove any reason to act justly, by arguing that moral knowledge depends on grasping the Form of the Good, which orders all other values. In his dialogue Euthyphro he also showed that the gods cannot make actions good simply by approving them, implying a standard of right independent of divine will. [[Aristotle and Aristotelianism|Aristotle]] (384-322 BCE), who studied at Plato's Academy, rejected the theory of Forms and held that the highest good is the rational activity of the soul in accordance with virtue, virtue being a habit formed through practice; from his hierarchy of nature he also defended slavery and the killing of animals for human use.

## Ethics in the Hellenistic World
The Stoics and Epicureans were two influential schools that shaped later ethics. Stoicism was shaped by Zeno of Citium (c. 335-c. 263 BCE) and Chrysippus (c. 280-206 BCE) and spread to Rome through Cicero (106-43 BCE) and Seneca (4 BCE-65 CE); strikingly, its leading voices included both a slave, Epictetus (55-c. 135), and an emperor, Marcus Aurelius (121-180). The Stoics rejected the passions as a basis for judging good and bad, holding that only reason can do so, and taught that true happiness comes from living in wisdom and virtue, which fortune cannot touch.

The Epicureans, founded by Epicurus (341-270 BCE), took pleasure as the sole good but understood it as pleasures of the mind rather than the body. The highest pleasure is tranquillity, reached by eliminating all but the simplest wants, which even the poor can satisfy; justice is good only as an expedient to prevent mutual harm, and friendship is prized for the pleasure it brings. that this stress on pleasure and pain later influenced utilitarian thinking.

## Christianity, Medieval Thought, and the Reformation

With the rise of Christianity, ethics became tied to religious belief. St. Augustine of Hippo (354-430), knowing a version of Plato's philosophy, made the first serious attempt to give Christianity a moral philosophy, treating happiness as the union of the soul with God after death and bodily pleasures as a threat to spiritual bliss. He insisted that those who keep the moral law only through fear of punishment are not really keeping it at all, so morality is rooted in love of God and neighbour rather than mere rule-following.

St. Thomas Aquinas later combined Aristotelian ethics with Christian theology. He argued that human beings seek happiness, but that true happiness is found ultimately in God. Aquinas also developed a natural law view: human beings can know moral principles through reason because these principles are related to the nature of human beings and the order of creation. This natural-law tradition, long thought a medieval relic, was revived in the late 20th century by John Finnis, who listed basic human goods—life, knowledge, play, aesthetic experience, friendship, practical reasonableness, and religion—that may never be acted against.
The Renaissance and Reformation followed. Niccolò Machiavelli (1469-1527), in The Prince (1513), advised rulers to learn how not to be good according to necessity, the frankest rejection of morality since the Sophists. After the breakup of Western Christendom led by Martin Luther (1483-1546) and John Calvin (1509-64), Thomas Hobbes (1588-1679) built a systematic ethics on psychological hedonism, holding that all voluntary acts aim at pleasure or self-preservation and that a thing is good if it is the object of a person's desire; morality and law arise from the need to escape fear and disorder through a social contract.

## The British Tradition: Reason, Feeling, and Utility

A major strand is the British moral tradition. Samuel Clarke and Joseph Butler defended forms of intuitionism, the view that moral truths are known by reason or conscience as self-evident, comparing them to truths of mathematics; Butler argued that conscience is a real moral faculty not reducible to self-interest. The rival moral sense school, led by Francis Hutcheson (1694-1746), based morality on a disinterested benevolence and first stated the principle that the best action procures the greatest happiness for the greatest numbers.

Special weight goes to David Hume (1711-76), who argued in A Treatise of Human Nature (1739-40) that reason is the slave of the passions: it can show how to reach our ends but cannot set those ends, so reason alone cannot produce moral judgments. Hume also pointed out that writers slide from statements about what is the case to claims about what ought to be done, the famous is-ought problem, and concluded that morality is determined by sentiment rather than fact.
Utilitarianism developed next. The theologian William Paley (1743-1805) held that whatever increases happiness is right because God wills his creatures' happiness, but Jeremy Bentham (1748-1832) is the father of modern utilitarianism, deriving a whole system from pleasure and pain and insisting that each is to count for one and none for more than one, even extending consideration to nonhuman animals. [[John Stuart Mill]] (1806-73), in Utilitarianism (1861), distinguished higher from lower pleasures and held it better to be Socrates dissatisfied than a fool satisfied, while Henry Sidgwick (1838-1900), in The Methods of Ethics (1874), reconciled utilitarianism with common-sense morality but could not reconcile it with egoism.

## Continental Ethics: Spinoza to Rousseau

On the European continent the Dutch-Jewish philosopher Benedict Spinoza (1632-77) opposed Hobbes by treating natural desires as a form of bondage; real freedom lies in transforming them by reason, which he saw as the master of the passions, and he viewed everything as part of one system that is at once nature and God. The mathematician Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz (1646-1716) gave ethics little attention, holding that a perfect God must have made the best of all possible worlds, a view later parodied in Voltaire's Candide (1759).

The French writer [[Jean-Jacques Rousseau]] (1712-78) took the next step. His A Discourse on Inequality (1755) imagined trouble-free "noble savages" corrupted only when someone first claimed possession of land, and The Social Contract (1762) called for rule by the general will, the true common will of all citizens rather than the mere sum of individual wills. Rousseau deeply moved Kant, who said that reading him taught him to respect the worth of every human being.

## Kant, Hegel, Marx, and Nietzsche
Immanuel Kant is one of the most important modern moral philosophers. Kant held that actions resulting from desire cannot be free, so freedom lies in rational action, and that whatever reason demands must hold for all rational beings as a universal law. His most distinctive claim is that an action has moral worth only when one does one's duty for its own sake, not from hope of reward or fear of punishment, which gave later ethics a strong emphasis on autonomy, dignity, and respect for persons.

G.W.F. Hegel (1770-1831) built the first philosophical system based on historical change, presenting all of history as the progress of mind toward freedom, which became possible only when the right of individual conscience emerged at the Reformation; for him ethical life is woven into institutions and shared practices. Karl Marx treated moral ideas as products of the economic stage a society has reached, declaring with Friedrich Engels (1820-95) in The Communist Manifesto (1848) that the ruling ideas of every age are the ideas of its ruling class, so morality often masks bourgeois interests.

Friedrich Nietzsche (1844-1900) attacked the Judeo-Christian tradition as a slave morality that makes virtues of meekness, poverty, and humility, the ethics of the weak who fear strength. Announcing that "God is dead," he called for a reevaluation of all values toward the Ubermensch and the will to power, by which he seems to have meant self-affirmation rather than the oppression of others. that his racist sister rearranged his unpublished writings after his death to make him appear to endorse Nazi ideals he had in fact despised.

## Metaethics, Applied Ethics, and Contemporary Debate
Modern metaethics studies what moral judgments are rather than their content. The English philosopher G.E. Moore (1873-1958), in Principia Ethica (1903), attacked the "naturalistic fallacy" of defining good in terms of any natural quality such as pleasure, using the open-question argument; his intuitionism dominated British philosophy for some 30 years. Emotivism, developed by the American Charles Stevenson (1908-79) in Ethics and Language (1945), held instead that moral judgments express and encourage approval or disapproval and so are neither true nor false.

On the Continent, existentialism, led by Jean-Paul Sartre (1905-80) in Being and Nothingness (1943), held that existence precedes essence: with no God-given purpose, humans are free to choose how to live, and to deny this freedom is "bad faith." The fact that the German existentialist Martin Heidegger (1889-1976) became a Nazi, while Sartre joined the French Resistance, sharpened doubts about whether any authentic choice is as good as another.

The final sections turn to applied ethics, revived in part by the U.S. civil rights movement and its debates over reverse discrimination. Philosophers also led rather than followed a social movement on animals: Animals, Men and Morals (1972) and Peter Singer's Animal Liberation (1975) gave a philosophical basis to the animal rights movement. In bioethics, a 1982 case in which a U.S. doctor, upheld by the Supreme Court of Indiana, withheld lifesaving surgery from an infant with Down syndrome shows how new medical power forces old questions about the value of human life.

Within normative ethics, the most influential modern work is John Rawls's A Theory of Justice (1971), which revived the social contract and Kant's principle of respect for persons against the utilitarians; in The Law of Peoples (1999) Rawls extended his reasoning to relations between societies. Debates over rights followed, with Robert Nozick treating rights as trumps over consequences and Ronald Dworkin grounding politics in equal concern and respect. From the 1990s philosophers such as Peter Unger, in Living High and Letting Die (1996), and Thomas Pogge, in World Poverty and Human Rights (2002), pressed the question of what the affluent owe to those dying of famine and disease in the developing world.

Environmental ethics raised the ancient question of intrinsic value in a new form: is it bad that the dodo became extinct or a rainforest is felled only because of lost human experience, or do trees, rivers, species, and whole ecosystems have value of their own? Some philosophers defended the status quo on animals—Peter Carruthers, in The Animals Issue (1992), limited obligation to beings who could join a social contract, and Roger Scruton, in Animal Rights and Wrongs (2000), defended foxhunting while condemning factory farms—while others argued for radical change, so that modern ethics now reaches beyond human communities to animals, future generations, and the planet.

Special importance attaches to the question of objectivity. Some theories treat moral judgments as true or false in a strong sense, while others interpret them as expressions of feeling, prescriptions, social conventions, or practical commitments. This debate matters because it affects whether moral disagreement can be settled by reason, experience, democratic deliberation, or only by persuasion.

Another major theme is the relation between individual conscience and social institutions. Kant emphasizes autonomous rational duty, Hegel emphasizes ethical life within institutions, Marx emphasizes class and material conditions, and contemporary applied ethics often asks how personal choice is shaped by law, medicine, business, technology, and global inequality.
Modern ethical problems often involve beings who cannot speak for themselves: animals, embryos, patients unable to consent, future generations, and damaged ecosystems. This expands ethics beyond the older focus on relations among adult citizens and makes responsibility a central modern concern.

In conclusion, ethics is presented as a history of rival answers to the same problem: how human beings can justify what they value and how they should live together. Its development moves from virtue and divine order to reason, utility, autonomy, social critique, and applied responsibility.
Several levels of ethical inquiry can be distinguished. Normative ethics asks which principles or ideals should guide action. Metaethics asks what moral language means and whether moral claims can be true. Applied ethics ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Applied_ethics)) tests theories against concrete problems in medicine, law, politics, economics, technology, war, and environmental policy.

In modern discussion, rights and justice become especially important. Questions about equality, punishment, discrimination, liberty, welfare, and global poverty show that ethics cannot be separated from institutions. Moral philosophy therefore moves between individual action and the design of social arrangements.

The treatment of bioethics shows how new technology forces old questions into new forms. Abortion, contraception, genetic engineering, cloning, organ transplantation, euthanasia, and allocation of medical resources raise questions about personhood, consent, harm, dignity, and the limits of human control.

Animal ethics and environmental ethics broaden moral concern beyond human communities. It asks whether sentience, life, species membership, ecosystems, or future consequences should determine moral standing. This is one reason modern ethics becomes both more global and more ecological than many earlier traditions.

The final impression is that ethical theory repeatedly changes because human life changes. Commerce, empire, science, democracy, industry, medicine, and ecological crisis all create new cases, but the underlying need remains the same: to give reasons for conduct that can withstand criticism.

[[War]] and violence show how ethics operates under extreme pressure. Just-war reasoning, pacifism, realism, punishment, deterrence, and civil disobedience all ask when force can be justified and whether good ends can excuse harmful means.

The discussion of economic life asks whether markets should be judged only by efficiency or also by fairness, dignity, exploitation, need, and social welfare. Poverty, aid, property, taxation, labour, and distributive justice connect personal morality to political economy.

Overall, ethics forms a cumulative argument across cultures and centuries. It begins with moral advice and religious order, becomes philosophical analysis in Greece, is transformed by Christianity and modernity, and finally becomes a plural field addressing medicine, animals, technology, politics, and the planet.
Moral motivation remains a persistent problem. It asks why people should act morally when duty conflicts with interest, desire, fear, custom, or advantage. Answers range from virtue and divine command to sympathy, rational autonomy, utility, social contract, conscience, and the hope for a just community.

This makes ethics both analytical and formative: it clarifies arguments, but it also asks what kinds of persons and societies moral reflection should help create.

These debates also show that moral philosophy ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethics)) is never finished. Each historical answer creates new questions about authority, freedom, happiness, duty, consequences, character, social power, and the limits of human responsibility.

That continuing debate is why ethics remains central to public life, education, professional conduct, law, politics, medicine, technology, economic justice, and environmental responsibility.

Its questions remain practical, public, and personal.

## Key Events & Developments

- c. 1500-1200 BCE: the Vedas ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vedas)) set out what may be the first philosophical ethics, built on the principle of rita.
- 5th century BCE: the Sophists and [[Socrates]] turn Greek moral precepts into systematic inquiry about virtue and justice.
- 1513: Machiavelli's The Prince openly separates political necessity from moral idealism.
- 1739-40: [[Hume]]'s A Treatise of Human Nature argues that morality rests on sentiment, not reason.
- 1861: Mill's Utilitarianism refines Bentham's principle with higher and lower pleasures.
- 1903: G.E. Moore's Principia Ethica reshapes 20th-century metaethics with the open-question argument.
- 1970s: applied ethics revives, addressing equality, animals, the environment, and bioethics.
