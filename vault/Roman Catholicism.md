# Roman Catholicism

## Introduction

![[attachments/roman_catholicism_001.webp|420]]

*Interior dome of Saint Peter's Basilica in Vatican City*

Roman Catholicism ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catholic_Church)) is one of the three major branches of Christianity, alongside Eastern Orthodoxy and Protestantism. It has been a decisive spiritual force in Western civilization and traces its history to Jesus Christ and the Apostles. Over centuries it developed a complex theology, a worldwide sacramental life, and an elaborate organization headed by the papacy.

The Roman Catholic Church ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catholic_Church)) has nearly 1.1 billion members, more than all other Christians combined and more than many other major religious traditions considered separately. Its influence is essential for understanding the Middle Ages, the work of Thomas Aquinas, [[Dante]]'s Divine Comedy ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Divine_Comedy)), Gothic cathedrals, and much music by composers such as Haydn and [[Mozart]].

Roman Catholic identity is defined both by continuity with early [[Christianity]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christianity)) and by relation to other Christian bodies. Questions about the schism with Eastern Orthodoxy, the break with the Church of England, and the Protestant Reformation are also questions about what Roman Catholicism is.

## History of Roman Catholicism

The elements of catholicity, including doctrine, authority, and universality, appear in early form in the New Testament. Acts begins with a small group of demoralized disciples in Jerusalem and ends with Christianity reaching Rome. Later New Testament writings urge believers to guard apostolic teaching and describe the church as Christ's body.

Early Christianity was more pluralistic than later idealized pictures suggest. Challenges in the 2nd and 3rd centuries required clearer standards of authority. Irenaeus of Lyon described three sources of catholic authority: Scripture, apostolic episcopal centres, and a rule of faith preserved in the churches.

Roman Catholic teaching gives special importance to Peter. The New Testament lists Peter first among the Apostles, and later interpretation emphasized Jesus' words about Peter as rock and holder of the keys. The link between Peter and Rome is not explicit in most New Testament texts, but early tradition held that Peter and Paul died as martyrs in Rome.

## Early Roman Primacy

The church of [[Rome]] gained prestige through association with Peter, Paul, and many martyrs. Tertullian praised Rome near the end of the 2nd century as a church where apostolic doctrine and blood had been poured out. Roman political importance also strengthened claims of primacy.

Roman primacy was not simply self-evident. It grew through scriptural interpretation, tradition, Roman legal ideas, and practical leadership. Later popes and theologians used Matthew 16, Luke 22, and John 21 to argue that the bishop of Rome inherited Peter's pastoral and governing role.

## The Early Middle Ages

During the Middle Ages, from the fall of Rome to the Renaissance, the papacy matured and became the preeminent authority in the Western church. Monks preserved classical and Christian learning, missionaries expanded Christianity into pagan Europe and the Islamic world, and the Crusades attempted to retake the Holy Land by force.

After 1000, cathedral schools and then universities replaced monasteries as intellectual centres. Scholasticism, drawing heavily on Aristotle transmitted through Arab scholars, shaped Roman Catholic thought into the 20th century. Medieval society formed a threefold pattern of imperium, sacerdotium, and studium: political, ecclesiastical, and intellectual authority.

By the 10th century, Christendom had become a religious and cultural community ready for growth. Medieval Catholic institutions emerged from late Roman, Frankish, monastic, episcopal, and papal developments, creating a society in which church and civilization were deeply intertwined.

## Gregorian Reform

The Gregorian Reform, beginning with Pope Leo IX in 1049, was one of the most important events in church history. It aimed to abolish simony, clerical marriage, and lay control of church office, while returning the church to moral purity. It also strengthened a hierarchical structure headed by the pope.

The reform began amid papal disorder. In the 1040s, three rival claimants held sway in central Italy. Henry III held the Council of Sutri in 1046, deposed them, and supported new popes. Leo IX brought reform north of the Alps and demanded that bishops prove they were free from simony.

The movement emphasized priesthood, sacraments, moral clergy, and freedom from lay interference. It also laid groundwork for later ideas of church-state separation, even though medieval reformers wanted a strong, purified church rather than a secular modern state.

## The Investiture Controversy

The Investiture Controversy centred on whether kings and emperors could invest bishops with ring and staff. Since the time of Charlemagne, rulers had depended on church officials for government. Reformers saw lay investiture as simony and as a violation of church independence.

Conflict between Gregory VII and Henry IV grew from disputes over Milan and royal intervention in church affairs. Henry's association with excommunicated advisers and his resistance to papal demands led to a break. The controversy showed the new papal claim that spiritual authority could judge secular rulers in church matters.

The struggle continued until compromise under Calixtus II, but its deeper effects lasted. It clarified papal authority, episcopal election, and the relation between sacred office and political power.

## The Crusades

The [[Crusades]] revealed the increased authority of the papacy. After the Byzantine defeat by the Seljuq Turks at Manzikert in 1071, Alexius I Comnenus appealed to Urban II for help. Urban's call at Clermont in 1095 inspired a large army of volunteers.

The capture of [[Jerusalem]] in 1099 and creation of a Latin kingdom raised papal prestige, even though later Crusades brought disasters and conflict. Crusading ideals spread to Spain, eastern Europe, campaigns against heretics, and military orders. The movement reflected devotion to the church and to the pope but also produced violence and long-term division.

## Theology, Apostolic Life, and Religious Orders

Medieval theology developed through Scholastic method. Anselm used rational argument in theology and wrote Cur Deus homo? on the Incarnation. Abelard used dialectic in Sic et non, presenting contradictions from earlier authorities. Peter Lombard's Sentences became the syllabus of theological study.

Thomas Aquinas and Albertus Magnus recast Aristotle in Christian terms, while Bonaventure renewed Augustinian and Victorine traditions. The condemnations of 1272 and 1277 signaled tension between Aristotelian naturalism and orthodoxy and foreshadowed later separation of philosophy and theology.

Religious life shifted toward the apostolic life and imitation of Christ. Movements of poverty, itinerant preaching, and devotion to the humanity of Jesus grew after 1000. Bernard of Clairvaux and Francis of Assisi expressed this spirituality, while devotion to Mary became visible in great Notre Dame churches.

## The Persecuting Society and Late Medieval Crisis

The centralized medieval church also helped form a persecuting society. It defined who was Christian and took measures against heretics, Jews, Muslims, and others. Innocent III sanctioned the Albigensian Crusade against the Cathari in southern France, and new judicial practices led toward inquisitorial procedures.

The late Middle Ages brought papal crisis. Boniface VIII fought Philip IV of France, issued Unam sanctam, was humiliated at Anagni in 1303, and died soon after. The papacy then resided at Avignon from 1309 to 1377, a period called the Babylonian Captivity.

Franciscan disputes over absolute poverty produced critics such as Michael of Cesena and William of Ockham. Marsilius of Padua argued for a secular state in which the church became a government department. Conciliarism, papal weakness, and reform movements challenged papal claims.

## Jan Hus and Failed Reunion

Jan Hus criticized the hierarchy and emphasized a spiritual understanding of church authority. He was influenced by John Wycliffe but was less radical on the Eucharist. Summoned to the Council of Constance with promised safe-conduct, he was arrested, condemned, and burned on July 6, 1415.

The Hussite movement joined reform with Czech nationalism and demanded communion for the laity under both bread and wine. Around the same period, efforts to reunite East and West at Ferrara-Florence produced agreement on the Filioque and a union document in 1439, but Eastern churches largely rejected it.

## Eve of the Reformation

Before the Reformation, Scholastic theology declined, lay piety expanded, and anticlericalism grew. The Imitation of Christ, traditionally attributed to Thomas a Kempis, represented the devotio moderna, emphasizing inner life over ritual and works. Printing spread vernacular Bible reading and preaching.

Renaissance [[Humanism|humanism]] had a complex relation to Roman Catholicism. Some older historians described it as anti-Christian or neopagan, but many popes and churchmen supported Renaissance learning and art. Yet skepticism, anticlericalism, and demands for reform all prepared the conditions for upheaval.

## The Council of Trent and Catholic Reformation

The Council of Trent ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Council_of_Trent)), meeting intermittently from 1545 to 1563, was the central event of the Catholic Reformation. It answered Protestant doctrines with Roman Catholic both-and formulations: faith and works, Scripture and tradition, church authority and biblical teaching. It reaffirmed the Latin Vulgate against Protestant stress on Hebrew and Greek originals.

Trent also reformed church discipline. It required seminaries for clergy, strengthened preaching, controlled financial abuses, required bishops to reside in dioceses, and regularized liturgy. The council shaped modern Roman Catholic identity as both doctrinally anti-Protestant and internally reforming.

The Counter-Reformation was the part of Catholic reform aimed at resisting Protestantism. The papacy created the Roman Inquisition and Index of Forbidden Books. Bavaria, Austria, Poland, and Bohemia were regained or preserved for Catholicism, while religious wars devastated Europe and the Peace of Westphalia in 1648 confirmed a permanently divided Christendom.

## Post-Reformation Movements

After 1648, Roman Catholicism had to live as one church among many in a nation-state world. Gregory XV created the Congregation for the Propagation of the Faith in 1622 to direct missions and church affairs in territories without ordinary hierarchy.

Jansenism, based on Cornelius Jansen's Augustinus of 1640, defended Augustine against Jesuit theology, stressing original sin, irresistible grace, and strict morality. Blaise Pascal's Lettres provinciales made anti-Jesuit criticism famous. Popes condemned Jansenism in 1653 and 1713.

Quietism emphasized passive contemplation as the highest religious activity and raised questions about authentic Catholic mysticism. Francois de Fenelon combined mystical teaching with orthodoxy, but the movement was condemned as dangerous. The Chinese rites controversy, associated with Matteo Ricci, raised similar questions about adapting Catholicism to local religious culture.

## Revolution, Pius IX, and Leo XIII

The French Revolution forced Roman Catholicism to confront a political and philosophical system that did not assume Christianity and often opposed it. The church was tied in public imagination to the ancien regime, even though church and monarchy had often clashed. Anti-clerical and secular movements demanded a radical redefinition of church-world relations.

Pius IX, pope from 1846 to 1878, moved from early liberalism to strong ultramontanism after the revolutions of 1848. Vatican I defined papal infallibility in 1870, teaching that the pope speaks infallibly when defining faith or morals ex cathedra for the whole church.

Leo XIII, pope from 1878 to 1903, was also conservative in doctrine but addressed modern social and political questions. He opposed Modernism, denied Anglican orders in 1896, condemned Americanism, and issued Rerum novarum, which became foundational for modern Catholic social teaching.

## The Second Vatican Council

John XXIII, pope from 1958 to 1963, surprised observers by calling the Second Vatican Council. His encyclicals Mater et magistra in 1961 and Pacem in terris in 1963 emphasized justice, the common good, and peace among nations.

Vatican II ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_Vatican_Council)) changed Catholic worship and self-understanding. It encouraged vernacular liturgy, greater lay participation, respect for Orthodox and Protestant Christians, reconciliation with Jews, appreciation of other world religions, and respect for human freedom and conscience. It rejected coercion as a means of bringing people to faith.

The council's legacy remains divided. Paul VI implemented reforms, including vernacular Mass and altars facing the people, but also reaffirmed clerical celibacy and rejected artificial birth control in Humanae vitae in 1968. John Paul II, pope from 1978 to 2005, was charismatic and global but moved toward stronger papal centralization.

Postconciliar tensions appeared on both sides of Catholic life. Some Catholics believed Vatican II did not go far enough in reforming authority, sexuality, ministry, and social engagement. Others believed it weakened inherited liturgy and doctrine, a reaction visible in the 1988 schism connected with Marcel Lefebvre and the movement to restore the Latin Mass.

## The New World and Global Catholicism

Spain and Portugal carried Catholicism to the Americas after European contact with the Western Hemisphere. Franciscans, Dominicans, Augustinians, and Jesuits evangelized under royal patronage. Catholicism was imposed on conquered Aztecs and Incas, but missionaries such as Bartolome de Las Casas defended Indigenous peoples against abuse.

In Latin America, Catholicism remained strong but faced anticlerical states, secularism, Protestant missions, and social inequality. Vatican II and the 1968 Latin American episcopal conference encouraged advocacy for the poor, liberation theology, vernacular liturgy, and Christian base communities.

In North America, Spanish missions reached Florida, New Mexico, Texas, Arizona, and California. Junipero Serra founded the San Diego mission in 1769. In the United States, Catholics were few at independence, but Charles Carroll and John Carroll gave Catholicism public standing. In Canada, French Catholic missions began with early settlements at Port Royal in 1604 and Quebec in 1608.

North American Catholicism developed under Protestant political dominance but gradually built dioceses, parishes, schools, and public legitimacy. In the United States, trusteeism raised conflict over lay control of church property, and the First Provincial Council of Baltimore in 1829 strengthened episcopal authority. In Canada, the Quebec Act of 1774 protected Catholic rights after British conquest and helped preserve a large French Catholic community.

In [[Africa]], Catholicism expanded rapidly in the 20th century. African clergy and bishops increased, several Africans became cardinals, and the church contributed to education, postcolonial public life, opposition to oppressive regimes, interfaith dialogue, and inculturation through African languages, music, and dance.

## Structure of the Church

Roman Catholic theology defines the church as both visible society and spiritual mystery. Robert Bellarmine's 1621 definition emphasized profession of faith, sacraments, and government under the pope. Vatican II and later theologians emphasized fellowship with God, sacrament of salvation, People of God, body of Christ, and temple of the Holy Spirit.

The older saying no salvation outside the church once drew sharp boundaries. Modern Catholic theology seeks ways to speak of membership or relation to the church for non-Catholic Christians and others. Vatican II even used the word church for Protestant communities, encouraging the view of one divided church rather than one true church and many false ones.

Apostolic succession supports the claim that Catholic bishops continue the authority of the Apostles. Clement of Rome mentioned succession around AD 95, and Irenaeus used it against Gnostics. Bishops teach doctrine, sanctify through sacraments, and govern the church.

## Papacy and Bishops

The papacy is the office of the bishop of Rome. The pope's titles include bishop of Rome, vicar of Jesus Christ, successor of Peter, supreme pontiff, primate of Italy, sovereign of Vatican City, and servant of the servants of God. His uniqueness rests on the claim that he inherits Peter's primacy.

Vatican I defined Petrine primacy through texts such as Matthew 16, John 21, and Luke 22. It taught that Christ gave Peter true jurisdiction, not merely honour, and that this primacy passes to the bishops of Rome. It also defined papal infallibility in faith and morals under specific conditions.

The theory of Roman primacy developed over a long period. Damasus I called Rome the apostolic see despite other churches with apostolic origins, and Leo I described himself not only as Peter's successor but also as Peter's vicar. Roman legal ideas about inheritance helped explain how Peter's powers could be understood as continuing in the bishop of Rome. Later medieval popes, especially Gregory VII, Innocent III, and Innocent IV, expanded the language of papal fullness of power.

The Council of Florence expressed this claim in Laetentur coeli, teaching that the Roman pontiff was successor of Peter, vicar of Christ, head of the whole church, and father and teacher of Christians. Vatican I later used that tradition in Pastor aeternus, adding that no higher ecclesiastical authority, not even an ecumenical council, could receive an appeal from a papal judgment. This did not remove bishops from Catholic structure, but it placed their collegial authority within communion with Rome.

The college of bishops succeeds the college of Apostles. Each bishop is tied to a place or titular see and has teaching, sanctifying, and governing responsibility. Vatican II emphasized episcopal collegiality, while later papacies balanced collegiality with Roman central authority.

The laity gained recognized roles after Vatican II ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_Vatican_Council)) and the revised canon law. Laypersons can serve in diocesan and parish councils, as lectors, commentators, cantors, chancellors, advisers, and in some circumstances as preachers, baptismal ministers, and distributors of Holy Communion. John Paul II's Christifidelis laici stressed lay mission in the world.

## Canon Law, Faith, Heresy, and Revelation

Canon law ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canon_law)) developed from early church rules, diocesan synods, regional councils, and ecumenical councils. Gratian's Decretum around 1140 became the first definitive medieval codification, later joined by papal decretals and other collections into the Corpus Juris Canonici.

Faith in Roman Catholicism is rooted in the New Testament as personal knowledge of God revealed in Christ. [[Augustine]] treated faith as acceptance of revelation and gift of grace. The Council of Orange in 529 taught that even the beginning and desire of faith come from grace. Thomas Aquinas defined faith as intellectual assent to divine truth commanded by the will and moved by grace.

Heresy is the obstinate denial by a baptized Christian of a revealed truth proposed by the church. Historically it brought excommunication and sometimes civil penalties. Catholic theologians also saw heresy as forcing clearer dogmatic definition, since one-sided errors often raised legitimate questions.

Revelation means God's self-disclosure. Catholic theology distinguishes broad natural knowledge of God from formal revelation as divine utterance. Jesus is both revealer and revealed, the incarnate Word. Catholic teaching treats Scripture and tradition as preserving the deposit of faith, with modern theology often trying to understand them as one living source.

## Sacraments and Liturgy

A sacrament ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sacrament)) is an outward sign instituted by Christ that gives inner grace. Medieval theology settled on seven: baptism, confirmation, Eucharist, penance or reconciliation, anointing of the sick, marriage, and holy orders. Trent confirmed this number against Protestant claims that there were only two sacraments.

The traditional Catholic view of sacramental effectiveness is ex opere operato, meaning the sacrament works from the act done because its power rests on God's saving act in Christ, not on the minister's holiness. The minister acts as agent of the church.

Baptism is regeneration and initiation into the church. It uses natural water and the Trinitarian formula, removes past sins, incorporates the person into Christ's body, and cannot be repeated. Catholicism recognizes many non-Catholic baptisms when properly administered.

The Eucharist is the centre of Catholic worship, while confirmation, reconciliation, marriage, holy orders, and anointing of the sick structure Christian life. Liturgy includes seasonal colours, prayers such as the Hail Mary, the Nicene Creed, and forms of worship that changed after Vatican II through vernacular language and lay participation.

Confirmation developed from baptism into a distinct rite of anointing and laying on of hands. Catholic interpretation links it to passages in Acts where already-baptized Christians received the Holy Spirit through apostolic hands. In modern practice it often functions as a mature affirmation of baptismal commitment, with the bishop or authorized priest praying for the sevenfold gift of the Holy Spirit and anointing the forehead with chrism.

The Eucharist is both sacrament and sacrifice in Catholic teaching. The mass repeats the pattern of bread, wine, thanksgiving, and communion rooted in the Last Supper, while also presenting the sacrifice of Christ as the centre of worship. Early Christian eucharistic meals gradually became a distinct liturgical rite, and daily celebration became common by the 4th century. Catholic teaching on communion under one or both kinds rests on the belief that the whole Christ is present in either the consecrated bread or wine.

Reconciliation changed historically from severe public penance to private confession. Early penitents who committed grave sins could be excluded from communion until fasting, humiliation, sackcloth, and public discipline were completed. Later Irish and Anglo-Saxon monastic practice helped spread auricular confession, in which a penitent privately confesses sins to a priest, receives absolution, and performs penance. Marriage also has a distinctive Catholic form: the spouses administer the sacrament through consent, while the priest and witnesses safeguard the church's public recognition of the bond.

Catholic liturgy ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liturgy)) also joins doctrine to repeated public action. The mass includes readings, prayers, creed, intercessions, offertory, eucharistic prayer, Lord's Prayer, sign of peace, communion, and blessing. Seasonal colours, holy days, devotions, processions, and eucharistic adoration extend worship beyond Sunday obligation, making Catholic belief visible in calendar, gesture, music, and sacred space.

Popular devotion gives Catholicism another layer beside official sacramental rites. Veneration of saints, relics, pilgrimage sites, Marian prayers such as the Hail Mary, rosaries, processions, feast days, images, and local shrines make doctrine tangible in household and community practice. These devotions can be regulated by church authority, but they also show how Catholic life is carried by memory, place, story, bodily gesture, and attachment to holy figures.

## Mysticism, Missions, and Education

Mysticism is an important strand in Catholic life, joining contemplation, prayer, asceticism, and union with God. Catholic tradition also gives major place to Mary, saints, creeds, devotional prayer, and sacramental imagination.

Catholic mysticism often used the older language of theologia mystica, the direct but obscure experience of God in prayer. [[Augustine]]'s account of ascent toward God, the Greek Fathers, Carthusian spirituality, the Carmelite writers Teresa of Avila and John of the Cross, and the 20th-century monk Thomas Merton all belong to this contemplative tradition. Catholic caution about visions, stigmatization, and other extraordinary signs made charity the test of authentic prayer: mystical experience had to increase love of God and neighbour.

Missions spread Catholicism across Europe, the Americas, Africa, and Asia. Mission history includes heroic service, cultural adaptation, colonial entanglement, coercion, education, and conflict with local traditions. Vatican II strengthened respect for non-Christian religions and encouraged inculturation.

Catholic [[Education|education]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Education)) has included monasteries, cathedral schools, universities, seminaries, missions, parish schools, and modern institutions. The church also engaged science and culture, including later review of its condemnation of [[Galileo]]. Education remains one of the church's major global activities.

Catholic charity was another institutional expression of doctrine. Organized care for the poor, sick, orphaned, and vulnerable grew from the church's reading of the Gospel works of mercy and from the healing mission of Jesus. Hospitals, schools, orphanages, relief institutions, religious communities, and lay benefactors made benevolence a public dimension of church life. This tradition later influenced modern governments and continued even where Protestant churches separated from Rome.

Education also shows Catholicism's changing relation to culture. Monasteries preserved biblical, patristic, and classical texts after the ancient world fragmented; cathedral schools and universities later made theology, law, medicine, and philosophy institutional disciplines. Printing, missions, parish schooling, religious orders, and modern university systems extended this work beyond Europe. Conflict with modern science and secular schools was real, but Catholic institutions also educated children, poor communities, clergy, professionals, and converts across many societies.

Church-state relations changed sharply in the modern period. Earlier Catholic theory often assumed union of church and state, and 19th-century popes rejected separation as dangerous. Vatican II accepted the modern secular and plural state as a valid political entity, denied that the church should seek political power for ecclesiastical ends, and endorsed religious freedom as part of human dignity. This shift gave Catholicism a new way to live within democratic and religiously plural societies.

Roman Catholicism is held together by sacramental imagination. Visible things such as water, bread, wine, oil, hands, vows, confession, and anointing become signs of invisible grace. This makes Catholic life concrete and communal: birth, sin, healing, marriage, ordination, sickness, death, feast days, pilgrimage, relics, images, and liturgical time all connect doctrine with ordinary life.

The church also developed a distinctive teaching authority. Scripture, tradition, councils, creeds, papal definitions, bishops, theologians, canon law, and liturgy all interact in defining faith. Catholic doctrine therefore rarely depends on one source alone; it normally joins biblical interpretation, inherited worship, philosophical reasoning, episcopal judgment, and pastoral practice.

Catholic history repeatedly turns on the relation between reform and authority. Monastic reform, Gregorian reform, mendicant poverty, Trent, Vatican I, Vatican II, missionary adaptation, social teaching, and modern debates over liturgy and sexuality all show the same tension: the church claims continuity with apostolic faith while also responding to corruption, new cultures, political change, and intellectual challenge.

The papacy is central but never the whole church. Dioceses, parishes, religious orders, universities, councils, lay movements, missions, hospitals, schools, and local devotions have all shaped Catholic life. Franciscans, Dominicans, Jesuits, Benedictines, Carmelites, missionaries, bishops, sisters, catechists, and lay organizations carried Catholicism into daily practice far beyond Rome.

Catholic social teaching became especially important in the modern world. Rerum novarum and later documents addressed labour, property, wages, unions, poverty, peace, development, human dignity, and the common good. This tradition tried to answer both laissez-faire capitalism and revolutionary socialism while grounding social obligation in Christian anthropology.

Global Catholicism also changed the church's centre of gravity. Latin America, Africa, Asia, and immigrant communities made Catholicism less simply European. Inculturation, liberation theology, interreligious dialogue, postcolonial politics, charismatic renewal, and new patterns of vocation forced Catholic identity to become more visibly worldwide while still tied to Rome.

## Key Events & Developments

- AD 95: Clement of Rome expressed early ideas of apostolic succession.
- 1054: Eastern and Western churches entered lasting schism.
- 1049: Leo IX began the papal reform movement later associated with Gregory VII.
- 1095: Urban II called the First Crusade at Clermont.
- 1309-77: The papacy resided at Avignon in the period called the Babylonian Captivity.
- 1545-63: The Council of Trent ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Council_of_Trent)) defined Catholic responses to Protestantism and reformed church discipline.
- 1870: Vatican I defined papal infallibility.
- 1962-65: Vatican II reshaped Catholic liturgy, ecumenism, and church-world relations.
