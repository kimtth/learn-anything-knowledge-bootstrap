# Literatures of Australia and New Zealand (Australia Literature)

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> Australian literature combines Aboriginal oral traditions rooted in country, law, ceremony, story, and song with colonial, nationalist, postwar, and contemporary writing in English. Its development repeatedly addresses settlement, cultural continuity, landscape, community, and contested national identity.

## Introduction

![[attachments/australia_literature_001.webp|420]]

*Portrait of Banjo Paterson*

Australian literature ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Australian_literature)) includes oral and written works produced in Australia. It often measures individual experience through collective values, typical lives, ordinary endurance, and representative social attitudes rather than through heroic individual triumph.

For white Australian writing, community identity grew from links to England, settler history, pioneer values, and attachment to land. For Aboriginal Australians, story, song, and legend traditionally defined relationships to people and country, while modern written work became a way to claim voice and defend cultural continuity.

Aboriginal oral literature existed before written Aboriginal languages were recorded. Songs, chants, legends, and stories formed a rich oral tradition ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oral_tradition)), but because Aboriginal groups had no single common language, stories were shaped by local allegiances, country, law, memory, and ceremonial life.

## Early European Versions of Aboriginal Stories

Early European versions of Aboriginal stories, such as Catherine Langloh Parker's Australian Legendary Tales of 1896 and Alan Marshall's People of the Dreamtime of 1952, often reshaped material to fit European narrative expectations. These versions helped circulate stories but did not fully preserve original form.

Catherine H. Berndt and Ronald M. Berndt published traditional narratives and songs in fuller form, with original language, translation, and commentary. Their work showed that Aboriginal song-poetry had subtle form and artistry, not simply mythic content.

Three Faces of Love of 1976 showed how Aboriginal song-poetry could be presented with linguistic and cultural care. Later writing in Aboriginal English used short sentences, repetition with variation, dignity, and humour, as in Sally Morgan's My Place and Paddy Roe's Gularabulu.

From the 1970s and 1980s, Aboriginal writers used formal English and Aboriginal English to maintain oral traditions and make modern claims. Maureen Watson kept oral performance alive through radio, television, and school readings.

The century after British settlement began with practical reports. After 1788, writing sent to England focused on strange flora, fauna, exploration, new rivers, and discoveries rather than ordinary convict life. Early local pride appeared in Charles Tompson and William Wentworth's Australasia of 1823, while Barron Field's First Fruits of Australian Poetry of 1819 treated the colony with comic disbelief.

## The Colonial and Nationalist Period

By the later 19th century, fiction, journalism, and exploration narratives gave colonial writing stronger forms. Rolf Boldrewood's Robbery Under Arms of 1888 became a classic partly because its narrator, Dick Marston, used Australian vernacular and expressed stoic resignation.

Other early novelists included Rosa Praed, whose Policy and Passion of 1881 explored Queensland political life, and Ada Cambridge. Explorer journals by Charles Sturt, Edward John Eyre, and Thomas Livingstone Mitchell also mattered as literature, and Eyre and Ludwig Leichhardt later influenced Patrick White's Voss.

The centenary year 1888 encouraged nationalism, federation feeling, and the influence of the Bulletin, founded in 1880. Its call for “Australia for the Australians” urged writers to celebrate the bush worker, egalitarian mateship, vernacular speech, and local democratic myth.

Writers linked with nationalist expansion included Henry Lawson, A.B. Paterson, Joseph Furphy, and Miles Franklin. Their works used bush settings, humour, hardship, irony, and ordinary Australian speech to imagine a national literature distinct from English models.

The Jindyworobak movement sought a more authentically Australian literature by emphasizing Aboriginal presence, the Outback, social protest, and the spirit of place. Xavier Herbert's Capricornia displayed many of these concerns before world events overtook the movement.

From 1940 to 1970, literary magazines such as Southerly and Meanjin supported Australian writing, while international interest grew. The Ern Malley ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ern_Malley_hoax)) hoax of 1944, created by James McAuley and Harold Stewart, showed conflict over experimental verse and literary standards.

Patrick White, Nobel Prize winner in 1973, became the major modern Australian novelist. His novels The Tree of Man, Voss, and Riders in the Chariot used Australian themes and landscapes while rejecting narrow nationalism through epic scope, symbolic force, and questions of belief.

## Postwar Poetry and Prose

Postwar poets and writers widened the range of Australian literature. Martin Boyd explored family, past, and moral memory, while poets such as Judith Wright, David Campbell, Rosemary Dobson, Vivian Smith, and Gwen Harwood developed meditative, lyric, satiric, and vernacular modes.

Australian drama gained stronger recognition with Douglas Stewart's radio plays and especially Ray Lawler's Summer of the Seventeenth Doll in 1955. Its naturalistic idiom, Australian attitudes, and universal themes helped begin a revival followed by Alan Seymour and Patrick White's plays.

Nonfiction also expanded through histories, biographies, and autobiographical works where the self was tied to family and society. Judith Wright's The Generations of Men, Mary Durack's Kings in Grass Castles, Martin Boyd's Day of My Delight, and Hal Porter's The Watcher on the Cast-Iron Balcony linked personal memory with national history.

From the 1980s into the 1990s, women writers such as Janette Turner Hospital, Kate Grenville, Helen Garner, Glenda Adams, Barbara Hanrahan, and Elizabeth Jolley became prominent. Grenville mixed darkness and comedy, Garner moved between fiction and factual writing, and Jolley produced enigmatic fiction.

Male writers such as Brian Castro, Robert Drewe, David Foster, and Tim Winton also became important. Winton's Cloudstreet and Foster's The Glade Within the Grove showed continued energy in fiction.

Late 20th-century poetry included Les Murray's allusive, humane search for spiritual meaning in ordinary life, Bruce Dawe's contemporary Australian voice, Robert Gray's spare lyricism, and more experimental work by Robert Adamson and John Tranter.

Drama remained socially alert through David Williamson, whose topical plays caught Australian idiom, prejudice, politics, and social attitudes while touching deeper collective identity. Australian literature by this period had moved from colonial report and nationalist bush myth into many forms of fiction, poetry, theatre, autobiography, and Aboriginal self-expression.

## Key Events & Developments

- 1788: British settlement of New South Wales began the first century of colonial writing.
- 1819: Barron Field's First Fruits of Australian Poetry appeared.
- 1880: The Bulletin began promoting nationalist Australian writing.
- 1888: The centenary encouraged literary reassessment and nationalist feeling.
- 1944: The Ern Malley ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ern_Malley_hoax)) hoax exposed disputes over experimental poetry.
- 1955: Ray Lawler's Summer of the Seventeenth Doll helped revive Australian drama.
- 1973: Patrick White won the Nobel Prize.
- 1980s-1990s: Aboriginal, women, experimental, and contemporary voices broadened Australian literature.

## Key People

- Henry Lawson ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henry_Lawson)): Nationalist writer associated with bush life, ordinary hardship, and Australian idiom.
- A.B. Paterson ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Banjo_Paterson)): Writer linked with bush balladry and nationalist literary culture.
- Patrick White ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Patrick_White)): Major modern novelist whose work gave Australian themes international literary force.
- Judith Wright ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Judith_Wright)): Poet and prose writer whose work joined lyric intelligence with family and social history.
- Ray Lawler ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ray_Lawler)): Dramatist whose Summer of the Seventeenth Doll brought Australian drama wider acclaim.
- Kate Grenville ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kate_Grenville)), Helen Garner ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Helen_Garner)), and Janette Turner Hospital ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Janette_Turner_Hospital)): Prominent late-century fiction writers.
- Les Murray: Poet concerned with ordinary life, spiritual reality, and the importance of poetry.

## Interesting Facts

- Australian literature ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Australian_literature)) often values endurance and representative experience more than solitary heroism.
- Aboriginal English uses repetition with variation and can carry dignity, humour, and cultural memory.
- Edward John Eyre's explorer narrative helped inspire Patrick White's Voss.
- The Bulletin promoted the slogan “Australia for the Australians.”
- The Ern Malley ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ern_Malley_hoax)) poems were invented as a parody but became a major literary controversy.
