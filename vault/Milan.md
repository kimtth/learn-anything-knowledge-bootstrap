# Milan

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> Milan is a wealthy northern Italian metropolis whose manufacturing, finance, transport, fashion, media, and cultural institutions give it unusual national influence. Its history and civic self-image connect its urban form with the idea of Italy's practical and innovative moral capital.

## Italian Milano

![[attachments/milan_001.webp|420]]

*Milan Cathedral*

Milan, or Milano, is the capital of Milano province and of Lombardy in northern [[Italy]]. It is Italy's leading financial centre ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Financial_centre)) and its most prosperous manufacturing and commercial city.

The city has often seen itself as a possible national capital, even though Italian unity was completed through Turin and [[Rome]]. Milan's claim rests on industry, finance, innovation, efficiency, and the idea that it is Italy's moral capital.

Milan is one of Italy's richest cities and one of the richest in [[Europe]]. Its practical image still attracted writers, artists, intellectuals, mass media, fashion, and debate about Italy's future.

## Landscape

![[attachments/milan_002.webp|420]]

*Milan skyline*

Milan stands in the Po Basin, between the Ticino and Adda rivers, about 400 feet above sea level. The Alps rise to the north, while the south grades toward the Po River, marshes, groves, and rice fields.

The city developed near a boundary between drier northern land and wetter southern lowland. Early inhabitants used small watercourses such as the Seveso, Nirone, Lambro, and Olona for defense.

## Climate

Milan has a continental climate ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continental_climate)) with chilly, damp winters and hot, humid summers. Snow can fall from December to February, spring is often rainy, and winter fog is characteristic of the Po Basin.

Industrial change reduced some older fog-forming conditions, especially after rice fields disappeared from southern neighbourhoods and heavy industry declined. At the same time, continuous urban growth reduced air circulation, and traffic produced gray smog.

Milan's layout records repeated historical change. Ancient Mediolanum near the present Duomo influenced later roads, while medieval, Spanish, Napoleonic, and modern rings can still be traced in streets and boulevards.

The city centre is shaped by the Sforzesco Castle, Piazza Mercanti, Piazza del Duomo, and Piazza Cordusio. Modern planning added wide avenues, business districts, and buildings such as the Pirelli Building and Velasca Tower.

Industrial districts expanded beyond the city, especially toward Sesto San Giovanni. Milan now forms part of a large conurbation across the Po Basin, linking toward Turin and [[Venice]].

## Suburbs

![[attachments/milan_003.webp|420]]

*Sforza Castle in Milan*

Suburban centres such as Bollate, Novate Milanese, Cusano Milanino, Cinisello Balsamo, Sesto San Giovanni, and Monza are connected to the historic core by major arteries. Their growth shows how Milan's economy and population spread beyond the old municipal boundary.

## People

Milan grew rapidly after [[World Wars (World War II)|World War II]], adding about 400,000 people in the 15 years after 1950. Much of this growth came from migrants from southern and northeastern Italy who sought factory work during the economic miracle of 1958 to 1963.

Population pressure created self-built villages and urban expansion, but growth slowed in the 1970s. Birth rates fell, the population aged, and migration increasingly came from abroad, including Morocco, [[Egypt]], Senegal, the [[Philippines]], [[Ecuador]], [[China]], Albania, and eastern Europe.

## Economy

Milan's economy grew from its position at a traffic and market crossroads in the Po River valley and on routes between [[Italy]] and northern Europe. Wholesale markets, export trade, and links between irrigated southern plains and poorer northern agriculture helped make it Italy's chief economic centre.

By the late 20th century, services had overtaken manufacturing. Milan remained important for exports, finance, trade, fashion, communications, design, and high-technology industries.

## Manufacturing

Mechanical industries dominated Milan during the first half of the 20th century. Automobiles such as Alfa Romeo, scooters such as Lambretta, aircraft, electrical appliances, rail materials, metalworking, rubber from Pirelli, chemicals, and textiles all shaped the industrial city.

After [[World Wars (World War II)|World War II]], industrial growth moved mainly to peripheral areas, while the city centre became known for ready-made clothing and designer fashion. Designers such as Armani, Versace, Ferre, Prada, Dolce & Gabbana, and Missoni helped make Milan a global fashion centre.

Milan became Italy's leading postindustrial city. Banks, the Borsa Italiana, advertising, wholesale and retail trade, furniture and graphic design, publishing, newspapers, magazines, private television, telecommunications, software, Internet design, and specialized trade fairs all reinforced its service economy.

## Transportation

Milan is a national transportation focus. Roads, railways, transalpine tunnels, airports, and mass transit connect it with Italy and Europe, while the Central Station is a major railway site.

Highways such as the Autostrada del Sole, international airports at Malpensa and Linate, and nearby airports at Bergamo and Brescia support regional and international movement. Local buses, trams, and subways serve the metropolitan area; the first subway line opened in 1964.

Private car ownership brought traffic, parking problems, and pollution. Since the early 1980s, officials have sometimes restricted traffic to reduce smog.

## Administration

Milan is governed through municipal, provincial, and regional structures under the Republic of Italy. The elected city government manages education, traffic, roads, and local transport; the province handles surrounding infrastructure and cultural policy; and Lombardy's regional government gained power after national devolution.

After [[World Wars (World War II)|World War II]], city politics were shaped by the Christian Democratic Party, the Italian Socialist Party, centre-left alliances, and later centre-right strength. The shift in the 1990s partly reflected the decline of Milan's old working-class base.

The Duomo, begun in 1386, is Milan's most famous monument and one of Europe's largest churches. Its Gothic marble exterior, huge windows, pinnacles, turrets, and more than 3,000 statues dominate the city centre.

Major cultural institutions include the Palazzo di Brera, Pinacoteca di Brera, Braidense National Library, Ambrosian Library, museums, galleries, and the restored Santa Maria delle Grazie, home of [[Leonardo da Vinci]]'s The Last Supper.

## University

Milan's higher education includes the Catholic University of the Sacred Heart, Bocconi University, Milan Polytechnic, IULM University, the University of Milan, and the Milan Conservatory.

The city is also a major centre for performance and sport. La Scala, built in 1776 to 1778, reopened after wartime damage in 1946. San Siro hosts AC Milan and Inter Milan, while Monza is known for Grand Prix racing.

## History

A Gaulish settlement was founded on the site about 600 BC. Rome conquered Mediolanum in 222 BC, and by the late empire it became one of the West's leading cities, serving as an imperial residence and administrative centre.

The city suffered devastation from Attila in 452 and the Goths in 539, but revived by the 10th century. Under archbishops such as Ansperto da Biassono and Ariberto da Antimiano, religious authority became political power, helping prepare Milan's later greatness.

In 1045, Milan became a commune. It fought neighbouring Lombard cities and then Frederick I Barbarossa, who destroyed the city in 1162. Rebuilt under the Lombard League, Milan helped defeat Barbarossa at Legnano in 1176 and regained privileges through the 1183 Peace of Constance.

Medieval politics shifted from commune to lordship. The della Torre family led popular Guelf forces, while the Visconti led aristocratic Ghibellines. After Ottone Visconti defeated the Torriani at Desio in 1277, Visconti rule expanded, and Gian Galeazzo Visconti became duke in 1395.

Francesco Sforza founded a new dynasty in 1450, opening a Renaissance period strengthened by silk and court culture. Foreign wars then brought French, Swiss-backed Sforza, and finally Habsburg rule; in 1535, Milan passed under Charles V.

Spanish rule from the 16th to early 18th century brought stagnation and decline, while the 1630 plague later became central to Alessandro Manzoni's The Betrothed. Austrian rule after 1706 eventually produced enlightened growth, reform, Neoclassical architecture, and figures such as Cesare Beccaria and Pietro Verri.

Napoleon Bonaparte entered Milan in 1796, and the city became capital of his Kingdom of Italy in 1805. Austrian authority returned after 1814, but Milan became a key centre of the Risorgimento. The Five Days uprising in 1848 briefly expelled Austrian forces, and victory after the 1859 Battle of Magenta linked Milan to the new Italian state.

In the late 19th century, Milan became a centre of socialist reform, cooperatives, unions, and working-class politics, with figures such as Filippo Turati, Anna Kuliscioff, and Claudio Treves. After [[World Wars (World War I)|World War I]], strikes, factory occupations, and anti-socialist forces helped make Milan one birthplace of fascism.

During [[World Wars (World War II)|World War II]], German occupation made Milan a centre of resistance, and Italian partisans liberated it before Allied arrival in April 1945. Postwar politics, the economic miracle, student protest, the 1969 Piazza Fontana bombing, terrorism, depression, and the 1980s boom all reshaped the city.

The 1990s corruption investigations by Milanese magistrates exposed Tangentopoli, damaging Milan's moral-capital image and helping dissolve Italy's Cold War party system. Silvio Berlusconi, based in Milanese business, media, and football, used the city as a foundation for Forza Italia and national political power.

By the early 21st century, Milan had become a postindustrial powerhouse of fashion, design, finance, business services, media, publishing, and redevelopment.
