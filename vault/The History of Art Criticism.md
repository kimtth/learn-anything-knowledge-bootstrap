## Introduction

![[attachments/art_criticism_001.webp|420]]

*Portrait of Denis Diderot*

Art criticism ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Art_criticism)) is the analysis and evaluation of works of art. It is often tied to theory because critics interpret particular works from a broader perspective and try to establish their meaning and place in art history.

Many cultures have traditions of art evaluation. African traditions may value beauty, order, form, utility, and communal or spiritual function. Islamic traditions include historiographical writing on calligraphy, woodwork, glass, metalwork, textiles, and decorative arts.

Chinese art criticism reaches back to Xie He, whose Six Principles included qi yun sheng dong, or spirit resonance and life-motion. Chinese literati also wrote artist biographies and evaluations.

The Western tradition developed a distinctive critical discipline through theory. Its analyses often defended or attacked current artistic practice, and art criticism grew alongside Western aesthetics, becoming fully formed in the 18th century.

## Ancient and Medieval Antecedents

Ancient philosophers theorized about art before art criticism became a distinct discipline. [[Plato and Platonism (Plato)|Plato]] treated art as imitation and illusion, a kind of knowledge inferior to truth.

In the Republic, Plato described the painter as a maker of appearances, removed from true ideas. A painting of a carpenter could deceive viewers even though the artist had no carpenter's knowledge.

Plato's view can be called metaphysical criticism: art simplifies and communicates ideas but remains far from absolute truth. It is imitation, image-making, and potentially deception.

[[Aristotle and Aristotelianism|Aristotle]] also saw art as imitation, but in the Poetics he treated imitation as natural to humans and morally important because art represents character and action. His approach made art a structured human activity rather than merely false appearance.

## Classicism and the Academy

In 17th-century France, Nicolas Poussin's ideas about the grand manner shaped classicist criticism. He favoured grand subjects such as battles, heroic actions, and religious themes, and rejected base subjects and distracting details.

Andre Felibien canonized Poussin's ideas, and Charles Le Brun turned them into academic rules at the French Academy in Rome. The Academy taught that Classical art, not nature alone, was the proper model.

Academic Classicism valued restraint, moderation, taste, hierarchy, noble subjects, and disciplined composition. But it could become authoritarian and formulaic, turning rational theory into empty rules.

Charles Perrault challenged ancient authority by arguing for modern superiority in Parallels Between the Ancients and the Moderns. This idea of artistic progress opposed Poussin's deep debt to antiquity.

## Enlightenment Criticism

![[attachments/art_criticism_002.webp|420]]

*Painting of a Paris Salon exhibition*

Johann Joachim Winckelmann transformed art history and criticism through The History of Ancient Art. He idealized Greek art for noble simplicity and quiet grandeur and helped inspire Neoclassicism.

Winckelmann developed the idea of period style: art grows, flowers, matures, and decays through organic historical stages. His model suggested that visual forms have discernible historical trajectories.

Denis Diderot opposed purely rule-based criticism. Reviewing Salons from 1759 to 1781, he responded to increasingly informal, romantic, and unruly art with personal, moral, and imaginative criticism.

Diderot saw that loosened rules created new critical freedom. If art no longer followed fixed standards, criticism could become more subjective, idiosyncratic, theatrical, and responsive to moral and emotional effect.

## Modernist Art History and Formal Lines

In the early 20th century, institutions shaped modern art history. Alfred H. Barr of the Museum of Modern Art made a famous diagram of avant-garde development through 1935.

Barr placed Cubism at the centre, deriving it from Cezanne, Neo-Impressionism, and Henri Rousseau and linking it to Suprematism, Constructivism, De Stijl, and abstract art. German Expressionism, Dada, and Surrealism were pushed aside.

Barr's diagram became an orthodox formal high line, but later it looked narrow and institutional. It showed how museums could legislate art history, much as academies had done earlier.

## Abstract Expressionism and American Criticism

After World War II, avant-garde leadership shifted from Europe to the United States. Abstract Expressionism brought American art to international prominence and prompted intense criticism.

Newspapers and magazines such as the New York Times and Time covered art events. Edward Alden Jewell was puzzled by abstraction, while John Canaday was skeptical and sometimes hostile.

Artists became critics of their own work. In 1943, Adolph Gottlieb and Mark Rothko published a statement defending their obscure paintings and arguing that artists often had to explain themselves against misunderstanding.

In 1961, artists and supporters attacked Canaday's insulting views of contemporary art. The exchange showed that criticism had real public power and that artists took critical reception seriously.

## Clement Greenberg and Formalism

Clement Greenberg became the dominant mid-20th-century American critic and a standard-bearer of formalism. He defended abstract artists such as Jackson Pollock, David Smith, Clyfford Still, Barnett Newman, Mark Rothko, Morris Louis, and Jules Olitski.

Greenberg developed ideas from Clive Bell and Roger Fry, especially the importance of significant form and aesthetic unity. He treated literary and anecdotal content as secondary or surplus.

For Greenberg, modern art advanced by purifying each medium. Painting should explore flatness, colour, surface, and material conditions, eliminating elements borrowed from literature, illusion, or sculpture.

His essays in Art and Culture made abstraction seem autonomous, self-sufficient, and historically necessary. But his formalism also became a new orthodoxy, vulnerable to the same institutional rigidity as academic classicism.

## Rosenberg, Steinberg, and Other Criteria

Harold Rosenberg opposed Greenberg's formalism through existential and lifeworld criticism. He described the canvas as an arena in which to act, where the painting was not simply a picture but an event.

Rosenberg's idea of Action painting emphasized self-creation, risk, process, anxiety, and encounter between painter and material. Art criticism became a way to perceive the self acting through art.

Leo Steinberg criticized Greenberg's formalist ethic in Other Criteria. He argued that critics should not ignore intention, culture, irony, iconography, and historical meaning.

Other critics and historians, including Meyer Schapiro, Steinberg, Robert Rosenblum, and others, remained passionate, partisan, political, and culturally engaged. Schapiro stressed the deep connection between art and the totality of culture.

## Late 20th-Century Pluralism

By the late 20th century, art criticism became more plural and less unified by single theory. Critics such as Michael Kimmelman, Kim Levin, and Robert Pincus-Witten often responded to museum and gallery art rather than following one system.

Some critics were devoted to particular movements. Nicolas Calas tracked Surrealism, while artist-critics such as Donald Judd and Patrick Heron wrote to advocate their own art against others.

Postmodernism claimed there is no single objective set of values or perspective. In the 1980s and 1990s, feminist, psychoanalytic, Marxist, semiotic, institutional, philosophical, technological, and poststructural approaches multiplied.

Arthur Danto argued that art objects approached zero as theory approached infinity, suggesting art had become philosophy. This gave conceptual art a central place and renewed Hegelian ideas about the end of art.

Donald Kuspit used psychoanalysis, philosophy, and art history to examine emotional depth and the state of the self in modern and postmodern art. Douglas Davis put technology at the centre of art's future.

## Key Events & Developments

- Ancient Greece: Plato and Aristotle established major philosophical approaches to art.
- 1665: French Academy in Rome helped institutionalize classicist rules.
- 1759-1781: Diderot reviewed Salons and modeled modern subjective criticism.
- 1764: Winckelmann published The History of Ancient Art.
- 1935: Barr's diagram helped canonize a formal modernist lineage.
- 1943: Gottlieb and Rothko published a statement defending abstract painting.
- 1961: Greenberg's Art and Culture codified formalist modernism.
- 1972: Steinberg's Other Criteria criticized Greenbergian formalism.
- 1980s-1990s: Postmodern pluralism reshaped art criticism.

## Interesting Facts

- Art criticism ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Art_criticism)) became a distinct Western discipline only around the 18th century.
- Barr's diagram made Cubism the central path to abstraction but minimized other movements.
- Greenberg defended Abstract Expressionism before it was widely accepted.
- Rosenberg saw the canvas as an arena for action, not only an image surface.
- Postmodern criticism replaced one dominant standard with many competing viewpoints.
