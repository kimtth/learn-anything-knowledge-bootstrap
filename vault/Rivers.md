# Rivers

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> Rivers are channelled flows that cycle water and sediment through landscapes, connect drainage basins with oceans, and reshape land over geologic time. The source relates their hydrology, erosion, settlement, agriculture, navigation, industry, hydroelectricity, networks, and environmental pressures.

## Introduction

Rivers ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/River)) are natural streams of water flowing in channels with defined banks, though modern usage also includes multichannel, intermittent, ephemeral, and nearly bankless channels. The central idea is channeled surface flow. Smaller streams may be called brooks, branches, burns, creeks, arroyos, washes, rills, or runnels depending on region and size.

Rivers are fed by precipitation, overland runoff, springs, seepage, and meltwater from snowfields and glaciers. They lose water through seepage into aquifers, percolation, and evaporation. The balance between input and loss sustains discharge, or streamflow.

Only a tiny share of Earth's water is in river channels. About 97 percent of all water is in oceans, about three-quarters of fresh water is in land ice, and nearly all the rest is groundwater. River channels contain only about 0.025 percent of fresh water, yet they are vital because water constantly cycles through rivers, land ice, soil, lakes, and groundwater.

Rivers deliver water and sediment to oceans. They are far more effective than coastal erosion in moving rock debris to the sea. Sediment travels as bed load, suspended load, and dissolved load, and it may be stored temporarily in riffles, bars, floodplains, levees, alluvial fans, terraces, deltas, and estuary fills.

Because water is constantly exchanged among atmosphere, ice, soil, groundwater, lakes, rivers, and oceans, a small channel volume can do enormous work. Mean river discharge to the sea balances much of the precipitation delivered over land, and the sediment moved by rivers represents a persistent lowering of continents over geologic time.

## Sediment and River Work

Rock debris enters river systems as eroded fragments or dissolved material. Downstream, solid particles become systematically smaller and rounder. Coarse bed load rarely reaches the sea except in steep or high-latitude settings, while fine suspended and dissolved material can travel long distances.

Sediment delivery lowers land surfaces over long time. Average global rates are enough to remove existing continental relief over tens of millions of years. Some rivers, especially those draining weak rocks such as the Loess Plateau tributaries of the Huang He, have exceptionally high sediment yields.

## Importance of Rivers

River valleys ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Valley)) supported many early permanent settlements. Sedentary settlement near ancient Jericho began about 10,000 years ago. The Tigris-Euphrates and Nile valleys supported settlement by at least 6000 BP, the Indus valley civilization by about 4500 BP, and Ganges valley civilization by about 3000 BP.

Rivers made agriculture ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agriculture)), irrigation, surplus food, and urban life possible. Floodplains provided fertile land, while rivers supplied water and transport. In dry regions, dependence on rivers may have been forced by postglacial desiccation from about 12,000 or 10,000 BP onward.

Rivers also shaped [[Cities|cities]] in Europe and elsewhere. Many national and provincial capitals developed on sizable rivers, including Vienna, Budapest, and Belgrade on the Danube. In North America and northern Asia, river systems guided exploration, conquest, settlement, and movement across interior regions.

River corridors joined economic and political space before roads and railways could do the same work. Portages between adjacent drainage systems helped explorers and settlers cross divides, while fall lines, estuaries, and confluences became preferred sites for towns because they combined fresh water, transport, power, defensible ground, and exchange between inland and maritime routes.

## Trade, Agriculture, and Industry

River use changed as [[Navigation|navigation]], ship size, canals, and industry changed. External freight moved increasingly to estuarine ports as ocean vessels grew larger. The Amazon remains navigable by ocean ships for about 3,700 kilometres, the Yangtze for about 1,000 kilometres, and the St. Lawrence Seaway for about 2,100 kilometres.

Irrigation has a history of at least 5,000 years and now affects about 2,000,000 square kilometres of land. Three-quarters of this irrigated land lies in East and South Asia, and two-fifths in mainland China. The Indus valley uses barrages and distributor canals along floodplains and alluvial fans.

Modern water demand adds competition among farms, cities, power plants, navigation, fisheries, and ecosystems. In dry basins, open-channel supply becomes more valuable as groundwater declines and population rises. Irrigation can stabilize harvests, but it can also spread salinity, alter flood rhythms, and make downstream users dependent on upstream storage and releases.

Rivers supply industry through water transport, timber flotation, cooling water, manufacturing, and hydroelectric power. Hydroelectric generation began more than a century ago, but most installations were built after 1950. Many industrial nations have developed much of their hydropower potential.

## Environmental Problems

Heavy river use creates serious environmental problems. Navigation improvements deepen and straighten channels, sometimes lowering surrounding water tables and damaging agriculture. Canalization with locks and dams can disrupt river ecosystems.

Pollution is a wider problem. Pesticides, herbicides, phosphates, and nitrates enter rivers through runoff and groundwater seepage. Biocides can kill fish, especially in slow-flowing rivers. Fertilizers can cause eutrophication, producing algal blooms that later decay and deplete oxygen.

When oxygen falls, river water can become foul, unfit for drinking, and hostile to fish and other organisms. Industrial cooling water, chemicals, urban waste, and agricultural runoff all show how river systems connect human activity across entire drainage basins.

## Drainage Networks and Morphometry

![[attachments/rivers_001.png|420]]

*Diagram of river meanders and cutoffs*

Drainage networks can be studied through stream order, number, length, slope, area, and discharge. Horton's laws state that stream numbers tend to form an inverse geometric series by order and average stream lengths tend to form a direct geometric series. These relations appear as straight lines on logarithmic plots.

Network morphometry allows comparison among basins with different geology or climate. Stream width, discharge, main-stem length, drainage area, and channel slope can often be expressed as exponential or power functions of order or area. These patterns connect river networks to general growth laws and topology.

Such measurements matter because rivers are networks, not isolated channels. A trunk stream's flood, sediment load, and shape depend on thousands of tributaries, hillslopes, aquifers, soils, and human alterations upstream. Morphometry provides a way to compare basins that look different on maps but obey similar scaling relations.

## Waterfalls

Waterfalls ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Waterfall)), or cataracts, occur where a river channel steepens abruptly and water drops vertically or nearly so. Smaller, gentler falls are cascades, and turbulent steep reaches are rapids. Waterfalls have strong erosive power and tend over time to smooth the river's longitudinal profile.

Falling water often scours a plunge pool at the base of a falls. A waterfall may migrate upstream by headward erosion or bevel the reach downward. The rate depends on height, discharge, rock type, structure, time, and the shape of the crest.

World waterfalls cluster in three main settings: margins of high plateaus, fall lines between resistant crystalline rocks and weaker coastal sediments, and high mountains, especially formerly glaciated ones. Deserts, low plains, and ice-covered polar regions are less favourable.

Waterfalls can also be classified by process. Some reflect discordant profiles produced by faulting, uplift, glaciation, karst springs, or hanging valleys; others form where resistant rock overlies weaker beds and differential erosion undercuts a cap rock; still others are created by natural dams, lava, landslides, or other constructional barriers.

High-plateau falls include Angel Falls in Venezuela, dropping 979 metres, Tugela Falls in South Africa at 948 metres, and Victoria Falls on the Zimbabwe-Zambia border at 108 metres with large flow. Former Guaira Falls on the Parana had a huge average discharge before being submerged by Itaipu Dam in 1982.

Fall-line and glaciated examples include Jog Falls in India, Paulo Afonso Falls in Brazil, Churchill Falls in Labrador, Yosemite Falls in California, Yellowstone Falls in Wyoming, Sutherland Falls in New Zealand, and Krimmler Waterfall in Austria. Cities such as Philadelphia, Baltimore, and Washington developed near fall-line barriers to navigation.

## Niagara and Waterfall Recession

Niagara Falls shows how waterfalls migrate. Horseshoe Falls has retreated about 12 kilometres in roughly 12,500 years, averaging about one metre per year. Surveyed rates varied, with about 1.2 metres per year in 1842-75 and 2 metres per year in 1875-1905.

The American Falls retreat much more slowly, about 0.08 metre per year, while Iceland's Gullfoss has averaged about 0.25 metre per year. Differences reflect rock resistance, jointing, crest shape, flow distribution, and cap-rock structure.

## Streamflow and Flooding

Discharge is calculated from flow velocity and cross-sectional area. Stage measurements record water height, but discharge requires velocity observations. Because repeated velocity measurement on large rivers is difficult, hydrologists establish rating curves relating water level to discharge.

Rating curves can change when channels scour, fill, are embanked, or have mobile beds. During a flood, the stage-discharge relation may differ between rising and falling water, forming a hysteresis loop. Floods in hydrology are peak discharges, whether or not water overtops the valley floor.

## Sediment Yield and Load

Sediment yield is the amount of erosional debris exported from a drainage basin, measured by volume or weight per unit area per year. Sediment load is the material actually transported by the stream. It includes bed load, suspended load, and dissolved load.

The Colorado River at Grand Canyon shows how climate can change sediment yield. After 1940, annual sediment load was 50,000,000 to 100,000,000 tons lower than expected from earlier data. Drought reduced runoff from weak-rock, high-sediment areas in the Colorado Plateau while harder-rock runoff areas remained more normal.

Measuring sediment is difficult because discharge, bed form, grain size, and channel storage change through storms and seasons. A river may move boulders and cobbles near the bed, carry silts and clays in suspension, and transport dissolved chemicals invisibly, so total load requires combining samples, ratings, and long records.

Human land use also alters sediment yield. In the Piedmont of the eastern United States, forested yields were about 37 tons per square kilometre. Farming after 1820 raised yields, intense farming in 1850-1930 reached about 310 tons per square kilometre, and later forest regrowth reduced them before urban construction raised them again.

## Rivers as Agents of Landscape Evolution

Rivers ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/River)) are among the most important agents shaping landscapes. Landforms reflect geology, process, and time, a view associated with William Morris Davis. Wind may dominate deserts and volcanoes may shape volcanic regions, but fluvial processes occur almost everywhere that surface runoff exists.

Sediment-laden water erodes, transports, and deposits material. Some fluvial landforms are mainly erosional, others mainly depositional, and many combine both. Rivers turn geologic surfaces into valleys, floodplains, terraces, alluvial fans, deltas, and estuaries.

## Floodplains

Floodplains ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Floodplain)) are flat surfaces beside river channels, occupying much of valley bottoms and underlain by alluvium deposited by the current river. They are partly or totally inundated during floods and are therefore both landforms and active parts of the river system.

Floodplains store floodwater and reduce downstream peak discharge. During the 1937 Ohio River flood, water stored on floodplains equaled about 2.3 times the volume of Lake Mead and represented roughly 57 percent of direct runoff. Without this storage, flood crests would have been much higher downstream.

Floodplains also record channel migration. Lateral erosion on outer bends, deposition on point bars, overbank silt, natural levees, abandoned channels, and backswamps build surfaces that look stable between floods but remain part of the river. Embankments can protect selected land while reducing storage and raising risk elsewhere.

## Deltas

Deltas ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/River_delta)) are depositional plains formed where rivers enter standing water. Herodotus named the Nile mouth deposit because its shape resembled the Greek letter delta, but many deltas are not triangular. Their shape depends on sediment supply, river discharge, sediment size, waves, currents, tides, basin climate, lithology, tectonics, and basin size.

A delta forms where river-borne sediment accumulates faster than shoreline processes can remove it. The shoreline becomes a battleground between sediment delivery and ocean energy. Deltas occur on all landmasses, in all climates, and in many geologic settings.

## Estuaries

Estuaries ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Estuary)) are partly enclosed coastal waters where downstream river flow mixes with seawater. Their landward limit is often defined by salinity, though the exact boundary changes with tidal range, river chemistry, and physical conditions. The word comes from Latin terms linked to tide and boiling, reflecting the meeting of tidal and river flow.

Estuaries are important for shipping, commerce, fishing, and early settlement. Many seaports began at the seaward edge of river systems. Fish and shellfish often spend important life stages in estuarine environments, but pollutants can accumulate in bottom sediments and harm marine life.

Most modern estuaries formed after global sea level began rising about 18,000 years ago as the Wisconsin Glacial Stage waned. When ice sheets were largest, sea level was lower and rivers cut valleys farther seaward. Rising seas drowned lower valleys and created estuarine basins.

## River Systems Through Time

River systems have existed since continental surfaces received enough precipitation to sustain runoff. The Precambrian record is difficult because many rocks are metamorphosed, but cross-bedded shallow-water sands preserve evidence of ancient flow. Cambrian and later rocks contain many deltaic sandstones deposited by rivers.

Existing river systems cannot be older than the latest emergence of their basins above sea level, but some may have histories of 100,000,000 years or more. Over geologic time, rivers shift drainage areas, network patterns, profiles, and channel geometry.

Stream capture occurs when a stronger river erodes headward and diverts part of a neighbouring drainage system. Competing rivers can shift divides, especially where height differences are large. This process helps explain changes in river courses and drainage networks through time.

River history is therefore not simply a story of gradual downcutting. Basins expand, shrink, reverse, and reconnect as uplift, sea-level change, climate, sediment supply, and capture alter the balance among neighbouring systems. Existing channels may preserve traces of older drainage patterns even after the controlling landscape has changed.

River channels also change through meandering, braiding, avulsion, bank erosion, and bar building. A meandering river migrates laterally by eroding outer banks and depositing point bars on inner bends, while braided rivers split around shifting bars where sediment supply is high or banks are easily eroded. Avulsion can suddenly move flow to a new course across a floodplain or delta.

Human engineering often tries to fix these naturally mobile systems. Levees, dams, channelization, dredging, reservoirs, and diversions can reduce some floods or improve navigation, but they may also trap sediment, shrink deltas, lower floodplain fertility, block fish migration, and transfer risk downstream.

## Key Events & Developments

- 10,000 years ago: Permanent settlement began near ancient Jericho, showing the early importance of river valleys.
- 6000 BP: Settlements were established in the Tigris-Euphrates and Nile valleys.
- 1950 onward: Most modern hydroelectric installations were built after this period.

## Interesting Facts

- River channels ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Channel_%28geography%29)) hold only a tiny fraction of Earth's fresh water but move immense water and sediment each year.
- Floodplains ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Floodplain)) are natural reservoirs that can greatly reduce downstream flood peaks.
- Waterfalls ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Waterfall)) are temporary features in the long effort of rivers to smooth their profiles.
- Deltas ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/River_delta)) can form many shapes; the name no longer requires a triangular outline.
- Estuaries ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Estuary)) are both river mouths and marine nurseries, making them economically and ecologically important.
