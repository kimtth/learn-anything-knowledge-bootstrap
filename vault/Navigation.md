# Navigation

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> Navigation determines a craft's position, course, and distance so that ships and aircraft can reach destinations safely and efficiently. The account traces maritime observation, charts, compasses, dead reckoning, speed measurement, modern instruments, and collision-avoidance systems as traffic and velocities increased.

## Introduction

Navigation ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Navigation)) is the science of directing a craft by determining position, course, and distance traveled. It serves several goals: reaching a destination, avoiding collision, conserving fuel, and meeting schedules. The word comes from Latin navis, ship, and agere, to drive. Early mariners built navigation by observing routes, winds, currents, hazards, havens, and landfalls, then recording them so voyages could be repeated and extended.

As traffic grew, collision avoidance became as important as finding the way. Ships are large and slow to turn, so busy ports use separate lanes for incoming and outgoing traffic. Aircraft move so fast that visual avoidance is often inadequate, so civil air navigation depends heavily on air traffic controllers who assign safe paths and separation.

## Development of Marine Navigation

Early marine navigation ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marine_navigation)) began with piloting, or steering between familiar landmarks near shore. Mariners later crossed open water long before modern instruments: Crete and Egypt traded across about 300 miles more than 25 centuries before the Christian era; Phoenicians reached Cornwall by 600 BC; Irish seafarers reached northern Atlantic islands before the 10th century AD; Vikings settled Greenland and reached North America; Polynesians reached Hawaii from the Marquesas by about AD 400.

## Direction Finding

Early direction finding used Sun, stars, shadows, and regular celestial motion. East and west aligned with sunrise and sunset, while noonday shadows helped mark north and south. At night, stars rising in the east and setting in the west, plus the apparent rotation around the Pole Star in the Northern Hemisphere, helped navigators maintain direction beyond sight of land.

## Marine Charts

![[attachments/navigation_001.webp|420]]

*Fifteenth-century portolan chart by Gabriel de Vallseca*

Classical coastal pilot books developed over about 15 centuries into the portolano, or portolan chart, of the Middle Ages. Portolans such as Lo compasso da navigare in 1296 gave directions using compass points. Mediterranean charts, often made in Genoa, Venice, and Majorca, placed north at the top, used distance scales, and showed coloured rhumb lines so pilots could determine a constant bearing between ports.

## The Mercator Chart

Portuguese voyages down Africa under Prince Henry the Navigator exposed the limits of Mediterranean chart assumptions. Over long distances, rhumb lines could not be treated as straight on ordinary charts. The Mercator projection solved this by representing rhumb lines as straight lines, making it practical to plot a constant compass course across open oceans, though scale distortion increased toward the poles.

## Modern Navigation

![[attachments/navigation_002.webp|420]]

*Diagram of a marine sextant*

By about 1900, marine navigation had become a systematic technique combining charts, sailing directions, almanacs, sextants, chronometers, dead reckoning, compasses, logs, and astronomical observation. Thomas H. Sumner in 1837 and Marcq Saint-Hilaire in 1875 introduced routine trigonometric methods for position finding. Air and space travel later required radio, electronics, computers, and new concepts beyond surface navigation.

## Speed Measurement

The Pitot tube, invented by Henri Pitot in the mid-18th century, measures speed by comparing dynamic and static pressure. In ships or aircraft, forward motion creates a pressure difference proportional to the square of speed. Mechanical or electrical systems translate that difference into a speed reading, such as knots for a ship or airspeed for an aircraft.

## Dead Reckoning

Dead reckoning ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dead_reckoning)) starts from a known position, then uses compass course and logged distance to estimate a new position. Ocean currents, tides, wind, and instrument errors require correction. In aviation the problem is larger because aircraft speed through air differs from ground speed when wind moves the air mass. The triangle of velocities combines aircraft motion through air and wind motion over ground to determine true track and ground speed.

## Improved Compasses

Aircraft made ordinary liquid-filled magnetic compasses unreliable because acceleration, turning, metal, electrical equipment, and the vertical component of Earth's magnetic field caused errors such as northerly turning error. The direct magnetic compass became secondary, replaced for many uses by gyromagnetic compass systems that combine magnetic direction with gyroscopic stabilization.

## Gyrocompass

A gyrocompass avoids many turning and acceleration errors by keeping a reference platform horizontal or by comparing gyroscope heading with a magnetic element. A free gyroscope holds a direction in space but drifts gradually, while magnetic input corrects it. These systems became essential for aircraft and fast vessels that could not rely on old direct-reading magnetic compasses.

## Instruments

Modern craft need instrument systems for heading, speed, propulsion, engine temperature, fuel, rate of climb or descent, rate of turn, altitude, attitude, and airspeed. Aircraft combine indicated airspeed, altitude, temperature, and computers to produce true airspeed and Mach number. Attitude and heading reference systems integrate compass, artificial horizon, and related data, while digital displays reduce the need to interpret many separate dials.

## Collision Avoidance

Collision-avoidance theory uses relative motion. If the line of sight to another craft changes in one direction, altering course in the opposite direction increases miss distance. Ships meeting head-on traditionally both turn right, while aircraft depend on systematic route separation because visual avoidance is too uncertain. Radar and traffic-control systems extend the navigator's ability to detect and avoid other craft.

## Operation

[[Radar]] uses centimetre-wavelength radio waves beamed by a reflector. A rotating antenna sends short pulses; when pulses hit objects, echoes return and appear on a cathode-ray display at positions corresponding to direction and distance. Ship antennas are shaped to give a narrow horizontal and broad vertical beam, avoiding loss of target as the ship rolls. Persistent phosphor lets the operator study the image.

## Applications

Radar's main nonmilitary application is collision avoidance. Ships can proceed cautiously in fog because radar shows other vessels regardless of light conditions, though interpreting courses and intentions requires skill. Air traffic controllers use radar to monitor aircraft over wide areas and guide landings. Other systems include infrared detection, laser-based precision devices, low-light television, and sonar for underwater detection of submarines or fish.

## Satellites as Navigation Aids

Artificial satellites can transmit precisely timed and controlled signals for navigation. The U.S. Navy's Transit satellites of the early 1960s used Doppler shift to locate ships and submarines near the surface with accuracy around 180 yards, but sudden velocity changes made the system unsuitable for aircraft. The U.S. Department of Defense began the Global Positioning System in 1973, and the first Navstar GPS satellites launched in 1978.

## The Navstar

Navstar GPS uses 24 operational satellites and does not depend on Doppler shift for position. Satellites transmit pulses controlled by atomic clocks, and a receiver selects four or more favourable satellites, measures signal travel time, and solves equations for position and exact time. GPS also uses Doppler measurements for velocity and can update position, velocity, and time many times per second for aircraft, ships, vehicles, soldiers, hikers, and private cars.

## Key Events & Developments

- 600 BC: Phoenician sailors routinely imported tin from Cornwall.
- 1296: Lo compasso da navigare recorded Mediterranean portolan directions.
- 1837: Thomas H. Sumner introduced position-line computation methods.
- 1875: Marcq Saint-Hilaire introduced another key celestial-navigation method.
- 1960s: Transit satellites provided worldwide radio navigation for ships and submarines.
- 1978: The first Navstar GPS satellites were launched.
