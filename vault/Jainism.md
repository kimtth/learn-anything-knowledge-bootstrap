# Jainism

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> Jainism is an Indian religious tradition centered on nonviolence, ascetic discipline, karma, the soul's liberation, and the example of the Tirthankaras. Its ethical and philosophical traditions developed alongside Hinduism and Buddhism while sustaining distinctive rituals, texts, pilgrimage, and communities.

## Introduction

*Ranakpur Jain temple*

Jainism ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jainism)) is an Indian religion that teaches spiritual purity and enlightenment through disciplined life, ascetic effort, and ahimsa, or nonviolence toward all living beings. It developed from about the 7th to 5th century BCE and became a major contributor to Indian philosophy, logic, art, architecture, mathematics, astronomy, astrology, and literature.

Jainism ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jainism)) shares concepts and vocabulary with Hinduism and Buddhism because all three traditions arose in related South Asian cultural and linguistic worlds. Yet Jainism is an independent tradition, not a Hindu sect or Buddhist heresy. Its name comes from the Sanskrit root meaning to conquer, referring to the ascetic struggle against passions and bodily senses.

Jains ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jainism)) are followers of the Jinas, or Conquerors, enlightened beings who have overcome bondage and teach the path across rebirth. Jainism has remained largely Indian, though migration has carried Jain practice to Commonwealth countries and the United States. India has roughly four million Jains, with about 100,000 elsewhere.

## History and Tirthankaras

Early Jainism arose in the Ganges basin of eastern India, a region of intense religious experimentation also associated with Buddhism and other renunciant traditions. These movements challenged Brahmanic ritual authority, promoted asceticism, and sought liberation through disciplined knowledge and effort.

Jains ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jainism)) believe their tradition has no historical founder. The first figure with reasonable historical evidence is Parshvanatha, or Parshva, a renunciant teacher who may have lived in the 7th century BCE. Jain tradition regards him as the 23rd Tirthankara of the current age.

Mahavira, also called Vardhamana and the Great Hero, is the 24th and last Tirthankara of the current age. Traditionally dated 599-527 BCE, he was probably a close contemporary of the Buddha. Like the Buddha, he was born into the Kshatriya warrior class and renounced princely life at about age 30.

Tirthankara means Ford Maker, one who shows beings how to cross the stream of rebirth toward salvation. Jain devotion and teaching centre on these perfected figures, especially Mahavira, whose life gave later Jain communities a model of right knowledge, right faith, and right conduct.

## Karma, Soul, and Liberation

Jain doctrine rests on a universal chain of cause and effect. Every event has a definite cause. Each soul is by nature pure, with infinite knowledge, bliss, and power, but these qualities are restricted by contact with matter.

Karma in Jainism is an atomic material substance, not only a moral process. Passionate intentional action draws karmic particles to the soul, binding it to birth, death, and rebirth. Some early teachings held that any action, even unintentional action, attracted karma.

Liberation requires stopping the influx of new karma and wearing away karma already acquired. This wearing away, called nirjara, includes fasting, dietary restriction, control of taste, solitude, bodily mortification, atonement, modesty, service, study, meditation, and renunciation of ego.

Spiritual progress unfolds through stages known as gunasthanas, in which the soul's innate knowledge and power manifest more fully while sinfulness decreases and purity increases. Jain epistemology also distinguishes kinds of knowledge, from sensory and scriptural cognition to immediate forms of insight and omniscience.

## Festivals and Ritual Life

Jain festivals are connected with major events in the lives of Tirthankaras: descent into the mother's womb, birth, renunciation, omniscience, and final emancipation. Important days are called parvan and often involve fasting, preaching, recitation, confession, forgiveness, and devotional display.

The monsoon season is the most significant ritual period. For about four months, usually from late July to early November, monks and nuns stop wandering and reside among lay communities. This creates intense contact between renunciants and householders.

Paryushana is the most important Shvetambara festival, held in Bhadrapada, or August-September. It emphasizes abiding, pacification, forgiveness, fasting, preaching, scriptural recitation, almsgiving, processions, confession, and letters asking forgiveness for conscious and unconscious wrongs.

Dashalakshanaparvan is the Digambara counterpart, centred on the 10 religious qualities and public display of the Tattvartha-sutra. Jains also observe Mahavira's nirvana at the same time Hindus celebrate Diwali, lighting lamps to mark his final liberation.

## Pilgrimage and Sacred Places

Pilgrimage is highly meritorious for both renunciants and laity. Medieval pilgrimage sites often marked events in the lives of Tirthankaras, though some were damaged or destroyed during Muslim invasions beginning in the 8th century.

Important Jain sites include Parasnath Hill and Rajgir in Bihar, Shatrunjaya and Girnar on the Kathiawar Peninsula, Shravana Belgola in Karnataka, Mounts Abu and Kesariaji in Rajasthan, and Antariksha Parshvanatha in Maharashtra.

Local temples can function as substitutes for famous pilgrimage destinations by displaying depictions of major sites. Regional networks of shrines may also serve as simulacra of the great pilgrimage places, making sacred geography available to communities unable to travel far.

## Scriptures and Literature

Jain scripture does not come from one period, and no text is free from revision or addition. Oral teaching was systematized at councils, including one at Patna near the end of the 4th century BCE, later councils at Mathura and Valabhi, and a final Valabhi council in the mid-5th century CE associated with the existing Shvetambara canon.

The Purvas, 14 ancient texts believed to contain the original teachings of the Tirthankaras, are considered lost. Shvetambaras and Digambaras agree that the teachings will one day disappear from the world and reappear in a future time cycle, but they disagree about the extent of loss and corruption already present.

Jain literature includes canonical, commentarial, philosophical, narrative, ritual, and sectarian writings. Jains also retold major South Asian stories, including the story of Rama, in their own terms; Jain literature preserves 16 versions of this narrative in Sanskrit and Prakrit.

## Jainism, Hinduism, Buddhism, and Islam

Jainism, Hinduism, and Buddhism share terms such as karma, dharma, yoga, and yajna, but each gives them distinct meaning. Jainism's karma doctrine, radical nonviolence, ascetic discipline, and view of the soul distinguish it sharply from the other two traditions.

Shared ritual forms also receive different meanings. The abhiseka, or head-anointing ritual, appears in all three traditions. A major Jain example is the periodic ritual performed on the statue of Bahubali at Shravana Belgola every 12 to 14 years.

Islamic influence affected Jainism in temple ideas and sectarian practice. Some ideas about unsuitable behaviour in temples may reflect Muslim notions of holiness, and the Shvetambara Lonkasaha sect's rejection of image worship shows a clear Islamic influence. Jains also influenced the Mughal court in certain periods.

## Key Events & Developments

- 7th-5th century BCE Origins: Jainism developed in eastern India's Ganges basin.
- 7th century BCE Parshvanatha: The 23rd Tirthankara may have lived in this period.
- 599-527 BCE Mahavira: Traditional dates for the 24th Tirthankara.

## Interesting Facts

- Ahimsa ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ahimsa)) is so central that Jain discipline extends nonviolence to all living creatures.
- Karma in Jain thought is material, atomic substance attached to the soul.
- Paryushana includes formal requests for forgiveness after the past year's wrongs.
- The Purvas, believed to preserve ancient teachings, are now lost.
- Jain pilgrimage can be practiced through local temple representations of distant sacred sites.
