# Atlantic Ocean

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> The Atlantic Ocean is the world's second-largest ocean basin, opened by the breakup of Pangaea and still widening along the Mid-Atlantic Ridge. Its circulation, tides, weather systems, fisheries, offshore energy resources, and historically important routes of exploration connect the Americas, Europe, Africa, and global ocean circulation.

## Introduction

The Atlantic Ocean ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atlantic_Ocean)) is the world's second-largest ocean and covers roughly one-fifth of Earth's surface. It separates Europe and Africa from North and South America and is generally S-shaped and relatively narrow compared with its great north-south length.

Although smaller than the Pacific, the Atlantic has by far the largest drainage area ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drainage_basin)). Its bordering continents slope toward it, and it receives the waters of many of the world's major rivers. It is therefore not only a body of water but the central collector of an immense share of global runoff.

## Extent and Boundaries

The Atlantic includes many major marginal seas and embayments, especially in the north, where the coastline is irregular and island-rich. These include the Caribbean Sea ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caribbean_Sea)), the gulfs of [[Mexico]] and St. Lawrence, Hudson and Baffin bays, and the Baltic, North, Mediterranean, and Black seas.

Its exact boundaries are not universally agreed upon, particularly toward the Arctic and Southern oceans. There are several ways of defining these limits, including latitudinal lines, shallow submarine rises, and the distinction between different water masses ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water_mass)).

The most important feature of the Atlantic floor is the Mid-Atlantic Ridge, a vast submarine mountain chain running nearly the whole length of the ocean. It occupies the central third of the basin and forms the Atlantic portion of the world-encircling oceanic ridge system.

On either side of the ridge lie deep basins, abyssal plains, seamounts, and continental margins. The Atlantic floor is not a simple flat basin but a highly structured landscape with volcanic islands, trenches, hills, plateaus, and sediment ponds.

## Islands and Marginal Features

Atlantic islands fall into several types. Some, such as [[Iceland]], the Azores, Ascension, St. Helena, Tristan da Cunha, Bouvet, and Gough, are purely oceanic and often volcanic in origin. Others rise from continental margins, such as the Canary, Madeira, Cape Verde, and Fernando de Noronha islands.

Still others are parts of volcanic arcs, such as the Lesser Antilles and South Sandwich Islands, or are continental in character, like the British Isles, Newfoundland, the Falklands, and Greenland. This variety reflects the Atlantic's complex tectonic setting.

## Geologic Origin
The Atlantic is the youngest of the oceans. Its origin is explained through continental drift and [[Plate Tectonics|plate tectonics]], especially the breakup of Pangaea about 180 million years ago.

As North and South America separated from Europe and Africa, the Atlantic basin opened. The Mid-Atlantic Ridge remains the clearest evidence of this continuing process, because new crust is still being created there and the basin is still widening through seafloor spreading.

## Bottom Deposits

Much of the Atlantic floor is covered with calcareous ooze, while deeper zones below about 5,000 metres shift to red clay. In high southern latitudes, diatom ooze is especially important. Turbidity currents transport great quantities of sediment downslope into deep basins. Core samples from the Atlantic floor have helped reconstruct climatic changes over the last several million years.

## Weather and Climate

The North Atlantic is strongly shaped by the interaction between the Icelandic low-pressure area and the Azores high-pressure system, which together generate prevailing westerlies. These patterns help create the extratropical cyclones that move toward Newfoundland and Iceland and carry heat and moisture across the basin.

In the South Atlantic, high pressure around about 30° S and the surrounding belt of westerlies shape the winds, including southeast trade winds and the intertropical convergence zone near the Equator. Fog, storms, and cloud systems are all discussed as important elements of Atlantic atmospheric behaviour.

## Surface and Deep Currents

![[attachments/atlantic_ocean_003.webp|420]]

*Benjamin Franklin's chart of the Gulf Stream in the North Atlantic*

Atlantic surface currents broadly follow the prevailing winds, though they are modified by continents, evaporation, precipitation, and Earth's rotation. In the north, warm equatorial waters pass through the Caribbean and Gulf of Mexico and emerge as the Gulf Stream, which then becomes the North Atlantic Current and carries warmth toward Europe.

Cold currents such as the Labrador Current and East Greenland Current descend from the Arctic. In the south, the Brazil Current, Benguela Current, Falkland Current, and the Antarctic Circumpolar Current form an analogous but not identical circulation system. Deepwater formation in the North Atlantic plays an important role in global ocean circulation.

## Tides, Salinity, and Temperature

Atlantic tides behave broadly like a great standing wave in the S-shaped basin. Most are semidiurnal, though mixed and diurnal patterns appear in some regions. Tidal ranges vary greatly, with especially large tides in the Bay of Fundy and along Brittany.

Surface salinity is generally highest in the North Atlantic because of Mediterranean influences and strong evaporation in subtropical belts. Surface temperature closely follows current systems, producing especially sharp contrasts where warm and cold currents meet, such as along the Gulf Stream's cold wall near the Labrador Current.

## Fisheries and Resources

The Atlantic historically held some of the world's richest fishing grounds, especially over nutrient-rich continental shelves. Important commercial species have included cod, haddock, mackerel, herring, tuna, pilchard, shellfish, shrimp, and many others. Many stocks have been overfished or pushed near collapse. Atlantic countries have tried various management measures, but exploitation remains severe. Coastal aquaculture, particularly for shellfish and Atlantic salmon, has become increasingly important.

## Mineral Wealth

The Atlantic contains major submarine hydrocarbons, especially in the Gulf of Mexico, the North Sea, off west-central Africa, and east of Newfoundland and Nova Scotia. Offshore oil and gas dominate the ocean's nonrenewable resources.

Other resources include sand, gravel, diamonds off Namibia, phosphorite, sulfur, ferromanganese nodules, and salt recovered from seawater. Some of these have been exploited only irregularly or remain technologically difficult to use.

## Exploration and Science

The Atlantic played a central role in world exploration, from [[Columbus]] to Magellan and Drake. Fishing, colonization, and navigation drove early interest, while later scientific work led to modern oceanography.

Major contributions came from figures such as Benjamin Franklin, Matthew Fontaine Maury, and the Challenger expedition. Cable laying, bathymetric surveys, current studies, and marine biology all helped make the Atlantic one of the best-studied oceans.

## Conclusion

The Atlantic Ocean ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atlantic_Ocean)) is a geologically young but historically central ocean basin. Its ridge system, currents, fisheries, marginal seas, and resources make it a major part of Earth's physical and human geography.

Though smaller than the Pacific, it has been more deeply tied to exploration, colonization, and scientific study. The Atlantic emerges here as both a dynamic natural system and one of the world's most consequential oceanic spaces.

## Key Events & Developments

- Continental drift and seafloor spreading explain the Atlantic's origin.
- The Mid-Atlantic Ridge continues to widen the basin.
- Atlantic exploration drives the Age of Discovery and later colonization.
- Modern oceanography grows through cable surveys, the Challenger expedition, and current studies.
- Offshore oil, gas, and fisheries become major economic concerns.
