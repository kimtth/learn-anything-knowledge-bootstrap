## Introduction

The House of Habsburg, also spelled Hapsburg and also called the House of Austria ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/House_of_Habsburg)), was one of Europe's principal ruling dynasties from the 15th century to the 20th century. Its power rested on hereditary lands, royal elections, imperial office, strategic marriages, and a long association with Austria, Germany, Spain, Hungary, Bohemia, and central Europe.

## Origins

The name Habsburg ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/House_of_Habsburg)) came from Habsburg, or Habichtsburg, meaning Hawk's Castle, built in 1020 by Werner, bishop of Strasbourg, and his brother-in-law Count Radbot in the Aargau of present-day Switzerland. The earliest traceable ancestor was probably Guntram the Rich, possibly the same Count Guntram who rebelled against Otto I in 950. Radbot's son Werner I bore the title count of Habsburg.

Early Habsburg holdings grew through inheritance and division. Rudolf II of Habsburg acquired Laufenburg and the Waldstatte, but his sons divided the inheritance. The Laufenburg branch later sold its portion to the descendants of Albert IV and died out in 1408, helping consolidate the family line that would rise in Austria.

## Austria and the rise of the Habsburgs in Germany

Rudolf IV of Habsburg, son of Albert IV, was elected German king as Rudolf I in 1273. In 1282, he gave Austria and Styria to his sons Albert and Rudolf, beginning the long identification of the Habsburgs with Austria. Family custom gave hereditary lands to male members in common, but this created repeated difficulties and partitions.

In 1379, the Treaty of Neuberg ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Treaty_of_Neuberg)) divided lands between Albert III, who took Austria, and Leopold III, who took Styria, Carinthia, and Tirol. Despite these divisions, the dynasty gained strength through German kingship, territorial claims, and marriage policy. The Habsburg habit of gaining power by marriage later became famous as a dynastic method of expansion.

The family's European rise accelerated when Albert V became German king as Albert II and united claims to Germany, Hungary, and Bohemia in 1438. From this period, the Habsburgs held the Holy Roman Empire ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holy_Roman_Empire)) title almost continuously until its end in 1806. Maximilian I strengthened the dynasty through Burgundian and Spanish marriage alliances, setting up a vast European inheritance.

## Spanish and Austrian lines

![[attachments/house_of_habsburg_003.webp|420]]

*Habsburg-Lotharingiai Mihály*

Under Charles V, the Habsburg inheritance reached its widest early form, joining the Holy Roman imperial title, Austrian lands, Burgundy, Spain, and Spanish overseas possessions. The dynasty then divided into a Spanish Habsburg line under Philip II and an Austrian line under Ferdinand I. This split allowed both branches to rule large but different political worlds.

The Spanish Habsburgs ruled Spain and its empire until 1700, when Charles II died without an heir, leading to the War of the Spanish Succession. The Austrian branch faced the Reformation, Ottoman pressure, German politics, and the Thirty Years' War. It kept the imperial title and central European lands but often had to balance dynasty, religion, and the many peoples of its territories.

## Habsburg-Lorraine

The male Habsburg line ended with Charles VI, whose Pragmatic Sanction allowed his daughter Maria Theresa to inherit. The War of the Austrian Succession cost her most of Silesia, part of Lombardy, and Parma and Piacenza under the 1748 Treaty of Aix-la-Chapelle, but she retained most hereditary lands. Her husband, Francis Stephen of Lorraine, became emperor as Francis I, and their descendants formed the House of Habsburg-Lorraine.

After 1748, the Habsburgs wanted to recover Silesia and protect their western possessions. The Diplomatic Revolution before the Seven Years' War reversed old alliances, bringing reconciliation with France and a long Austro-French entente confirmed by the marriage of Marie-Antoinette to the future Louis XVI in 1770. The dynasty also worked to consolidate central Europe against Prussia.

Territorial expansion under Joseph II came partly through outside events. The First Partition of Poland in 1772 brought Galicia and Lodomeria, while Bukovina came in 1775 after the Russo-Turkish settlement. Joseph wanted Bavaria more than eastern gains, but Prussian opposition limited his success to the Innviertel in 1779 and blocked later exchange plans.

The French Revolutionary and Napoleonic Wars transformed the dynasty's position. In 1804, Francis II began calling himself hereditary emperor of Austria, and in 1806 the Holy Roman Empire ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holy_Roman_Empire)) was formally dissolved. The Habsburgs lost the southern Netherlands, faced modern nationalism, and then recovered influence at the Congress of Vienna in 1814-15, regaining Lombardy, Venetia, Dalmatia, Tirol, Tuscany, and Modena connections.

## Nineteenth-century empire and decline

After 1815, the Habsburg monarchy remained a major conservative power in central Europe, but nationalism and liberal movements challenged its multiethnic structure. The revolutions of 1848, the reign of Francis Joseph I, military defeats, and pressure from Hungarians forced reorganization. The Ausgleich of 1867 created the Austro-Hungarian Dual Monarchy, joining Austrian and Hungarian halves under one ruler.

The Austro-Hungarian arrangement preserved dynasty and empire but did not solve national tensions among Czechs, Slovaks, Croats, Serbs, Romanians, Poles, Italians, Germans, Magyars, and others. [[World Wars (World War I)|World War I]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_War_I)) exposed these weaknesses. In 1918, defeat and national movements ended Habsburg rule, and the empire broke into successor states, closing the long political history of the dynasty.
