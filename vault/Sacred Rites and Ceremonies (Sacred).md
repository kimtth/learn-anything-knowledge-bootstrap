## Introduction

Sacred ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sacredness)) refers to the power, being, or realm that religious people understand as central to existence and transformative for life and destiny. Related terms include holy, divine, transcendent, ultimate reality, mystery, perfection, and purity. The sacred is also a technical term in the scholarly study of religion.

The concept of the sacred became especially important in comparative religion during the first quarter of the 20th century. Nathan Soderblom argued in 1913 that holiness is the central idea of religion and that the sacred-profane distinction is basic to religious life.

Rudolf Otto's The Idea of the Holy, published in German in 1917 and translated in 1923, deeply influenced the field. He described the experience of the numinous as a mysterious, majestic presence that inspires both dread and fascination. He treated this experience as rooted in sacred reality, not reducible to ordinary feelings.

Other scholars developed different views. Emile Durkheim understood sacred things as those set apart and forbidden by society, so sacred force was society itself. Max Scheler argued that the sacred or infinite is not limited to finite objects and exists prior to human social expression.

## Sacred, Profane, Pure, and Impure

Different traditions have terms related to sacredness: Greek hagios, Hebrew qadosh, Polynesian tapu, Arabic haram, Melanesian mana, Sioux wakanda, old German haminja, and Sanskrit Brahman. These words do not always mean the same thing, but they show how religions mark special power, separation, and danger.

The sacred is often contrasted with the profane, but it also includes other oppositions, such as pure and impure or pollutant and free. In ancient Rome, sacer could mean something reserved for divine use or something dangerous and polluting. In Polynesia, tapu could mark something blessed with power or something accursed, such as a corpse.

The sacred is therefore ambivalent. It may create life, health, strength, luck, and divine contact, but it may also destroy anyone who approaches without protection or proper ritual. Sacred restrictions keep ordinary people safe from extraordinary energy.

In the pure-impure distinction, purity is linked with health, vigour, fortune, and long life, while impurity is linked with weakness, illness, misfortune, and death. Purification rituals, fasting, continence, and meditation can move a person from the profane and decaying world into the divine realm. This transition may be marked as rebirth.

## Human Response to the Sacred

Because the sacred contains both creative power and danger, human response often combines fear and fascination. Otto made this ambiguity central to his account of the holy. The sacred fulfils deep hopes, yet it also overwhelms ordinary human control.

Religious reverence is therefore not simple affection or simple terror. It is a response to mystery, power, otherness, and promise. The sacred can attract people because it offers meaning and transformation, but it also demands distance, discipline, and ritual care.

The sacred/profane distinction helps explain why religions create ritual boundaries. Sacred places, times, objects, persons, words, and actions are set apart from ordinary use. Rituals protect the sacred from misuse and protect people from approaching it wrongly.

## Key Events & Developments

- 1913: Nathan Soderblom argued that holiness is central to religion.
- 1917: Rudolf Otto published The Idea of the Holy in German.
- 1923: Otto's work appeared in English and strongly influenced comparative religion.
- Early 20th century: Durkheim, Scheler, and others made the sacred a key interpretive term.
