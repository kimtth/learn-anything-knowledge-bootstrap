## Introduction

![[attachments/peru_001.webp|420]]

*Physical map of Peru showing coast, Andes, and Amazon basin*

Peru ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peru)) is a western South American country whose geography, society, and economy are shaped by the Pacific coast, the Andes, and Amazonia. Though tropical in latitude, Peru has great climatic diversity because of elevation extremes and the cold Peru, or Humboldt, Current. Its capital is [[Lima]], while major attractions include Machu Picchu ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Machu_Picchu)), Cuzco, northern coastal tombs, and museums of ancient Andean civilizations.

The name Peru ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peru)) comes from a Quechua word implying land of abundance, reflecting the wealth and organization of the Inca civilization. The Andes long made internal travel difficult; before air transport, moving from Lima to Iquitos could be easier by a vast route through the Amazon, Atlantic, Caribbean, Panama, and Pacific than by crossing the mountains.

## Relief

Peru is traditionally divided into three longitudinal regions: the arid Costa on the Pacific, the mountainous Sierra, and the wet forested Amazonia. The Costa narrows from north to south and includes plains, deserts, irrigated valleys, and coastal hills. The Sierra consists of Andean ranges formed where the South American Plate overrides the Nazca Plate. Amazonia occupies more than three-fifths of Peru and includes cloud forests, rolling lowlands, and tropical plains.

## Drainage and Soils

![[attachments/peru_003.webp|420]]

*Sunrise over Lake Titicaca near Puno, Peru*

More than 50 short, steep rivers cross the Costa from the Andes to the Pacific, most flowing seasonally. The Sierra contains headwaters for Pacific and Amazon drainage and internal basins such as Lake Titicaca, the world's highest navigable lake. Amazonia is dominated by great rivers, including the Amazon, Ucayali, Maranon, Huallaga, and tributaries near Iquitos. Fertile soils are limited mainly to coastal river valleys and some highland basins.

## Climate and El Nino

Peru has three broad climatic regions. The coast is one of Earth's driest deserts because the Andes block Amazonian moisture, Pacific high-pressure air brings little rain, and the Humboldt Current cools air masses. The Sierra varies by elevation, latitude, rain shadow, and winds. Amazonia is hot, humid, and rainy. El Nino periodically replaces cold coastal waters with warm water, causing desert rains, highland drought, fish and bird losses, and damage to roads, canals, and farms.

## Plant and Animal Life

The Costa has sparse vegetation except fog-fed lomas and northern desert plants, but its waters teem with anchovies, corvina, tuna, swordfish, marlin, sea lions, pelicans, cormorants, gulls, and Humboldt penguins. The Sierra has puna grasslands supporting llamas, alpacas, vicunas, and guanacos, plus lower-elevation crops such as potatoes, quinoa, and corn. Amazonia contains dense tropical forests with jaguars, capybaras, tapirs, monkeys, birds, fish, reptiles, insects, hardwoods, softwoods, and coca plantations.

## Peoples and Languages

Peru's pre-Hispanic peoples were often isolated by rugged terrain, though Chavin, Huari, and Inca cultures unified wide areas. Modern Peru has a complex ethnic mosaic: Quechua Indians form nearly half the population, mestizos slightly less than one-third, people of European ancestry about one-eighth, and smaller Aymara, Japanese, African-descended, Chinese, and other groups. Spanish dominates public life, but Quechua and Aymara have official regional status, and Amazonian languages remain numerous.

## Religion and Settlement

Most Peruvians are Roman Catholic, though Protestant and traditional beliefs also exist. Andean religion blends Catholicism with older worship of Viracocha, Pachamama, the Sun, Moon, mountains, lightning, and local spirits. Settlement reflects pre-Hispanic cultures, Spanish colonial patterns, urban migration, and Amazonian colonization. Since World War II, Peru has become predominantly urban, with more than one-fourth of the population in greater Lima.

## Urban Peru

[[Lima]] is Peru's commercial and industrial heart, combining colonial architecture, high-rises, factories, workshops, and vast squatter settlements. Urban infrastructure has struggled to keep pace with growth. Other major cities include Arequipa, the White City, whose historic centre is a World Heritage site; Trujillo, the historic northern power centre; Chimbote, Piura, Chiclayo, Cuzco, and other regional cities.

## Economy

Peru is a less-developed economy historically dependent on raw-material exports to the Northern Hemisphere. It is a major fishing country and a leading producer of bismuth, silver, copper, gold, zinc, fish meal, textiles, agricultural goods, petroleum, and other commodities. Natural disasters such as earthquakes, landslides, El Nino rains, and flooding periodically damage transportation and agriculture.

Nationalization and agrarian reform in the late 1960s and early 1970s aimed to modernize the economy but produced debt, inflation, deficits, and strained trade relations. Later governments reopened sectors to private enterprise, and 1990s structural reforms helped produce rapid growth in the early 21st century.

## Agriculture, Manufacturing, Finance, and Trade

Agriculture ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agriculture)) declined in relative importance but remains socially important. Peru imports grains, soy, oils, and foodstuffs while producing domestic crops and livestock in limited fertile zones. Manufacturing is concentrated in greater Lima and includes petroleum products, textiles, processed foods, steel, cement, fertilizer, and chemicals. Finance is led by the Central Reserve Bank, National Bank, and Development Finance Corporation. Trade links Peru to the United States, China, Ecuador, Chile, Colombia, Brazil, the Andean Community, APEC, and the WTO.

## Government, Society, and Services

Peru's judiciary includes the Supreme Court, lower courts, tribunals, and a Constitutional Court. Voting is compulsory for citizens ages 18 to 70, and parties range from conservative to socialist and communist. The military includes army, navy, air force, and marines. Health care expanded through public hospitals and clinics, but shortages persist outside Lima. Housing ranges from apartments and single-family dwellings to informal settlements that gradually acquire utilities and durable structures.

## Education and Culture

Education is free and compulsory from ages 6 to 15, though enforcement, quality, facilities, and teacher training remain uneven. The National University of San Marcos, founded in 1551, claims to be South America's oldest university. Peruvian culture mixes Indigenous, Spanish, and African traditions in music, literature, textiles, metalwork, food, theatre, and festivals. Quenas, zamponas, charangos, harps, drums, huayno, chicha, folk dance, and Peruvian cuisine express this blended heritage.

## Early History and the Inca

![[attachments/peru_002.webp|420]]

*Machu Picchu in the Andes of Peru*

Humans have lived in Peru for more than 13,000 years. Advanced cultures such as Chavin, Moche, Nazca, Tiwanaku, and Chimu preceded the Inca. The Inca empire expanded rapidly after Pachacuti's accession in 1438, controlling much of modern Peru, Bolivia, northern Argentina, central Chile, and highland Ecuador. Inca Cuzco, roads, masonry, and state organization remain central to Peru's historical identity.

## Conquest, Colony, and Independence

Francisco Pizarro conquered the Inca in 1532, and Peru became the centre of a Spanish viceroyalty. Colonial society exploited Indigenous labour and resources, while Lima became a major administrative city. The 1780 revolt of Tupac Amaru II reflected Indigenous suffering under taxes and forced labour. Independence came largely through outsiders, especially Jose de San Martin and later forces associated with Simon Bolivar, because Peru's conservative elite remained loyal to Spain for longer than many colonies.

## Republic and Modern Politics

After independence in 1824, Peru struggled with caudillos, liberal and conservative rivalries, weak civilian rule, and conflicts with Bolivia, Chile, and neighbours. Ramon Castilla stabilized politics and exploited guano wealth. The APRA movement, founded in 1924 by Victor Raul Haya de la Torre, challenged oligarchic politics. Later Peru experienced military rule, reform, economic crisis, terrorism, Fujimori's authoritarian presidency, and 21st-century democratic contests including Ollanta Humala's narrow victory over Keiko Fujimori in 2011.

- c. 1000 BCE: Chavin culture spread across the Andes.
- 1438: Pachacuti began rapid Inca expansion.
- 1532: Francisco Pizarro conquered the Inca.
- 1780: Tupac Amaru II led a major revolt.
- 1824: Spanish rule ended in Peru.
- 1924: APRA was founded by Victor Raul Haya de la Torre.
- 1990s: Structural reforms supported renewed growth.
- 2011: Ollanta Humala won a close presidential runoff.
