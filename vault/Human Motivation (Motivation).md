# Human Motivation (Motivation)

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> Motivation denotes internal and external forces that initiate and direct behavior. Its primary needs, learned secondary motives, internal pushes, and goal-based pulls explain why action reflects both bodily states and social or personal circumstances.

## Introduction

Motivation ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Motivation)) refers to forces acting on or within a person to initiate behaviour. The word comes from the Latin motivus, meaning a moving cause, and points to the activating processes behind action. Psychologists infer motivation from behavioural change, such as a person becoming more likely to seek food as time since the last meal increases, rather than measuring it directly.

Motives are often divided into primary motives and secondary motives. Primary motives are unlearned and common to humans and animals, including hunger, thirst, sex, pain avoidance, and perhaps aggression and fear. Secondary motives are learned and vary more across individuals and species, including achievement, power, and specialized goals. Motives may also be described as pushes, from internal changes, and pulls, from external goals.

Most real motivational situations combine internal push and external pull. Hunger may be signaled by blood glucose or fat stores, but eating is also shaped by taste, social setting, availability, habit, stress, and learned preference. This mixture makes motivation hard to measure directly and hard to explain with one theory.

## Debates in motivational study

Researchers have debated whether motivation should be studied through general laws or through individual uniqueness. The nomothetic approach studies groups to draw broad conclusions, while the idiographic approach studies what makes individuals distinctive. Both contribute, but nomothetic methods have dominated research.

Another debate asks whether motives are innate or acquired. Early theorists often emphasized instinct, but behaviourism later replaced instinct with learning. Research since the 1960s supports a middle position: some behaviours, such as stickleback courtship, are largely innate, while motives such as achievement depend strongly on learning. Traits such as extreme shyness may combine biological predisposition with environment.

Debates also contrast internal needs with external goals and mechanistic responses with cognitive processes. Some theories explain behaviour through bodily needs and homeostasis; others emphasize incentives, expectations, interpretation, and choice. Human motivation often includes all of these, so the main problem is separating their contributions.

## Historical and physiological background

Early theories of motivation drew from philosophy, physiology, and psychology. Cartesian dualism separated animal and human motivation, while later thinkers increasingly emphasized bodily mechanisms and learning. Discoveries by Luigi Galvani, Emil Du Bois-Reymond, Georg E. Muller, and Hermann von Helmholtz showed that nerves transmit electrical signals, making the nervous system a scientific object for explaining behaviour.

Research on brain localization grew partly from the discredited phrenology of Franz Josef Gall, but it indirectly encouraged the search for motivational systems in the brain, especially the hypothalamus. These physiological discoveries prepared the way for biological explanations of hunger, thirst, sex, arousal, and other motives.

## Behaviourism

![[attachments/human_motivation_motivation_003.png|420]]

*English diagram relating situation, thoughts, and emotion in motivation and appraisal*

By the late 19th century, William James and William McDougall emphasized instinctive components of behaviour, while John B. Watson rejected both instinct and will and focused on learned responses to environmental stimuli. Behaviourism treated behaviour as a response to stimulation and became dominant in motivational theory by the 1920s.

The concept of drive, introduced by Robert S. Woodworth and developed by Clark Hull, fit behaviourism well. Drive was treated as an internal state caused by bodily need, energizing behaviour that would reduce that need, such as hunger drive leading to food seeking. Critics such as Edward C. Tolman and Wolfgang Kohler argued that organisms process information more actively than simple stimulus-response theory allowed.

## Biological approaches

Biological approaches include genetic contributions, arousal mechanisms, and monitoring systems. Ethologists such as Karl von Frisch, Konrad Lorenz, and Nikolaas Tinbergen, who received a Nobel Prize in 1973, studied animal behaviour in natural habitats and kept the idea of inherited behaviour alive. They described fixed-action patterns, species-specific behaviours released automatically under proper conditions.

Some ethologists extended these methods to humans. Irenaus Eibl-Eibesfeldt used cross-cultural research to study possible human genetic behaviour patterns, and research on facial expressions gave some support to innate motivational tendencies. These findings did not mean all human motives are instinctive; they showed that biological programs and learning can interact.

## Motivation as arousal

![[attachments/human_motivation_motivation_002.webp|420]]

*Basic Motivation*

Arousal theories studied motivation through changes in bodily activation. The James-Lange theory, proposed by William James and Carl Lange in 1884-85, argued that emotion follows perception of bodily changes. Walter B. Cannon challenged this, leading to the Cannon-Bard view that bodily response and emotional experience occur together rather than in a simple bodily-first sequence.

## Hunger

Hunger motivation involves short-term and long-term regulation. Short-term regulation balances immediate energy use and intake, while long-term regulation stores energy as fat for later need. Early local theories placed hunger signals in the stomach, but removal of much of the stomach or interruption of the vagus nerve did not eliminate hunger, showing that the explanation was incomplete.

Research then focused on the hypothalamus. Damage to the ventromedial area can produce overeating and obesity, while other hypothalamic regions affect eating differently. Blood glucose, fat stores, stomach signals, hormones, brain monitoring, taste, stress, social influence, and learned eating patterns all help regulate hunger.

## Thirst

Thirst is regulated by mechanisms that monitor fluid balance inside and outside cells. Osmoreceptors respond to fluid loss within specialized brain cells, while other mechanisms respond to fluid loss outside cells, such as after bleeding. The hypothalamus is also involved in thirst motivation, linking bodily fluid integrity to drinking behaviour.

## Sexual motivation

Sexual motivation is more strictly controlled by hormones in many animals than in humans. In many species, females show sexual interest only during estrus, while males are often ready but depend on female receptivity. The anterior hypothalamus and ventromedial hypothalamus help regulate estrus and sexual behaviour, with hormone receptors and brain damage studies showing how hormonal conditions and neural systems interact.

## Behavioristic approaches to motivation

Drive theory treated motivation as a homeostatic process. Clark Hull argued that drives such as hunger, thirst, and sex add to a general drive level, which energizes activity. Internal drive stimuli direct behaviour toward responses previously linked to drive reduction, and learning was thought to occur when responses reduced drive or drive stimuli.

Classical conditioning showed how emotional motives could be learned. In 1920, John B. Watson and Rosalie Rayner paired a white rat with a frightening noise, producing fear in a young boy. This suggested that phobias, taste aversions, eating problems, and other motives can arise from accidental pairings of events.

Instrumental conditioning, or operant conditioning, changes future behaviour by its consequences. Aggressive behaviour may increase if it produces a desired reward, and neutral objects can become conditioned reinforcers if linked to primary rewards. Money is a common example because it motivates behaviour through the food, clothing, sex, status, and other goods it can buy.

## Self-actualization

Cognitive and humanistic approaches emphasize competence, control, expectation, and meaning. Abraham Maslow's self-actualization theory describes a hierarchy of needs: physiological needs, safety, belonging, esteem, and finally self-actualization. As lower needs are met, higher needs become active, though Maslow believed few people fully reach self-actualization.

Maslow described self-actualized people as accepting of themselves and others, relatively independent of culture, somewhat detached but deeply connected to a few people, committed to important problems, appreciative of simple or natural events, and sometimes marked by peak experiences. This approach made motivation more than drive reduction; it included growth, meaning, and becoming what one can become.

## Applications in society

Motivational methods have been applied in schools, hospitals, institutions, animal training, and behaviour management. Food deprivation can shape animal behaviour, while human settings may use loss of privileges, rewards, or token economies. Tokens, such as poker chips, can be exchanged for privileges or goods and can reinforce socially desired acts like cleanliness, cooperation, or school achievement.

Such reinforcement systems have worked most clearly in controlled institutions, where conditions and rewards can be managed carefully. Reports describe improvements in personal care, behaviour, and scholastic achievement among some institutionalized groups. These applications show the practical power of reinforcement but also raise questions about control, ethics, and how well such systems work outside institutions.

## Summary

Motivation ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Motivation)) spans nearly all of psychology, and no single theory explains all motivational processes. Hunger, thirst, and sex are strongly biological; many fears and social motives are learned; and cognitive interpretation shapes future motivation. Even basic motives are influenced by energy needs, stress, loneliness, social influence, taste, reward, habit, and expectation.

Understanding human motivation therefore requires careful separation of biological, learned, and cognitive components. The more subtle the motive, the harder this becomes. Despite extensive research, much remains unknown because behaviour usually results from several motives working together.

## Key Events & Developments

- 1770s-1780s: Luigi Galvani's experiments helped show the electrical nature of nerve activity.
- 1884-85: William James and Carl Lange proposed the James-Lange theory of emotion.
- 1918: Robert S. Woodworth introduced the term drive.
- 1920: John B. Watson and Rosalie Rayner demonstrated learned fear conditioning.
- 1940s: Clark Hull developed a major drive theory.
- 1973: Karl von Frisch, Konrad Lorenz, and Nikolaas Tinbergen received a Nobel Prize for ethological work.
