# South America

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> South America is a triangular continent whose ancient eastern cratons, vast river basins, and active western Andes reflect a long history of continental assembly, rifting, subduction, volcanism, sedimentation, and glaciation. ## Introduction

![[attachments/south_america_001_mw.webp|420]]

*Relief map of South America showing the Andes and major lowland basins*

South America is the fourth largest continent and the southern part of the Americas. It is compact and roughly triangular, broad in the north and narrowing toward Cape Horn in Chile. It is bounded by the Caribbean Sea, Atlantic Ocean, and Pacific Ocean, and it joins North America at the Isthmus of Panama.

The continent covers about 6,878,000 square miles, roughly one-eighth of Earth's land surface. Its greatest north-south distance is about 4,700 miles, and its greatest east-west distance is about 3,300 miles. Mount Aconcagua in Argentina reaches 22,831 feet and is the highest point in the Western Hemisphere, while the Valdes Peninsula includes a low point below sea level.

South America's coastline is short for its area, and few islands border it except in the south. The Falkland Islands lie east of southern Argentina, while the Galapagos Islands lie off Ecuador in the Pacific. The name America comes from Amerigo Vespucci, and the term first applied to South America before extending to the whole landmass.

Geologically, South America has an old eastern structure of shields, highlands, and basins, and a younger western structure dominated by the Andes. The Andes formed as the South American Plate moved west and forced oceanic crust beneath it. Sediments from great rivers filled the basins east of the mountains.

## Geologic History

The geologic history ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geological_history_of_Earth)) of South America has three broad stages. Precambrian time formed ancient cratons and stable crustal blocks. The Paleozoic Era helped assemble Gondwana and then Pangaea. The Mesozoic and Cenozoic eras brought the breakup of those supercontinents, the opening of the South Atlantic, and the rise of the Andes.

The present framework includes five main cratons: Amazonia, Sao Francisco, Luis Alves, Alto Paraguay, and Rio de la Plata. These form the Precambrian core of the continent, especially in the east. Other crustal blocks were added to the margins through long tectonic processes.

## The Precambrian

Precambrian rocks are preserved in the core cratons. Some Archean rocks are more than 3 billion years old, with especially old samples in Venezuela, Brazil, and the Sao Francisco craton. Greenstone belts in old suture zones contain many large gold deposits, including areas near Belo Horizonte.

Two major deformation cycles shaped these rocks. The Trans-Amazonian cycle occurred about 2.2 to 1.8 billion years ago, and the Brazilian cycle occurred about 900 to 570 million years ago. These cycles formed old mountain belts, platform deposits, and igneous dike swarms.

## The Paleozoic Era

During the Paleozoic Era, rifting along the western side of South America helped form passive continental margins and large intracratonic basins such as the Amazonas, Parnaiba, Parana, and Chaco basins. Later subduction and collisions added allochthonous continental blocks along the western margin.

Some blocks in Ecuador, Colombia, and Venezuela show links to North America through rock ages and fossil affinities. These events helped prepare the later Andean margin and created basins that would hold thick sedimentary deposits.

## The Andes

![[attachments/south_america_002.webp|420]]

*Central Andes landscape in Argentina*

The Andes ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andes)) are the continent's young western backbone. The Northern Andes include accreted oceanic terranes, volcanic arcs, metamorphic rocks, and ophiolitic suites, especially in Ecuador, Colombia, and Venezuela. Lake Maracaibo occupies a basin with very large hydrocarbon deposits.

The Central Andes, extending through southern Ecuador, Peru, Bolivia, northern Argentina, and Chile, formed mainly through subduction without major plate collisions. Cretaceous compression created foreland basins that hold many petroleum resources, while volcanic activity produced rocks associated with gold and copper deposits.

The Southern Andes south of the Gulf of Penas include a long batholith and back-arc and foreland basins with very thick sediments. Pleistocene glaciations shaped much of the southern landscape. Ice once covered the Andes from Ecuador to Tierra del Fuego and retreated about 12,000 years ago.

## Relief

South America's relief is strongly unbalanced. The Andes stand close to the Pacific coast, so the main drainage divide lies far west. Rain falling only 100 miles east of the Pacific may flow thousands of miles to the Atlantic.

The continent's major landforms include the Andes Mountains, the Guiana and Brazilian highlands, the Patagonian plateaus, and the lowland basins of the Orinoco, Amazon, Paraguay-Parana, and Pampas. The Andes run about 5,500 miles and are second only to the Himalayas in average elevation.

In the north, the Andes ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andes)) divide into ranges in Venezuela, Colombia, Ecuador, and Peru. Farther south, the Altiplano forms high plateaus between 12,000 and 15,000 feet, with closed basins, marshes, and Lake Titicaca. The Pampas of Argentina are broad sedimentary plains, while the Pantanal and Gran Chaco occupy lower, flood-prone or arid interior zones.

## Rivers, Lakes, and Marshes

The Amazon River system is the largest drainage basin in the world. It carries about one-fifth of the world's flowing fresh water and drains about two-fifths of South America. It has more than 1,000 tributaries, many over 1,000 miles long, and flows east from the Andes across low plains to the Atlantic.

The second great system is the Rio de la Plata basin, formed by the Paraguay, Parana, and Uruguay rivers. Other major systems include the Orinoco and Sao Francisco. Together, the four largest drainage systems cover nearly three-fourths of the continent.

Most important lakes lie in the Andes or near their foothills. Lake Titicaca, between Peru and Bolivia, is the largest permanent Andean lake and lies at about 12,500 feet. Patagonian lakes such as Buenos Aires, Argentino, and Nahuel Huapi formed in basins left by melting glaciers.

Vast marshes occur in poorly drained depressions. The Pantanal in the Paraguay River basin floods seasonally into an enormous swamp. Amazonian varzeas flood annually, while mangrove swamps border lower river valleys and coasts.

## Soils

South America has more than 20 soil regions shaped by geology, relief, climate, and vegetation. Low natural fertility is common in the humid tropics. About half the continent's soils are nutrient-poor sediments, latosols, red-yellow podzols, or regosols.

Arid soils cover about one-fifth of the continent and make agriculture risky without irrigation. Poorly drained soils, lithosols on steep Andean slopes, and shallow valley soils further limit agriculture. Fertile soils occupy only about 10 percent of the surface, mainly in the Pampas, parts of the Andes, and eastern and southeastern Brazil.

Soil erosion has severely damaged or ruined large areas of arable land, especially through poor land management and forest clearing. Mountain forests cleared for cattle and crops often suffer long-term soil damage, though many countries have begun conservation or restoration campaigns.

## Climate

South America spans a wide latitudinal range, so it has many climates. Tropical conditions dominate more than half the continent because its broadest part lies near the equator. Elevation, especially in the Andes, creates strong local differences.

Three main factors shape climate: subtropical high-pressure cells over the South Atlantic and South Pacific, cold Pacific currents along the west coast, and the Andean rain shadow. The cold Peru, or Humboldt, Current and Pacific high-pressure system create stable dry air along the west, producing the Atacama Desert, one of the driest regions on Earth.

The Atlantic side receives more moisture from warm currents and winds. Interior arid regions include Patagonia, northwestern Argentina, northeastern Brazil, and warm coastal deserts from Colombia to Venezuela. Cold climates occur in southern Argentina and Chile and in the high Andes above about 11,500 feet.

## Plant Life

South America's plant region belongs to the Neotropics. It has high endemism and great diversity, especially in the tropical north. Vegetation distribution reflects climate, soils, relief, and human influence.

Grasslands include tropical savannas such as the Llanos and subtropical Pampas. The Pampas ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pampas)) are mainly grass-covered plains of eastern Argentina, later enriched with introduced trees. The Patagonian steppe farther south has tussock grasses, low bushes, and spiny plants.

The Amazon region contains the world's largest and probably oldest rain forest. Trees may exceed 300 feet, and species diversity is extremely high. Swamp forests, flooded varzeas, and nonswampy forests each support different trees, palms, rubber plants, Brazil nut trees, epiphytes, orchids, and ferns.

Dry regions support caatinga, xerophytic thickets, cacti, thorny shrubs, and drought-resistant trees. South Brazilian forests include araucaria, hardwoods, yellowwood, and South American holly, whose leaves are used to make mate.

## Animal Life

South America's animals reflect long isolation and later migrations. [[Mammals]] include marsupials, sloths, monkeys, rodents, tapirs, deer, bears, rabbits, camelids, llamas, alpacas, vicunas, and guanacos. Amphibians and reptiles include caecilians, frogs, turtles, caimans, iguanas, boas, anacondas, coral snakes, and vipers.

The Amazonian and Guianan forests are the richest animal communities. Rivers support pacu, electric eels, pirarucu, piranhas, manatees, river dolphins, turtles, crocodiles, and caimans. Many forest animals became arboreal because trees dominate the environment.

Other regions have specialized life. The Pampas have rheas, ovenbirds, maras, viscachas, guanacos, and Pampas deer. Southern Chilean forests have many endemic species, including primitive marsupials. The high Andes support flamingos, giant Lake Titicaca toads, guanacos, vicunas, chinchillas, pumas, spectacled bears, and condors.

## Conservation

South America has many national parks, including Nahuel Huapi in Argentina, protected areas around Iguazu Falls, Manu National Park in Peru, and Canaima National Park in Venezuela. These parks preserve rain forests, waterfalls, highlands, and rich plant and animal life.

The continent has about 58,000 square miles of parks, but protection is uneven. Economic development pressures threaten some sanctuaries, so conservation depends on political commitment, enforcement, and local land-use choices.

## The People

![[attachments/south_america_003.webp|420]]

*Machu Picchu in the Andes of Peru*

Four main groups shaped South America's population: Amerindians, Iberians, Africans, and postindependence immigrants. Indigenous peoples occupied most environments before European conquest and developed agriculture, irrigation, raised fields, chiefdoms, and complex states in places such as the Pacific coast, Andes, and Amazon basin.

Most anthropologists believe South America's first peoples descended from migrants who came from Asia through North America between about 60,000 and 20,000 years ago. People were certainly present in Chile by 11,000 BCE. More recent estimates place South America's preconquest population around 20 million.

Spanish and Portuguese colonizers dominated until the early 19th century and largely excluded other Europeans. Africans were brought as enslaved workers from the early 16th century, especially to Brazil, Spanish American plantation zones, colonial cities, and the Guianas. Postindependence immigration after 1850 brought 11 to 12 million people, mainly to Argentina and Brazil, from Italy, Germany, Lebanon, South Asia, Japan, and other regions.

South America is a hollow continent demographically, with most population concentrated around the margins. Dense populations occur in old Andean Indian cores, northeastern Brazil, southern Brazil, Uruguay, and Argentina. Public health improvements after World War II reduced death rates and produced rapid population growth, especially in tropical regions.

Urbanization became one of the strongest modern forces in South America. Capitals and port cities such as [[Buenos Aires]], [[Rio de Janeiro]], [[Sao Paulo]], [[Lima]], Bogota, Santiago, and [[Caracas]] drew migrants from rural districts, small towns, highlands, plantations, and interior frontiers. This growth concentrated industry, universities, finance, administration, media, and political protest, but it also produced informal settlements, infrastructure shortages, congestion, and sharp inequalities between formal urban centres and peripheral neighbourhoods.

## The Economy

Since the 1970s, South American economies have changed deeply through external pressures and internal policy choices. Many countries export lower-value primary products and semiprocessed goods while importing higher-value manufactured products. From the 1930s to late 1980s, many followed import-substitution industrialization to protect domestic industry.

Governments used tariffs, subsidies, state enterprises, and foreign borrowing to fund industry, infrastructure, education, housing, energy, and transportation. Heavy borrowing at high interest rates helped produce the debt crisis, forcing many countries into insolvency and later policy changes.

## Resources

South America is rich in minerals, though resources are unevenly distributed. Venezuela has major oil and natural gas deposits near Lake Maracaibo and El Tigre. Ecuador, Peru, Argentina, Chile, Brazil, Colombia, and Bolivia also have petroleum or gas resources. Coal is less abundant, though Colombia exports coal from La Guajira and the lower Magdalena basin.

The continent has major iron ore, manganese, copper, molybdenum, and other metal deposits. Brazil, Bolivia, Chile, Peru, Colombia, and Argentina all have important mineral regions. Chile and Peru hold more than one-quarter of the world's known copper reserves, including Chuquicamata and El Teniente in Chile and major Andean deposits in Peru.

Forestry and fishing are important but uneven. Amazon timber harvesting expanded after 1950 despite the mixed forest structure. Chile and Argentina developed plantations, and eucalyptus spread after its introduction in the early 1800s. Freshwater fisheries serve local needs, while Peru, Chile, and Ecuador developed major marine fishing or shrimp-export industries.

## Agriculture, Livestock, and Industry

Agriculture ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agriculture)) is important in both tropical and temperate regions, though only about one-eighth of the land is suitable for permanent crops or grazing. Many small farmers own less than 25 acres, and productivity is limited by credit, transport, storage, marketing, education, and unfavourable trade terms.

Livestock is culturally and economically important. High-quality cattle are raised in the Argentine Pampas and Uruguay, Brahman crosses graze in the Llanos and Amazon pastures, and sheep, goats, llamas, alpacas, vicunas, and guinea pigs are important in colder, higher, or drier zones.

The industrial sector has grown but often has not created enough jobs for the expanding labour force. Argentina and Brazil are the most industrialized countries, with Brazil's manufacturing core centred on Sao Paulo. Key industries include food, beverages, tobacco, metallurgy, machinery, chemicals, petroleum refining, textiles, cement, steel, automobiles, and appliances.

## Infrastructure and Power

Hydroelectric power is a major regional resource. Itaipu Dam on the Paraguay-Brazil border and Guri Dam in Venezuela are among the world's largest hydroelectric projects. Major rivers often require binational cooperation, such as projects between Brazil and Paraguay or Argentina and Uruguay.

Irrigation remains limited even though some hydroelectric projects were designed partly for that use. Less than one-tenth of arable and permanent-crop land is irrigated. Large-scale irrigation is especially important in countries with scarce farmland, including Suriname, Peru, Chile, and Guyana.

## Key Events & Developments

- 11,000 BCE: Humans were present in Chile.
- 3000 BCE: Agriculture-based village cultures developed in Amazonian and coastal lowland regions.
- 16th century: Iberian conquest and African slavery transformed population and economy.
- Early 19th century: Most South American countries gained independence.
- 1850-1930: Mass overseas immigration reshaped Argentina, Brazil, Uruguay, and southern Brazil.
- 1970s-1980s: Borrowing and development strategies contributed to a continental debt crisis.

## Key People

- Amerigo Vespucci ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amerigo_Vespucci)): Italian navigator whose name became attached to America.
- Amerindians ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amerindians)): Original inhabitants whose societies developed agriculture, irrigation, raised fields, and complex states.
- Iberians ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iberians)): Spanish and Portuguese colonizers who dominated the continent until independence.
- Africans ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Africans)): Enslaved peoples whose labour and culture became central in Brazil, the Guianas, and many Spanish American regions.
