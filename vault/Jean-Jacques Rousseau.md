# Jean-Jacques Rousseau

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> Jean-Jacques Rousseau was an Enlightenment writer and political thinker whose works challenged assumptions about progress, inequality, education, freedom, and legitimate political authority. His social-contract theory and autobiographical writing influenced revolutionary politics, Romanticism, and modern debates over individuality and citizenship.

## Introduction

![[attachments/jean_jacques_rousseau_remediate_001_mw.webp|420]]

*Portrait of Jean-Jacques Rousseau*

Jean-Jacques Rousseau, born in 1712 in Geneva and dead in 1778 at Ermenonville, was a Swiss-born philosopher, writer, musician, and political theorist. His treatises and novels inspired leaders of the French Revolution and the Romantic generation. He was not an academic system-builder, but his influence redirected political thought, ethics, education, music, religion, taste, family life, emotional expression, and ideas of liberty.

Rousseau's thought marked a break with the Age of Reason because it challenged the belief that progress, learning, and refinement naturally improve human beings. He argued that people are naturally good but become corrupted by society and civilization when these take a wrong direction. He helped make nature, sentiment, childhood, conscience, and liberty central themes in modern European culture.

## Formative Years

Rousseau lost his mother at birth and was raised by a father who filled him with pride in Geneva as a republic like Sparta or ancient Rome. His father, a watchmaker with aristocratic pretensions, fled Geneva after trouble with civil authorities. Rousseau then lived as a poor relation in his mother's family until age 16, when he fled Geneva and became an adventurer and Roman Catholic convert in Savoy and France.

Baronne de Warens became Rousseau's protector in Savoy. She gave him shelter, employed him as steward, developed his education, and helped turn an unschooled apprentice into a philosopher, writer, and musician. Their relationship later became intimate, though her morals troubled him. Her intelligence, taste, energy, and sentimental religious background helped form his belief in natural human goodness.

Denis Diderot became Rousseau's friend after Rousseau reached Paris at age 30. Around Diderot's Encyclopedie, Rousseau joined the philosophes, who were reforming, radical, and often anticlerical writers. He soon stood out because his ideas were original and his prose was forceful, though he also remained uneasy with worldly success.

## Music, Progress, and the First Discourse

Rousseau first gained public success in music. His opera Le Devin du village ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Le_devin_du_village)) of 1752 pleased the royal court and could have made him a fashionable composer. Yet he rejected that career because he wished to break with worldly glory and devote himself to moral, literary, and philosophical work.

The Discourse on the Sciences and the Arts, written for the Academy of Dijon in 1750, began Rousseau's mature argument. After what he called an illumination while walking to visit Diderot in prison at Vincennes, he concluded that modern progress had corrupted rather than improved people. The essay argued that human history was a history of decline and introduced the theme that society had damaged natural goodness.

Jean-Philippe Rameau became Rousseau's major opponent in the musical controversy of the early 1750s. Rousseau defended Italian opera and the priority of melody, while Rameau defended French opera and the priority of harmony. Rousseau's side valued freedom, expression, and creative spirit over formal rule, helping prepare Romantic ideas in art. His influence changed musical taste and affected later composers such as Gluck and [[Mozart]].

## Political Philosophy

The Discourse on Inequality of 1755 developed Rousseau's speculative account of human decline. He distinguished natural inequality, such as differences in strength or intelligence, from artificial inequality created by social conventions. Original human beings, unlike the warlike beings imagined by Hobbes, were solitary, healthy, happy, good, and free.

Society began to corrupt people when families, neighbours, comparison, jealousy, pride, and the desire for recognition appeared. Property deepened inequality because law and government arose to protect possession. Rousseau thought civil society offered peace and legal order but mostly benefited the rich by turning de facto ownership into protected right.

The Social Contract ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_contract)), published in 1762, asked how people might recover liberty within political life. Its famous opening claimed that man is born free but everywhere in chains. Rousseau argued that a genuine social contract could replace mere independence with republican liberty: obedience to a law one has prescribed for oneself as part of the sovereign people.

The general will is Rousseau's answer to the problem of many individual wills inside one society. Citizens alienate natural rights to the whole community and receive civil rights protected by collective force. When he wrote that a person may be forced to be free, he meant that a lawbreaker can be compelled back to obedience to the general will, which represents the citizen's true public interest.

True law differs from actual law because true law is made by the people as sovereign and obeyed by the people as subjects. Rousseau also believed that a people may need a lawgiver like Solon, Lycurgus, or [[Calvin and Calvinism (Calvin)|Calvin]] to frame institutions. His chapter on civil religion angered Geneva because it treated Christianity as politically weak for republican patriotism and proposed a minimal civic creed.

## Seclusion, Exile, and Major Works

Rousseau left Paris for a life closer to nature near Montmorency, first on Mme d'Epinay's estate and then at Montlouis under the protection of the Marechal de Luxembourg. This became his most productive period. Within about a year, Julie: or, The New Eloise of 1761, The Social Contract ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_contract)) of 1762, and Emile ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emile%2C_or_On_Education)) of 1762 appeared.

The New Eloise ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Julie%3B_or%2C_The_New_Heloise)) was Rousseau's most popular book in his lifetime and powerfully shaped Romantic sensibility. Its story contrasts public political life with domestic happiness. Saint-Preux loves his noble pupil Julie, but class difference blocks marriage; Julie marries Wolmar, builds a household of order and feeling, and later discovers that her love for Saint-Preux has never disappeared.

Emile ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emile%2C_or_On_Education)) is part novel, part educational treatise. It argues that vice and error are not native to the child but introduced by external forces, so education must work with nature against corrupting pressure. The tutor uses strict control and even manipulation, while avoiding corporal punishment. The book also presents a personal religion centred on God, immortality, conscience, and the presence of divinity in nature.

Persecution followed the publication of Emile ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emile%2C_or_On_Education)) and The Social Contract ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_contract)). French and Genevan authorities ordered books burned and Rousseau arrested. He fled France in 1762, renounced Genevan citizenship in 1763, and spent the rest of his life moving between refuges. His Letters Written from the Mountain of 1764 attacked Geneva's regime as despotic.

David Hume helped Rousseau find refuge in England after he was expelled from Swiss territory, but Rousseau suspected British intellectuals, and Hume himself, of mocking him. Signs of paranoia grew. Rousseau returned to France incognito and married Therese Levasseur in 1768, long after she had become his companion.

## Last Decade and Autobiographical Writings

Rousseau's last decade was dominated by autobiographical writing meant to answer enemies and justify himself. The Confessions, written from 1781 to 1788 in publication history, followed the model of St. Augustine and became a classic of self-examination. Rousseau, Judge of Jean-Jacques answered specific accusations, while Reveries of the Solitary Walker softened earlier intensity into lyrical calm.

Rousseau recovered some peace in his final years under the protection of French noble patrons, first the Prince de Conti and then the Marquis de Girardin. He died in the park at Ermenonville in 1778. His legacy joined democratic aspiration, suspicion of social corruption, Romantic feeling, educational reform, natural religion, and the modern idea that inner sincerity has philosophical and literary value.

## Principal Works

- 1750 Discourse on the Sciences and the Arts: Rousseau's first major work argued that cultural progress had corrupted morals.
- 1755 Discourse on Inequality ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Discourse_on_Inequality)): A speculative history of natural goodness, social comparison, property, and artificial inequality.
- 1758 Letter to Monsieur d'Alembert on the Theatre: A defense of Geneva and attack on theatre as harmful to an innocent community.
- 1761 Julie: or, The New Eloise: A bestselling novel of love, class, domestic feeling, and Romantic emotion.
- 1762 The Social Contract ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_contract)): Rousseau's central statement on republican liberty, sovereignty, law, and the general will.
- 1762 Emile ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emile%2C_or_On_Education)): A treatise-novel on education, nature, happiness, conscience, and personal religion.
- 1764 Letters Written from the Mountain: Rousseau's attack on Genevan political authority.
- 1780-1782 Late autobiographical works: Dialogues, confessions, and reveries defended and examined the self.

- Rousseau's religion stressed conscience, God, immortality, and nature more than church dogma.
- Rousseau's final writings helped make autobiography and self-justification central modern literary forms.
