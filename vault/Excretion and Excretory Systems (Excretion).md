# Excretion and Excretory Systems (Excretion)

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> Excretion removes metabolic wastes while regulating water, salts, and acidity to maintain an organism's internal environment. Its mechanisms range from diffusion in simple organisms to specialized organs integrated with circulation and metabolism in complex animals.

## Introduction

![[attachments/excretion_001.webp|420]]

*Human kidney anatomical illustration*

Excretion ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Excretion)) is the process by which living organisms remove waste products and harmful by-products of metabolism. It is closely connected with the control of water balance, salts, and acidity, and it helps keep the organism's internal environment stable. This process is essential for survival, because waste substances can damage cells if they build up. It also distinguishes excretion from related ideas such as secretion, egestion, and elimination, noting that each term refers to a different kind of disposal.

The main idea is that waste removal must be matched to the organism's size, complexity, and environment. Simple organisms often get rid of waste by diffusion, while larger and more advanced animals need specialized organs and transport systems. Excretion is therefore a broad biological theme that connects cell chemistry, organ function, and whole-body homeostasis.

## Elimination

Elimination is vital for health because energy use always produces by-products that must be removed. It compares the body to an engine: fuel is burned to produce energy, but waste gases and residues must be discharged or efficiency is lost. In living systems, failure to remove wastes can be as dangerous as lack of food, oxygen, or water.

It also notes that many waste products are toxic, so they must be expelled at the same rate they are produced. The same principle applies at the level of communities of organisms: if waste accumulates, the whole system can be damaged. This gives excretion a broad ecological meaning, not only a physiological one.

## Protista

In [[Protists|protists]], which include algae, fungi, protozoans, and slime molds, there are usually no special excretory organs. Wastes such as carbon dioxide, water, oxygen, and nitrogen compounds simply pass through the cell membrane into the environment. Some protozoans also expel solid material through pores in the body surface. Because this happens by passive diffusion, it costs little energy.

## Plants

Plants are generally not treated as having specialized excretory systems. During photosynthesis, oxygen is released through stomata or cell walls, and excess water is removed by guttation and transpiration. In darkness or in non-green tissues, plants release carbon dioxide and water as respiratory wastes. Some plants also exude substances such as resin, latex, or sap by pressure differences inside the body, which again is largely a passive process.

## Animals
Animal excretion becomes more complex as body size and structural organization increase. In simple animals, wastes can move by diffusion across a thin body surface. In larger animals, this is not enough, so specialized waste-disposal systems develop. These systems are especially important for moving wastes quickly to the outside environment and for regulating the body's internal chemical balance.

## Sponges

Sponges, the simplest multicellular animals, dispose of gaseous wastes by diffusion into surrounding water. Solid wastes and indigestible material are expelled from digestive cells into the water streams that constantly flow through the sponge. Their system is simple but effective because their bodies are built around constant water circulation.

## Cnidarians

In [[Cnidarians|cnidarians]] such as jellyfish, corals, and comb jellies, the body has a simple cavity for digestion and waste handling. Gaseous wastes leave by diffusion, while solid wastes pass out through an opening that also serves for food intake. This arrangement shows how even rudimentary animals can combine feeding and waste disposal in one body opening.

## Flatworms

Flatworms have a more developed digestive tract, including a mouth, pharynx, and gastrovascular cavity. Waste and food pass through the same opening, but the body plan is still simple. That proboscis worms have a more efficient arrangement, with a distinct mouth, intestine, and excretory opening, so food and wastes move in one direction and waste removal improves.

## Nematodes

Roundworms have an additional excretory structure: canals on both sides of the intestine carry waste material to an excretory pore. This is one of the clearest examples of how increasing body complexity is matched by more specialized excretory mechanisms. This show a general pattern in invertebrates: as anatomy becomes more complex, waste disposal becomes more efficient.

## Vertebrates

In vertebrates, excretion must serve not only waste disposal but also regulation of water, salts, and organic substances. The kidney becomes the central organ for this task. Vertebrates live in very different environments, and their excretory systems are adapted to those conditions. This is why the kidney is described as a highly sophisticated structure rather than a simple filter.

## Mammals

![[attachments/excretion_002.png|420]]

*Diagram of transport processes along a nephron*

The mammalian kidney is made of two main regions, the cortex and the medulla, and its working unit is the nephron. Each nephron begins with Bowman's capsule and a glomerulus, then continues through a proximal tubule, the loop of Henle, and a distal tubule before reaching a collecting duct. The kidney forms urine through three linked processes: filtration, reabsorption, and secretion.

Filtration removes water and dissolved substances from the blood, but then the body selectively reabsorbs what it needs and secretes additional substances into the forming urine. This selective process is what makes the kidney so efficient. It also allows the organism to remove foreign substances, because unwanted materials are not reabsorbed.

## Amphibians

Amphibians show the importance of water balance in excretion. They excrete nitrogen mainly as urea and cannot produce highly concentrated urine. When they are on land, they lose water quickly; in water, they absorb water by osmosis. Their kidneys and bladder therefore play an important role in storing and conserving water, while antidiuretic hormone helps reduce urine volume and increase water reabsorption.

## Fishes

A comparison of freshwater fishes and marine fishes shows how different environments create different excretion problems. Freshwater fishes gain water by osmosis and lose salts, so they produce large amounts of dilute urine and take in salts through their gills. Marine fishes lose water and gain salt, so they produce little urine and drink seawater, while their gills actively excrete excess salt.

The discussion also notes that sharks and rays convert ammonia to urea, which helps them retain water and maintain internal balance. This case shows how evolutionary adaptation changes the chemistry of excretion to fit a species' habitat.
