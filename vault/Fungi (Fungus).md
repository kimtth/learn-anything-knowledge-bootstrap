# Fungi (Fungus)

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> Fungi are eukaryotic organisms that digest food externally and absorb nutrients rather than photosynthesizing. Their hyphae and mycelia support roles as decomposers, parasites, symbionts, crop threats, and industrial organisms, spanning yeasts, molds, mushrooms, rusts, and smuts.

## Introduction

Fungi are a kingdom of about 80,000 known species—including yeasts, rusts, smuts, mildews, molds, mushrooms, and toadstools—that are among the most widely distributed organisms on Earth. The Latin word for mushroom, fungus, came to stand for the whole group, and the study of fungi is called mycology, from the Greek mykes.

Fungal biology emphasizes that fungi are eukaryotic but are not plants: they lack chlorophyll, do not photosynthesize, and absorb nutrients only after secreting enzymes that digest organic matter externally. Ancient peoples blamed crop-destroying fungi on the gods—the Romans named Robigus the god of rust and held an annual festival, the Robigalia, in his honour—though the poisonous fly agaric (Amanita muscaria) shows that some fungi were long familiar. This mode of life makes them central decomposers, parasites, symbionts, and industrial organisms.

## Form and Function

![[attachments/fungi_fungus_001.webp|420]]

*Fungi collage*

Fungi ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fungus)) are built from hyphae, which form a branching network called a mycelium. The visible part is usually the fruiting body, or sporophore, and these range from microscopic forms to gigantic ones: some mushrooms reach 20-25 cm across, bracket fungi exceed 40 cm, and the largest puffballs on record measured 150 cm (5 feet). A specimen of the bracket fungus Fomitiporia ellipsoidea ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phellinus_ellipsoideus)) found in 2010 on Hainan Island in southern China was 10.8 metres long, may have held some 450 million spores, and weighed an estimated 400-500 kg, the largest fungal fruiting body ever documented.

Hyphae may be divided by septa or may form continuous tubes with many nuclei. Their walls contain chitin, and their growth occurs almost exclusively at the apical tip, directed by a body known by its German name, the Spitzenkorper, allowing fungi to penetrate soil, wood, food, and living tissue. Mushrooms were the only fungi known before the invention of the microscope in the 17th century, which revealed the vast diversity of microscopic forms; the visible mushroom is only the reproductive structure of a larger hidden mycelium.

[[Reproduction and Reproductive Systems (Reproduction)|Reproduction]] is one of the major themes of fungal biology. Fungi produce spores in many ways, both sexually and asexually. Sporangia, conidia, asci, basidia, and fruiting bodies allow fungi to disperse widely and survive changing conditions.

## Nutrition and Ecological Roles

![[attachments/fungi_fungus_002.webp|420]]

*Geweihförmige Holzkeule - Staghorn fungus - Xylaria hypoxylon - 01*

Fungi are important because they break down dead organic matter, recycle nutrients, and help maintain soil fertility.

Saprotrophic fungi digest dead plants, wood, leaf litter, and animal remains, returning carbon, nitrogen, phosphorus, and minerals to ecosystems. Without them, forests and soils would accumulate undecomposed organic matter.

Parasitic fungi attack plants, animals, and other fungi. Rusts, smuts, mildews, and rots can damage crops and forests, while fungal diseases affect humans and animals. Fungi are therefore both ecological helpers and serious pathogens.

Mycorrhizae are crucial mutualisms between fungi and plant roots, relied on by about 90 percent of land plants, especially for phosphorus; in return the fungus receives carbohydrates from the plant. There are two main types, ectomycorrhizae (external, as in boletes associated with larch and other conifers) and endomycorrhizae (formed within the root cells). In some forests these mycelial networks link many trees together, letting them exchange nutrients and chemical signals.

## Lichens and Special Relationships

One of the most remarkable fungal partnerships is the formation of lichens, in which fungi live together with algae or cyanobacteria. The result is a stable symbiotic organism with distinctive structure and ecology.

Lichens can live on rocks, bark, soil, and exposed surfaces where few organisms survive. About 15,000 kinds have been described; half the partnerships contain the green alga Trebouxia, and others pair the fungus with cyanobacteria such as Nostoc. The largest, foliose species of Sticta, may reach about a metre across. Lichens help form soil, tolerate drought and cold, provide forage for reindeer, and serve as indicators of air pollution.

## Evolution and Classification

Fungi ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fungus)) have a very old evolutionary history but a scanty fossil record. The giant Prototaxites, common worldwide in the middle and late Devonian Period (400 to 350 million years ago), is interpreted as either a lichen or a large fungus. Because fossils are rare, scientists relied first on biochemical traits and then, from the 1990s, on molecular phylogenetics—initially the small-subunit ribosomal RNA gene—to map fungal relationships.

Classification ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taxonomy)) has changed as molecular evidence clarified relationships among chytrids, zygomycete-like forms, ascomycetes, basidiomycetes, and other lineages. A modern phylogenetic scheme, adapted from Ainsworth & Bisby's Dictionary of the Fungi (9th ed., 2001) and the U.S. National Science Foundation's AFTOL project, divides the kingdom into 7 phyla, 35 classes, and 129 orders. Older groupings based only on visible structures, which once placed fungi in the plant kingdom, were useful but incomplete.

Ascomycetes include many yeasts, molds, cup fungi, morels, truffles, and important plant pathogens. Basidiomycetes include mushrooms, puffballs, bracket fungi, rusts, and smuts. These groups show the range from microscopic forms to elaborate fruiting bodies.

## Importance to Humans

![[attachments/fungi_fungus_003.webp|420]]

*Edible fungi in basket 2012 G1*

Fungi matter for medicine, food production, and industry. They are used in fermentation, cheese ripening, and the production of medicines such as antibiotics.

Yeasts such as Saccharomyces cerevisiae are essential in bread, beer, and wine; humans have used fungi since the first leavened loaf and the first tub of grape must turned to wine. Edible mushrooms and truffles are foods, and molds ripen cheeses—Penicillium camemberti matures Brie and Camembert. At the same time, poisonous mushrooms and food-spoiling molds make fungal identification and control important.

Penicillin and other fungal products transformed medicine, while fungal enzymes and metabolites became industrial tools. These benefits must be balanced against the damage fungi cause to crops, stored food, buildings, and living tissues.

Plant diseases caused by fungi include rusts, smuts, mildews, blights, wilts, rots, and cankers; Claviceps purpurea, for example, attacks rye and other cereals to cause ergot. These diseases can reduce harvests, destroy stored crops, and reshape agricultural practice, which is why fungal pathology is a major reason fungi matter economically.

Human fungal diseases range from superficial infections to serious systemic disease, especially when immunity is weakened. Fungi also produce toxins, including poisonous mushroom compounds and mycotoxins in contaminated food. This makes fungi medically important even when they are not directly infectious.

Ecological succession often depends on fungi because they colonize dead material, form soil, support plant roots, and participate in food webs. Some fungi are specialized predators: oyster mushrooms (Pleurotus) coat their hyphae with adhesive to catch nematodes, while soil fungi such as Arthrobotrys and Dactylella form constricting rings, usually of three touch-sensitive cells, that swell within a fraction of a second to trap a passing worm before a hypha grows in and digests it.
Fungal life cycles can be complex. A single species may have yeastlike and filamentous forms, multiple spore stages, or different hosts. Rust fungi, for example, may require more than one host plant to complete their development.

In summary, fungi are presented as a kingdom defined by absorption, hyphae, spores, symbiosis, and decomposition. They are invisible architects of soil fertility, visible producers of mushrooms and molds, destructive agents of disease and decay, and useful partners in food, medicine, and industry.
Fungal growth can be remarkably effective. A mycelium spreads through its food source, exposes a large surface area, and digests material externally before absorbing it. This makes fungi well suited to wood, soil, dung, leaf litter, fruit, stored grain, and living hosts.

Dimorphism and environmental response show the flexibility of fungal form. Temperature, nutrients, moisture, host tissues, and chemical signals can change growth patterns, spore production, and pathogenic behaviour. Fungi are therefore dynamic organisms rather than fixed mushroom-shaped forms.

Fungal ecology links microscopic activity to global processes. Decomposition affects carbon cycling, mycorrhizae influence plant communities, lichens colonize exposed surfaces, pathogens regulate populations, and symbiotic fungi help many plants survive poor soils.

For humans, fungi are simultaneously useful, dangerous, and scientifically revealing. They provide model organisms for genetics and cell biology, but they also challenge agriculture, medicine, forestry, food storage, and conservation. Their importance is out of proportion to their often-hidden bodies.

Fungi ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fungus)) occupy boundaries: between death and renewal, disease and medicine, hidden mycelium and visible fruiting body, independent organism and symbiotic partner. Their biology explains why they are essential to ecosystems and unavoidable in human life.

This makes mycology important for agriculture, ecology, medicine, evolution, food technology, and environmental science.

## Key Events & Developments

- Fungi were separated from the plant kingdom because they lack chlorophyll and digest food externally.
- The invention of the microscope in the 17th century revealed the huge diversity of fungal forms.
- Mycorrhizal and lichen associations showed the ecological importance of fungi.
- Molecular phylogenetics from the 1990s reshaped fungal classification into 7 phyla and 129 orders.
