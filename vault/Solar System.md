## Introduction

![[attachments/solar_system_001.webp|420]]

*Diagram of selected moons of the solar system with Earth for scale*

The solar system ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solar_System)) consists of the Sun, eight planets, about 170 known planetary satellites, dwarf planets, asteroids, comets, icy bodies, and the thin gas and dust of the interplanetary medium. The Sun is an average star in the Milky Way Galaxy but contains more than 99 percent of the system's mass.

Ancient astronomers could see the Sun, Moon, and brightest planets with the naked eye, and their observations helped create astronomy. Modern knowledge comes from telescopes, instruments above and below Earth's atmosphere, meteorites, Moon rocks returned by astronauts, and spacecraft probes and landers sent to planets, moons, asteroids, and other bodies.

The planets in order from the Sun are Mercury, Venus, Earth, Mars, Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus, and Neptune. Pluto was counted as a planet after its discovery in 1930, but after many similar icy bodies were found beyond Neptune, the International Astronomical Union reclassified it in 2006 as a dwarf planet.

## Orbits

![[attachments/solar_system_002.webp|420]]

*Solar system bodies classified by orbit type*

Most major bodies move around the Sun in prograde, or direct, motion, the same direction as the Sun's rotation. Seen from above Earth's North Pole, their orbits are counterclockwise. The planets, dwarf planets, rocky asteroids, and Kuiper belt bodies mostly follow this pattern.

Oort cloud comets are different. Their orbits point in random directions, matching a spherical distribution around the planetary plane. Some have inclinations greater than 90 degrees and therefore move in retrograde motion, opposite the Sun's rotation.

An orbit's shape is measured by eccentricity. A circular orbit has eccentricity 0, while more elongated orbits approach 1. Venus and Neptune have very circular orbits, while Mercury is the most eccentric major planet and Pluto is even more eccentric. Inclination measures the tilt of an orbit relative to Earth's orbital plane, the ecliptic.

## Planets and Their Moons

The planets divide into two main groups. The four inner terrestrial planets, Mercury, Venus, Earth, and Mars, are rocky and dense. They have solid surfaces, no rings, and few or no moons. Only Earth has a strong magnetic field that forms a magnetosphere and traps charged particles in the Van Allen belts.

The four outer Jovian planets, Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus, and Neptune, are much larger and less dense. Jupiter and Saturn are mainly hydrogen and helium, while Uranus and Neptune include more ice and rock. All have magnetic fields, ring systems, and many moons.

Pluto is unlike the major planets. It is icy, low-density, smaller than Earth's Moon, and more similar to comets or large icy moons than to the terrestrial or giant planets. Its place in the Kuiper belt explains why it is now treated as a dwarf planet.

The moons are diverse. Io, orbiting Jupiter, has intense volcanism. Titan, Saturn's largest moon, is larger than Mercury and has a dense primitive atmosphere. Triton orbits Neptune in retrograde motion and has a thin atmosphere above an extremely cold surface.

## Asteroids and Comets

![[attachments/solar_system_003.webp|420]]

*Illustration of an asteroid and comet-like small body in space*

Asteroids and comets are remnants of planet formation. Most asteroids orbit between Mars and Jupiter in the asteroid belt. They range from Ceres, about 940 km across and also classed as a dwarf planet, down to dust particles.

Some asteroids cross Earth's orbit and can collide with the planet. Large impacts are rare but dangerous; one impact is thought to have helped cause the mass extinction at the end of the Cretaceous Period, about 65 million years ago. Smaller fragments reach Earth as meteorites.

Asteroids vary in composition. Some are mainly metal, especially iron; some are stony; and some are rich in organic compounds like carbonaceous chondrite meteorites. Spacecraft visits show irregular, cratered bodies, some preserving primitive material from the early solar system.

Comet nuclei are very different. They are mainly ices, especially frozen water, with frozen carbon dioxide, carbon monoxide, methanol, rock dust, and organic compounds. Some contain more dust than ice. When comets approach the Sun, material escapes and leaves dust trails that can produce meteor showers when Earth crosses them.

Long-period comets, with periods above 200 years, come from the distant Oort cloud, a spherical shell around the solar system about 50,000 AU away. Short-period comets come mainly from the Kuiper belt, a disk beyond Neptune. Centaur objects between Jupiter and Neptune may be comet nuclei perturbed inward from the Kuiper belt.

## Interplanetary Medium

The interplanetary medium includes dust, protons, electrons, ions, and solar-wind plasma moving through planetary space. Solar flares add matter and high-energy radiation. Earth's atmosphere receives more than 80 tons of mostly asteroidal and cometary dust grains on an average day.

In 2012, Voyager 1 crossed the heliopause, the boundary between the interplanetary medium and interstellar space. This made it possible to measure conditions beyond the Sun's direct plasma environment.

## Origin of the Solar System

Theories of solar system origin developed as observations improved. Early scientific work after Newton's laws tried to explain planetary motions, satellites, comets, and asteroids. Immanuel Kant proposed in 1755 that the system began as a cloud of dispersed particles.

About 40 years later, Pierre-Simon Laplace proposed a rotating Sun with an extended atmosphere that cooled, contracted, and left rings of material that formed planets and moons. The combined Kant-Laplace nebular hypothesis explained why planets orbit in the same direction and nearly the same plane, but it struggled with asteroids, retrograde moons, and angular momentum.

In the early 20th century, some scientists proposed catastrophic theories in which a close stellar encounter pulled material from the Sun to form planets. Later evidence led researchers back toward nebular ideas. Modern theory treats solar-system formation as part of star formation.

The favoured modern model begins with collapse of part of an interstellar cloud of gas and dust, only about 10 to 20 percent more massive than the present Sun. The collapse forms a central condensation and a rotating disk called the solar nebula. Planets and smaller bodies formed from material in this disk while the young Sun formed at the centre.

## Key Events & Developments

- 1755: Immanuel Kant proposed an early nebular idea for solar-system origin.
- About 1790s: Pierre-Simon Laplace developed a rotating nebular model.
- 1930: Pluto was discovered beyond Neptune.
- 1992: An icy body beyond Pluto helped reveal the larger Kuiper belt population.
- 2006: The International Astronomical Union reclassified Pluto as a dwarf planet.
- 2012: Voyager 1 crossed the heliopause into interstellar space.

## Interesting Facts

- One astronomical unit is the average Earth-Sun distance, about 150 million km.
- Saturn is so low in density that it would float in water if such an ocean existed.
- Oort cloud comets can have orbital periods of millions of years.
- Earth's atmosphere is bombarded daily by more than 80 tons of dust grains.
- Kuiper belt objects helped explain why Pluto differs so much from the eight major planets.
