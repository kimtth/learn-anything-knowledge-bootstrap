## Introduction

Physical science ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Outline_of_physical_science)) uses procedures and concepts for studying the inorganic world. Like all natural sciences, it tries to describe shared experiences of the surrounding world in ways that different observers can agree upon. Its central field, physics, studies matter, forces, motion, fields, energy, and the deeper structure of material objects.

Physics has two linked roles. It seeks the most basic constituents and laws of nature, but it also develops techniques for understanding complex systems that cannot be calculated directly from first principles. Even when living organisms are considered, no special principles appear necessary for matter itself, though mind raises a separate issue.

This distinction matters because knowing fundamental particles and forces does not automatically make weather, fluids, crystals, or living matter easy to calculate.

The methods of physical science combine experiment, measurement, graphing, mathematical modelling, approximation, and repeated testing. A law or equation gains support when it not only fits one experiment but predicts different arrangements and survives more precise tests.

Approximation and correction work together: a simple law is tested, its limits are found, and a more exact treatment explains the departures.

Ideal models such as frictionless planes, point masses, ideal gases, and continuous fields are useful because they expose structure before complications are restored. Physical science often advances by moving between such simplified models and more realistic corrections.

## Galileo and Experiment

![[attachments/principles_of_physical_science_001.png|420]]

*Diagram of Galileo's inclined plane experiment*

The discussion of [[Galileo]]'s rolling-ball experiment illustrates scientific reasoning. Measurements of distance and time may contain random errors and systematic bias, so a formula such as distance proportional to time squared must be tested by plotting data and considering possible timing offsets.

If measured times all include a constant error, the data may fit x = a(t + t0)2 rather than x = at2. A graph can reveal the offset, and corrected values can show whether distance truly varies as the square of time. The goal is not just to fit numbers but to uncover the physical rule behind them.

The rolling-ball analysis leads to constant acceleration. Instantaneous speed is the derivative of distance with respect to time, and acceleration is the derivative of velocity. If velocity is proportional to elapsed time, acceleration is constant.

A pendulum provides a stronger test. For small oscillations, the period should be independent of amplitude and proportional to the square root of length. Careful experiments show slight departures for large amplitudes, but those departures follow from a more exact equation, strengthening rather than weakening the theory.

## Newtonian Dissection of Systems

Newton's laws allow complex mechanical systems to be divided into simpler parts. A system of two masses joined by a string over a pulley can be analyzed by isolating each mass, identifying gravity and tension, and requiring the accelerations to match because the string does not stretch.

This dissection method extends beyond pulleys. A liquid can be imagined as many small volume elements acted on by gravity, pressure, and viscous drag while remaining in contact. From such local interactions, differential equations of fluid motion are derived.

The same strategy appears throughout physics. Complex behaviour is reduced to elementary units, local forces, constraints, and equations that describe how parts interact. The total result may be difficult, but the method gives a disciplined path from simple laws to real systems.

This approach also explains why physical science often uses differential equations: local rules for small parts can be assembled into a description of whole systems.

## Fields, Vectors, and Potential

Field theory describes forces spread through space. Newton's gravitation and Coulomb's electrostatic law both give inverse-square forces along the line between particles. A force is a vector, with magnitude and direction, and multiple forces combine by vector addition.

An electric charge creates an electric field E at each point in space. A second charge placed there feels a force proportional to its charge and the field. Instead of adding all forces separately each time, physicists describe the field as a function of position.

The gradient of a scalar quantity is a vector pointing in the direction of steepest increase. On a map, it points uphill and has magnitude equal to the steepest slope. In three dimensions, gradients help connect scalar potential functions with vector fields.

A potential such as A/r produces an inverse-square field when its gradient is taken. Electrostatic and gravitational fields from point sources can be represented by potentials, and adding potentials is often easier than adding vectors. Conservative forces are those that can be derived from a potential.

Equipotential surfaces are surfaces where potential is constant. Field lines are normal to these surfaces and show field direction; for inverse-square fields, their density can also represent field strength. This gives a visual and mathematical way to understand charges and gravitational sources.

## Gauss's Theorem, Images, and Laplace's Equation

Gauss's theorem relates electric flux through a closed surface to the charge enclosed. It leads to differential ideas such as divergence, which measures net outward flux per unit volume. Where charge density is present, divergence of the electric field is proportional to that density.
The idea of divergence reappears in fluid flow, where an incompressible liquid has no net accumulation inside a small volume.

This shared [[The History of Mathematics (Mathematics)|mathematics]] lets one physical idea reappear across electricity, magnetism, fluids, and diffusion.

The method of images solves some conductor problems by replacing a conducting boundary with fictitious charges that produce the same external field. A charge near a grounded conducting sphere, for example, can be treated as interacting with an image charge at a calculated position.

Where there is no charge, potential obeys Laplace's equation. This equation appears in electrostatics, gravitational theory, fluid flow, and other fields. It shows how a single mathematical structure can describe different physical situations.

## Magnetic Fields and Curl

Magnetic fields require another vector operation, curl. Around a current-carrying wire, magnetic field lines circulate, and the line integral of the magnetic field around a closed path depends on the current enclosed.

Inside regions with distributed current, a simple potential is not enough. The current density J describes current flow per unit area, and curl B is related to J. This local differential relation connects magnetic fields with the currents that generate them.

Changing electric fields can also generate magnetic fields, and changing magnetic fields can generate electric fields. These relations are at the heart of Maxwell's electromagnetic theory, showing the power of differential equations in physical science.

Maxwell's equations illustrate a major theme: fields are not merely bookkeeping devices but physical structures whose changing values can propagate as waves.

## Continuity, Diffusion, and Field Equations

Continuity equations express conservation. In an incompressible fluid, the velocity field has zero divergence because no net fluid accumulates or disappears in a small volume. In a compressible fluid, mass flux must be related to changes in density.

Diffusion describes random wandering of molecules or particles, as in Brownian motion or ink spreading in water. A continuity equation combines with Fick's law, which says particles drift from high concentration to low concentration at a rate proportional to the density gradient.

The resulting diffusion equation predicts how a concentrated cloud spreads with time. The radius grows roughly with the square root of time while central density falls to conserve total particle number. A single-particle probability problem is thus transformed into a field equation.

Field equations also describe waves, quantum probability waves, special relativity, and general relativity. In general relativity, the geometry of four-dimensional space-time becomes non-Euclidean, showing that mathematical structure itself can become part of physical explanation.

## Conservation Laws and Extremal Principles

Conservation laws follow from Newton's laws and deeper symmetries. In an isolated system, total momentum remains constant because internal forces occur in equal and opposite pairs. Similar conservation ideas apply to energy, angular momentum, and charge.

Extremal principles offer another way to state physical laws. Pierre de Fermat proposed that light takes a path of least time between two points through mirrors and lenses. Reflection and refraction can be derived from this principle if light travels more slowly in media with higher refractive index.

In mechanics, least action was developed by Maupertuis and later given rigorous form by William Rowan Hamilton. Such principles do not replace local equations but show that physical paths can often be characterized globally, as those that make a quantity stationary.

## Symmetry and Crystals

Symmetry helps classify physical structures. Crystals contain atoms or molecules arranged in regular repeating patterns, and a unit cell is the smallest block that can generate the whole crystal by repetition.

X-ray crystallography made it possible to determine these structures experimentally. Mathematics alone rarely predicts a complex crystal arrangement from an isolated atom. Sulfur, for example, forms rings of eight atoms and complex unit cells, facts discovered through experiment and chemical expertise rather than simple deduction.

Even so, symmetry can simplify possible arrangements and identify patterns. It gives physical science a bridge between geometry, observation, and material structure.

## Entropy, Fluctuation, and Irreversibility

Entropy is connected with probability and the direction of macroscopic change. In a small system of 100 atoms divided by a partition, random fluctuations may temporarily put many more atoms on one side than the other. Such a fluctuation can reverse, and detailed motion remains reversible.

For systems with enormous numbers of [[Atoms|atoms]], however, large entropy-decreasing fluctuations become overwhelmingly unlikely. If a system is found far from the mean, it is much more likely to return toward equilibrium than move farther away. This explains why entropy almost always increases in macroscopic experience.

The statistical view reconciles microscopic reversibility with macroscopic irreversibility. It does not make entropy decrease impossible in principle; it makes it fantastically improbable for ordinary large systems.

## Chaos

Chaos ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chaos_theory)) appears in deterministic systems that are extremely sensitive to initial conditions. A regularly driven pendulum may return to a stable oscillation after disturbance, but a bouncing ball on a vibrating plate can become unsynchronized and irregular while still obeying simple equations.

Chaotic behaviour is not random like raindrops. It is determined by equations, but prediction fails beyond a horizon because tiny differences in starting conditions grow exponentially. A numerical sequence with initial values differing by one part per million may become unrelated after enough iterations.

This horizon of predictability is practical and physical. Measurements have finite precision, and real systems suffer outside disturbances. In chaos, every extra decimal place of initial knowledge only extends prediction a little farther.

## Core Equations

Physical models often begin by relating force, energy, waves, and conserved quantities:

$$\vec{F}=m\vec{a}$$

This is Newton's second law: force is the product of mass and acceleration, so a larger mass or a faster change in motion requires a larger net force.

$$E=h\nu$$

This relation says that photon energy is proportional to frequency; higher-frequency radiation carries more energy per photon.

$$E=mc^2$$

This expresses mass-energy equivalence: mass is a form of energy, with the speed of light squared as the conversion factor.

## Key Events & Developments

- about 1660: Pierre de Fermat formulated the principle of least time for light.
- 1835: William Rowan Hamilton gave rigorous form to the principle of least action.
- early modern period: [[Galileo]]'s experiments linked motion, acceleration, and mathematical testing.
- classical physics: [[Newton]]'s laws supported conservation of momentum and mechanical analysis.
- modern physics: Maxwell's field theory, quantum mechanics, relativity, and chaos extended the mathematical methods of physical science.
