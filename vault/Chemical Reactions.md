# Chemical Reactions

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> Chemical reactions rearrange atoms by breaking and forming bonds, converting reactants into products while conserving matter. Their rates, energy changes, equilibria, and conditions explain chemical transformation in laboratories, organisms, and industry.

## Introduction

Chemical reactions ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemical_reaction)) are processes in which reactants are converted into different products by rearranging atoms. They are central to technology, culture, geology, the atmosphere, the oceans, and life itself. Burning fuel, brewing beer, making glass, and forming biological molecules all depend on chemical change.

Chemical reactions are also distinct from physical changes ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physical_change)). A physical change alters state or form without changing chemical identity, while a chemical reaction produces new substances. This distinction is the starting point for everything that follows.

## Historical Overview

The concept of a chemical reaction developed over about 250 years. Its roots lie in early gas studies and in experiments that separated elements from compounds. These investigations helped define chemistry as a science with its own rules of change and combination.

The first major studies were connected with the discovery of oxygen in the 18th century by Carl Wilhelm Scheele and Joseph Priestley. Their work, together with later theories of combustion and composition, helped establish the idea that substances can transform into one another through ordered chemical processes.

## Synthesis and Conservation

Synthesis is the making of a new substance from simpler substances. Chemical equations represent this process, with reactants on the left and products on the right. The equation must be balanced because matter is neither created nor destroyed in ordinary chemical reactions.

The reaction of iron and sulfur illustrates stoichiometry. The masses of the reactants must match the mass of the product, and if one reactant is present in excess, some will remain unused. Reactions are not only symbolic but also quantitative.

The same logic extends to combustion, using methane and oxygen as an example. In such reactions, the atoms in the reactants are redistributed into products such as carbon dioxide and water. The equation tells the proportions in which the reactants combine and the products appear.

Chemical equations are not just shorthand; they are claims about conservation and proportion. The equation mirrors the real atomic bookkeeping of the reaction.

## Acid-Base Reactions

Several theories describe acid-base behaviour. In the Brønsted-Lowry view, acids donate protons and bases accept them, so the reaction between hydrochloric acid and ammonia produces ammonium and chloride ions. Conjugate acid-base pairs determine the direction of equilibrium, which moves toward the weaker acid and base.

The Lewis theory broadens the idea further by defining bases as electron-pair donors and acids as electron-pair acceptors. Ammonia and boron trifluoride form a Lewis acid-base pair, and coordination compounds can be treated as acid-base reactions in this wider sense.

## Classification by Reaction Outcome

Reactions can be classified by what they accomplish. Decomposition reactions break compounds apart, combination reactions join substances together, and displacement reactions replace one element or group with another. Some reactions are also isomerizations, in which the atoms remain the same but are rearranged into a different structure.

This classification helps chemists identify common patterns across many specific reactions. The point is that reactions can be grouped by result even when the detailed mechanisms differ.

## Polymerization

Polymerization deserves special attention, because it underlies plastics and many natural substances. In addition polymerization, many small monomers join together without loss of atoms, as in the formation of polyethylene from ethylene. In condensation polymerization, molecules join with the elimination of a small molecule such as water.
Starch, cellulose, and nylon are examples of condensation products, and protein formation also relies on peptide bonds formed with water loss. This shows that polymer chemistry is not limited to synthetic plastics; it is also fundamental to biology.

## Photolysis and Chain Reactions

Photolysis is a reaction initiated or sustained by light. One example is the light-driven formation of chloromethane from methane and chlorine. Such reactions can proceed by chain mechanisms, in which radicals formed in one step continue the reaction through many propagation steps.

Chain reactions involve initiation, propagation, and termination steps. This matters because it shows that reactions may be fast or slow not simply because of what molecules are present, but because of how the reaction pathway is organized.

## Conclusion

Chemical reactions are the basic language of change in chemistry. Reactions can be described by equations, measured quantitatively, grouped by type, and explained through acid-base, polymer, and photochemical mechanisms. The larger point is that chemistry studies how substances transform, not just what they are.

## Core Equations

Reaction stoichiometry and rate laws are core quantitative tools:

$$\mathrm{aA+bB \rightarrow cC+dD}$$

Read the left side as the reactants or starting materials and the right side as the products; the coefficients show the proportions needed to conserve atoms or charge.

$$r=k[A]^m[B]^n$$

This rate law says the reaction rate depends on a rate constant k and on reactant concentrations raised to experimentally determined powers.

Combustion of methane is a simple balanced example:

$$\mathrm{CH_4 + 2O_2 \rightarrow CO_2 + 2H_2O}$$

## Key Events & Developments

- 18th century: Oxygen studies help launch the modern concept of chemical reaction.
- 19th century: Balancing equations and stoichiometry become central tools in chemistry.
- 20th century: Acid-base theory, radical chemistry, and polymer chemistry deepen the understanding of reactions.

## Interesting Facts

- Chemical reactions are central to both life and industry.
- Water does not change chemically when it changes state, but it does when it reacts with sodium metal.
- Polymerization can create both synthetic plastics and natural biopolymers.
