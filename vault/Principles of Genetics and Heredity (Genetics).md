## Introduction

Genetics ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Genetics)) is the study of heredity in general and genes in particular. It is one of the central pillars of biology and overlaps with agriculture, medicine, and biotechnology. Human beings recognized heredity long before genetics became a science, using it to improve crops and domestic animals.

Ancient evidence shows early practical knowledge of heredity. A Babylonian tablet more than 6,000 years old records horse pedigrees and possible inherited traits, and old carvings show cross-pollination of date palms. The mechanisms of inheritance, however, remained unclear until genetics became systematic in the 19th century.

Modern genetics began with the idea that genes are the units responsible for heredity. It studies genes at all levels: how they act inside cells, how they pass from parents to offspring, what chemical substance they are made of, and how they interact with environment. That chemical substance is DNA.

Gene action depends on environmental interaction. Green plants contain genes needed to synthesize chlorophyll, but chlorophyll is produced only when those genes act in the presence of light. In darkness, the same genetic capacity remains, but expression stops.

## Mendel and Chromosomal Genetics

![[attachments/principles_of_genetics_and_heredity_genetics_001.png|420]]

*Chart of pea plant traits studied by Gregor Mendel*

Gregor Mendel founded genetics as a scientific discipline in the mid-19th century. He suspected that traits were inherited as discrete units, even though he did not know their physical or chemical nature. Later work showed that these units corresponded to genes carried on chromosomes.

Work with Drosophila fruit flies helped connect genes with chromosomes. Thomas Hunt Morgan and his students showed sex-linked inheritance and gene linkage. Calvin Bridges used fruit flies with an extra chromosome to show that abnormal inheritance could be explained only if genes were part of that chromosome.

Hermann Joseph Muller showed that X-rays could produce new alleles, or mutations, at high frequencies. This was the first demonstration of an environmental mutagenic agent, though mutations can also occur spontaneously. Harriet Creighton and Barbara McClintock later showed that new linked-gene combinations correlated with exchanged chromosome parts.

## Early Molecular Genetics

In 1908, Archibald Garrod proposed that alkaptonuria and some other hereditary diseases were inborn errors of metabolism. This suggested that genes have molecular effects inside cells. Molecular genetics began in earnest in 1941 when George Beadle and Edward Tatum showed that genes in Neurospora crassa code for enzymes.

The idea that genes code for proteins was then extended to many organisms. Soon afterward, Oswald Avery, Colin M. MacLeod, and Maclyn McCarty showed that bacterial genes are made of DNA. This finding was later extended to all organisms.

## DNA and the Genetic Code

A major landmark came in 1953, when James D. Watson, Francis Crick, and Maurice Wilkins proposed the double helix model of DNA. The model explained how DNA could replicate: the two complementary strands separate and serve as templates for new DNA molecules.

DNA consists of chains of nucleotides. Because proteins are chains of amino acids, scientists proposed that DNA nucleotide sequences contain a code for amino acid sequences and therefore protein structure. Seymour Benzer later showed that mutant sites within a gene could be mapped linearly, indicating that the gene itself has linear structure.

In 1958, Matthew Meselson and Franklin W. Stahl demonstrated semiconservative DNA replication. In 1961, Francis Crick and Sydney Brenner showed that the genetic code is read in nucleotide triplets called codons. Charles Yanofsky showed that positions of gene mutations matched positions of altered amino acids in the corresponding protein.

In 1966, Marshall Nirenberg and Har Gobind Khorana deduced the complete genetic code of 64 codons and their amino acid meanings. Later work showed that DNA structure, replication, and the genetic code are nearly universal across plants, animals, fungi, bacteria, and viruses.

## Gene Regulation and Recombinant DNA

In 1961, Francois Jacob and Jacques Monod established a model of gene regulation. They showed that bacterial genes can be turned on and off when regulatory proteins bind near the coding region, controlling transcription into RNA and protein synthesis.

Technical advances transformed genetic research. Restriction enzymes, discovered by Daniel Nathans and Hamilton Othanel Smith in 1970, allowed DNA to be cut at specific sites. Recombinant DNA technology and the polymerase chain reaction later made it possible to copy, analyze, and manipulate genetic material with great precision.

## Cytogenetics and Microbial Genetics

Cytogenetics combines cytology and genetics by studying chromosomes under the microscope. Cytologists discovered chromosomes and their duplication and separation during cell division just as geneticists were learning how genes behave. Plant cytogenetics grew early because plant chromosomes are often larger than animal chromosomes.

Animal and human cytogenetics advanced with techniques such as the squash method, in which cells are flattened on glass for microscopic observation. Later molecular labels made it possible to mark specific genes, chromosomes, RNAs, and proteins, greatly improving research.

Microbial genetics became important after scientists realized that microorganisms have variable traits and reproduce rapidly. Bacteria became powerful model organisms and central to cloning technology. Viral genetics, especially viruses that attack bacteria, also became a key field and produced discoveries of broad genetic importance.

## Genomics and Population Genetics

Genomics arose from the ability to sequence entire genomes routinely. It studies genome structure, function, and evolutionary comparison. It also reveals interacting gene sets that affect biological properties and depends heavily on bioinformatics, the computer-based analysis of large biological data sets.

Population genetics ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Population_genetics)) studies genes in populations of animals, plants, and microbes. It uses statistical methods to analyze allele frequencies, genotype distributions, migration, evolutionary relationships, adaptation, mutation, selection, and random change. The Hardy-Weinberg formula predicts genotype frequencies in randomly mating populations.

Human population geneticists use DNA to trace origins, migrations, and invasions of Homo sapiens. Comparisons among present-day peoples point to an African origin for modern humans. Specific gene forms help reconstruct likely migration routes and mixing among populations.

## Medicine, Agriculture, and Industry

In [[Medicine|medicine]], genetic techniques diagnose and treat inherited disorders. Family history can reveal hereditary risk for cancer or other disease. Tests on embryonic tissue or fluid can identify abnormalities before birth, and newborn screening can detect enzyme deficiencies such as phenylketonuria, which causes brain damage if untreated.

Gene therapy aims to modify defective genotypes by adding functional genes made through recombinant DNA technology. Bioinformatics is also used to search the human genome for gene products that may become designer pharmaceutical drugs.

In agriculture and animal husbandry, genetics improves plants and animals through breeding analysis, artificial insemination, transgenic modification, embryo transfer, and cloning. Prize bulls and cows can pass desirable genes to many offspring. Dolly the sheep, cloned in 1996, showed that mammals could be copied from adult cell nuclei.

Industry uses biotechnology to improve yeast, molds, bacteria, plants, and animals for commercial production. Fungi and bacteria have been selected for high antibiotic yield, producing examples such as penicillin, cyclosporin, streptomycin, and ampicillin. Transgenic organisms can manufacture drugs and chemicals such as citric acid.

## Core Equations

Mendelian inheritance uses simple probability ratios for independent alleles:

$$P(Aa\times Aa\rightarrow aa)=\frac{1}{4}$$

This ratio turns the preceding idea into a measurable comparison, with the numerator as the quantity of interest and the denominator as the scale used for comparison.

Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium relates allele and genotype frequencies:

$$p^2+2pq+q^2=1$$

Hardy-Weinberg proportions describe expected genotype frequencies when allele frequencies are p and q under idealized assumptions.

## Key Events & Developments

- mid-19th century: Gregor Mendel established the basis of genetic inheritance.
- 1908: Archibald Garrod linked hereditary disease with metabolism.
- 1941: George Beadle and Edward Tatum showed genes code for enzymes.
- 1953: Watson, Crick, and Wilkins proposed the DNA double helix.
- 1966: The complete genetic code was deduced.
- 1996: Dolly the sheep was successfully cloned.

## Interesting Facts

- DNA ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/DNA)) structure, replication, and the genetic code are nearly universal across life.
- Mutations can arise spontaneously or be induced by environmental agents such as X-rays.
- Bioinformatics became essential because genomics produces enormous data sets.
- Population genetics ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Population_genetics)) can use DNA to reconstruct ancient human migrations.
- Biotechnology turns genetic knowledge into medicines, crops, animals, and industrial chemicals.
