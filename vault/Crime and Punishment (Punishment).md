# Crime and Punishment (Punishment)

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> Punishment is the legally imposed response to wrongdoing, involving penalties such as fines, imprisonment, or other sanctions. Its purposes and limits raise questions of deterrence, retribution, rehabilitation, and justice.

## Introduction

Punishment is the infliction of pain, loss, or restraint on a person for a misdeed. Punishment can take forms ranging from capital punishment and flogging to imprisonment, fines, and deferred penalties that apply only if an offense is repeated.

## Theories and objectives of punishment

![[attachments/crime_and_punishment_punishment_001.webp|420]]

*Louis Huard - The Punishment of Loki*

Punishment has been debated for centuries by philosophers, political leaders, and lawyers. Each theory tries to justify punishment in a different way, whether by making offenders pay for harm, by deterring future crime, or by protecting society through control and reform.

## Retribution

![[attachments/crime_and_punishment_punishment_002.webp|420]]

*Johann Moritz Rugendas in Brazil 2*

Retribution says punishment is justified because the guilty should make amends for the harm they caused. This is a theory of moral balance, in which the seriousness of the penalty should match the gravity of the offense.

## Incapacitation

![[attachments/crime_and_punishment_punishment_003.webp|420]]

*L'Exécution de la Punition de Fouet et Negres ao Tronco by Jean-Baptiste Debret*

Incapacitation ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Incapacitation_%28penology%29)) makes an offender unable to commit crime, historically by banishment or execution and more recently by imprisonment. This approach is often used for repeat offenders and dangerous criminals, especially under habitual-offender laws.

## Rehabilitation

Rehabilitation ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rehabilitation_%28penology%29)) aims to treat and train offenders so that they can return to society as law-abiding members. This theory became important in the 19th century and often justified probation, treatment, or longer custody for reform rather than simple revenge.

## Asia

In China, punishment was long tied to reform and political study. Later economic change brought stronger deterrence campaigns, and Chinese punishment now combines reform, public control, and severe sanctions such as imprisonment and the death penalty. [[Japan]], Singapore, [[Indonesia]], and South Korea illustrate other combinations of low crime, severe penalties, or mixed reform policies.

## Key Events & Developments

- earlier societies: Punishment is often public, physical, and meant to restore authority quickly.
- 18th and 19th centuries: Reformers push for proportion, restraint, prisons, and more humane treatment.
- modern period: Punishment becomes tied to detention, deterrence, rehabilitation, and supervised release.

## Key People

- Cesare Beccaria ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cesare_Beccaria)): Argued for rational and limited punishment.
- Jeremy Bentham ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jeremy_Bentham)): Promoted utilitarian ideas that influenced prison and penalty reform.
- John Howard ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Howard)): Associated with prison reform and the improvement of conditions.
- Elizabeth Fry ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elizabeth_Fry)): Linked with humane reform, especially for prisoners.

## Interesting Facts

- Punishment can aim at deterrence, retribution, reform, or incapacitation.
- Punishment is a social choice, not only a response to guilt.
- The form of punishment often reflects the values of the state as much as the offense itself.
