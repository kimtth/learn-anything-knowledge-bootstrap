# Publishing (Publishing, History of)

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> Publishing selects, prepares, produces, and markets written works, linking writing, paper, printing, literacy, distribution, and readers. Its development from manuscript culture to an industrial media business made knowledge, law, religion, entertainment, and opinion widely reproducible and commercially organized.

## Introduction

Publishing ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Publishing)) is the selection, preparation, production, and marketing of written and printed material. Its history moves from ancient written communication to modern book, newspaper, and magazine industries. The activity began with small acts of recording and copying but became a large cultural industry that spreads knowledge, entertainment, opinion, law, religion, and commerce.

The history of publishing depends on three major inventions: writing, paper, and printing. It also depends on the spread of literacy. Writing first fixed religious formulas, laws, genealogies, and official records; publishing became possible only when the control of writing by priestly or official groups weakened and writing became useful in trade, law, education, and public communication.

Technical change and [[Social Structure and Change (Social Change)|social change]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_change)) repeatedly strengthened each other. Better scripts, paper, movable type, mechanical presses, illustrations, cheaper formats, copyright, postal systems, advertising, and mass education all changed what could be published and who could read it. Publishing therefore developed as both a craft and a business.

## Italy

[[Italy]] brought early book printing to maturity after its birth in medieval Germany. Printing reached Italy in 1462-63 through the Benedictine monastery of Subiaco near Rome, where German printers Konrad Sweynheim and Arnold Pannartz first worked before moving to Rome in 1467. The church encouraged inexpensive book production, but commercial towns became the strongest centres.

By 1500, [[Venice]] had about 150 presses. Nicolas Jenson perfected roman type in 1470, while Aldus Manutius became the leading printer-publisher of his age. Aldus issued Greek texts, then produced compact and inexpensive Latin classics for humanist readers beginning in 1501. His editions used italic type designed by Francesco Griffo, larger print runs of about 1,000, and the dolphin-and-anchor publisher's device.

## France: Renaissance Book Publishing

In [[France]], printing began under strong scholarly direction. In 1470, the rector and librarian of the Sorbonne invited three German printers to Paris and supervised book choice, printing, and type. Their preference for roman type helped weaken Gothic black-letter.

Early French publishers included Jean Dupre, known for luxury editions, and Antoine Verard, who began printing in 1485 and set standards for elegant Books of Hours. After 1500, printers such as Josse Bade, Geoffroy Tory, and the Estienne family made France a leader in compact, scholarly, roman-and-italic book production.

The reign of Francis I from 1515 to 1547 is treated as a golden age of French typography. He supported Robert Estienne, ordered copies of Greek books for the royal library in 1538, and created a printer's code in 1539. Lyon was the main French printing centre outside Paris and was freer to publish humanist and Protestant works.

## England: Early Printing

![[attachments/publishing_publishing_history_of_001.webp|420]]

*William Caxton showing printed specimens to King Edward IV and Queen Elizabeth*

England adopted printing later than continental Europe. Printing arrived in 1476, and by 1500 only five printers worked in England, all in London and all foreign. Type and paper were long imported, and foreign book-trade workers were encouraged before Tudor regulation later protected native stationers.

William Caxton, a native Englishman, introduced printing to England after learning the craft at Cologne and setting up a press at Bruges. He returned to England with royal support and printed more than 90 books. His importance lies less in technical quality than in publishing in English rather than Latin, helping shape the language while it was still changing.

## The Netherlands

After 1550, the [[Netherlands]] led European book publishing for a time. Christophe Plantin, a French-born printer in Antwerp, dominated Roman Catholic southern publishing, especially with the eight-volume Biblia regia of 1569-72. His firm continued through descendants of Joannes Moretus.

In the Protestant north, the Elzevir house became similarly important after Louis Elzevir issued his first book in 1593. The Elzevirs produced small-format classical Latin texts from 1629, matching Aldine quality at reasonable prices. Dutch publishers also excelled in atlases, emblem books, maps, and engraved illustration.

## France: Centralization and Regulation

After the religious wars, French publishing became more centralized in Paris. Louis XIII created a private press in the Louvre in 1620, and Cardinal Richelieu made it the state Imprimerie Royale in 1640. This press maintained high standards but also belonged to a regulated royal system.

The Chambre des Syndicats, created in 1618, regulated the book trade, and censorship passed increasingly from the Sorbonne to crown officials. Controversial works often appeared abroad or under false imprints. Yet French taste remained powerful, and vernacular scholarship advanced when [[Descartes and Cartesianism (Descartes)|Descartes]] published Discours de la methode in French in 1637.

## Germany

After the Reformation, German intellectual and publishing life became mostly Protestant. Frankfurt was the major book-fair centre and European clearinghouse, but imperial censorship from 1579 weakened it. After about 1650, the trade shifted toward Leipzig, where a university and more enlightened government supported publishing.

German firms such as Johann Friedrich Gleditsch's house and Moritz Georg Weidmann's house survived from the 17th century. Philipp Erasmus Reich helped develop the net price principle and the idea of a booksellers' association in 1765, later the Borsenverein der Deutschen Buchhandler. Late 18th-century publishers such as Georg Joachim Goschen, Johann Friedrich Cotta, and Johann Friedrich Unger published major authors including Schiller, [[Goethe]], and August von Schlegel.

## England: Licensing, Monopolies, and Literature

Under Elizabeth I, English publishing was turbulent and tightly controlled. The 1559 Injunctions confirmed the Stationers' Company charter and licensing system, while the 1586 Star Chamber decree confined printing mainly to London, with one press each at Oxford and Cambridge. The Stationers' Company inspected presses and seized offending works.

Booksellers gained power over printers through copies, or rights to publish particular works. Monopolies and patents gave rich stationers control over valuable texts, while printers bore high costs. Some figures, such as John Wolfe, resisted by claiming a right to print freely, but even rebels were often absorbed into the company.

English literary publishing included John Lyly's Euphues, Sir Thomas North's translation of Plutarch's Lives, Edmund Spenser's Faerie Queene, and the 1611 Authorized Version of the Bible. Drama and popular writing often reached print through fringe publishers and pirates. Thomas Thorpe published [[Shakespeare]]'s sonnets in 1609, and John Danter printed early defective Shakespeare quartos.

## America: Colonial Book Publishing

Printing in the American colonies began in 1639 when Stephen Day and his sons moved from Cambridge, England, to Cambridge, Massachusetts. Their early products included The Oath of a Free-Man, an almanac for 1639, and The Whole Booke of Psalmes in 1640. Boston, Philadelphia, and New York later developed presses.

Colonial book publishing was limited by shortages of type and paper, but government patronage supported dignified work. Almanacs, primers, law books, and theology were staples. Books were sold by subscription, printers, hawkers, and shopkeepers, and book peddlers helped reach rural readers.

## Literacy, Libraries, and Censorship

After about 1650, more reading matter both reflected and promoted wider education, especially among the middle classes and women. Prose novels served middle-class readers, while almanacs and chapbooks reached poorer readers. Benjamin Franklin's Poor Richard's Almanack mixed information with moral sayings and became a famous American example.

The growth of the book trade encouraged libraries. Sir Thomas Bodley opened his Oxford library in 1602, and in 1610 the Stationers' Company agreed to supply copies of books printed in England. Later copyright-deposit libraries developed, including the British Museum library and the Library of Congress, whose librarian became copyright officer in 1870.

Censorship declined in many Western countries from the 18th century. It was abolished in Sweden in 1766, Denmark in 1770, and Germany in 1848. The French National Assembly declared in 1789 that free communication of thought and opinion was a precious human right.

## Copyright and Price Regulation

Copyright ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copyright)) helped protect authors and publishers, but piracy remained a major issue. Cheap unauthorized editions could spread culture widely while also weakening local authors and publishers. Harriet Beecher Stowe's Uncle Tom's Cabin rapidly appeared in about 1,500,000 copies in England after its American publication in 1852.

American writers such as Washington Irving, James Fenimore Cooper, Ralph Waldo Emerson, and Henry Wadsworth Longfellow became famous, but many American authors struggled because cheap foreign books dominated the market. This pressure encouraged the magazine short story and lecture tour as alternative sources of income.

As publishers and booksellers separated, price regulation became important. The net price principle, promoted earlier by Philipp Erasmus Reich, was adopted in Germany in 1887 through the Borsenverein. In Britain, booksellers and publishers created the Net Book Agreement in 1901, largely through Frederick Macmillan. International copyright later developed through the Berne Convention and the 1952 Universal Copyright Convention.

## The Early 20th Century and World War I

In the early 20th century, state education, rising living standards, and cheaper printing expanded book publication. Many new publishing houses appeared, especially because small print runs of about 1,000 copies were economically possible and printers often granted long credit. Educational publishing became a major specialty as school populations grew.

[[World Wars (World War I)|World War I]] disrupted European publishing but did not destroy it. Britain rationed paper to two-thirds of prewar use, while soldiers created strong demand for light reading. Paper prices rose sharply, book sales increased, and quality fell because publishers had to use poor paper and binding.

After the war, European middle-class purchasing power fell because of taxation, inflation, and trade depression. Some publishers failed or were absorbed by larger firms. In the [[United States of America (United States)|United States]], by contrast, postwar prosperity created a larger middle-class market for books.

## The Great Depression

The [[Great Depression]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Depression)) beginning in 1929 weakened American and European publishing. By 1931, British publishers could no longer rely on strong American sales. Sales fell, profits became small, and bankruptcies increased.

Publishers sought new outlets through exhibitions, newspaper coupon schemes, heavy advertising, and reprint book clubs. Harold Raymond introduced the Book Token in 1932, a gift certificate exchangeable for books, which became important in Christmas sales and was copied in other countries and trades.

Even in depression, publishers still looked for a wider readership, preparing the ground for cheaper formats and more aggressive mass-market distribution.

## Newspaper Publishing

Newspapers differ from books through immediacy, headlines, periodic issue, and coverage of many current topics. They serve local towns, whole nations, or international audiences. Modern electronics later transformed newspaper production by allowing articles and photographs to be transmitted and printed in many places at once.

Newspapers have shaped literacy, human rights, democratic freedoms, and public opinion. In the 19th century, independent newspapers helped spread liberal and democratic ideas. In the 21st century, newspapers still influenced opinion even while facing competition from radio, television, and the Internet.

## The Roman Empire

The Roman Empire used the Acta Diurna, a daily gazette from 59 BCE associated with Julius Caesar, to inform the public of official developments. Handwritten copies were posted in Rome and the provinces. They included not only political items but also gladiatorial contests, omens, marriages, births, deaths, appointments, trials, and executions.

## China

In [[China]], the pao, or reports of court affairs, circulated among educated civil servants in Peking for more than a thousand years from 618 to 1911 CE. Their format and title changed by dynasty, and production moved from hand copying to wooden-type printing in the 17th century. Their durability reflected the stability of the civil-service class.

## Medieval Europe

After Rome, Europe lacked regular newspaper publication for centuries. Printing in the 15th and 16th centuries produced books and pamphlets rather than true newspapers. Handwritten newssheets, town criers, and printed newsbooks spread reports of battles, scandals, heroic feats, and wonders.

The Venetian Republic recognized commercial demand for news by charging one gazeta for public readings about the war with Turkey in 1563. The word gazette later became common for newspapers. Early rudimentary newspapers appeared across Europe in the 17th century, but censorship often restricted their growth.

## Britain: Early Newspapers

The British press began in the early 17th century with foreign-news corantos. Nathaniel Butter translated a Dutch coranto in 1621, and with Nicholas Bourne and Thomas Archer published corantos and Weekley Newes. Licensing, censorship, and Star Chamber bans limited the field.

The English Civil Wars from 1642 to 1651 stimulated news publication, with about 300 titles appearing between 1640 and 1660. Names such as Intelligencer, Scout, Spy, Post, and Mercury reflected political conflict. The restored monarchy again restricted the press, while the London Gazette became a newspaper of record.

After the Glorious Revolution of 1688-89, publishing became more permissive. Provincial presses appeared, and London remained the national centre. The Daily Courant of 1702-35 attempted daily publication, while Daniel Defoe's Review introduced the editorial or leading article.

## North America

In colonial [[North America]], British law discouraged free newspapers. Benjamin Harris's Publick Occurrences in Boston in 1690 was quickly stopped. The official Boston News-Letter began in 1704, and James Franklin's New-England Courant of 1721 became the first independent American newspaper.

By the American Revolution, the colonies had 37 titles. Printing offices became community centres, and newspapers helped create political memory and revolutionary debate. The Boston Tea Party of 1773 was said to have been planned in a back room of the Boston Gazette.

## The United States: Independent and Popular Press

The [[United States of America (United States)|United States]] led the movement toward a popular and politically independent press. James Gordon Bennett founded the New York Herald in 1835 and declared it independent of party. Its mix of news, views, and sensational social commentary reached more than 30,000 copies.

Horace Greeley's New York Tribune of 1841 was more crusading and strongly opposed slavery. Other editors used sensationalism, investigation, and reform campaigns. Joseph Pulitzer later raised the New York World from 15,000 to 250,000 circulation, while William Randolph Hearst pushed sensationalism further, especially during the 1898 circulation battles.

## Great Britain: Independent and Popular Press

In Britain, The Times became a model serious newspaper under John Walter II, John Walter III, Thomas Barnes, and John Thaddeus Delane. It reported the Peterloo Massacre in 1819, campaigned for parliamentary reform, exposed Crimean War failures, and adopted advanced printing machinery.

The reduction and abolition of newspaper taxes changed the market. The stamp tax fell in 1836 and ended in 1855; paper duty ended in 1861. Cheaper papers such as the Daily Telegraph reached large middle-class audiences, while later titles such as The Star used short lively items, bold display, human interest, and racing tips for a wider public.

## Advertising and Contemporary Newspaper Challenges

Advertising became the main profit source for newspapers, including display and classified ads. Local and rural papers remained important to small businesses even after online classified advertising appeared. In the 1970s and 1980s, free newspapers spread, relying entirely on advertising revenue.

Free papers and free news websites challenged paid-subscription newspapers by teaching readers that news need not be paid for. The Wall Street Journal began charging for online content in 1996, while many other publishers initially offered free online access in hope of advertising income and print-subscription support.

## Digital Publishing

Digital publishing changed every older part of the trade: writing, editing, typesetting, printing, distribution, libraries, advertising, copyright, and reading. Desktop publishing reduced production barriers, while the Internet made newspapers, magazines, journals, reference works, and books available through websites, databases, e-books, and online retailers. This widened access and lowered some costs, but it also weakened old revenue models, increased piracy concerns, shifted power toward platforms, and forced publishers to rethink authority, permanence, citation, and audience trust.

## Magazine Publishing

The magazine developed between the book and the newspaper. It grew from pamphlets, broadsides, ballads, chapbooks, almanacs, and regular collections of varied material. The earliest known magazine was the German Erbauliche Monaths-Unterredungen of 1663-68, followed by learned journals such as Journal des Scavans and Philosophical Transactions in 1665.

Learned journals spread new scholarship by summarizing important books and discoveries. Book advertisements and catalogs also helped create periodical habits. These early forms prepared the way for literary, political, scientific, women's, illustrated, and popular magazines.

## Great Britain: Early Magazines

British magazine development was shaped by rising literacy, especially among women. Daniel Defoe's The Review, Richard Steele's The Tatler, and Joseph Addison and Steele's The Spectator influenced manners, taste, politics, and essay style. The 1712 Stamp Tax slowed but did not stop magazines.

Edward Cave's The Gentleman's Magazine, begun in 1731, gave the word magazine its modern periodical meaning. It first collected material from elsewhere and later printed more original matter, including parliamentary reports. Women's periodicals, literary reviews, and family magazines followed.

## Continental Europe

On the European continent, magazine growth was similar but often limited by censorship. Pierre Bayle published Nouvelles de la Republique des Lettres largely in Holland to escape French limits. German literary movements used magazines heavily, including Schiller's Horen and [[Goethe]]'s Propylaen.

Russia's first learned periodical was Monthly Works, published by the Academy of Sciences in 1755-64. Catherine II used her periodical Vsiakaia Vsiachina to attack opponents, while Nikolay Novikov's satirical journals faced suspension or pressure.

## America: Magazines and Mass Circulation

The first American magazines appeared in Philadelphia in 1741: Andrew Bradford's American Magazine and Benjamin Franklin's General Magazine. Both were short-lived. By the end of the 18th century, about 100 American magazines had appeared, most local and unstable.

In the 19th century, cheaper magazines sought broader audiences. British pioneers included Charles Knight's Penny Magazine, the Chambers brothers' Chambers's Journal, and John Cassell's family and working-class periodicals. In the United States, compulsory education and mass literacy expanded the market for general and light entertainment magazines.

## Illustrated, Women's, Literary, and Scientific Magazines

Illustrated magazines grew when publishers recognized that pictures increased sales. Herbert Ingram launched The Illustrated London News in 1842, using woodcuts and later artists sent around the world. France, Germany, and the United States developed similar pictorial magazines, including L'Illustration, Leslie's Weekly, and Harper's Weekly.

Women's magazines moved from moral verse, fiction, and fashion toward practical household guidance. Samuel Beeton's Englishwoman's Domestic Magazine in 1852 offered home management, fashion plates, and dressmaking guidance. Later British and American titles such as Good Housekeeping, Vogue, Harper's Bazaar, McCall's, and Woman's Home Companion became major institutions.

Literary and scientific periodicals created forums for criticism, politics, art, and discovery. British reviews included the Edinburgh Review, Quarterly Review, Westminster Review, Athenaeum, and Spectator. American literary magazines published writers such as Ralph Waldo Emerson, Henry Wadsworth Longfellow, and Oliver Wendell Holmes, while Scientific American, Popular Science Monthly, and National Geographic Magazine spread science.

## Scholarly Journals and Global Magazine Publishing

Scholarly journals, begun in the 17th century, expanded greatly in the 19th century as disciplines divided into specialties. Learned societies in archaeology, philology, Egyptology, geography, philosophy, and science issued bulletins, proceedings, and transactions that let scholars follow new work.

Magazine publishing spread globally. [[Africa]] developed titles in English, French, Afrikaans, and African languages, including creative-writing magazines such as Black Orpheus. [[India]], Bangladesh, and Pakistan developed illustrated, political, women's, sports, and cultural periodicals. [[South America]] had strong magazine markets in Argentina, Mexico, and Venezuela.

## News, Photo, Political, and Literary Magazines in the 20th Century

The fast pace of the 20th century created demand for concise news presentation. Briton Hadden and Henry Luce founded [[Time]] in 1923 to summarize world news for busy readers. Its style, organization, and compression created a new class of newsmagazine.

British and American political and literary magazines remained influential. Britain had The Economist, Times Literary Supplement, New Statesman, New Scientist, Encounter, and many literary reviews. The United States had The Nation, The New Republic, Foreign Affairs, American Scholar, and smaller radical, liberal, conservative, and literary magazines.

Continental European magazines often carried strong political weight. In France, Mercure de France, Nouvelle Revue Francaise, Les Temps Modernes, and other journals shaped intellectual life. In Germany and Soviet-bloc countries, literary reviews could become centres of controversy, censorship, underground publishing, and political resistance.

## Key Events & Developments

- The 4th millennium BC: [[Writing]], probably first developed by the Sumerians, made durable records possible.
- 1462-63: Printing reached [[Italy]] through Subiaco and soon moved to Rome and Venice.
- 1476: William Caxton brought printing to England.
- 1559 and 1586: English licensing and Star Chamber controls strengthened regulation of printing.
- 1789: The French National Assembly declared free communication of thought and opinion a human right.
- 1901: Britain's Net Book Agreement formalized price regulation.
- 1923: [[Time]] created the modern compressed newsmagazine model.

## Interesting Facts
