# Africa

## Introduction and Basic Geography

![[attachments/africa_001.webp|420]]

*Location of Africa on a globe*

Africa is the second largest continent (after [[Asia]]), covering about one-fifth of the total land surface of the [[Earth - Its Properties, Composition, and Structure (Earth)|Earth]]. The continent is bounded on the west by the [[Atlantic Ocean]], on the north by the Mediterranean Sea, on the east by the Red Sea and the [[Indian Ocean]], and on the south by the mingling waters of the Atlantic and Indian oceans. Africa's total land area is approximately 11,724,000 square miles, measuring about 5,000 miles from north to south and about 4,600 miles from east to west. Its northern extremity is Al-Ghiran Point near Cape Blanc, Tunisia; its southern extremity is Cape Agulhas, South Africa; its farthest point east is Xaafuun Point near Cape Guardafui, Somalia; and its western extremity is Almadi Point on Cape Verde, Senegal. In the northeast, Africa was joined to [[Asia]] by the Sinai Peninsula until the construction of the Suez Canal ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Suez_Canal)).

Paradoxically, the coastline of Africa—18,950 miles in length—is shorter than that of [[Europe]], because there are few inlets and few large bays or gulfs. Off the coasts of Africa a number of islands are associated with the continent, the most significant being [[Madagascar]], one of the largest islands in the world. Other notable islands include the Seychelles, Mauritius, Reunion, Ascension, St. Helena, Cape Verde, Bioko, São Tomé and Príncipe, and the Azores, Madeira, and Canary islands. The continent is cut almost equally in two by the Equator, so that most of Africa lies within the tropical region, bounded on the north by the Tropic of Cancer ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tropic_of_Cancer)) and on the south by the Tropic of Capricorn ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tropic_of_Capricorn)). Because of the bulge formed by western Africa, the greater part of the continent's territory lies north of the Equator.

## Geologic History: The Precambrian

The African continent essentially consists of five ancient Precambrian cratons ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Craton))—Kaapvaal ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kaapvaal_Craton)), Zimbabwe ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zimbabwe)), Tanzania, Congo ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Congo_Basin)), and West Africa—formed between about 3.6 and 2 billion years ago and basically tectonically stable since that time. These cratons are bounded by younger fold belts formed between 2 billion and 300 million years ago, with all rocks extensively folded and metamorphosed. Precambrian rock outcrops appear on some 57 percent of the continent's surface, while the rest consists of largely undeformed younger sediments and volcanic rocks. The oldest rocks are of Archean age (about 4 to 2.5 billion years old) found in the granite-gneiss-greenstone terrains of the Kaapvaal ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kaapvaal_Craton)), Zimbabwe ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zimbabwe)), and Congo ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Congo_Basin)) cratons, consisting of gray banded gneisses, granitoids, and well-preserved volcanic rocks showing evidence of submarine extrusion and formation under high temperatures.

The oldest rock assemblages, consisting of gneisses, granites, metasediments, and metavolcanic rocks 3.6 to 2.5 billion years old, are all variably deformed and metamorphosed. The best-preserved assemblages occur in the Kaapvaal ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kaapvaal_Craton)) and Zimbabwe ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zimbabwe)) cratons and contain large deposits of gold and sulfide minerals. The volcanic suites are dominated by basaltic and komatiitic lavas, often interlayered with metasediments and generally referred to as greenstone belts. Several generations of greenstones have been recognized, with the oldest formed about 3.4 billion years ago, the second some 3 to 2.9 billion years ago, and the third some 2.7 to 2.6 billion years ago. Some of the oldest traces of life are preserved as unicellular algae in Precambrian cherts of the Barberton greenstone belt in the Transvaal region of South Africa.

The end of the Archean is marked by voluminous granite intrusions, after which Africa's cratons became tectonically stable. One of the most spectacular features marking the end of the Archean is the intrusion of the Great Dyke in Zimbabwe, a large layered body of mafic-ultramafic rocks with substantial deposits of chromium, asbestos, and nickel. The early Proterozoic (about 2.5 to 1.6 billion years ago) is characterized by cratonic clastic sediments on stable cratons—the best examples are the Witwatersrand-Ventersdorp-Transvaal basin of [[Southern Africa]] and the Francevillian basin in Gabon—and by metavolcanic-metasedimentary rocks and granitoids in noncratonic areas.

## The Paleozoic, Mesozoic, and Cenozoic Eras

The Paleozoic Era includes two major mountain-building episodes during which Africa took shape. A glacial period during the Ordovician is evidenced by widespread deposition of tillites in southern Morocco, throughout western Africa, and in subequatorial Africa as far south as Namibia. Marine fossils of the Cambrian Period (about 540 to 490 million years ago) are found in southern Morocco, the Western and Mauritanian Sahara, and Namibia. During the Ordovician Period (about 490 to 440 million years ago), fossiliferous marine sandstone covered northern and western Africa, including the Sahara. The Mesozoic Era (about 250 to 65 million years ago) is remarkable for ancient sea transgressions and the emergence of massive land formations containing fossil remains. During the Triassic (about 250 to 200 million years ago), ancient seas left marine deposits in [[North Africa]], the southern Sahara, [[Egypt]], and parts of Tanzania and northern [[Madagascar]].

The Cenozoic Era, the most recent major interval (the past 65 million years), is notable for great tectonic movements resulting in the Alpine orogeny. During this mountain-building episode, the Atlas Mountains ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atlas_Mountains)) of northwestern Africa were folded and uplifted. Notable too are the formation of the Red Sea rift valley and volcanism and rifting that took place during later stages of the period. The Paleocene Epoch (about 65 to 55 million years ago) is important for its marine formations with animal fossils including nummulites, nautiloids, and echinoids found in North and West Africa and in the Sahara.

## Relief and Physiographic Features

The physiography of Africa essentially reflects its geologic history and geology. The continent, composed largely of vast rigid blocks of ancient rocks, has geologically young mountains at its extremities in the highlands of the Atlas Mountains ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atlas_Mountains)) in the northwest and the Cape ranges in the south. Between these mountainous areas is a series of plateau surfaces with huge areas that are level or slightly undulating, above which stand occasional harder and more resistant rock masses. Kilimanjaro (19,340 feet) is the highest point on the continent; Lake Assal (515 feet below sea level) in Djibouti is the lowest. In proportion to its size, Africa has fewer high mountains and fewer lowland plains than any other continent.

The higher areas of the south and east are in marked contrast to the considerably lower elevation of the western and northern parts of the continent. South of a line drawn from near the mouth of the Congo River to the Gulf of Aden, most land lies 1,000 feet or more above sea level, with much exceeding 3,000 and even 4,000 feet. North of this line there is relatively little land above 3,000 feet, most of the area being between 500 and 1,000 feet above sea level. The highest extensive areas are found in Ethiopia, parts of Kenya, and the East African highlands. The East African Rift System constitutes the most striking and distinctive relief feature of the continent, with its formation associated with the volcanic activity responsible for most of the higher peaks of East Africa, including Kilimanjaro.

## The East African Rift Valley and Major Lakes

![[attachments/africa_002.webp|420]]

*Map of the Rift Valley in Kenya*

The rift valley extends for about 4,000 miles, its course clearly marked by many of the lakes of East Africa and by adjacent volcanic peaks. From the Gulf of Aqaba it can be traced southward along the Red Sea and into the Ethiopian Plateau to Lakes Rudolf, Naivasha, and Magadi in Kenya. Farther south through Tanzania, the line of the rift is less obvious, with walls more easily eroded and lakes generally smaller and not in line. The largest of these lakes are Natron and Manyara. The edges are obvious enough to the south in Malawi, where a huge crusted block collapsed along parallel faults, creating the steeply rising slopes of Lake Nyasa (Malawi), which is 360 miles long but never more than 50 miles wide, with a maximum depth of 2,310 feet.

The western branch, or Western Rift Valley, extends from the northern end of Lake Nyasa in a great arc, taking in Lakes Rukwa, Tanganyika (after Lake Baikal in Siberia the deepest lake in the world), Kivu, Edward, and Albert. Most lakes along the rift valley lie well below the general level of the plateau, ranging from about 1,300 to 3,000 feet above sea level, generally very deep and bearing striking resemblance to fjords. In complete contrast is Lake Victoria, the largest of all African lakes, occupying a shallow depression on a plateau 3,720 feet above sea level. Its greatest depth is only 270 feet, but with an area of 26,828 square miles, it is the third largest lake in the world after the Caspian Sea and Lake Superior.

## Major River Systems: The Nile and Other Drainage

The Nile River, about 4,132 miles long, is the longest river in the world. From Lake Victoria it flows as the Victoria Nile into Lake Albert, from which it emerges as the Albert Nile. Farther north it is known as the Al-Jabal River. Thereafter, having received several tributaries, it becomes the White Nile and finally the Nile, emptying into the Mediterranean Sea. Its major left-bank tributary is the Al-Ghazal, and the largest right-bank tributaries are the Sobat, Blue Nile, and Atbara. The Nile descends fairly rapidly from source to mouth, as do its major tributaries, especially the Blue Nile, which issues from Lake Tana on the Ethiopian Plateau at approximately 6,000 feet and flows through a steep gorge. Vast swamps interrupt the river's course, the largest being Al-Sudd, a vast area of floating swamp reeds, mostly papyrus. The river's regime is now controlled by a series of dams, with the largest being the Aswan High Dam.

The Niger River, which rises in the mountains of Guinea and enters the sea through its delta in southern Nigeria, is about 2,600 miles in length, with rapids interrupting its course at several points. The Niger receives its largest tributary, the Benue, in Nigeria. The Congo River, some 2,900 miles in length, has many waterfalls and rapids causing its valley to lose elevation quickly. The Zambezi River, about 2,200 miles in length, occupies a basin with an approximate area of 463,000 square miles and features numerous waterfalls, the most spectacular being the Victoria Falls. The Orange River, the longest in South Africa, flows across almost the entire width of the country, with its major tributary, the Vaal River, combining to have a total length of about 1,300 miles.

## Soils and Soil Classification

Soil types on the African continent may be divided into five or six broad categories. There are desert soils; chestnut-brown soils, which border deserts; and chernozem-like soils (dark black soils rich in humus and carbonates), found immediately south of chestnut soils from [[Sudan]] westward to beyond the Niger Bend and in pockets in East Africa, Zambia, Zimbabwe, and South Africa. Additionally, there are black soils often grouped with chernozems, found on the Accra Plains of Ghana; red tropical soils and laterites in tropical wet-and-dry and equatorial climatic zones; and Mediterranean soils found in the Atlas Mountains ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atlas_Mountains)) of [[North Africa]] and the Cape region of South Africa. The most important factors affecting soil formation are climate, parent material, relief, drainage, vegetation cover, and time. Where land has been stable and flat for prolonged periods, as in Africa, climate becomes the major determinant of soil groups.

## Climate: Factors and Zones

Most of the African continent—which extends from 35°S to about 37°N latitude—lies within the tropics. The near bisection of the continent by the Equator results in a largely symmetrical arrangement of climatic zones on either side, though this symmetry is imperfect because of the great east-west extent north of the Equator versus the narrow width to the south. In consequence, the influence of the sea extends farther inland in [[Southern Africa]]. A quasi-permanent subtropical high-pressure cell (the Saharan anticyclone) develops in the heart of northern Africa, while in [[Southern Africa]] the belt of high pressure on land weakens during the season when the Sun is overhead. Cool ocean currents chill the winds blowing over them and thereby influence the climate of neighbouring shores.

Because of the extensive plateau surfaces and the absence of high and long mountain ranges comparable to the Andes or Himalayas, climatic zones in Africa tend to shade into one another rather than change abruptly from place to place. The high mountains have climatic zones of their own that vary with altitude. Urban areas typically experience higher average temperatures and frequently have less wind and lower relative humidity than surrounding countryside, though there is too little relevant data from Africa to permit detailed study of urban climates.

## Vegetation: Zones and Ecological Relationships

Of the total land area of the continent, forests cover about one-fifth; woodlands, bushlands, grasslands, and thickets about two-fifths; and deserts and their extended margins the remaining two-fifths. African vegetation develops in direct response to the interacting effects of rainfall, temperature, topography, and soil type; it is further modified by fire, human agriculture, and grazing and browsing by livestock. Until about two million years ago, Africa's vegetation was controlled by climate; geology and soil conditions; and animal activities. The addition of humans has increasingly rendered unreal the concept of fully developed natural vegetation approximating a vegetational climax.

Climate remains the dominant control over vegetation through zonal belts of precipitation reflecting latitude and contrasting exposure to the Atlantic and Indian oceans and their currents. Desert vegetation in the Sahara has one of the lowest species densities in the world, with sustained vegetation cover occurring only in massifs and oases. Elsewhere vegetation is discontinuous, consisting of perennials with huge root systems and sparse aerial parts, often protected by waxy cuticles, thorns, and hairs; and ephemerals with slight root systems and little foliage but with the ability to flower profusely after occasional storms and seed quickly.

## Major Vegetation Types: Desert, Grassland, and Forest

The Highveld grassland, restricted to regions with 10 percent or less woody plant cover, probably owes much to unaided nature for its creation and perpetuation, since fires caused by lightning strikes are relatively frequent. Its extent has been fairly precisely defined as areas with more than 15 inches of rainfall during the summer. Highveld vegetation has been differentiated into sweet veld (dominated by Themeda) or sour veld (Andropogon and Eragrostis), the latter making poorer pasturage. The Mediterranean vegetation zone is determined chiefly by its climate, characterized by very dry summers and mild, rainy winters, though it has been long much differentiated by its inhabitants. Large tracts have been degraded into maquis (macchie), garigue, or dry semidesert vegetation.

Sudd is a special vegetation occurring in the great Nile, Niger, and Zambezi drainage systems. Sedges (especially papyrus), reeds, and other water plants form masses of waterlogged plant material that are largely unproductive and are a nuisance to fishing and navigation. [[Madagascar]] physically and biologically has long formed a separate entity, with eastern and western regions of endemic vegetation. In the eastern centre, about one-sixth of the plant genera and more than three-fourths of the thousands of species are regarded as endemic.

## Long-term Changes in Vegetation

Africa's basic vegetational zones are believed to have existed in approximately the same climatically controlled series for a long period, with some ancient African plant families—such as the cycads, which evolved some 200 million years ago—still having living representatives. Nevertheless, the continent's vegetation has been altered continuously by geologic and climatic changes and by the movement of the caloric Equator. The past million years have been a time of unusually rapid changes, with major consequences for Africa's vegetation. The two most important geologic modifications have been the ancient separation of [[Madagascar]] from the mainland, giving rise to distinct speciation of the island's flora, and the long-continuing faulting and volcanism along East Africa's rift system.

The repercussions of the great Pleistocene Ice Ages of [[Europe]] constitute the most notable climatic influence on African flora in relatively recent geologic history. These consist of successive colder periods marked by glacial advances interrupted by warmer, drier interglacials; the last series ended between about 5,000 and 10,000 years ago. Tropical Africa experienced contemporaneous fluctuations in its climate. During wetter times (pluvials) in Africa, equatorial forests spread, separating northern woodlands from southern counterparts; mountain vegetation descended onto plateaus; and the Saharan climate was greatly ameliorated.

## African Fauna: Herbivores and Carnivores

![[attachments/africa_003.webp|420]]

*Wildebeest in Serengeti National Park*

The main group of herbivores are the African antelope, belonging to four subfamilies of the ox family (Bovidae). The first subfamily is the oxlike Bovinae, subdivided into the African buffalo and twist-horned antelope, including the eland (the largest antelope), kudu, nyala, and bushbuck. The second subfamily is the duiker, a small primitive bovid living in thickets, bush, and forests. Third is the horse antelope, further divided into sabre-horned sable, roan, Cape buffalo, and oryx antelope; the deer antelope; kongonis; hartebeest; topi; gnu (wildebeest); and blesbok, mostly inhabitants of open plains; and the marsh antelope, waterbuck, lechwe, kob, puku, and reedbuck. The fourth subfamily is the antelope proper, including royal, dik-dik, klipspringer, oribi, steenbok, grysbok, gazelle, impala, springbok, and gerenuk.

Other well-known large African herbivores include the zebra, giraffe, hippopotamus, rhinoceros, and African elephant. Probably no group of animals is more identified with Africa than its Carnivora, of which there are more than 60 species. In addition to the better-known big (or roaring) cats—the lion, leopard, and cheetah—are the wild dog, hyena, serval, wildcat, jackal, fox, weasel, civet, and mongoose. These predators and scavengers are vital in maintaining the ecological equilibrium of areas they inhabit.

## Primates, Birds, and Reptiles

The primates include some 45 species of Old World monkeys, as well as two of the world's great apes—the chimpanzee and the world's largest ape, the gorilla. Presimian primates—such as pottos (African lemurs) and galagos (bush babies or small arboreal lemurs)—are mainly small and nocturnal, but in [[Madagascar]], where there are no true monkeys, they are much more varied and abundant. South of the Sahara, the birdlife includes nearly 1,500 resident species, to which must be added another 275 species that are either resident in northwestern Africa or are Palaearctic winter migrants. Notable endemic birds include the ostrich, shoebill, hammerkop, and secretary bird. Other families, such as bustards, sand grouse, honey guides, and larks, are predominantly African.

[[Reptiles]], of which there are few endemic families, have mainly Old World affinities. Those most likely to be seen include lizards of the agamid family, skinks, crocodiles, and tortoises. Large vipers are abundant and varied; certain species have extremely toxic venom, but they are seldom encountered. Africa possesses both colubrine snakes (with fangs at the posterior end of the upper jaw) and elapine snakes (with fixed poison fangs at the front of the upper jaw), including such highly venomous species as mambas.

## Arthropods and Aquatic Life

Africa possesses an abundant and varied population of [[Arthropods|arthropods]], including large butterflies of the Charaxes and Papilio genera, stick insects, mantises, grasshoppers, driver ants, termites, and dung beetles. Spiders abound throughout the continent, and scorpions and locusts can be plentiful locally. Periodically, huge swarms of locusts spread over wide areas, causing enormous destruction to vegetation. Other serious pests are mosquitoes, which act as vectors in spreading diseases such as malaria, and tsetse flies, which transmit the parasite causing African trypanosomiasis (sleeping sickness) in humans and nagana in livestock.

Freshwater fish include both remarkable archaic forms and examples of rapid recent evolution. Ancient forms include lungfish, bichirs, and reedfish, which can breathe air, a property also possessed by certain catfish. Characteristic of recent evolutionary trends are the approximately 200 species of fish found in Lake Nyasa, four-fifths of which occur only there. The coelacanth, an archaic marine form believed extinct for more than 60 million years, was discovered to be alive off the east coast of South Africa in 1938.

## Human Origins and Migration

Africa is now widely recognized as the birthplace of the Hominidae, the taxonomic family to which modern humans belong. Archaeological evidence indicates that the continent has been inhabited by humans and their forebears for some 4,000,000 years or more. Anatomically modern humans are believed to have appeared about 100,000 years ago in the eastern region of sub-Saharan Africa. Somewhat later these early humans spread into northern Africa and the Middle East and, ultimately, to the rest of the world. Throughout human history there have been movements of peoples within, into, and out of Africa along its northern coasts, across the Sinai Peninsula, along the Red Sea, and especially in the Horn of Africa and coastal areas as far south as [[Southern Africa]].

## Physical Variations and Genetic Diversity

Africa is the most tropical of all continents; some four-fifths of its territory rests between the Tropics of Cancer and Capricorn. As a consequence, the cultures and physical variations of peoples reflect adaptation to both hot, dry climates and hot, wet climates. Dark skin is the dominant characteristic of indigenous African peoples, but skin colour is not uniform, showing clinal variation from light or tan colour in the northern fringe with Mediterranean climate to very dark skin in certain Sudanic regions in western and East Africa, where solar radiation has been most intense. Africa has the most physically varied populations in the world, from the tallest peoples to the shortest; body form and facial and other morphological features vary widely. It is the continent with the greatest human genetic variation, reflecting its evolutionary role as the evidence of all human DNA.

## European Settlement and Colonialism

[[North Africa]] from the Strait of Gibraltar to the Nile River delta has been the site of conquests and movements of peoples for more than 3,000 years. Along the east coast, trading cities arose and fell, cities that had overseas contacts during the past two millennia. Dutch settlers first arrived in South Africa in 1652; their descendants now constitute the main Afrikaner, or Boer, population. The vast majority of European settlers arrived after the 1885 [[Berlin]] West Africa Conference and the resulting scramble for Africa, during which European leaders carved out spheres of influence. French and Italian settlers also established new communities in [[North Africa]] and, to some extent, western Africa. Much earlier, in several waves of migrations beginning in the 7th century, Arabs spread across northern Africa and, to a lesser extent, into western Africa, bringing a new religion ([[Muhammad and the Religion of Islam (Islam)|Islam]]) and a new language (Arabic), along with new cultural and political institutions.

## Ethnic Groups and Languages

Although the precise number is unknown, there are several thousand different societies or ethnic groups in Africa, identified by their recognition of a common culture, language, religion, and history. Most Africans speak more than one language, and frequent migrations and interactions, including intermarriage, with other peoples have often blurred ethnic distinctions. There are an estimated 900 to 1,500 different languages, though many distinct political units share a common or similar language. Complicating this in the 20th century was the creation of new tribes that had not been distinct polities before the colonial era. Ethnic identities in modern times have often been heightened by the creation of these colonial boundaries.

## Regional Diversity: Northern Africa

Northern Africa is differentiated from the rest of the continent by its Mediterranean climate and long history of political and cultural contacts with peoples outside Africa. It is physically separated from the rest of the continent by the Atlas Mountains ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atlas_Mountains)) and inhabited primarily by peoples who speak languages belonging to the Afro-Asiatic group, including the Imazighen (Berbers) of Morocco, Algeria, and Tunisia. The Berbers are most numerous in Morocco and least in Tunisia, where, as a result of culture contact and intermarriage, they have become largely assimilated with Arabs, who speak a Semitic language. Arabs migrated into [[North Africa]] from [[Arabia]] in waves; the first occurred in the 7th century CE.

## Western Africa and the Sahel

[[Western Africa]] contains a remarkable diversity of ethnic groups and can be divided into two zones: the Sudanic savanna and the Guinea Coast. The savanna stretches over 3,000 miles east to west along the southern Saharan borderland, with vegetation consisting of extensive grasslands and few forests with little rain. The savanna supports pastoralism and horticultural economies dependent on grain. The Guinea Coast experiences heavy rainfall and is characterized by hardwood tropical forests and dense foliage, producing primarily root crops such as yams. Among important savanna peoples are the Mande, Gur-speaking groups, and the Fulani, a cattle-keeping Muslim people who have conquered indigenous peoples or live in symbiotic relationships. In Nigeria are the Igbo, Ibibio, Tiv, Edo, and several powerful kingdoms of the Yoruba.

## Central, Eastern, and Southern African Peoples

West-central Africa may be considered an eastern extension of western Africa, with the vast majority of peoples speaking related languages of the Bantu family. The Luba, Lunda, Fang, Mongo, Kuba, Songe, and Chokwe are among the larger ethnic groups. [[Eastern Africa]] can be divided into several regions, with the northern mountainous area known as the Horn of Africa comprising Djibouti, Ethiopia, Eritrea, and Somalia. The coastal area extends from Kenya to [[Southern Africa]], where numerous trading cities arose. Central and [[Southern Africa]] may be considered a single large culture area, most consisting of open and dry savanna grasslands. The region was once populated by Khoisan-speaking peoples; the San are today restricted to arid areas of southwestern Africa and Botswana.

## Madagascar and Its Distinct Culture

The island of [[Madagascar]] forms a distinct culture area. The various Malagasy ethnic groups, politically most important being the Merina, are mainly of Indonesian origin, following migrations across the [[Indian Ocean]] probably during the 5th and 6th centuries CE. The Malagasy language, spoken by 98 percent of the island's population, is classified as Austronesian, distinguishing [[Madagascar]] culturally and linguistically from the rest of Africa.

## Demography and Economy

Africa has one of the world's fastest-growing and youngest populations. High birth rates, falling but still serious mortality, uneven health conditions, and large numbers of children make schools, jobs, food supply, housing, and public services central development problems. Most people still depend directly or indirectly on agriculture, whether through subsistence cultivation, pastoralism, cash crops, local markets, or seasonal labour. Urbanization has accelerated around capitals, ports, mining towns, and inland market centres, but rapid city growth often brings unemployment, informal housing, congestion, and pressure on water, transport, and sanitation.

The continent's economy combines abundant resources with structural vulnerability. Africa has major deposits of petroleum, natural gas, coal, uranium, iron ore, copper, cobalt, gold, diamonds, phosphates, bauxite, and other minerals, yet many countries rely heavily on a few exports for foreign exchange. Colonial transport systems, commodity specialization, limited industrialization, drought-prone drylands, and changing world prices have made many economies fragile. At the same time, long-distance trade, craft production, mining, services, telecommunications, regional trade organizations, and new urban markets show that African economic life is more varied than a simple picture of underdevelopment suggests.

## Wildlife Conservation and Ecology

There are still sufficiently large tracts of relatively unspoiled country in which animal life may be studied in its environment. The complementary roles of wild ungulates show that in any area inhabited by a wide variety of species, grass is grazed in regular succession by zebra, gnu, hartebeest, and gazelle at different stages of growth. Overpopulation by domestic or wild species may upset the delicate natural balance, as seen by elephant overpopulation in Murchison Falls and Tsavo national parks; whether elephants survive, they have ineradicably altered the environment to the detriment of many typical species. Many countries have set aside large tracts as national parks, game reserves, or forest reserves. Only some are large enough to be self-contained ecosystems, and most have been set aside to accommodate large mammals. East Africa has sanctuaries for birds and marine organisms as well.

## Modern Conservation Efforts

Conservation of vegetation is undertaken mainly in forest reserves but also in national parks. A number of countries attempt to conserve wildlife by refusing export licenses for certain skins, especially those of leopard, cheetah, and zebra. Conservation efforts in [[Southern Africa]] have been aided by the creation of transfrontier parks and conservation areas, linking nature reserves and parks in neighbouring countries to create large international conservation areas protecting biodiversity. One such park is the Great Limpopo Transfrontier Park, linking Kruger National Park in South Africa with Limpopo National Park in Mozambique. Kenya's parks include Tsavo, one of the largest with more than 8,000 square miles, Lake Nakuru National Park for flamingos, and marine parks. Tanzania has the famous Serengeti National Park with unrivaled populations of plains ungulates.
