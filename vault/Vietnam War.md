# Vietnam War

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> The Vietnam War was a prolonged conflict rooted in Vietnamese anticolonial struggle, Cold War rivalry, divided state formation, guerrilla warfare, and foreign intervention. The source follows the political and military escalation, the experiences of Vietnam and the United States, and the war's regional and international consequences.

## Introduction

The Vietnam War ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vietnam_War)) was a long conflict from 1954 to 1975 between communist North Vietnam and its southern allies, especially the Viet Cong, against the government of South Vietnam and its chief ally, the [[United States of America (United States)|United States]]. In Vietnam it was also called the American War, or the War Against the Americans to Save the Nation. It was part of the wider Indochina wars and also part of the Cold War ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cold_War)) rivalry between the United States, the Soviet Union, China, and their allies.

The central issue was national reunification. North Vietnam, after defeating French colonial rule in 1954, wanted to unite all Vietnam under a communist system influenced by the Soviet Union and [[China]]. South Vietnam fought to preserve a state aligned with the West. American advisers were present in small numbers during the 1950s, expanded sharply from 1961, and were joined by U.S. combat troops in 1965. By 1969, more than 500,000 American military personnel were in Vietnam.

The war's human cost was enormous. Vietnam later estimated as many as 2 million civilian deaths and about 1.1 million North Vietnamese and Viet Cong military deaths. The U.S. military estimated 200,000 to 250,000 South Vietnamese military deaths. The Vietnam Veterans Memorial in [[Washington, D.C. (Washington)|Washington, D.C.]], dedicated in 1982, listed more than 57,000 American dead or missing, later rising past 58,200. South Korea, Thailand, Australia, and New Zealand also suffered losses while supporting South Vietnam.

The aftermath reshaped both countries. Vietnam emerged as a powerful military state in Southeast Asia, but its countryside, towns, agriculture, business, and industry were badly damaged by bombs, defoliation, mines, and fighting. Refugees fled in 1975 and again in 1978 as “boat people.” The [[United States of America (United States)|United States]] faced military demoralization, deep civic division, and the challenge of accepting defeat in its longest and most controversial war to that date. Formal U.S.-Vietnam diplomatic relations resumed in 1995.

## French Rule Ended, Vietnam Divided

![[attachments/vietnam_war_002.webp|420]]

*VietnamMural*

The war grew out of the broader Indochina wars of the 1940s and 1950s. Nationalists such as Ho Chi Minh and the communist-led Viet Minh fought Japanese and then French colonial rule. The French Indochina War ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_Indochina_War)) began in 1946 and lasted eight years, with much of France's war effort funded and supplied by the [[United States of America (United States)|United States]].

France's defeat at the Battle of Dien Bien Phu in May 1954 ended French rule in Indochina. The Geneva Accords of July 1954 made the 17th parallel a temporary military boundary. North of it stood the Democratic Republic of Vietnam, controlled by the Vietnamese Communist Party under Ho Chi Minh, with its capital at Hanoi. South of it, France transferred authority to the State of Vietnam, centred on Saigon and nominally led by former emperor Bao Dai.

The accords called for a demilitarized zone, civilian movement across the line within 300 days, and nationwide elections in 1956. The [[United States of America (United States)|United States]], accepting partition but determined to stop communism in Asia, backed South Vietnam. At the same time, Viet Minh leaders left political agents in the South, expecting unrest and eventual reunification.

## The Diem Regime and the Viet Cong

![[attachments/vietnam_war_001.webp|420]]

*Thuong Duc, Vietnam - A Viet Cong prisoner awaits interrogation at the A-109 Special Forces Detachment in T...*

Ngo Dinh Diem, newly installed premier of South Vietnam, faced communist agents, armed religious sects, dissident officers, and weak state institutions. With U.S. advisers, CIA support, and American aid, he defeated sect forces, arrested many communist operatives, and declared himself president of the Republic of Vietnam after a southern referendum in October 1955. He refused nationwide elections, while North Vietnam was not ready to restart open war.

Diem's early success hid severe weaknesses. He distrusted anyone outside his family, and his brother Ngo Dinh Nhu controlled the secret Can Lao network of payoffs, influence, and surveillance. Corrupt local officials, many of them northern Catholics like Diem, alienated rural people. Reform programs weakened, police abused power, and many noncommunists became open to anti-Diem resistance.

By 1957, the communists, now widely called the Viet Cong, began terrorism and assassination against government officials. In 1959, Hanoi approved armed struggle to overthrow Diem. Trained southerners returned from the North with weapons and equipment, and the new war began. The Army of the Republic of Vietnam, or ARVN, was badly suited to counterinsurgency because it was road-bound, politically appointed, corrupt, and penetrated by communist agents.

In 1960, southern communists created the National Liberation Front, or NLF, as the political arm of the Viet Cong. Its armed structure, the People's Liberation Armed Forces, included village guerrillas, regional units, and main-force troops. Guerrillas recruited, intimidated, protected political cadres, laid traps, raided posts, kidnapped enemies, and supplied experienced fighters to higher units.

## The U.S. Role Grows

By 1960, South Vietnamese forces were failing against the insurgency. Viet Cong attacks and assassinations rose sharply. The new administration of John F. Kennedy treated Vietnam as both a Cold War test and a chance to prove American counterinsurgency against communist “wars of national liberation.” Kennedy accepted the domino theory, which held that communist success in one Southeast Asian country would weaken the others.

U.S. aid expanded quickly. In 1961, Kennedy sent Walt W. Rostow and Maxwell Taylor to assess the war. They recommended helicopters, armoured carriers, advisers at many government levels, and even limited U.S. combat troops. Kennedy avoided combat deployment but created U.S. Military Assistance Command Vietnam, or USMACV. American military personnel rose from fewer than 800 in the 1950s to about 9,000 by mid-1962.

The Strategic Hamlet Program tried to move rural people into defended villages, separating them from the Viet Cong. It failed because forced labour, relocation, and poor screening angered villagers; many hamlets contained Viet Cong supporters inside their walls. At Ap Bac in January 1963, a Viet Cong battalion escaped a much larger ARVN force, destroyed helicopters, and exposed the weakness of U.S.-backed tactics.

Diem's political crisis deepened in 1963. Buddhist protests against the regime were met with violence, and monk Thich Quang Duc's public self-immolation became a worldwide symbol of repression. South Vietnamese generals sounded out Washington about a coup. On November 1, 1963, ARVN units seized Saigon, and Diem and Nhu were captured and murdered. Kennedy was assassinated less than three weeks later, leaving Lyndon B. Johnson a war of indecision and growing commitment.

## The Gulf of Tonkin

Johnson inherited a worsening situation: unstable governments in Saigon, a growing Viet Cong movement, and increasing North Vietnamese commitment. In 1964, Hanoi sent troops and whole units of the North Vietnamese Army, or NVA, south along the Ho Chi Minh Trail. China and the Soviet Union increased support to the North.

The United States considered air strikes against North Vietnam. On August 2, 1964, the destroyer USS Maddox was attacked by North Vietnamese torpedo boats in the Gulf of Tonkin while on intelligence patrol, shortly after South Vietnamese raids against northern islands. A second reported attack on August 4 was uncertain, but Johnson treated it as real.

Johnson ordered retaliatory air strikes and asked Congress for broad authority. The Gulf of Tonkin Resolution ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gulf_of_Tonkin_Resolution)), passed on August 7, 1964, allowed him to take necessary action in Southeast Asia. Few members of Congress knew of doubts about the second attack or the link between the incidents and U.S.-sponsored raids. The resolution removed Vietnam as an election issue, and Johnson won a landslide in November 1964.

## The United States Enters the War

After the election, conditions worsened. South Vietnamese governments changed rapidly, ARVN units struggled, and the communists began striking U.S. bases, including Bien Hoa. Johnson's advisers argued that bombing North Vietnam would raise South Vietnamese morale, punish Hanoi, and slow men and supplies moving south. Warnings from George Ball and Hubert Humphrey did not stop escalation.

In February 1965, the United States began sustained bombing of North Vietnam under Rolling Thunder. The campaign was carefully controlled from the White House to avoid provoking China or the Soviet Union, but it expanded steadily. Bombing did little to change the war in the South, where communist forces continued gaining ground.

In March 1965, U.S. Marines landed at Da Nang. By July 1965, Johnson authorized 100,000 troops immediately and another 100,000 in 1966. There was no declaration of war, no reserve mobilization, and no full economic war footing. The United States entered a full-scale war while trying to preserve normal domestic politics.

## Firepower Comes to Naught

General William Westmoreland commanded U.S. operations in South Vietnam. He left South Vietnamese forces in a secondary role and built a huge American logistics system: jet-capable air bases, deepwater ports, tactical air bases, hospitals, and warehouses. By late 1965, U.S. troops were fighting NVA and Viet Cong main forces in places such as the Batangan Peninsula and the Ia Drang valley.

Westmoreland believed American firepower could destroy enemy main forces in jungles and mountains while South Vietnamese forces restored control in populated areas. This became a war of attrition, measured by enemy body counts. The counts were often inaccurate and exaggerated. Large sweeps such as Cedar Falls and Junction City captured supplies but rarely held territory, so communist forces returned after U.S. units withdrew.

The United States used helicopters, artillery, B-52 bombers, fortified bases, electronic sensors, minefields, and herbicides such as Agent Orange. Agent Orange killed vegetation but caused serious ecological damage and exposed many people to toxic chemicals. Along the DMZ, bases such as Con Thien, Gio Linh, Camp Carroll, and Khe Sanh drew attacks, but most combat remained brief, small-unit fighting.

North Vietnam endured bombing because it had few industries and received major aid from [[China]] and the Soviet Union. By the end of 1966, the United States had dropped more bombs on North Vietnam than on Japan in World War II, yet supplies continued. The North strengthened air defenses, and the United States lost hundreds of aircraft and airmen.

## Tet Brings the War Home

![[attachments/vietnam_war_003.webp|420]]

*Vietnam War Monument in Welcome Home Soldier Memorial*

By 1967, American opposition grew. Students, clergy, intellectuals, and other critics condemned civilian suffering and U.S. support for a corrupt Saigon government. Others opposed rising casualties and the lack of clear progress, while some argued Johnson was not using enough force. Public support for his war leadership fell below half.

Hanoi also grew impatient. Communist leaders planned a general offensive/general uprising during Tet, the lunar New Year of 1968. Diversionary attacks at Dak To, Loc Ninh, and Khe Sanh helped draw American attention away from urban targets.

On January 31, 1968, communist forces attacked across South Vietnam: provincial capitals, district capitals, major cities, airfields, bases, Westmoreland's headquarters, and even the U.S. embassy compound in Saigon. In Hue, they held much of the city for nearly three weeks. Militarily, U.S. and South Vietnamese forces recovered quickly and inflicted heavy communist casualties. Politically, the offensive shocked Americans who had been told victory was near.

A reported request by Westmoreland for 206,000 more troops deepened the crisis. New defense secretary Clark Clifford concluded that escalation was unwise and that the war needed reassessment. On March 31, 1968, Johnson announced a partial bombing halt, offered negotiations, and declared he would not seek reelection.

## De-escalation, Negotiation, and Vietnamization

Talks began in [[Paris]] on May 13, 1968, but progress was slow. Fighting remained intense, with thousands of American casualties after Johnson's speech. In October 1968, Soviet messages suggested Hanoi would talk seriously if the bombing stopped. Johnson halted bombing of the North at the end of October, but negotiations remained procedural and difficult.

Richard M. Nixon, elected president in 1968, and adviser Henry A. Kissinger believed the United States could not win a military victory but needed an “honourable” settlement that gave South Vietnam a chance to survive. Nixon used diplomacy toward China and the Soviet Union, threats of force, and secret bombing of communist bases in Cambodia. The leak of that bombing helped lead to illegal surveillance measures later connected to Watergate.

Nixon accelerated Vietnamization, giving ARVN better weapons and training while withdrawing U.S. troops. In June 1969, he announced the first withdrawal of 25,000 troops, followed by larger reductions. The policy was popular in the United States but hurt morale among remaining troops. Drug abuse, racial conflict, desertion, “fragging,” and revelations of the My Lai Massacre damaged confidence in the U.S. military effort.

The war also deepened domestic conflict over conscription. Between 1965 and 1973, many soldiers were dishonourably discharged for desertion-related reasons, and about half a million men evaded or refused the draft. College deferments were restricted in 1971, draft calls ended in 1972, and the draft was abolished in 1973 in favour of an all-volunteer military.

## The United States Negotiates a Withdrawal

Public negotiations stayed deadlocked while Kissinger opened secret talks with North Vietnamese diplomats. The United States proposed mutual withdrawal, while Hanoi demanded unconditional U.S. withdrawal and replacement of Nguyen Van Thieu's government. Nixon avoided bombing the North at first but expanded the war into Cambodia after Lon Nol overthrew Norodom Sihanouk in 1970.

The Cambodian incursion captured supplies but failed to destroy large enemy forces. It triggered major U.S. protests, intensified after National Guard troops killed four students at Kent State University on May 4, 1970. Congress repealed the Gulf of Tonkin Resolution, leaving Vietnamization and withdrawal as Nixon's main path.

Vietnamization had limits. In March 1971, ARVN's Lam Son 719 attack into Laos against the Ho Chi Minh Trail ended in heavy casualties and retreat. In the United States, disillusionment spread widely, with polls showing most Americans believed intervention had been a mistake.

On March 30, 1972, Hanoi launched the Easter Offensive with tanks and artillery. South Vietnam initially suffered badly, but Nixon responded with Linebacker bombing, mining Haiphong Harbour, and heavy air attacks on northern forces and supply lines. The offensive stalled by mid-June, and both sides moved toward compromise: the United States no longer demanded North Vietnamese withdrawal from the South, and Hanoi no longer demanded Thieu's immediate replacement.

Kissinger and Le Duc Tho reached a draft agreement in October 1972, but Saigon objected. After renewed disagreement, Nixon ordered the intense Christmas bombing of Hanoi. North Vietnam returned to Paris, and Thieu accepted under U.S. pressure and reassurance. On January 27, 1973, the Agreement on Ending the War and Restoring Peace in Viet-Nam was signed. It provided for a cease-fire, U.S. withdrawal within 60 days, dismantling of U.S. bases, release of prisoners, peaceful reunification, and continued North Vietnamese troop presence in the South without reinforcement.

## The Fall of South Vietnam

The last U.S. military unit left Vietnam on March 29, 1973. Fighting continued almost immediately in what journalists called the postwar war. The United States still gave military aid to Saigon, but Watergate weakened Nixon, and Congress barred further U.S. military operations in Indochina after August 15, 1973.

By 1974, aid cuts, inflation, unemployment, corruption, desertion, and the nearing defeat of the Lon Nol regime in Cambodia weakened South Vietnam. North Vietnamese success at Phuoc Long in December 1974-January 1975 convinced Hanoi that victory was near. A planned two-year offensive began in March 1975, but South Vietnam collapsed in less than two months.

ARVN troops retreated in disorder from the central highlands, Hue, and Da Nang. President Gerald R. Ford asked Congress for more aid, but Congress and the public were unwilling. Nguyen Van Thieu resigned on April 21, 1975 and fled to Taiwan. On April 30, South Vietnam surrendered unconditionally as NVA tanks entered Saigon. Remaining Americans and Vietnamese associates escaped in frantic air- and sealifts.

A military government took control. On July 2, 1976, Vietnam was officially united as the Socialist Republic of Vietnam, with its capital at Hanoi. Saigon was renamed Ho Chi Minh City. The struggle for Vietnam, which had lasted roughly 30 years from the beginning of the anti-French war, was over.

- July 1954: The Geneva Accords divided Vietnam temporarily at the 17th parallel.
- 1955: Ngo Dinh Diem became president of the Republic of Vietnam.
- 1960: The National Liberation Front was formed in South Vietnam.
- November 1963: Diem was overthrown and killed in a military coup.

- April 30, 1975: Saigon fell to North Vietnamese forces.
- July 2, 1976: Vietnam was unified as the Socialist Republic of Vietnam.
