## Introduction

Personality ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Personality)) is a characteristic way of thinking, feeling, and behaving. It includes moods, attitudes, opinions, and behavioural characteristics, both inherited and acquired, that distinguish one person from another in relation to the environment and social group. Psychologists use the term in two main ways: to classify stable individual differences and to identify the general patterns that define human psychological nature.

The study of personality begins from the idea that people show distinctive patterns in speech, movement, living spaces, motives, urges, and social relations. Personologists examine both how people differ and how psychological functions form organized wholes through biological, social, and personal development.

## Modern Personology

Modern personality studies drew on major 20th-century works by Gordon W. Allport, Henry A. Murray, and Gardner Murphy. Allport's Personality: A Psychological Interpretation, Murray's Explorations in Personality, and Murphy's biosocial approach helped make personality a systematic field of psychological investigation.

## Physiological Type Theories

Ancient and modern theorists often tried to relate personality types to bodily characteristics. Rigid type categories are now largely rejected, but two traditions are historically important: humoral theories and morphological theories. These treated temperament as rooted in body chemistry, bodily form, or physiological organization.

## Humoral Theories

The oldest known personality theory appears in ideas associated with Empedocles and Hippocrates. Air, earth, fire, and water corresponded to qualities and bodily humours: blood, black bile, yellow bile, and phlegm. These were linked to sanguine, melancholic, choleric, and phlegmatic temperaments. The theory survived for more than 2,500 years as an explanation of emotional balance and traits.

## Freud and Psychoanalysis

*Freudian iceberg diagram showing conscious, preconscious, and unconscious layers*

Sigmund Freud's psychoanalysis became one of the most influential integrative personality theories. Beginning with hysteria, obsessive-compulsive disorders, phobias, and other neurotic conditions, Freud and Josef Breuer proposed that symptoms could be psychologically determined by unconscious processes. Psychoanalysis shifted attention from describing types to explaining how people become what they are.

Psychoanalytic theory emphasizes unconscious motivation, conflict, sexuality, aggression, family influence, early development, and lifelong change. It treats personality as a changing system with fragile boundaries, shaped by biological and psychosocial forces. Its evidence came largely from clinical interviews rather than controlled experiments, which later critics saw as a limitation.

## Jung

Carl Gustav Jung accepted the importance of unconscious processes but reduced Freud's emphasis on sexual motivation. He stressed abstract, spiritual, and symbolic sources of behaviour and developed a typology of reaction styles. His most influential distinction was introversion versus extroversion: orientation toward the inner world or toward social interaction.

## Adler

Alfred Adler emphasized compensation, the process by which people respond to felt inferiority or deficiency by exaggerating other behaviours. A physical or mental inadequacy might produce controlling or domineering patterns. Adler also gave family dynamics and birth order a prominent role in personality development.

## Erikson

Erik H. Erikson modified Freud by integrating psychological, social, and biological development across the whole lifespan. His epigenetic scheme proposed eight stages, each marked by a developmental conflict: trust versus mistrust, autonomy versus shame, initiative versus guilt, industry versus inferiority, identity versus confusion, intimacy versus isolation, generativity versus stagnation, and integrity versus despair.

## Aggression and Learning

Theories of aggression ask whether humans possess aggressive drives and why they may lack internal inhibitions against killing members of their own species. Social learning theorists emphasize situations, models, rewards, and social controls. Research suggests aggressive acts are most likely from people with histories of aggressive behaviour, but context strongly affects expression.

## Genetic Aspects

Evidence from animals, infants, twins, and adopted children indicates that genetic factors influence readiness to respond to environmental pressure in particular ways. Infants differ in activity, passivity, fussiness, cuddliness, and responsiveness. Such early tendencies shape how a child interacts with the environment, although genes do not simply transmit fixed behaviours.

## Key Events & Developments

- Ancient Greece: Humoral theories linked temperament to bodily fluids.
- 1937: Gordon W. Allport published a major personality interpretation.
- 1938: Henry A. Murray published Explorations in Personality.
- 1947: Gardner Murphy offered a biosocial personality synthesis.
- 20th century: Psychoanalysis transformed theories of personality development.
