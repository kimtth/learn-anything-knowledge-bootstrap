# Sikhism

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> Sikhism is a Punjab-based religion founded around the teachings of Guru Nanak and nine succeeding Gurus, whose authority culminates in the Guru Granth Sahib. The source follows its devotional, anti-caste, communal, scriptural, institutional, and political development, including the transformation of the Panth into a community with both spiritual and temporal responsibilities.

## Introduction

![[attachments/sikhism_001.webp|420]]

*Painting of Guru Nanak with companions*

Sikhism ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sikhism)) is an Indian religion founded in the Punjab in the late 15th century. Sikhs call their faith Gurmat ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gurmat)), the Way of the Guru. Tradition holds that Guru Nanak founded the faith and that nine later human Gurus continued his authority.

Sikhs believe that one spirit lived in all ten Gurus. After the death of Guru Gobind Singh, the tenth Guru, this eternal Guru passed to the sacred scripture, the Guru Granth Sahib ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guru_Granth_Sahib)), also called the Adi Granth. By the early 21st century, nearly 25 million Sikhs lived worldwide, most of them in Punjab.

The word Sikh means learner. The early Sikh community, or Panth, consisted of people seeking spiritual guidance. Sikhism began within the broad Hindu setting of northern India and was linked to the Sant tradition, associated with devotion, hymns, and direct experience of the divine. Like the Sants, Sikhs rejected empty ritual and caste pride while stressing devotion to the divine name.

## Guru Nanak

Guru Nanak was born in Punjab and belonged to the Khatri trading caste. He was not illiterate, and his hymns later entered the Adi Granth. Certain facts are secure: he composed hymns, visited pilgrimage sites across India, and died in 1539. Much else comes from janam-sakhis, devotional life stories written decades after his death.

The more restrained Puratan tradition describes Nanak making five journeys: east, south to Sri Lanka, north into the Himalayas, west toward Muslim lands, and finally within Punjab. He taught remembrance of the divine name, spiritual discipline, and a community life not defined by caste or empty ceremony.

## Guru Angad

Guru Angad, originally Lahina, became Nanak's successor in 1539. He had once worshiped the goddess Durga but was drawn to Nanak's hymns at Kartarpur. His loyalty persuaded Nanak to choose him over Nanak's own sons. His leadership was quiet and made no major change to Nanak's teaching.

## Guru Amar Das

Guru Amar Das became Guru at age 73 after years of seeking a spiritual guide. He strengthened the Panth as the first excitement of the movement settled into a lasting community. He created festival days such as Baisakhi, Maghi, and Diwali, ordered the digging of a sacred well, compiled the Goindval Pothis, and established manjis, or dioceses, to organize Sikhs across Punjab.

## Guru Ram Das

Guru Ram Das, son-in-law of Amar Das, founded Amritsar, the future capital of Sikh religious life and site of the Harmandir Sahib, later called the Golden Temple. He replaced manjis with masands, local representatives who cared for congregations and brought reports and offerings to the Guru. He also stressed hymn singing, still central to Sikh worship.

## Guru Arjan

![[attachments/sikhism_002.webp|420]]

*Golden Temple in Amritsar*

Guru Arjan faced hostility from his older brother Prithi Chand, who circulated hymns falsely attributed to earlier Gurus. To protect authentic teaching, Arjan compiled the Adi Granth ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guru_Granth_Sahib)) in 1604, using Bhai Gurdas as scribe and the Goindval Pothis as a guide.

Under Arjan, the Panth gained many converts, especially from the Jat agricultural caste. Mughal suspicion increased, and Emperor Jahangir heard rumours that Arjan had supported the rebel Khusro. Arjan was arrested and tortured to death. Before dying, he urged his son Hargobind always to carry arms, changing Sikh history.

## Guru Hargobind and the Armed Panth

Guru Hargobind made the Panth both spiritual and temporal. Arjan's command became the idea of miri/piri, joining worldly authority and spiritual authority. Hargobind remained a pir, or spiritual guide, but also became a mir, a protector of the community against tyranny.

This new balance appeared in Amritsar's architecture. The Harmandir Sahib symbolized piri, while the Akal Takht opposite it symbolized miri. Hargobind's leadership marked the move from a mainly devotional community toward one prepared for armed defence.

## Guru Har Rai

Guru Har Rai led during a mostly peaceful period, withdrawing to the Shiwalik Hills and preaching during visits to Punjab. His masands carried news and offerings from Sikh communities. Conflict returned when Aurangzeb demanded Har Rai's son Ram Rai as a hostage after the Mughal succession struggle.

Ram Rai tried to satisfy Aurangzeb by changing the meaning of a line in the Adi Granth. Har Rai rejected him for altering Guru Nanak's words. The succession therefore passed not to Ram Rai but to his younger brother Hari Krishen.

## Guru Hari Krishen

Guru Hari Krishen inherited the title at age five. Aurangzeb summoned him to Delhi, where he contracted smallpox and died. Before death, he said "Baba Bakale," pointing followers toward the old man in Bakala, Tegh Bahadur, son of Guru Hargobind.

## Guru Tegh Bahadur

Many claimants rushed to Bakala, but Sikh tradition says the trader Makhan Shah identified the true Guru by testing who knew his promised offering of 501 gold coins. Guru Tegh Bahadur's hymns were later added to the canon by Guru Gobind Singh, after which the Adi Granth remained closed.

Tegh Bahadur's death is central to Sikh memory. Tradition says Mughal authorities arrested him for helping Kashmiri Brahmans resist forced conversion to Islam. Offered conversion or death, he chose death and was beheaded. Sikhs secretly recovered his body and head, and the Ranghreta Sikhs gained honour for carrying his head to Anandpur.

## Guru Gobind Singh and the Khalsa

Guru Gobind Singh became Guru after Tegh Bahadur's execution and completed the militarization of the Panth. His most important act was founding the Khalsa, the community of initiated Sikhs bound by a distinctive code and identity.

The Khalsa gave Sikhs a disciplined collective form. It joined devotion, equality, courage, and readiness for sacrifice. Gobind Singh also ended the line of human Gurus by transferring authority to the Guru Granth Sahib, making scripture the permanent Guru after his death in 1708.

## The Adi Granth and the Dasam Granth

![[attachments/sikhism_003.webp|420]]

*Illuminated Guru Granth Sahib manuscript*

Two major volumes stand above other Sikh texts. The Adi Granth ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guru_Granth_Sahib)), accepted as Guru Granth Sahib, is the greater and unquestioned scripture. Carefully compiled by Guru Arjan in 1603-1604, it has 1,430 pages in the modern printed edition and focuses on remembrance of the divine name.

The Adi Granth opens with the Mul Mantra, the basic statement that there is one Supreme Being, creator, fearless, without enmity, immortal, unborn, self-existent, and known through the Guru's grace. It continues with Guru Nanak's Japji, the Sodar hymns sung at sundown, and Kirtan Sohila for evening recitation.

The Dasam Granth is associated with Guru Gobind Singh but is controversial because of questions about authorship and composition. Its status differs from the Adi Granth, which has no comparable uncertainty in Sikh devotion.

## Sikh Historical and Theological Literature

Several later works shaped Sikh memory. Gur-bilas texts told heroic stories of the warrior Gurus, while Sainapati's Gur Sobha of 1711 described Guru Gobind Singh and the founding of the Khalsa. Ratan Singh Bhangu's Panth Prakash praised the Khalsa, and Gian Singh wrote major later histories.

Modern commentary on the Guru Granth Sahib grew after the rise of the Tat Khalsa. Faridkot Tika answered Ernest Trumpp's insulting translation. Shabadarath Sri Guru Granth Sahib Ji, mainly by Teja Singh, became important, and Sahib Singh later issued the ten-volume Guru Granth Sahib Darapan.

Vir Singh was one of the most influential Sikh writers and theologians. He wrote novels, poetry, pamphlets, newspaper pieces, and scriptural commentary. Kahn Singh Nabha's Ham Hindu Nahin, first issued in 1898, argued that Sikhs are not Hindus and became a slogan of Sikh self-definition.

## Sects

Several Sikh sects exist alongside orthodoxy. The Nirankaris, followers of Baba Dayal, preached return to remembrance of the divine name and differ from orthodox Sikhs mainly by accepting a continuing line of Gurus. The Nam-Dharis, or Kuka Sikhs, also accept a continuing line and believe Guru Gobind Singh lived secretly before passing authority to Balak Singh.

The Akhand Kirtani Jatha emerged in the early 20th century. It gives special importance to kirtan, often singing hymns all night, and interprets one of the Five Ks as keski, a small turban, rather than simply uncut hair. The Sikh Dharma of the Western Hemisphere was founded in the United States in 1971 by Harbhajan Singh, known as Yogi Bhajan.

## Conclusion

Sikhs understand their religion through five decisive events. Guru Nanak taught liberation through meditation on the divine name. Guru Hargobind armed the Sikhs. Guru Gobind Singh founded the Khalsa and its code. At his death, the eternal Guru passed to the Guru Granth Sahib. In the early 20th century, the Tat Khalsa reformed Sikh identity.

Together these events produced a distinct Sikh faith marked by devotion, scripture, community discipline, memory of martyrdom, and pride in a separate religious identity. The Panth's history moves from Sant devotion to organized community, armed defence, scriptural authority, and modern self-definition.

## Key Events & Developments

- 1469-1539: Guru Nanak founded the Sikh path through teaching and hymns.
- 1604: Guru Arjan compiled the Adi Granth ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guru_Granth_Sahib)).
- 1606: Guru Arjan was tortured to death, leading toward the armed Panth.
- 17th century: Guru Hargobind joined miri and piri in Sikh leadership.
- 1675: Guru Tegh Bahadur was executed and remembered as a martyr.
- 1699: Guru Gobind Singh founded the Khalsa.
- 1708: Authority passed from human Gurus to the Guru Granth Sahib ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guru_Granth_Sahib)).
- Early 20th century: The Tat Khalsa reshaped modern Sikh identity.
