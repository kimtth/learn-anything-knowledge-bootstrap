# Stars and Star Clusters (Star Cluster)

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> Star clusters are gravitationally related stellar groups of common origin, including irregular open clusters, ancient compact globular clusters, and loose associations. The source shows how their shared distances and populations reveal stellar evolution, cluster ages, interstellar absorption, and the structure of the Milky Way.

## Introduction

![[attachments/star_cluster_001.webp|420]]

*Messier 13 globular cluster*

Star clusters ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Star_cluster)) are stellar assemblages held together by mutual gravity and related by common origin. The two main types are open clusters and globular clusters. A third kind of grouping, called an association, contains stars of similar type and origin but has lower density than the surrounding stellar field.

Open clusters ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Open_cluster)) contain from a dozen to many hundreds of stars and are usually irregular or unsymmetrical. Globular clusters ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Globular_cluster)) are old, compact systems containing thousands to hundreds of thousands of stars in nearly spherical form. Associations contain dozens to hundreds of young stars spread over much wider regions.

Several open clusters have been known since antiquity, including the Pleiades, Hyades, Praesepe, and Coma Berenices. The Pleiades were important to some early peoples because their rising at sunset marked the start of the year. Some globular clusters, such as Omega Centauri and Messier 13, are visible as hazy patches, but they were studied seriously only after the telescope.

## General Description and Classification

The study of star clusters helped reveal the structure of the Milky Way. In 1917, Harlow Shapley used globular cluster distances and distributions to show that the galactic centre lies in the Sagittarius region. In 1930, Robert J. Trumpler used open clusters to show that interstellar light is absorbed as it travels through space.

The discovery of stellar associations depended on recognizing the characteristics and motions of individual stars spread over large areas. In the 1920s, astronomers noticed that young, hot O and B stars gather together. In 1949, Victor A. Ambartsumian identified these as physical groups with common origin and named them O or OB associations; he also identified T associations of T Tauri variable stars.

## Colour-Magnitude Diagrams

![[attachments/star_cluster_003.png|420]]

*Hertzsprung-Russell diagram for a stellar population*

Because stars in one globular cluster are almost the same distance from Earth, their apparent brightness differences mainly reflect real luminosity differences. Astronomers can therefore plot colour-magnitude and spectrum-magnitude diagrams without distance confusion.

Globular cluster diagrams show a lower main sequence, a red giant branch, and a horizontal branch extending toward the blue. These patterns reflect stellar evolution: stars of different masses, but similar age and composition, move away from the main sequence at different times.

The turnoff point, where the brightest main-sequence stars leave the main sequence, is a measure of cluster age. Milky Way globular clusters are nearly as old as the universe, averaging perhaps 14 billion years, though exact values continue to be revised.

RR Lyrae variables, when present, occupy a special region called the RR Lyrae gap near the blue end of the horizontal branch. They are important because they help astronomers compare cluster distances, ages, and evolutionary states.

Two problems remain notable. Blue stragglers are stars that look hotter and more massive than they should for an old cluster, possibly formed by coalescence of lower-mass stars. The second-parameter problem asks why clusters with similar age and metal abundance can have very different horizontal branches.

## Globular Clusters

Globular clusters are dense, ancient stellar systems. Some are strongly concentrated toward their centres. The cluster 47 Tucanae, near the Small Magellanic Cloud but not connected to it, is one of the most impressive. An observer at its centre would see a sky bright like twilight because of nearby stars.

In the Northern Hemisphere, Messier 13 in Hercules is the best-known and easiest globular cluster to see. It is about 23,000 light-years away. M3 in Canes Venatici, about 33,000 light-years away, is especially rich in variables, with more than 200 known.

Globular clusters are important for studying Population II stars, old low-metallicity stars, galactic halo structure, stellar evolution, and the age of the Milky Way. Their spherical distribution helped astronomers understand that the Sun is not near the Galaxy's centre.

## Open Clusters

![[attachments/star_cluster_002.webp|420]]

*The Pleiades open cluster*

Open clusters ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Open_cluster)) are concentrated toward the Milky Way plane and form a flattened disk system about 2,000 light-years thick and 30,000 light-years across. Young open clusters trace the spiral arms of the Galaxy because they are born there.

Open clusters are classified by central concentration and richness. About half of known open clusters contain fewer than 100 stars, while the richest contain 1,000 or more. Some have large apparent diameters, such as the Taurus cluster and Perseus cluster.

Many open clusters have a visible core plus a wider halo of members. Membership is established through common motion, common distance, and related properties. Over long periods, tidal forces and stellar encounters break up open clusters, spreading their stars into the general galactic field.

## Clusters in Other Galaxies

The Magellanic Clouds contain clusters similar to, but not identical with, those of the Milky Way. Their globular clusters fall into two groups. Red clusters resemble Milky Way globular clusters in metal deficiency and may contain RR Lyrae variables. Blue clusters are large, circular, younger, and resemble very populous open clusters.

The Large Magellanic Cloud also contains associations and star clouds, including groupings of thousands of stars spread over hundreds or thousands of light-years. Differences between Magellanic Cloud clusters and Milky Way clusters may reflect small differences in helium and heavy-element abundances.

The Andromeda Galaxy, or M31, about 2.2 million light-years away, has about 400 globular clusters. Some have higher metal content than Milky Way globulars. M31 also contains many OB associations and rich star clouds, including NGC 206.

Other Local Group galaxies also contain clusters. M33 has hundreds of clusters, though fewer with globular characteristics. Some dwarf spheroidal galaxies have globular clusters, showing that star-cluster formation is not limited to large spiral galaxies.

## Key Events & Developments

- 1665: First recorded globular cluster in Sagittarius, later called Messier 22.
- 1677: Edmond Halley recorded Omega Centauri.
- 1917: Harlow Shapley used globular clusters to locate the Milky Way centre toward Sagittarius.
- 1930: Robert J. Trumpler showed that interstellar light is absorbed in space.
- 1949: Victor A. Ambartsumian identified OB and T associations as real stellar groupings.

## Key People

- Harlow Shapley ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harlow_Shapley)): Used globular clusters to revise understanding of the Milky Way's size and centre.
- Robert J. Trumpler ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_J._Trumpler)): Used open clusters to demonstrate interstellar absorption.
- Victor A. Ambartsumian: Named and interpreted stellar associations.
- Edmond Halley ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edmond_Halley)): Recorded Omega Centauri as a globular cluster candidate.

- Globular clusters ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Globular_cluster)) can contain hundreds of thousands of stars.
- Open clusters ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Open_cluster)) often dissolve over time because of tides and stellar encounters.
- 47 Tucanae would make the sky look like twilight to an observer near its centre.
- Blue stragglers look younger than the old clusters they inhabit.
- Open clusters ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Open_cluster)) help trace the Milky Way's spiral arms.
