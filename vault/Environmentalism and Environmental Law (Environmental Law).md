# Environmentalism and Environmental Law (Environmental Law)

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> Environmental law is the body of public rules, institutions, and agreements governing human effects on the nonhuman world. It addresses pollution, resource use, biodiversity, health, and environmental protection across local, national, and international scales.

## Introduction

Environmental law ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Environmental_law)) is the body of principles, policies, directives, and regulations enacted and enforced by local, national, or international entities to govern human treatment of the nonhuman world. This vast field spans a wide range of topics across diverse legal settings, from state bottle-return laws in the [[United States of America (United States)|United States]] and emission standards for coal-fired power plants in [[Germany]], to China's Green Great Wall, a shelterbelt of trees meant to protect [[Peking (Beijing)|Beijing]] from sandstorms, and to international treaties safeguarding biological diversity and the ozonosphere. During the late 20th century, environmental law grew from a modest adjunct of public health regulation into an almost universally recognized independent field that protects both human health and nonhuman nature.

## Historical Development

National governments have long passed occasional laws to shield human health from environmental harm. About AD 80 the Senate of [[Rome]] enacted legislation to protect the city's supply of clean water for drinking and bathing. In the 14th century, England prohibited both the burning of coal in [[London]] and the dumping of waste into waterways. In 1681 the Quaker leader of the English colony of Pennsylvania, William Penn, ordered that one acre of forest be preserved for every five acres cleared for settlement, and in the following century Benjamin Franklin led campaigns to curtail the dumping of waste.

During the 19th century, amid the Industrial Revolution ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Industrial_Revolution)), the British government passed regulations to reduce the harmful effects of coal burning and chemical manufacture on public health and the environment. Before the 20th century, few international environmental agreements existed, and those that were reached dealt mainly with boundary waters, navigation, and fishing rights along shared waterways while ignoring pollution and broader ecological concerns. Only in the early 20th century did conventions begin to protect commercially valuable species.

## Environmental Impact Assessment

A central regulatory tool requires governments to study harm before it occurs. The U.S. National Environmental Policy Act ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Environmental_Policy_Act)) of 1969 requires an environmental impact statement for any major federal action significantly affecting the quality of the human environment; the statement must analyze the proposed action's effects and weigh a range of alternatives, including a so-called no-action alternative. The Council on Environmental Quality, created under the same act, coordinates federal initiatives and obliges agencies to wait for completed statements before foreclosing alternatives.

The European Union applies a comparable environmental impact assessment ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Environmental_impact_assessment)) to two categories of projects. Annex-I projects, such as oil refineries, toxic waste landfills, and thermal power stations of 300 or more megawatts, are generally subject to the requirement, while annex-II projects in the chemical, food, textile, leather, wood, and paper industries require assessment only where member states judge it necessary. Each assessment must describe and evaluate the direct and indirect effects of a project on humans, fauna, flora, soil, water, air, climate, and landscape, together with the interactions among them.

## Economic Incentives

The use of economic instruments to encourage environmental protection is a popular form of environmental law, employing tools such as pollution taxes, subsidies for clean technologies and practices, and the creation of markets in either environmental protection or pollution. [[Denmark]], the [[Netherlands]], and [[Sweden]] impose taxes on carbon dioxide emissions, and the EU has debated whether to adopt such a carbon tax at the supranational level to combat climate change. In the [[United States of America (United States)|United States]], water pollution legislation passed in 1972 provided subsidies to help local governments upgrade publicly owned sewage treatment plants.
