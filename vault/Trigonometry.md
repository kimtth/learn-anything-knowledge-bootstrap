## Introduction

![[attachments/trigonometry_001.webp|420]]

*Unit circle trigonometry diagram*

Trigonometry ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trigonometry)) is the branch of mathematics concerned with functions of angles and their use in calculation. It began with triangle measurement but later became linked with algebra, analysis, complex numbers, waves, and periodic functions.

The six standard trigonometric functions ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trigonometric_functions)) are sine, cosine, tangent, cotangent, secant, and cosecant, abbreviated sin, cos, tan, cot, sec, and csc.

In a right triangle, sine of an angle is the ratio of the opposite side to the hypotenuse. The other functions are defined by related side ratios and depend on the angle, not the triangle's size.

## Uses

![[attachments/trigonometry_002.png|420]]

*Right triangle trigonometry diagram*

Trigonometry helps determine unknown angles and distances from known or measured data. It is used in astronomy, mapmaking, surveying, navigation, artillery, engineering, physics, and many applied sciences.

Plane trigonometry studies problems in one plane, while spherical trigonometry applies related methods to triangles and distances on curved surfaces or in three-dimensional settings.

Before computers, values of trigonometric functions were carefully tabulated for many angles so that calculations could be performed efficiently.

## Classical Development

The word trigonometry comes from Greek trigonon, meaning triangle, and metron, meaning measure. Until about the 16th century, the subject mainly computed missing parts of triangles.

If two sides and the included angle are known, trigonometric methods can find the third side and the other angles. Such calculations distinguished trigonometry from ordinary geometry.

Trigonometry developed through ancient and medieval work in astronomy, where angles and distances were essential for describing heavenly motions.

## Algebra and Infinite Processes

The unification of trigonometry with [[Algebra|algebra]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algebra)) grew in early modern mathematics. Trigonometric expressions appeared in solving algebraic equations and later in formulas involving complex numbers.

Francois Viete used trigonometric ideas in algebraic equations and discovered an infinite product for pi. This helped legitimize infinite processes in mathematics.

James Gregory found a power series for inverse tangent, and Isaac Newton found power series for sine and cosine. Some related formulas were known earlier in Indian astronomy through Madhava.

## Practical Applications

Trigonometry remained important in practical problems. Galileo Galilei showed that projectile motion could be resolved into horizontal and vertical components.

For artillery, the range of a cannonball without air resistance depends on muzzle velocity, gravity, and the angle of elevation. The maximum range occurs at 45 degrees.

Surveying used triangulation. A known baseline and measured angles to distant points allow distances to be calculated, then new triangles extend the survey.

Gemma Frisius suggested triangulation in 1533, Willebrord Snell carried it out on a large scale, and later national surveys used networks of triangles to map entire countries or regions.

## Angles and Units

Trigonometry uses a broader concept of angle than elementary geometry. An angle can be generated by rotation from an initial side to a terminal side.

Counterclockwise rotation gives a positive angle, while clockwise rotation gives a negative angle. Angles with the same initial and terminal sides can differ by whole revolutions.

Common units are the degree and the radian. A full revolution has 360 degrees or 2 pi radians, so one degree equals pi divided by 180 radians.

Radians are especially useful in theoretical mathematics because they connect angle measure directly to arc length and simplify formulas in calculus.

## Periodic Functions

![[attachments/trigonometry_003.png|420]]

![[attachments/trigonometry_004.webp|420]]

Sine and cosine can be defined using a rotating point on the unit circle. Their values repeat periodically as the point completes rotations.

This periodicity links trigonometry to waves, oscillations, circular motion, Fourier series, sound, light, and many physical systems.

## Core Equations

Trigonometry relates angles, sides, and periodic functions:

$$\sin^2\theta+\cos^2\theta=1$$

This identity follows from the unit circle and underlies many trigonometric transformations.

$$\frac{a}{\sin A}=\frac{b}{\sin B}=\frac{c}{\sin C}$$

This ratio turns the preceding idea into a measurable comparison, with the numerator as the quantity of interest and the denominator as the scale used for comparison.

## Key Events & Developments

- 1533: Gemma Frisius proposed triangulation for surveying.
- 1593: Viete discovered an infinite product for pi.
- 17th century: [[Galileo]], Gregory, and [[Newton]] connected trigonometry with mechanics and infinite series.
- 1800-1913: the Great Trigonometric Survey mapped the Indian subcontinent and identified Mount Everest.

## Interesting Facts

- Sine ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sine_and_cosine)) and cosine are independent of a triangle's size because they depend only on angle.
- Triangulation can map large areas from one measured baseline and many angle measurements.
- Radians make calculus formulas for trigonometric functions simpler than degree measure does.
