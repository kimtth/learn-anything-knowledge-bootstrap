# Hungarian Literature

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> Hungarian literature is writing in the Hungarian language, preserving early folk and religious material before developing through medieval translation, Renaissance institutions, Reformation texts, and later national forms. Its linguistic distinctiveness and long historical pressures give the tradition a particular place in central European culture.

## Introduction

Hungarian literature ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hungarian_literature)) is the body of written work in the Hungarian language. Its earliest recoverable elements survive through folk songs, folktales, and Latin legends about the origins of the Hungarian people, the conquest of [[Hungary]], and 10th-century campaigns. The first continuous Hungarian text is the Halotti beszed, a funeral oration from about 1200, and the oldest surviving Hungarian poem is Omagyar Maria-siralom, a free version of a poem by Godefroy de Breteuil from about 1300.

Early writing was strongly religious. The Jokai codex of about 1440, the oldest extant Hungarian codex, preserved the legend of St. Francis of Assisi. Followers of Jan Hus translated parts of the Bible in the 15th century, creating vocabulary still in use. Other translated works included sermons, saint legends, the first Hungarian drama, and traces of secular Trojan and Alexander stories.

## Renaissance and Reformation

The Renaissance ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Renaissance)) began institutionally with the university at Pecs in 1367 and later with King Matthias I Corvinus's library, printing press, and patronage. Latin literature reached a peak in Janus Pannonius. After the 1526 Battle of Mohacs and Ottoman occupation, the divided country entered the Reformation, when national literature in Hungarian truly began.

Humanist translators such as Benedek Komjati, Gabor Pesti, and Janos Sylvester translated biblical and classical material with philological care. Sylvester published the first Hungarian grammar and a 1541 New Testament. Peter Bornemisza became the first important Protestant writer, and Gaspar Karolyi's 1590 Bible translation shaped Hungarian much as the Authorized Version shaped English.

Secular writing grew through minstrels, romances, and entertainment literature. Sebestyen Tinodi recorded wars against the Turks, while Peter Ilosvai Selymes's Toldi romance later helped inspire Janos Arany. The greatest poet was Balint Balassi, whose love, war, and religious poems combined Petrarchan models, border warfare, nature, bravery, and Mannerist imagery.

## The 17th century

In the 17th century, divided [[Hungary]] developed along different cultural lines: Ottoman-held regions contributed little, Habsburg lands absorbed Catholic Italian and German influences, and Transylvania remained close to Dutch and English Protestant thought. Janos Apaczai Csere produced a Hungarian encyclopaedia at Utrecht in 1653, expanding technical vocabulary.

The Counter-Reformation made Peter Pazmany, a Jesuit cardinal, the master of clear, vigorous Baroque prose. Miklos Zrinyi, statesman and commander, wrote political prose and the epic Szigeti veszedelem in 1651, recounting the 1566 siege of Szigetvar. Istvan Gyongyosi's narrative and nuptial poems were widely read in the 17th and 18th centuries.

## Period of decline

From 1700 to about 1770, Hungarian literature ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hungarian_literature)) declined but consolidated. Its strongest prose was memoir writing, especially works by Miklos Bethlen, the exiled Ferenc Rakoczi II, and Kelemen Mikes, whose Letters from Turkey used French-inspired epistolary style and dry humour. Historical scholarship by Matyas Bel, Gyorgy Pray, Istvan Katona, David Czvittinger, and Peter Bod was also important.

## The period of the Enlightenment

The Hungarian Enlightenment from about 1772 to 1825 was receptive to French and English ideas. Gyorgy Bessenyei began the era with a translation of Alexander Pope's Essay on Man and used drama and fiction for didactic liberal aims. Jozsef Gvadanyi and Andras Dugonics defended national or popular values and helped make common speech literary.

Poetic language became experimental. Benedek Virag adapted classical metres, Daniel Berzsenyi made them powerful, and Janos Batsanyi warned tyrants after the 1789 French Revolution. Jozsef Karman's sentimental fiction, Mihaly Csokonai Vitez's lyric and comic poetry, Sandor Kisfaludy's Himfy verse, and Ferenc Kazinczy's language reform prepared modern literary Hungarian.

## Romanticism

Romanticism ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Romanticism)) rose under Karoly Kisfaludy, who founded Aurora in 1822 and became the first popular Romantic playwright. Jozsef Katona's Bank ban, published in 1821, joined national and personal conflict and made the peasant Tiborc a symbol of oppression. Ferenc Kolcsey's Hymnusz of 1823 became the national anthem.

Mihaly Vorosmarty became the central writer after Kisfaludy, producing the epic Zalan futasa and the symbolic play Csongor es Tunde. The novel developed through Miklos Josika's Abafi and Jozsef Eotvos's political novels on injustice and revolt. Folk collections influenced Sandor Petofi and Janos Arany, whose popular language, patriotic poetry, ballads, and Toldi trilogy became the century's peaks.

## Writers of the late 19th century

After the failed 1848-49 war of independence and the 1867 Compromise, rapid modernization mixed with literary decline. Laszlo Arany expressed disillusionment, and Janos Vajda bridged Romantic populism and fin-de-siecle imagery. Drama had a national theatre from 1837, but only Ede Szigligeti's village plays and Imre Madach's philosophical The Tragedy of Man rose high.

The major novelists were Zsigmond Kemeny, whose psychological novels were powerful but difficult; Mor Jokai, a popular storyteller with more than 200 books; Kalman Mikszath, a humorous observer of society; and Geza Gardonyi, whose early 20th-century novels still belonged to 19th-century taste.

## Literary Institutions

Literary institutions strengthened around the Hungarian Academy of 1825, the Kisfaludy Society of 1836, and the Petofi Society of 1876. Pal Gyulai led criticism, while Jeno Peterfy and Frigyes Riedl advanced literary history. The periodical A het, founded by Jozsef Kiss in 1890, gathered writers such as Zoltan Ambrus and Sandor Brody.

## The 20th century

The 1906 publication of Endre Ady's Uj versek marked a modern turn, combining French Symbolist style with radical politics and new imagery. Nyugat, launched in 1908, became the major modern forum for Mihaly Babits, Dezso Kosztolanyi, Milan Fust, Zsigmond Moricz, Margit Kaffka, and Gyula Krudy.

Before and after [[World Wars (World War I)|World War I]], Ferenc Molnar wrote social comedies, Ferenc Herczeg represented conservative nationalism, Lajos Kassak became the first major Hungarian avant-garde poet, and Dezso Szabo's The Village That Was Swept Away mixed antiwar feeling with a romantic cult of the peasantry.

## The interwar period

The interwar period saw major poetry and fiction. Lorinc Szabo led the 1920s, while the 1930s were dominated by Attila Jozsef, Gyula Illyes, and the tragic late work of Miklos Radnoti. Novelists such as Sandor Marai, Lajos Zilahy, Janos Kodolanyi, Laszlo Nemeth, Zsigmond Remenyik, Aron Tamasi, and Tibor Dery explored bourgeois life, peasantry, injustice, and modern identity.

## Writing after 1945

After 1945, Socialist Realism was imposed most harshly in 1948-53, and writers were silenced or imprisoned after the failed 1956 uprising. By the mid-1960s, control eased, allowing modern fiction by Geza Ottlik, Miklos Meszoly, Istvan Orkeny, Gyorgy Konrad, Peter Esterhazy, and Jozsef Lengyel.

Postwar poetry ranged from Sandor Weores's broad imagination to Janos Pilinszky's Catholic poems on the universe of camps. Ferenc Juhasz and Laszlo Nagy were also important. Hungarian writing continued outside [[Hungary]] through minorities in Czechoslovakia, Yugoslavia, and Romania, and through western diaspora writers such as Gyozo Hatar and Gyorgy Faludy.

## Key Events & Developments

- c. 1200: The Halotti beszed became the first continuous Hungarian text.
- 1590: Gaspar Karolyi's Bible translation strongly shaped written Hungarian.
- 1848: Sandor Petofi's patriotic poetry helped inspire revolution.
- 1908: Nyugat became the main modernist forum for Hungarian writers.
- 1948-53: Socialist Realism was enforced as the approved literary method.

## Key People

- Balint Balassi ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Balint_Balassi)): Major Renaissance poet of love, war, and religion.
- Ferenc Kazinczy ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ferenc_Kazinczy)): Organizer of language reform and literary refinement.
- Sandor Petofi ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sandor_Petofi)): Revolutionary poet whose direct language transformed Hungarian poetry.
- Janos Arany ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Janos_Arany)): Ballad and narrative poet who raised popular language to high art.

## Interesting Facts

- Hungarian literature ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hungarian_literature)) began with many Latin and religious materials before a strong vernacular tradition formed.
- Gaspar Karolyi's Bible had an influence on Hungarian comparable to the Authorized Version in English.
- Tiborc from Bank ban remained a lasting symbol of the oppressed peasant.
- Nyugat gathered poets, prose writers, and editors who shaped modern Hungarian literary language.
