# Animals

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> Animals are multicellular eukaryotes characterized by specialized tissues, mobility, nerves, muscles, and diverse strategies for feeding, reproduction, coordination, and adaptation. Their many body plans reflect evolutionary solutions to common biological problems.

## Introduction

Animals ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Animal)) are multicellular eukaryotic organisms belonging to the kingdom Animalia. They differ fundamentally from plants and fungi in form and physiology, above all because they evolved [[Muscles and Muscle Systems (Muscle)|muscles]], nerves, and the mobility that follows from them. Movement is the central fact of animal existence, shaping feeding, defense, reproduction, and the development of tissues and organ systems. Animals dominate human awareness of life: their mobility, diversity, and often obvious behaviour make them especially visible, even though plants may exceed them in size on land. Animals occupy nearly every mode of life on Earth, and their enormous variety reflects the opportunities opened by active movement and complex interaction with the environment.

## Origins and General Nature

Animals trace back to unicellular eukaryotes. Because eukaryotic cells possess nuclei and more elaborate internal organization than bacteria, they allow greater control over protein production and thereby make cellular specialization possible. Animal tissues and organs are understood as consequences of this capacity for differentiation.

Another defining feature is the absence of the semirigid cell walls found in plants and fungi. Without such walls, highly specialized cell types such as nerve cells and muscle cells can evolve. Animality is therefore not simply a taxonomic label but a structural solution to life based on mobility, coordination, and multicellular division of labor.

## Individuality, Coloniality, and Sociality

Some forms of life complicate the idea of a single individual animal. Colonial animals such as some hydrozoans may act like one organism even though they are built from repeated genetically identical units. This raises questions about what counts as an individual in the animal kingdom.

At the other end of the scale, large mobile animals often live socially. Social life can provide advantages in protection, communication, and resource sharing. Both very small colonial animals and some of the largest vertebrates show forms of sociality, though for different reasons. In humans, social complexity reaches a level at which societies themselves begin to resemble integrated organisms.

## Form and Function

To live, animals must obtain food, water, and oxygen and must dispose of waste, so form and function go together. Animal diversity is not presented as random variation but as many ways of solving common problems of support, movement, digestion, circulation, coordination, and reproduction.

Because animals exploit food that already contains complex organic matter, they can reduce many synthetic pathways found in plants. This allows specialization but also ties species closely to particular diets and ecological strategies. The structure of organs such as guts therefore reflects what each animal typically eats and how food is processed.

## Body Cavities and Major Structural Grades

One of 's main organizational principles is the distinction among acoelomates, pseudocoelomates, and coelomates. Acoelomates, such as flatworms, lack a true body cavity and are therefore constrained in size and locomotor force. Pseudocoelomates possess a body cavity that is not fully lined by mesoderm. Coelomates possess a true coelom, which allows suspension of the gut and greater flexibility in body design.

A true coelom supports larger size and more complex movement, especially in animals that use the body cavity in burrowing or internal support. In arthropods the coelom becomes less important because locomotion is transferred to limbs supported by an exoskeleton, while in vertebrates large body size depends chiefly on the endoskeleton.

## Protostomes and Deuterostomes

Among coelomates, protostomes and deuterostomes are distinguished by features of early development. These include the relation of the blastopore to mouth and anus, cleavage pattern, and the way the coelom forms. Although these developmental differences are technical, they are treated as major clues to deep animal relationships.

This division helps organize much of animal diversity. Mollusks, annelids, and arthropods are among the protostomes, while echinoderms and chordates are among the deuterostomes. These broad groupings connect embryology with large-scale classification.

## Hormones and Integration

Hormones are one of the key mechanisms that integrate multicellular animal life. While nerves coordinate rapid actions such as immediate movement, hormones coordinate slower and broader processes including maintenance, reproduction, and development. They act as chemical messengers transported through the circulatory system to receptive tissues.

Only larger and more complex animals usually possess specialized endocrine glands, but hormonal coordination in some form is widespread. Specificity lies not only in the messenger but also in the receptors of the tissues that respond. Hormonal systems therefore reveal how animal bodies achieve integration across many distant organs.

## Digestion and Nutrition

Animals must digest organic food obtained from other organisms, and the complexity of an animal's digestive system reflects the kind of food it normally consumes. In very simple animals, digestion may be partly or wholly intracellular. In more complex animals, digestion becomes largely extracellular within a gut, and the products are then transported to other tissues.

As animals evolved to eat larger or more complex food items, digestive systems also became more elaborate. Gut design is linked to diet, chemistry, transport, and the speed with which useful materials must reach the rest of the body. In this way, feeding biology helps explain much of animal anatomy.

## Classification and Diagnostic Features

A formal diagnostic definition describes animals as multicellular eukaryotes with cells bound by collagen, lacking semirigid cell walls, producing sperm, and forming polar bodies in oogenesis. Animals generally gain energy by consuming other organisms or their products and move by means of muscle and nerve cells.

It then provides a broad classification of living animal phyla, beginning with Porifera and moving through major invertebrate groups to Chordata. Short descriptions identify each phylum by features such as symmetry, body cavity, appendages, feeding mode, habitat, and approximate species richness. The classification is presented as useful but also provisional, since some groups had not yet been fully tested by newer comparative methods.

## Major Animal Groups

![[attachments/animals_002.webp|420]]

*Diagram showing diversity across major animal groups*

The diversity of the kingdom is illustrated by sponges, cnidarians, comb jellies, flatworms, ribbon worms, annelids, arthropods, mollusks, echinoderms, hemichordates, and chordates. Each group represents a different solution to common biological problems such as locomotion, feeding, support, and gas exchange.

Among these, [[Arthropods|arthropods]] stand out for their extraordinary richness in species and ecological roles, while vertebrates are notable for their internal skeleton, large size, and physiological complexity. No one lineage is the goal of evolution; instead, animal diversity reflects many successful evolutionary pathways.

## Conclusion

Animals ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Animal)) form the mobile multicellular kingdom, distinguished by specialized tissues, internal coordination, and the capacity to exploit almost every ecological setting on Earth. Their evolution from unicellular ancestors made possible nerves, muscles, organs, and complex behaviour, and these in turn generated extraordinary diversity in body plan and mode of life.

At the same time, the animal kingdom can be understood through a few large biological themes: mobility, multicellularity, integration, developmental pattern, and adaptation to different ways of obtaining energy. These themes connect the smallest invertebrates and the largest vertebrates within one broad history of living form.
