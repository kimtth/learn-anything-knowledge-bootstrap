# Mollusks

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> Mollusks are a diverse invertebrate phylum whose bivalves, gastropods, and cephalopods occupy aquatic and terrestrial habitats. Their anatomy, reproduction, larval development, feeding, ecological roles, parasites, invasive species, and economic uses explain their exceptional biological and human importance.

## Introduction

![[attachments/mollusks_001.png|420]]

*Mollusk anatomy diagram*

Mollusks ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mollusca)), also spelled molluscs, are soft-bodied invertebrates in the phylum Mollusca. Most are partly or fully enclosed by a calcium carbonate shell made by the mantle. They are one of the most diverse animal groups, with nearly 100,000 described species and perhaps up to 150,000. The phylum includes caudofoveates, solenogasters, chitons, monoplacophorans, gastropods, bivalves, scaphopods, and cephalopods, with sizes ranging from animals less than one millimetre long to giant squids that may reach 22 metres with arms extended.

Distribution is broad but not uniform. Mollusks occupy rocky, sandy, and muddy bottoms; some burrow, crawl, attach permanently, or swim freely. The group is mainly marine, but bivalves and gastropods also include freshwater species, and gastropods include thousands of fully terrestrial forms. Food dependence strongly shapes their ranges: herbivores depend on plant supply, shipworms are associated with wood, and cold-water regions generally support fewer species than warmer ones.

Mollusks matter in ecosystems and human economies. Bivalves filter water and help organic turnover, but this feeding can also affect planktonic larvae. The zebra mussel, carried from [[Europe]] to the Great Lakes in ship ballast water in 1986, became a harmful invader by clogging industrial pipes and threatening native freshwater bivalves. Many gastropods are intermediate hosts for trematode flatworms, including parasites that cause schistosomiasis in humans. Gastropods, bivalves, and cephalopods are also major food resources, while shells support ornament, pearl, and mother-of-pearl industries.

Molluscan reproduction and development include separate sexes, hermaphroditism, internal and external fertilization, and several larval forms. Many primitive species release eggs and sperm into water, while cephalopods often transfer spermatophores during copulation. Development commonly begins with spiral cleavage and may pass through pericalymma, trochophore, or veliger larvae. Cephalopods and many nonmarine gastropods often develop directly, and some cephalopods die soon after mating or egg laying.

Molluscan habitats and feeding habits differ sharply by class. Caudofoveates burrow in deep mud and eat microorganisms or detritus; solenogasters prey on cnidarians; chitons scrape algae; scaphopods capture small animals with captacula; and most bivalves are suspension feeders. Gastropods have the widest ecological range, from deep-sea trenches to land, while cephalopods are mainly carnivores. Some bivalves and sea slugs live with zooxanthellae, and some hot-vent mollusks depend on bacterial associations.

## Organizational

Mollusks use many forms of locomotion. Solenogasters and small gastropods glide on cilia over mucus; larger forms often move by muscular waves along the foot; burrowers use muscles and hydrostatic pressure; cephalopods use buoyancy and jet propulsion; scallops swim; and octopods crawl or swim with their arms. Defense includes shells, cuticles, adhesion, burrowing, mucus, poisonous or distasteful secretions, stored cnidarian nematocysts, cephalopod ink, deep-sea luminous clouds, the purple fluid of Aplysia, camouflage, and warning colours.

## Organizational (External and Internal Features)

![[attachments/mollusks_002.webp|420]]

*Octopus*

The main external parts of a typical molluscan body are the mantle, foot, head, and mantle cavity. The mantle may carry scales, spicules, eight chiton plates, or a shell. The foot may be a gliding sole, a burrowing organ, a bivalve axe-shaped structure, swimming lobes, or a cephalopod funnel. The mantle cavity usually holds ctenidia, mucus tracts, and body-system openings, while the hemocoel and muscles supply support and movement.

The [[Nerves and Nervous Systems (Nervous System)|nervous system]] begins with paired cerebral ganglia that serve the head, mouth, and sense organs. Lateral nerve cords innervate the mantle, pedal cords innervate the foot, a buccal loop serves the radula, and visceral ganglia serve internal organs. Gastropod torsion creates special nerve patterns, while cephalopods concentrate ganglia inside a cartilaginous capsule. Sensory structures include osphradia that monitor water entering the mantle cavity, photoreceptive papillae in chitons, scallop eye spots, and the highly developed eyes of cephalopods.

The digestive system is built around the radula in most groups, a toothed ribbon used to bite, scrape, and tear food. Bivalves and some other mollusks reduce or lose it. Mollusks usually have an open circulatory system with hemolymph, auricles, and a ventricle; respiratory pigments include hemoglobin and hemocyanin. Endocrine control is best known in gastropods and cephalopods, where hormones affect reproduction, metabolism, heart rate, and in some cephalopods death after mating or egg deposition.

The fossil record shows both limits and major patterns in molluscan evolution. Caudofoveates and solenogasters lack known fossils, but chitons and conchifers date from the earliest Cambrian, cephalopods appear by the Late Cambrian, and scaphopods are known by the Middle Ordovician. Evolutionary interpretation uses comparative anatomy, development, and molecular data, suggesting a shell-less base followed by separate aplacophoran, polyplacophoran, monoplacophoran, bivalve-scaphopod, and gastropod-cephalopod lines.

## Classification

![[attachments/mollusks_003.png|420]]

*Bivalve shell anatomy*

The classification follows a consensus associated with Luitfried v. Salvini-Plawen, Gerhard Haszprunar, and Kenneth J. Boss. It treats Mollusca as unsegmented, soft-bodied metazoans with mantle secretions, ciliary or muscular locomotion, ctenidia, osphradia, a tetraneurous nervous system, dorsoventral muscles, a radula, a hemocoel, and primitively spiral cleavage.

## Phylum Mollusca

Phylum Mollusca includes marine, freshwater, and terrestrial animals with a mantle or pallium, cuticular or calcareous coverings, a locomotory organ, ctenidia, osphradia, cerebral ganglia, a buccal loop, longitudinal nerve cords, and a radula. The classification lists about 50,000 marine, limnic, and terrestrial species.

## Class Aplacophora

Aplacophorans are worm-shaped, shell-less, mostly deep-water marine mollusks. They may represent a primitive molluscan condition or a secondary loss of shells from more advanced ancestors. The class includes about 300 species.

## Subclass Chaetodermomorpha (Caudofoveata)

Caudofoveates are worm-shaped burrowers with cuticle, aragonitic scales, reduced gliding area, terminal mantle cavity, and about 100 species. Solenogasters are narrow-footed gliders with scales or spicules, no true ctenidia, rudimentary radula, hermaphroditism, and about 200 species. Other major classes include chitons with eight plates, monoplacophorans with cap shells and repeated organs, bivalves with two hinged valves and suspension feeding, scaphopods with tusklike shells and captacula, and gastropods with torsion and at least 40,000 species.

## Subclass Prosobranchia

Prosobranchs are mostly marine limpets or operculate snails with at least 20,000 species. Opisthobranchs include sea hares, nudibranchs, snails, and slugs and have about 19,000 species. Cephalopods include nautiluses, cuttlefishes, squids, and octopuses, with large eyes, arms or tentacles, a funnel, strong jaws, often an ink sac, and a concentrated nervous system. Classification remains unsettled, especially because older groupings such as Amphineura and the familiar gastropod subclasses are no longer widely accepted.

## Key Events & Developments

- Earliest Cambrian: Chitons and conchifers appear in the fossil record, while caudofoveates and solenogasters remain unknown as fossils.
- Late Cambrian: Cephalopods appear and begin a long evolutionary radiation.
- Middle Ordovician: Scaphopods become known in the fossil record.
- End of the Cretaceous: Ammonites, a major fossil cephalopod group, disappear after a long history.
- 1986: Zebra mussels arrive in the Great Lakes from Europe in ballast water and become a damaging invasive species.

## Interesting Facts

- Giant squids are the largest living invertebrates and may reach 22 metres when the arms are included.
- Bivalves can filter up to 40 litres of water per hour, making them important in organic turnover but also influential in plankton communities.
- Cephalopod ink, deep-sea squid luminous clouds, and sea-hare purple fluid all function as predator-confusing defenses.
- Gastropods are the only molluscan group described here as having thousands of fully terrestrial species.
- Some mollusks live in obligatory symbiosis with algae or bacteria, including sea slugs, giant clams, and hot-vent species.
