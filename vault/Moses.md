## Hebrew Moshe

Moses, or Moshe, was the Hebrew prophet, teacher, and leader who, in the 13th century BCE, delivered the Hebrews from Egyptian slavery and organized the religious community known as Israel. At Mt. Sinai, where the Ten Commandments were given, he interpreted the covenant stipulations that shaped Israel's religious and civil traditions. Jewish tradition reveres him as the greatest prophet and teacher, and his influence remains important in Western religion, morality, and social ethics.

The historical problem of Moses has produced sharply different interpretations. Early Jewish and Christian traditions treated him as author of the Torah, or Pentateuch. Martin Noth was skeptical, seeing separate Exodus and Sinai traditions later woven around Moses as an obscure figure. The more moderate view associated with W.F. Albright accepts a historical core beneath the traditions while recognizing complex biblical sources and later shaping.

## From Goshen to Sinai

The Exodus narrative presents Egyptian escape through complex and sometimes contradictory traditions. One strand says Pharaoh ordered the Hebrews out after the death of his son; another suggests Moses used mourning for the first-born as a chance to flee secretly. Critical interpretation reduces the departing group from the traditional millions to perhaps about 15,000 people. Pharaoh pursued them toward the Sea of Reeds, probably a shallow freshwater lake in northeastern Egypt rather than the Red Sea.

The crossing of the Sea of Reeds ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yam_Suph)) became the decisive sign of Yahweh's deliverance. A strong east wind opened a dry passage through the water, allowing the Hebrews to cross, while returning waters destroyed the pursuing Egyptians. Moses and his sister Miriam then led a victory song to Yahweh. The poetry resembles 14th-century BCE Canaanite style, giving the tradition an early and powerful setting.

## The Covenant at Sinai

![[attachments/moses_001.webp|420]]

*Ancient synagogue fresco of Moses and the burning bush from Dura-Europos*

The Sinai covenant is explained through the model of Hittite treaties from the 14th century BCE. Those treaties joined a great king, or suzerain, to a subordinate ruler, or vassal, through a historical prologue and an oath of loyalty. Because Moses likely knew Egyptian court life and international politics, he may have recognized the treaty form as an analogy for Yahweh's relationship with Israel.

At Mt. Sinai, Yahweh's appearance in storm became a revelation for Moses, like the earlier burning bush. The covenant meant that Yahweh had a claim on the Hebrews because he had delivered them, and their proper response was obedience. The Ten Commandments were likely covenant stipulations rather than later agricultural rules. Their ban on other gods expressed practical monotheism: other nations might recognize gods, but Israel owed exclusive loyalty to Yahweh.

## From Sinai to Transjordan

After Sinai, Moses faced resistance, frustration, and repeated complaints. Miriam and Aaron opposed his Cushite wife, spies discouraged the people from invading Canaan from Kadesh-barnea, and the people nearly stoned him when he urged them to reconsider. Moses often interceded when Yahweh threatened judgment, but at Meribah his anger led him to strike the rock twice, a failure later interpreted as the reason he could not enter Canaan.

In Transjordan, Edom and Moab refused passage, so Moses moved east and north, defeating Sihon king of the Amorites and Og king of Bashan. Some tribes were allowed to settle in Transjordan, provoking Moabite and Midianite opposition. The diviner Balaam, hired to curse Israel, instead pronounced blessing, though later tradition connects him with seduction into idolatry. Moses' final work was to prepare the community to enter Canaan under new leadership.

## Key Events & Developments

- 14th-13th century BCE: Moses flourished as Hebrew prophet, teacher, and leader.
- 13th century BCE: Moses led the Hebrews out of Egyptian slavery.
- 13th century BCE: The Sinai covenant formed Israel as a religious community.
- 13th century BCE: The Hebrews crossed Transjordan and prepared for entry into Canaan.

## Key People

- Moses ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moses)): Hebrew leader, prophet, covenant mediator, and organizer of Israel's traditions.
- Miriam ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miriam)): Moses' sister, who joined the victory song after the Sea of Reeds crossing.
- Aaron ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aaron)): Moses' brother and supporter, though also involved in opposition to Moses' Cushite marriage.
- Pharaoh ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pharaoh)): Egyptian ruler whose resistance framed the Exodus deliverance.
- Balaam ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Balaam)): Syrian diviner hired to curse Israel but remembered for pronouncing blessing.

## Interesting Facts

- Sea of Reeds ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yam_Suph)) is probably a freshwater lake, not the Red Sea in the later common interpretation.
- Mosaic authorship of the Torah remains a traditional belief but is disputed by modern scholarship.
- Sinai covenant is compared to Hittite suzerain-vassal treaty forms.
- Practical monotheism at Sinai required exclusive loyalty to Yahweh without yet being later philosophical monotheism.
