# World Wars (World War I)

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> World War I was the 1914-18 international conflict between the Central Powers and Allied powers that brought unprecedented destruction across Europe, Russia, the Middle East, the United States, and other regions. The source follows its fronts and campaigns while explaining its role in imperial collapse, revolution, and the conditions preceding World War II.

## Introduction

World War I ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_War_I)), also called the First World War ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_War_I)) or Great War ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_War_I)), was an international conflict fought in 1914-18. It drew in most of [[Europe]], [[Russia]], the [[United States of America (United States)|United States]], the Middle East, and other regions. The main Central Powers were [[Germany]], Austria-Hungary, and [[Turkey and Ancient Anatolia (Turkey)|Turkey]]. The main Allies were [[France]], Great Britain, [[Russia]], [[Italy]], [[Japan]], and, from 1917, the [[United States of America (United States)|United States]]. The war ended with the defeat of the Central Powers.

The conflict was a major turning point in 20th-century geopolitics because its scale of slaughter and destruction was almost without precedent. It helped bring down four imperial dynasties: those of [[Germany]], [[Russia]], Austria-Hungary, and [[Turkey and Ancient Anatolia (Turkey)|Turkey]]. It also helped produce the Bolshevik Revolution ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/October_Revolution)) in Russia and destabilized European society in ways that later helped prepare the ground for [[World Wars (World War II)|World War II]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_War_II)).

The war began from the crisis in the Balkans. After the Balkan Wars of 1912-13, Serbian nationalists renewed their aim of freeing the South Slavs under Austro-Hungarian rule. Dragutin Dimitrijevic, known as Apis, led Serbian military intelligence and the secret society Union or Death. He planned the assassination of Archduke Francis Ferdinand, heir to Emperor Francis Joseph. On June 28, 1914, in Sarajevo, Gavrilo Princip shot and killed Francis Ferdinand and his wife, Sophie. The assassination gave Austria-Hungary the immediate cause for action against Serbia and set off the wider alliance crisis.

Modern weapons shaped the war from the start. Machine guns ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Machine_gun)), barbed wire, and rapid-firing artillery made frontal attacks extremely costly and gave the defense a strong advantage. Yet many armies still entered the war with doctrines based on courage, will, and offensive spirit. [[France]] emphasized bayonet charges and the offensive. [[Germany]], influenced by Alfred von Schlieffen, emphasized flanking attacks, reserve formations, and stronger defensive training with machine guns and field fortifications.

The result was attrition warfare. Trenches, wire, shellfire, machine-gun zones, rail supply, and mass conscript armies made it hard to restore movement after the opening campaigns failed. New tools such as poison gas, aircraft, storm troops, creeping barrages, tanks, submarines, and improved artillery changed tactics but rarely produced quick decision. World War I therefore became a struggle of endurance: states had to mobilize industry, shipping, food, finance, morale, and manpower as much as battlefield skill.

## The Schlieffen Plan

The Schlieffen Plan ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Schlieffen_Plan)) grew from Germany's fear of a two-front war against [[France]] in the west and [[Russia]] in the east. Earlier German chiefs of staff, including Helmuth von Moltke and Alfred von Waldersee, had favored staying defensive in the west while first striking Russia. Alfred von Schlieffen, chief of the German general staff from 1891 to 1905, took the opposite view.

Schlieffen believed [[Russia]] would need about six weeks to mobilize because of its size, weak rail network, and slow bureaucracy. Germany would therefore hold the east with minimum forces while concentrating most troops against [[France]]. The western offensive would pass through neutral [[Belgium]], avoid France's frontier defenses, sweep through northern France, take [[Paris]], and knock France out of the war quickly. Germany could then transfer troops eastward against Russia.

The plan showed Germany's search for a quick decision, but it also tied the opening of the war to a violation of Belgian neutrality and to a huge wheeling movement that demanded speed, coordination, and endurance. Once the advance slowed, Germany faced the very two-front struggle the plan was meant to avoid.

## The Eastern Front, 1914

On the Eastern Front in 1914, [[Russia]] attacked East Prussia before its army was fully ready, partly to relieve pressure on [[France]]. Grand Duke Nicholas directed the Russian war effort, while Ya.G. Zhilinsky oversaw two armies: P.K. Rennenkampf's 1st Army from the east and A.V. Samsonov's 2nd Army from the south. They aimed to trap the German 8th Army, but the Russian armies were separated and poorly coordinated.

The German commander Max von Prittwitz was alarmed after the Russian success at Gumbinnen and considered retreat. His staff, especially Max Hoffmann, proposed instead to move divisions by rail and strike Samsonov's exposed flank. Paul von Hindenburg and Erich Ludendorff arrived to replace Prittwitz and confirmed the plan. The result was the German victory at Tannenberg, where Samsonov's army was destroyed. Germany then defeated Rennenkampf near the Masurian Lakes, stabilizing East Prussia and giving Hindenburg and Ludendorff great prestige.

## The Western Front, 1915

The Western Front in 1915 showed the difficulty of breaking trench lines. French attacks in Champagne gained only about 500 yards at a cost of 50,000 men. At Neuve-Chapelle, Sir Douglas Haig's 1st Army tried a short, intense bombardment followed by infantry attack. The method suggested that surprise and speed could help, but shortages of shells and delays allowed the Germans to recover.

Allied commanders drew the wrong lesson, thinking heavier shellfire alone would solve the trench problem. The Germans learned more from the experiment than the Allies did. Meanwhile, German forces used chlorine gas at Ypres on April 22, 1915, the first major gas attack on the Western Front. The weapon caused terror and opened a gap, but the Germans had not prepared enough reserves to exploit it fully.

The Allies also debated whether to keep attacking in France or seek indirect routes against the Central Powers. The result was the Gallipoli campaign. British leaders hoped to force the Dardanelles, threaten Constantinople, and aid Russia. Naval attacks began in February 1915, and troops under Sir Ian Hamilton landed on April 25. Australian and New Zealand forces gained a bridgehead at Anzac Cove, while British troops landed near Cape Helles. Strong Turkish defenses, shaped by Otto Liman von Sanders and local commanders such as Mustafa Kemal, turned the campaign into another costly deadlock.

## The Eastern Front, 1915

In 1915, the Central Powers shifted major pressure to the east. Austria-Hungary needed relief in Galicia, and Erich von Falkenhayn agreed to help while still thinking in terms of attrition. A German-Austrian plan targeted the Russian center near Gorlice and Tuchow. August von Mackensen commanded the attack, with Hans von Seeckt helping shape tactics that stressed rapid movement and continuing pressure rather than fixed daily objectives.

The Gorlice-Tarnow offensive, launched on May 2, 1915, exceeded expectations. Russian forces were driven back from the Dunajec River and then from the San. The Central Powers occupied [[Poland]] and took about 750,000 Russian prisoners in four months. Yet they failed to destroy Russia's ability to continue the war because the Russian armies escaped eastward before the German northern envelopment toward Vilna could close.

By October 1915, the Russian retreat stopped along a line from near Riga to Czernowitz. The campaign was a huge territorial success for the Central Powers, but it did not produce a final decision. Russia had been badly weakened, not eliminated.

## Mesopotamia, 1914April 1916

The Mesopotamian campaign began with a practical British goal: protecting the oil wells of southern Persia and the Abadan refinery. British forces occupied Basra in November 1914, moved to al-Qurnah in December, and advanced up the Tigris to al-Amarah in 1915. These gains would have met the main strategic need.

The advance continued toward [[Baghdad]], however, stretching the British far from their base. Charles Townshend reached Ctesiphon, only 18 miles from Baghdad, in November 1915, but the battle there brought no decisive gain. His force retreated to al-Kut, where the Turks besieged about 10,000 men from December 7, 1915. Townshend surrendered on April 29, 1916, making the campaign a major British failure.

## The Egyptian frontiers, 1915July 1917

After Gallipoli, Britain kept large forces in [[Egypt]] because the Suez Canal remained vital. The main danger was a Turkish advance from [[Palestine]] across the Sinai Desert. That threat weakened when Husayn ibn Ali, the Hashimite amir in the Hejaz, rebelled against the Turks and when T.E. Lawrence helped turn the revolt into a wider threat to Turkish communications, especially the Hejaz Railway.

British operations under Sir Archibald Murray advanced across Sinai but faltered at Gaza in March 1917 and again in April. Command then passed to Sir Edmund Allenby in June. At the same time, Lawrence and Arab forces captured Aqaba on July 6, 1917, defeating a much larger Turkish force and improving the Allied position against Palestine and Syria.

## The Western Front, 1916

![[attachments/world_wars_world_war_i_001.webp|420]]

*Bataille de Verdun 1916*

In 1916, the war's center of gravity returned to [[France]]. Britain had expanded its army and introduced conscription under the Military Service Act of January 1916. Allied commanders planned simultaneous offensives by [[France]], Great Britain, [[Russia]], and [[Italy]]. German action disrupted this plan.

Falkenhayn chose Verdun as the point where Germany would try to bleed France white. He did not seek a simple breakthrough; he wanted to force the French command to defend a symbolic fortress zone and spend its manpower. The Battle of Verdun became one of the war's central battles of attrition. French resistance, later associated with Philippe Petain, prevented collapse but at enormous cost.

The Allied response included the Battle of the Somme, begun in July 1916. It was intended partly to relieve Verdun and partly to break the German line. The offensive produced huge casualties and limited gains. New weapons, especially the tank, appeared in 1916, but early use was still experimental and could not yet restore mobility to the Western Front.

## The Battle of Jutland

The Battle of Jutland ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Jutland)) in 1916 was the largest naval battle of the war. Reinhard Scheer, commander of Germany's High Seas Fleet, hoped to lure part of the British fleet into battle and destroy it before the full Grand Fleet could arrive. Franz von Hipper's battle cruisers were to draw out David Beatty's force, while Scheer's main fleet followed behind.

British codebreaking helped the Royal Navy send John Jellicoe's Grand Fleet toward the area. On May 31, 1916, Beatty and Hipper made contact, and the battle expanded as the larger fleets approached. Both sides suffered losses and both claimed success. Strategically, however, Britain kept command of the sea, while the German surface fleet did not break the British naval blockade.

## The Eastern Front, 1916

Russia tried to relieve pressure on Verdun with an offensive near Lake Naroch in March 1916, but it gained little at heavy cost. A larger offensive was planned for summer. The main effort was supposed to come from A.E. Evert's central armies, while A.A. Brusilov's southwestern armies were meant to make a diversion.

The Brusilov offensive, launched on June 4, 1916, became the main event instead. Brusilov attacked along a wide front and surprised Austrian forces near Lutsk, Tarnopol, Buchach, and Czernowitz. The offensive caused severe Austro-Hungarian losses and briefly revived Allied hopes in Russian power. Yet it also consumed Russia's remaining strength and contributed to the weakening of the imperial regime.

Romania entered the war on the Allied side in August 1916, hoping to gain territory from Austria-Hungary. The Central Powers responded quickly. Falkenhayn attacked from Transylvania, while Mackensen advanced from Bulgaria through the Dobruja. Bucharest fell on December 6, 1916. The Central Powers gained access to Romanian wheat and oil, while Russia had to defend a longer front.

## The Western Front, JanuaryMay 1917

The failures of 1916 led to leadership changes. In Great Britain, David Lloyd George replaced H.H. Asquith as prime minister in December 1916. In [[France]], R.-G. Nivelle replaced Joseph Joffre as commander in chief. Nivelle believed a decisive offensive was possible and planned for British attacks to draw German reserves while the French struck in Champagne.

Germany shortened its line by withdrawing to the Hindenburg Line, which disrupted Allied plans and created a stronger defensive position. Nivelle still attacked in April 1917. The offensive failed to deliver the promised breakthrough and caused heavy casualties. The disappointment helped produce serious mutinies in the French Army. Petain replaced Nivelle and restored discipline by improving conditions and avoiding reckless attacks while keeping the army in the war.

## Caporetto

On the Italian front, Luigi Cadorna's offensives on the Isonzo had strained Austria-Hungary but won little. The 11th Battle of the Isonzo in 1917 gained ground on the Bainsizza Plateau and threatened Austrian endurance. Ludendorff therefore sent German divisions to help Austria-Hungary strike back.

The Battle of Caporetto began on October 24, 1917. Otto von Below's German 14th Army, with Austrian units, attacked through the Julian Alps and surprised the Italians. Italian forces retreated in disorder; Udine fell, and the attackers reached the Tagliamento. Cadorna saved part of his army by retreating to the Piave River, but Italy suffered about 500,000 casualties and 250,000 prisoners. Armando Diaz replaced Cadorna and stabilized the front.

## The Western Front, JuneDecember 1917

After Nivelle's failure, Petain kept the French mostly defensive, giving Haig the chance to launch a British offensive in Flanders. The attack on Messines Ridge on June 7, 1917, led by Herbert Plumer, succeeded partly because 19 large mines exploded beneath the German lines.

Haig then pushed into the Third Battle of Ypres, also known as Passchendaele. The offensive began on July 31, 1917. Heavy bombardment, German pillboxes, and rain turned the battlefield into mud. The British made limited gains at great cost and finally took Passchendaele in November. The battle became a symbol of the terrible price of attrition.

## The Far East

[[China]] entered the war in 1917 on the Allied side, mainly because the [[Peking (Beijing)|Peking]] government feared that [[Japan]] would otherwise dominate Allied and American sympathy in later Far Eastern settlements. China broke relations with [[Germany]] in March and declared war on [[Germany]] and Austria-Hungary on August 14, 1917.

China's direct military contribution was small, but its entry showed how the war had become global. The decision was tied less to battlefield grievances than to the diplomatic balance in East Asia and the future settlement after the war.

## Peace moves, March 1917September 1918

By 1917, war-weariness had become widespread. Food shortages, army mutinies, and endless casualty lists made peace efforts more popular. In Austria-Hungary, Emperor Charles I and Ottokar Czernin tried to open peace channels, but these efforts failed. In [[Germany]], Matthias Erzberger proposed renouncing annexations, and the Reichstag passed a peace resolution on July 19, 1917, but it avoided clear commitments.

Pope Benedict XV issued a peace note on August 1, 1917, calling for German withdrawal from Belgium and France, Allied withdrawal from German colonies, and restoration of several occupied states. France and Britain waited for Germany's position on Belgium, but Germany avoided a firm answer.

Woodrow Wilson made American peace aims central through the Fourteen Points ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fourteen_Points)) of January 8, 1918, followed by later statements of principles. The Fourteen Points called for open diplomacy, freedom of the seas, freer trade, arms reduction, fair colonial settlements, evacuation of Russian and Belgian territory, restoration of France and Alsace-Lorraine, ethnic adjustment of Italy's frontiers, autonomy for Austria-Hungary's peoples, restoration of Balkan states, autonomy for non-Turkish Ottoman peoples, an independent Poland with sea access, and a general association of nations. These ideals became important when Germany later sought peace.

## The Western Front, MarchSeptember 1918

After [[Russia]] left the war, [[Germany]] transferred divisions west. Ludendorff planned to strike before large American forces arrived. German strength on the Western Front rose sharply, and the new offensive used brief intense bombardments, poison gas, smoke, creeping barrages, and storm troops trained to find weak points rather than attack every strong position.

The German spring offensives began on March 21, 1918. They broke into British-held sectors and created deep salients, but they did not destroy the Allied armies or seize decisive objectives. The danger led the Allies to appoint Ferdinand Foch as general coordinator and then generalissimo. American troops were still few in March, but by September about 1,200,000 were in France under John J. Pershing.

The Allied counterstroke began in July against the German bulge near the Marne. French, American, and other Allied forces forced a German retreat, helped by light tanks. On August 8, 1918, the British 4th Army, with Australian and Canadian forces and 450 tanks, attacked near Amiens. The German line collapsed in places, and Ludendorff called August 8 the black day of the German Army. From then on, the Allies held the initiative.

## Czechs, Yugoslavs, and Poles

National movements gained strength as the war weakened empires. The Czechs and Slovaks, the South Slavs or Yugoslavs, and the Poles sought new or restored states. In Bohemia and Moravia, Czechs lived under the Austrian half of the Habsburg monarchy, while Slovaks lived under the Hungarian half. South Slavs lived in Serbia and Montenegro but also under Habsburg rule in Bosnia, Hercegovina, Dalmatia, Croatia, Istria, and Illyria. Poland was divided among [[Germany]], Austria, and [[Russia]].

Tomas Masaryk and Edvard Benes organized a Czechoslovak National Council in 1916. The western Allies supported the Czechoslovak idea more strongly from 1917, partly because Russia's weakening made any pressure on Austria-Hungary valuable. The Yugoslav Committee worked for South Slav unity, while the Polish cause gained from the Bolshevik Revolution ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/October_Revolution)) and Wilson's principles. These movements helped shape the postwar map of eastern and central Europe.

## Vittorio Veneto

After the Italian front stabilized on the Piave River, Austria-Hungary tried a new offensive in June 1918 but lost heavily. Armando Diaz waited until Italy was ready for a more secure attack. The Italian offensive opened on October 24, 1918, the anniversary of Caporetto.

The Battle of Vittorio Veneto began with hard fighting near Monte Grappa and along the flooded Piave. Italian and British forces gained a foothold on the left bank at Papadopoli Island on October 27. Austrian units were already mutinying, and the high command ordered retreat on October 28. Vittorio Veneto fell the next day, and Austria-Hungary sought an armistice on November 3, 1918.

The collapse of Austria-Hungary followed quickly. National councils and separatist governments appeared, and Charles, the last Habsburg ruler, renounced participation in Austrian affairs on November 11 and Hungarian affairs on November 13.

## The Armistice

The final Allied offensive in the west combined American, French, British, Belgian, and other forces. Pershing's Americans attacked through the Argonne Forest from September 26, 1918, while British forces attacked the Hindenburg Line and Belgian-led forces advanced from Ypres. The goal was to threaten German railways, retreat routes, and the new defensive line Germany hoped to form.

Germany asked for peace while revolution spread at home. The armistice terms presented at Rethondes required Germany to evacuate Belgium, France, Alsace-Lorraine, and the left bank of the Rhine; neutralize the right bank; withdraw in eastern Europe; annul Brest-Litovsk and Bucharest; repatriate prisoners; and surrender large quantities of weapons, aircraft, locomotives, and railroad cars. The Allied blockade would continue.

The German delegation gained small mitigations, partly by warning about Bolshevism, but the combination of military defeat and revolution left little room to resist. The armistice took effect on November 11, 1918. World War I ended with the Central Powers defeated, the old imperial order shattered, and Europe left unstable.

## Key Events & Developments

- June 28, 1914: Gavrilo Princip assassinated Francis Ferdinand and Sophie in Sarajevo.
- 1914: The Schlieffen Plan ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Schlieffen_Plan)) brought Germany through [[Belgium]] and helped create the Western Front deadlock.
- 1915: The Gallipoli campaign, gas at Ypres, and the Gorlice-Tarnow offensive widened the war and showed the power of attrition.
- 1916: Verdun, the Somme, Jutland, and the Brusilov offensive marked the war's peak of mass endurance.
- 1917: The [[United States of America (United States)|United States]] entered the war, [[Russia]] entered revolution, and the French Army suffered mutinies after Nivelle's failure.
- 1918: German spring offensives failed, Allied counteroffensives succeeded, Austria-Hungary collapsed, and the Armistice ended the war.
