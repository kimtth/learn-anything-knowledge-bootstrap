# Circus

## Introduction

The circus is an entertainment form built around trained animal acts, acrobatics, equestrian feats, clowns, and other displays of skill and daring, usually performed in a ring. The circus has taken many forms, from one-ring family shows to large traveling enterprises and permanent urban theatres.

The circus also includes parades, menageries, sideshows, pantomime, and theatrical elements. Its history is tied to travel, spectacle, and the public appetite for extraordinary performance.

Circus elements trace back to ancient Rome, medieval fairs, wandering performers, and early modern horse shows. The modern circus emerged in the 18th century and expanded in the 19th through the tent, the menagerie, the clown, and the Wild West show.

Ancient Roman circuses differed from the modern circus. Roman circuses were arenas for chariot races, gladiatorial combats, animal slaughter, and mass spectacle, while the modern circus is organized around skilled performance in a ring. Still, Roman spectacles contributed traditions of trained animals and processional display.

Many other circus arts are much older than the modern institution. Acrobatics, balancing, juggling, ropedancing, clowning, and animal performance appeared in ancient Egypt, Greece, China, Africa, and medieval Europe. For centuries these acts were performed by wandering individuals or small troupes at courts, fairs, markets, and festivals rather than in a unified circus program.

## 19th-century development

Major American and European developments included the rise of traveling menageries, the circus tent, wild animal acts, and sideshows. Barnum, Bailey, Ringling, Buffalo Bill, and other impresarios helped create the circus as a modern commercial form.

The menagerie was especially important in America. Elephants such as Old Bet helped make exotic animals a central attraction, and traveling collections of wild animals became linked with circus entertainment. The big top, introduced in the early 19th century, allowed shows to travel to audiences rather than remain tied to permanent buildings.

The sideshow rose especially in the United States. Sideshows displayed human anomalies, illusions, magicians, inventions, and curiosities, with talkers or spielers drawing crowds through ballyhoo. Changing attitudes in the 20th century contributed to their decline.

## 20th-century circus

The 20th century saw the decline of some older forms and the rise of new performance styles, especially in equestrian acts, high-wire work, and modern clowning. Families such as the Wallendas and Schumanns grew important, as did circus companies in Europe, America, and the Soviet sphere.

The Ringling brothers eventually combined their circus with Barnum & Bailey, creating the dominant American circus enterprise. In Europe, by contrast, many circuses remained family-based and often split into related companies rather than forming a monopoly. Economic depression, war, travel restrictions, and changing transport all affected circus survival between the world wars.

Acts of skill deserve special attention. The Wallendas set standards for high-wire pyramids, Lillian Leitzel became famous for aerial rope work, Con Colleano thrilled audiences on the tight wire, and Antoinette Concello became the first woman to perform the triple somersault on the trapeze.

Modern clowning also changed. Emmett Kelly and Otto Griebling popularized the sad tramp figure, while Oleg Popov created a Russian streetwise clown character. Later New Vaudeville performers such as Bill Irwin and David Shiner used less makeup and built more direct relationships with audiences.

## Conclusion

The circus is a global performance tradition that combines virtuosity, popular entertainment, and commercial showmanship, while continually adapting to new technologies and audiences.

The circus is also a changing social mirror. Its history includes wonder, skill, travel, animal display, commercial promotion, popular fantasy, and changing ethical standards. The form survived because it could absorb fairs, theatre, sport, vaudeville, spectacle, and later modern performance styles while keeping the recognizable image of the ring.
