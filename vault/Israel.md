# Israel

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> Israel is a Middle Eastern state whose land, population, economy, and institutions developed through Zionism, the establishment of statehood in 1948, successive wars, and contested relations with Palestinians and neighboring states. Its political life combines Jewish national identity, democratic institutions, regional conflict, and peace diplomacy.

## Introduction

Israel ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Israel)), officially the State of Israel, is a small Middle Eastern country at the eastern end of the Mediterranean Sea. It borders Lebanon, Syria, Jordan, Egypt, the Mediterranean, and the Red Sea outlet at Elat. Jerusalem is the seat of government and the proclaimed capital, though that status has not received broad international recognition.

Modern Israel is the only Jewish nation-state of the modern period, but its territory belongs to a region with ancient, Roman, Byzantine, Islamic, Crusader, Ottoman, British Mandate, Jewish, and Palestinian Arab histories. Its establishment followed Zionist immigration, British rule, conflict between Jews and Arabs, the Holocaust, the United Nations partition plan, and Israel's declaration of statehood in 1948.

## Land and Relief

![[attachments/israel_004.webp|420]]

*Aerial view of the Temple Mount in Jerusalem*

Israel's topography is unusually varied for a country only about 290 miles north to south and 85 miles wide at its broadest point. Four major regions shape the land: the Mediterranean coastal plain, northern and central highlands, the Great Rift Valley, and the Negev desert.

The coastal plain stretches along the Mediterranean, widening toward the south and containing beaches, fertile farmland, Tel Aviv, Haifa, and expanding suburbs. Inland, the Galilee mountains form the highest northern zone, reaching Mount Meron at 3,963 feet. The Plain of Esdraelon links the coastal plain with the Great Rift Valley, while Mount Carmel projects toward Haifa.

The Great Rift Valley runs along the eastern side of the country. The Jordan River flows from the northern frontier through the Hula Valley and Lake Tiberias, also called the Sea of Galilee, then south along the West Bank into the Dead Sea, the lowest natural landscape feature on Earth's surface. Farther south, the Arava Valley extends toward Elat.

The Negev forms the southern half of Israel. It is sparsely populated, arid, and arrow-shaped, narrowing toward the Red Sea. Its flat sandy spaces give way southward to sandstone hills, wadis, canyons, cliffs, and desert landscapes.

## Water, Soils, Climate, and Nature

![[attachments/israel_005.webp|420]]

*The Dead Sea shoreline*

Water scarcity is a chronic national constraint. Lake Tiberias, the Jordan, the Yarqon, the Qishon, the Yarmuk, wadis, springs, and underground water tables are all heavily used. About three-fourths of available water has gone to irrigation, with the rest serving industry and households.

Water policy links geography to state-building. Irrigation, drainage, pipelines, reservoirs, groundwater pumping, desalination, wastewater reuse, and disputes over shared river basins all shape agriculture, urban growth, industry, and diplomacy. Because settlement and farming expanded in a dry environment, water became not just a natural resource but a strategic system connecting the coast, Galilee, the Jordan valley, the Negev, neighbouring states, and Palestinian territories.

[[Soils]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soil)) range from alluvial coastal soils to loess in the northern Negev, limestone formations in Galilee, rock salt and gypsum in the rift, and sandstone with granite veins in the southern Negev. These variations mirror the country's sharp environmental contrasts.

[[Climate and Weather (Climate)|Climate]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Climate)) has two main seasons: a cool rainy winter from October to April and a hot dry summer from May to September. Rainfall is high in Upper Galilee but extremely light south of the Dead Sea. Mediterranean coastlands are milder and more humid, while Elat and the Negev are hotter and drier.

Natural vegetation is diverse, with more than 2,800 plant species identified. Ancient forests were greatly reduced by timber cutting, cultivation, and grazing, leaving secondary growth and varied habitats across coast, mountains, rift, and desert.

## Peoples and Religions

![[attachments/israel_003.webp|420]]

*Flag-of-Israel-4-Zachi-Evenor*

Israel's population is socially and religiously diverse. Jewish immigration since the late 19th century brought communities from Europe, the Middle East, North Africa, Central Asia, North America, Latin America, Ethiopia, and the former Soviet Union. Hebrew revival and Israeli national consciousness helped integrate newcomers while preserving many ethnic traditions.

Jewish society includes Ashkenazic Jews from central and eastern European backgrounds and Sephardic Jews from Mediterranean and North African backgrounds, among many other communities. Religious Jews remain influential and often clash politically with secular Israelis over the role of religious authorities in public life.

Karaites maintain a separate Jewish identity based on the Torah as the sole source of religious law. Samaritans, a very small ancient community, live mainly near Holon and Mount Gerizim, preserve their own religious organization, and pray in an archaic form of Hebrew.

Arabs are Israel's largest minority. Most are Sunni Muslims, while Arab Christians are especially important in Galilee, and many Arabs speak Levantine Arabic and learn Modern Standard Arabic in school. Israeli Arabs are citizens with political and civil rights, though suspicion, inequality, and Palestinian identity continue to shape relations with the Jewish majority.

Muslim, Christian, and Druze communities have recognized religious autonomy in personal-status matters. Muslims have their own courts for marriage, divorce, and inheritance. Christian denominations include Greek Catholic, Greek Orthodox, Roman Catholic, Uniate, Russian Orthodox, Evangelical, Episcopal, and Lutheran communities. The Druze, concentrated in Galilee and near Mount Carmel, have been recognized as a separate Arab community since 1957, and Israeli Druze serve in the armed forces.

Other communities include the Bahai world centre on Mount Carmel in Haifa and Circassian Sunni Muslims who emigrated from the Caucasus in the 1870s. Circassians preserve their language and traditions, and their men serve in the Israeli military.

## Economy and Resources

Israel's economy developed despite limited raw materials, water scarcity, a small domestic market, and restricted regional trade. Early society emphasized agriculture, irrigation, kibbutzim, and moshavim. Later growth shifted strongly toward high technology, software, electronics, communications, weapons, medical equipment, chemicals, and advanced manufacturing.

Mineral resources include potash, bromine, and magnesium from the Dead Sea; copper in the Arava; phosphates and gypsum in the Negev; marble in Galilee; small oil deposits; and natural gas reserves in the northern Negev and offshore Mediterranean. Energy has depended heavily on imported fuel and thermal stations.

Agriculture ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agriculture)) expanded through irrigation, mechanization, and rural organization. Citrus, vegetables, flowers, peanuts, sugar beets, cotton, and dairy production became important, though water limits forced emphasis on higher yields rather than endless expansion.

Manufacturing and trade became increasingly export-oriented. Israel's diamond-cutting and polishing industry centred in Tel Aviv has been globally significant. Free trade agreements with the European Union and the United States, membership in the World Trade Organization, and scientific innovation helped Israel overcome lack of access to many Middle Eastern markets.

Tourism ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tourism)) draws on religious, archaeological, historical, climatic, and geographic attractions, including Jerusalem, the Western Wall, the Dome of the Rock, Nazareth, Bethlehem, coastal resorts, highlands, and desert settings. Regional conflict has repeatedly affected tourism's growth.

## Transportation, Government, and Justice

[[Transportation]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transport)) relies heavily on roads, buses, taxis, sheruts, shipping, and aviation. Haifa, Ashdod, and Elat are modern deepwater ports. Ben Gurion International Airport at Lod is the main international airport, and EL AL is the national carrier.

Israel's government is a democratic parliamentary republic without a single formal written constitution. Basic Laws, legislation, executive orders, and parliamentary practice shape the system. The Knesset has 120 members, the prime minister forms the cabinet, and the president serves mainly ceremonial functions while appointing key officials.

The judiciary is highly independent. Israeli law draws on Ottoman and British precedent, religious court decisions, and parliamentary enactments. Religious courts handle marriage and divorce for recognized communities, and capital punishment has been retained only for genocide and Nazi-era crimes.

Security institutions include the police, Border Guard, prison system, Civil Guard, and Israel Defense Forces. The IDF developed around the need to defend a small state against numerically larger and geographically advantaged neighbouring forces.

## Education, Culture, and Media

[[Education]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Education)) is compulsory and free for children ages 5 to 15 and free for ages 16 and 17. Families may choose secular state, religious state, or private religious schools; Arab students have schools using Arabic as the main language of instruction. Adult education helps immigrants integrate.

Higher education includes the Hebrew University of Jerusalem, the Technion-Israel Institute of Technology, the Weizmann Institute of Science, Tel Aviv University, Haifa University, Bar-Ilan University, Ben-Gurion University of the Negev, and the Open University of Israel.

Cultural life ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Culture)) includes libraries, museums, theatres, concert halls, archaeological institutes, film, newspapers, radio, and television in Hebrew, Arabic, English, Russian, Yiddish, Ladino, and other languages. Habima, founded in Moscow in 1917 and moved to Palestine in 1931, became Israel's national theatre.

[[Sports]] include football, basketball, cycling, windsurfing, scuba diving, the Maccabiah Games, and Olympic participation. Israel's Olympic history includes the murder of 11 Israeli team members by Palestinian terrorists at the Munich Games in 1972.

## Zionism and Statehood

Modern Zionism drew from religious memory of the land of Israel and political responses to European anti-Semitism. By the 1800s, fewer than 25,000 Jews lived in the ancient homeland, mostly around Jerusalem. In the 1880s, new Jewish agricultural colonies began in Ottoman Palestine.

Theodor Herzl turned Zionism into a modern political movement after the Dreyfus affair, and the First Zionist Congress met in 1897. Chaim Weizmann later sought British support, leading to the 1917 Balfour Declaration and the League of Nations Mandate for Palestine.

British Mandate Palestine saw growing Jewish immigration, Arab opposition, Zionist institutions, underground militias, and political factions. The Haganah, Palmach, Irgun, and Lehi later supplied figures such as Yigal Allon, Moshe Dayan, Yitzhak Rabin, Menachem Begin, and Yitzhak Shamir.

World War II and the Holocaust intensified the Zionist struggle for immigration and statehood. Britain's restrictions kept many Jews from entering Palestine even as Nazi persecution escalated. By the end of the war, the Jewish population of Palestine had risen to about 600,000, while the Arab population had also grown sharply.

The 1948 war followed partition, statehood, and invasion by neighbouring Arab forces. Israel survived, unified its command under David Ben-Gurion, and secured armistices by 1949. The war was devastating: more than 6,000 Israelis were killed, and Palestinian refugee displacement became a central unresolved issue.

## Wars and Territorial Change

The Suez War of 1956 grew from Egyptian leader Gamal Abdel Nasser's arms deal with Czechoslovakia, blockade of the Strait of Tiran, and nationalization of the Suez Canal. Israel joined a secret arrangement with Britain and France, captured Sinai under Moshe Dayan's command, and then withdrew under pressure from the United States and other powers.

The Six-Day War began in 1967 after tensions with Syria, Egyptian mobilization in Sinai, withdrawal of UN forces, blockade of Tiran, and Jordan's entry into the Arab coalition. Israel destroyed the Egyptian air force in a preemptive strike and captured Sinai, the West Bank, Gaza Strip, Golan Heights, and East Jerusalem.

The consequences of 1967 were profound. Israel gained strategic depth and access to the Old City of Jerusalem and the Western Wall, but it also took control over more than one million Palestinians. UN Security Council Resolution 242 called for withdrawal from occupied territories and recognition of every state's right to secure and recognized borders.

The Yom Kippur War began on October 6, 1973, when Egyptian and Syrian forces surprised Israel on the Suez Canal and Golan Heights. Israeli forces recovered with U.S. resupply, crossed the Suez Canal under Ariel Sharon, and stopped Syrian advances, while U.S.-Soviet crisis diplomacy helped impose a cease-fire.

## Peace Diplomacy and Palestinian Relations

Camp David in 1978 brought Anwar el-Sadat, Menachem Begin, and U.S. President Jimmy Carter together. The resulting accords led to the 1979 Egypt-Israel peace treaty, phased Israeli withdrawal from Sinai, full diplomatic relations, and large U.S. aid, while Palestinian autonomy remained unresolved.

The Palestinian issue became central after 1967. Israel refused for years to negotiate with the Palestine Liberation Organization, led by Yasir Arafat, which Israel treated as a terrorist organization. Palestinian resistance shifted through Jordan, Lebanon, the occupied territories, and the intifadah.

The Gulf War and Soviet collapse opened new diplomacy. After Iraq's invasion of Kuwait in 1990, the United States urged Israel not to respond to Iraqi missile attacks. Iraq's defeat and changing global politics enabled the Madrid Conference of 1991, the first direct official talks between Israel and neighbouring Arab parties since the Camp David period.

The Oslo Accords emerged from secret Norwegian talks. In 1993, Israel and the PLO signed the Declaration of Principles on Palestinian Self-Rule. The PLO recognized Israel, renounced terrorism, and agreed to revise charter provisions calling for Israel's destruction. Israel recognized the PLO as the representative of the Palestinian people.

The Cairo Agreement of 1994 implemented limited Palestinian self-rule in Gaza and Jericho, with later elections and final-status talks to cover borders, refugees, Jerusalem, and settlements. The process struggled with violence, Israeli politics, Palestinian divisions, and mutual distrust.

The Wye River Memorandum of 1998 linked phased Israeli withdrawals to Palestinian security measures and expanded the United States' monitoring role. Israeli coalition politics, recession, unemployment, and disputes over implementation weakened the agreement.

Later developments included Ariel Sharon's disengagement plan, Israel's 2005 withdrawal from Gaza, Hamas's victory in Palestinian elections in 2006, Hamas control of Gaza in 2007, blockade, rocket attacks, Israeli strikes, cease-fires, and Palestinian attempts at United Nations recognition in 2011 and 2012.

## Key Events & Developments

- 1897 First Zionist Congress: Political Zionism organized internationally.
- 1917 Balfour Declaration: Britain endorsed a Jewish national home in Palestine.
- 1948 Statehood: Israel declared independence and entered war with neighbouring Arab forces.
- 1949 Armistices: Israel ended the first Arab-Israeli war controlling more territory than the partition plan.
- 1956 Suez War: Israel, Britain, and France acted against Egypt after the Suez Canal crisis.
- 1967 Six-Day War: Israel captured Sinai, the West Bank, Gaza, East Jerusalem, and the Golan Heights.
- 1973 Yom Kippur War: Egypt and Syria launched a surprise attack on Israel.
- 1978 Camp David Accords: Egypt and Israel moved toward peace.
- 1979 Egypt-Israel treaty: Israel agreed to withdraw from Sinai.
- 1991 Madrid Conference: Direct Arab-Israeli talks resumed.
- 1993 Oslo Accords: Israel and the PLO recognized each other and began Palestinian self-rule arrangements.
- 2005 Gaza disengagement: Israel withdrew soldiers and settlers from Gaza.
