# Euripides

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> Euripides was a classical Athenian tragedian whose plays challenged inherited views of religion, war, heroism, and family duty. His psychologically searching dramas made him one of the enduring figures of Greek theatre.

## Introduction

Euripides, born about 484 BC in Athens and dead in 406 BC in Macedonia, was the last of classical Athens's three great tragic dramatists, after [[Aeschylus]] and [[Sophocles]]. Little is certain about his life. He first competed in 455 BC, won first prize in 441 BC, and received first prize only four times while alive and once after death.

Euripides owned a large library, served on a diplomatic mission to Syracuse, and was associated with thinkers such as Protagoras and Anaxagoras. His interest in new ideas appears in plays that question traditional religion, heroic values, war, and family duty. He left Athens in 408 BC for the court of Archelaus of Macedonia. Ancient readers knew 92 plays by him, and 19 survive.

## Alcestis

Alcestis ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alcestis_%28play%29)), produced in 438 BC, is tragic in form but ends happily. King Admetus may avoid death if someone dies for him. His wife Alcestis accepts the sacrifice, but Heracles later rescues her from Death and restores her to Admetus.

## Medea

Medea ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medea_%28play%29)), produced in 431 BC, studies a betrayed woman and her terrible revenge. Jason abandons Medea in Corinth to marry the local princess. Medea struggles between injury and love for her children, then kills the princess and her own sons to leave Jason ruined.

## Hippolytus

Hippolytus, produced in 428 BC, shows divine vengeance and human shame. Aphrodite punishes Hippolytus, who rejects sexual passion and honours Artemis. She makes Phaedra desire him; after he rejects her, she falsely accuses him and dies. Theseus curses Hippolytus, who dies after Artemis reveals his innocence.

## Andromache

Andromache, produced about 426 BC, is set after the Trojan War. It begins with strong anti-Spartan feeling and uses Andromache to show the misery left by Troy’s defeat, though the play loses focus as many early characters disappear.

## Hecuba

Hecuba, produced about 425 BC, turns grief into revenge. The aged Trojan queen Hecuba loses her daughter Polyxena, sacrificed to Achilles' ghost, and finds her son Polydorus murdered by Polymestor. She and her women blind Polymestor and kill his sons.

## Suppliants

Suppliants, produced about 423 BC, concerns the mothers of Argive leaders killed at Thebes. Their sons are left unburied, and they persuade [[Athens]] to recover the bodies. The play may praise Athenian democracy, though its tone is debated.

## Electra

Electra, produced about 418 BC, retells the murder of Clytemnestra by Electra and Orestes in revenge for Agamemnon. Euripides makes the revenge bitter and antiheroic: Electra is resentful, Orestes is reluctant, and both are consumed by remorse.

## Trojan Women

Trojan Women ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Trojan_Women)), produced in 415 BC, is a powerful indictment of war. After Troy falls, Cassandra is taken by Agamemnon, Andromache becomes a slave, and her son Astyanax is killed. The play’s bleak mood reflects Athenian violence against Melos shortly before its production.

## Ion

Ion, produced about 413 BC, is a tragicomedy solved by recognition. Creusa, once raped by Apollo, unknowingly meets her abandoned son Ion at Delphi. She nearly poisons him as a rival heir before a cradle reveals their relationship.

## Iphigenia Among the Taurians

Iphigenia Among the Taurians, produced about 413 BC, combines recognition with escape. Iphigenia, saved by Artemis from sacrifice, serves at Tauris, where strangers are killed. She recognizes her brother Orestes among the victims and escapes with him through Athena’s help.

## Helen

Helen, produced in 412 BC, overturns the usual Trojan myth. Only a phantom Helen went to Troy, while the real Helen remained faithful in Egypt. Menelaus discovers the truth after shipwreck, and the couple escape the Egyptian king.

## Phoenician Women

Phoenician Women, produced about 409 BC, is a crowded Theban play about the sons of Oedipus. Eteocles and Polyneices fight for power and kill each other, showing civil conflict inside a cursed family.

## Orestes

Orestes, produced in 408 BC, places matricide in a world with law courts. Orestes, Electra, and Pylades are condemned for killing Clytemnestra. Menelaus fails to help, violence spreads, and only Apollo restores order by divine intervention.

## Bacchants

Bacchants, produced after Euripides' death around 406 BC, is often judged his masterpiece. Dionysus comes to Thebes to establish his worship, but Pentheus rejects him. Dionysus drives Pentheus mad, and Pentheus is torn apart by his mother Agave and other women in a bacchic frenzy.

## Cyclops

Cyclops ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cyclops_%28play%29)) is the only complete surviving satyr play. Silenus and the satyrs serve the man-eating Polyphemus in Sicily until Odysseus arrives and blinds the Cyclops, allowing the victims to escape.

## Key Events & Developments

- 455 BC: Euripides first competed in the dramatic festival.
- 441 BC: Euripides won his first victory.
- 408 BC: Euripides left Athens for Macedonia.
- 406 BC: Euripides died, and Bacchants was produced after his death.

## Key People

- Euripides ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euripides)): Athenian tragedian who questioned myth, religion, war, and heroic values.
- Medea ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medea)): Heroine whose revenge exposes betrayal and women’s oppression.
- Dionysus ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dionysus)): God whose worship and power dominate Bacchants.
- Orestes ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orestes)): Mythic avenger whom Euripides repeatedly treats with moral unease.

## Interesting Facts

- Euripides ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euripides)) wrote about 92 plays, but only 19 survive.
- Cyclops ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cyclops_%28play%29)) is the only complete surviving satyr play.
- Alcestis ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alcestis_%28play%29)) replaced the normal satyr play even though it has tragic form.
- Trojan Women ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Trojan_Women)) was staged soon after Athens destroyed Melos.
