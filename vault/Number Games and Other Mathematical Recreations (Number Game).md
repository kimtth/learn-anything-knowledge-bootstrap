# Number Games and Other Mathematical Recreations (Number Game)

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> Mathematical recreations use puzzles and games to explore arithmetic, algebra, geometry, number theory, combinatorics, logic, probability, and other fields. Their history shows how popular amusements, paradoxes, number curiosities, and computational problems can lead into serious mathematical investigation.

## Introduction

Number games and mathematical recreations ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Recreational_mathematics)) are puzzles and games involving mathematics, from simple amusements to unsolved sophisticated problems. They may involve arithmetic, algebra, geometry, number theory, graph theory, topology, matrices, group theory, combinatorics, set theory, symbolic logic, or probability. The boundary between pastime and serious mathematics is often unclear because a simple puzzle may lead to graph theory, combinatorics, transformation geometry, or logic.

Mathematical recreations have broad popular appeal because people enjoy solving puzzles. G.H. Hardy noted that professional puzzle makers exploit the public's intellectual pleasure in such activities. Recreational mathematics literature grew especially after the beginning of the 20th century, often repeating old problems in new forms while continually producing fresh variations.

## History

People have long created mathematical puzzles ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematical_puzzle)) for challenge and amusement. Some survive from Greeks and Romans, while medieval interest increased through printing, arithmetic texts, and rivalry among algebraists. Early contributors included Rabbi ben Ezra, Fibonacci, Robert Recorde, and Girolamo Cardano. Problems often involved either manipulating objects, such as decanting and river crossings, or performing computations.

## Early Computational Problems

Old number puzzles ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematical_puzzle)) included finding a number, God-Greet-You problems, chessboard doubling, lion-in-the-well problems, and courier problems. The chessboard problem asks how many grains of wheat are needed when squares double from 1 to 2 to 4 across 64 squares. Such problems trained arithmetic reasoning while presenting it as entertainment.

## Pioneers and Imitators

The 17th century produced books devoted to recreational mathematics. Claude-Gaspar Bachet de Meziriac published Problemes plaisans et delectables in 1612 and edited Diophantus in 1621. Jean Leurechon, Claude Mydorge, Denis Henrion, Daniel Schwenter, Mario Bettini, Johann Mohr, and William Leybourn expanded the genre through arithmetic, geometry, mechanics, optics, astronomy, cryptography, and natural philosophy.

## 18th, 19th, and 20th Centuries

The recreational tradition continued through Jacques Ozanam, whose 1694 work was revised and translated for more than 200 years. The late 19th century brought major figures such as Edouard Lucas and C.L. Dodgson, or Lewis Carroll. Lucas's Recreations mathematiques became a classic, while Dodgson wrote Symbolic Logic, The Game of Logic, Pillow Problems, and A Tangled Tale.

## Number Curiosities and Multigrades

Multigrades are identities in which two sets of numbers have equal sums, equal sums of squares, or equal sums of higher powers. Simple equalities can be shifted and recombined to generate higher-order multigrades, including palindromic or prime-number examples. Other curiosities include narcissistic numbers, perfect digital invariants, automorphic numbers whose squares end in the original number, and strobogrammatic numbers that read the same after rotation.

## Digital Problems

The four n's problem asks for consecutive integers expressed using exactly four copies of a given digit and allowed operations. Four 4s is the famous example, where rules about factorials, decimals, roots, or elementary algebra change how many numbers can be represented. Related puzzles ask for values using digits 1, 2, 3, 4 or arranging digits 1 through 9 to make 100.

## Cryptarithms

Cryptarithms are arithmetic puzzles in which letters or symbols replace digits. The term crypt-arithmetic appeared in 1931 in the Belgian journal Sphinx. Solving them involves column arithmetic, digit constraints, carries, and logical elimination. Alphametics are cryptarithms whose letters form meaningful words; by convention, initial letters cannot be zero and different letters must represent different digits.

## Paradoxes and Fallacies

Mathematical paradoxes are valid conclusions that seem hard to accept, while fallacies are invalid arguments that produce false or absurd results. Sophisms deliberately hide errors, and howlers accidentally reach correct results by wrong reasoning. Many paradoxes involve infinity, such as infinite series that approach a limit or sets where even numbers can be put in one-to-one correspondence with all natural numbers.

## Polygonal and Figurate Numbers

Figurate numbers arrange points into geometric patterns. Triangular numbers, square numbers, oblong numbers, and gnomons fascinated Pythagoreans, who treated numbers as having shapes and personalities. Adjacent triangular numbers sum to square numbers, oblong numbers double triangular numbers, and odd-number gnomons build successive squares. These patterns connect ancient visual arithmetic with modern number theory.

## Pythagorean Triples

Pythagorean triples are positive integers satisfying a squared plus b squared equals c squared. Primitive triples have no common factor. The classic 3, 4, 5 is the only primitive triple with consecutive integers. Properties include divisibility by 3, 4, 5, 7, and 60 in various combinations, and a complete formula generates all primitive triples.

## Perfect and Mersenne Numbers

Perfect numbers equal the sum of their proper divisors, such as 6 and 28. Euclid showed that numbers of the form involving 2^n - 1 are perfect when that factor is prime, and Leonhard Euler proved every even perfect number has this form. Numbers 2^n - 1 are called Mersenne numbers after Marin Mersenne.

## Fibonacci and Lucas Numbers

Fibonacci's Liber abaci introduced the rabbit problem that generates the Fibonacci sequence, in which each term equals the sum of the two before it. The golden number and golden section connect Fibonacci numbers to proportions, rectangles, logarithmic spirals, and phyllotaxis in plants. Edouard Lucas's related sequence follows the same recurrence.

Geometric recreations include optical illusions, geometric fallacies, impossible figures, strange loops, pathological curves, mazes, dissections, graphs, flexagons, sliding puzzles, and the Tower of Hanoi. These problems use perspective, topology, graph theory, limiting processes, combinatorics, and spatial reasoning.

## Impossible Figures and Pathological Curves

Impossible figures look plausible at first but cannot exist in three dimensions. L.S. Penrose and Roger Penrose introduced strange loops such as the endless stair, later important in M.C. Escher's art. Pathological curves include von Koch's snowflake, space-filling curves, and the Sierpinski curve; Benoit Mandelbrot's fractal dimension generalized their irregular self-similarity.

## Mazes, Dissections, and Graphs

Mazes can be solved by wall-following if suitably connected, or by node-and-path algorithms in more complex cases. Geometric dissections cut one figure into pieces that form another, and equal-area polygons are equidecomposable. Graph theory studies vertices and edges, including complete, planar, and nonplanar graphs, the three-wells problem, paths, networks, and electrical-network design.

## Core Equations

Number games use symbols, sets, functions, and logical relations to structure puzzles and strategies:

$$f:X\to Y$$

This notation represents a function that maps each input from one set to an output in another set.

$$A\subseteq B \quad \Rightarrow \quad (x\in A \Rightarrow x\in B)$$

This states the meaning of subset: every member of A is also a member of B.

## Key Events & Developments

- 1202: Fibonacci published Liber abaci.
- 1612: Bachet published a major recreational number-problem collection.
- 1694: Ozanam's recreations began a long publishing tradition.
- 1882-1894: Edouard Lucas's four-volume work became a classic.
- 1931: The term crypt-arithmetic appeared.
- 1958: The Penroses introduced strange-loop impossible figures.

## Interesting Facts

- 153 is a narcissistic number because it equals 1 cubed plus 5 cubed plus 3 cubed.
- 69 and 96 are strobogrammatic under 180-degree rotation.
- Von Koch's snowflake has infinite perimeter but finite area.
- The three-wells problem is impossible because of planar graph constraints.
