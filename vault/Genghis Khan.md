# Genghis Khan

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> Genghis Khan, born Temujin, united Mongol tribes into a disciplined military state and then led conquests across much of Asia. His transformation of steppe political organization, military practice, literacy, and imperial rule had lasting consequences for the peoples and states his armies encountered.

## Introduction

Genghis Khan, born Temujin in 1162 near Lake Baikal and died on August 18, 1227, was the Mongolian warrior-ruler who consolidated the nomadic tribes into a unified [[Mongolia]] and then extended his empire across Asia from the Pacific coast of China to the Adriatic Sea. Rising from obscure beginnings, he brought all the Mongol tribes under his family in a rigidly disciplined military state before turning to conquer the settled peoples beyond the steppe. Near-contemporary information about him comes mainly from the Mongol Secret History of the Mongols ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Secret_History_of_the_Mongols)) and from horrified non-Mongol sources, since chroniclers dwelt above all on the destruction of the Mongol ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mongols)) invasions.

The year 1206 was the turning point, when the Mongols were first ready to move beyond the steppe and a unified Mongol nation emerged as the personal creation of Genghis Khan, sweeping away the old clan and tribal quarrels. The new nation was organized above all for war, and Genghis Khan came only gradually, through contact with settled peoples, to see that there were more sophisticated ways of holding power than raiding and plunder. A minister of the defeated Naiman taught him the uses of literacy and helped reduce the Mongol language to writing.

## The Mongol Army

Genghis Khan's troops were divided on the decimal system, rigidly disciplined, well supplied, and led by his own sons or by men chosen for their absolute loyalty. His military genius adapted quickly: at first his army was purely cavalry, mounted on the hardy grass-fed Mongol pony, which could beat other nomads but not take cities. Before long the Mongols mastered siege warfare, using mangonels, catapults, ladders, and burning oil, and even diverting rivers to break walled towns.

## Assessment

Genghis Khan's personality was complex. He had great physical strength, tenacity, and an unbreakable will, yet he listened to advice, valued loyalty, and was religiously minded, worshipping the Eternal Blue Heaven in moments of crisis. Above all he was adaptable, a man who could learn, and he could win the loyalty of both fellow nomads and civilized men—his fame even drew the aged Daoist sage Changchun across Asia to discuss religion. His successes rested on organization, discipline, mobility, and ruthlessness, and he made regular use of massacre and terror, summoning cities to surrender and methodically slaughtering those that refused in a deliberate form of psychological warfare.
