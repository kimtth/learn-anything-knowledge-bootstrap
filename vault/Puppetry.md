# Puppetry

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> Puppetry is theatrical performance created by live human manipulation of figures, from hand and rod puppets to marionettes and shadow forms. The source follows its ritual, folk, courtly, educational, and experimental traditions across cultures while emphasizing animation, performance, storytelling, and stylized character.

## Introduction

Puppetry ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Puppetry)) is the making and manipulation of puppets for theatrical performance. A puppet is a human, animal, or abstract figure moved by human aid rather than by machinery. This definition includes many forms of theatre but excludes dolls used only in play and automatons moved by clockwork.

A doll becomes part of puppet theatre only when it is made to act before an audience. Automaton figures, such as those on clocks at Strasbourg or Munich, are not puppets because they move mechanically rather than through a performer. The essential element is live manipulation by a human artist.

Puppet shows appear in almost every civilization and period. European written records go back to the 5th century BC, including Xenophon's Symposium. Ancient traditions also exist in [[China]], India, Java, and other parts of Asia, while American Indian ritual figures and African mask traditions show related forms where puppet, mask, and performer can overlap.

Puppet theatre ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Puppetry)) probably predates written drama and even writing. It grows from a basic human instinct to animate objects, give them voice, and use them in ritual, storytelling, comedy, satire, or religious drama. Its long history includes folk booths, court entertainments, shadow plays, marionette theatres, artistic experiments, and modern state-supported stages.

## Types of Puppets

![[attachments/puppetry_001.webp|420]]

*Wayang puppet performance with puppeteer and musicians*

Hand puppets, or glove puppets, have a hollow cloth body that fits over the performer's hand. The fingers move the head and arms, and the figure is usually seen from the waist upward. Heads may be made of wood, papier-mache, or rubber, and hands may be wood or felt.

The common Punch-type arrangement places the first finger in the head and the thumb and second finger in the arms. This allows the puppet to grasp props and wield a stick, but it can make one arm sit higher than the other. Hand puppets are quick, agile, portable, and suited to a single performer in a booth, though their small size limits broad arm gestures.

Rod puppets are full-length figures manipulated from below. A main rod supports the body and head, while thinner rods move hands or legs. They are traditional in Java and Bali as wayang golek, and they later became important in European and American experimental and educational puppet theatres.

Rod puppets can create slow, dignified, expressive drama. The hand-rod puppet lets the performer's hand enter the body and hold a short rod to the head, while the arms are moved by rods. This permits bending at the waist, but rod puppets require many manipulators, sometimes two or three for one figure.

Marionettes, or string puppets, are full-length figures controlled from above by strings or threads attached to a control held by the manipulator. They can show walking, dancing, and full-body movement more completely than glove puppets. Their strength is grace and illusion, while their weakness is the difficulty of precise and forceful action.

Other major forms include shadow puppets, which are seen as silhouettes against a lit screen, and large figures or masks that approach puppet theatre because they transform the performer into an animated image. These forms show that puppetry is not defined by one mechanism but by the theatrical animation of a figure.

## Richard Teschner and Rod-Puppet Experiment

Richard Teschner in Vienna explored the artistic possibilities of rod puppets in the early 20th century. His work showed that rod figures could support serious visual theatre rather than only folk entertainment. Similar experiments by Nina Efimova in Moscow helped inspire later Soviet development.

The State Central Puppet Theatre in Moscow, directed by Sergey Obraztsov, developed rod puppets strongly during the 1930s. After [[World Wars (World War II)|World War II]], its tours, especially in eastern Europe, encouraged the creation of state-supported puppet theatres using rod figures. In the United States, Marjorie Batchelder helped expand rod-puppet use in schools and colleges.

## Puppet Theatre Traditions

Puppet theatre ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Puppetry)) has always moved between popular entertainment and artistic drama. In Europe, many traditional folk heroes are glove puppets. Their booths are portable, their shows can be performed by one person, and their comedy often uses fast quarrels, beatings, tricks, and loud voices.

Asian puppet traditions often draw on sacred and heroic stories. In southern India and Indonesia, the Ramayana and Mahabharata provide major plots. In Java and Bali, puppet theatre connects storytelling, music, ritual, and local performance convention.

Puppetry ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Puppetry)) also developed sophisticated elite forms. Court theatres, literary marionette stages, and modern experimental theatres used puppets for visual beauty, symbolic action, satire, and stylized movement. The puppet can represent a god, a hero, a fool, a demon, an animal, or an abstract idea without needing natural human realism.

## Punch-and-Judy

Punch-and-Judy is one of the best-known European glove-puppet traditions. Punch descends from the Italian Pulcinella, but local comic heroes developed across Europe around the time of the French Revolution. France had Guignol, Germany had Kasperl, the Netherlands had Jan Klaassen, and Spain had Christovita.

These figures often use a squeaker in the performer's mouth, producing a sharp, unhuman voice. They share fights, tricks, and comic violence, but they are not the same character. Each belongs to a national or local tradition with its own audience, jokes, language, and moral style.

In Greece, the comic hero Kararkiozis is a shadow puppet related to the Turkish Karagoz. This shows how puppet figures travel across borders and change identities. A character may keep a recognizable comic function while becoming part of a different language, religion, and social world.

## Repertory and Literary Sources

Puppet drama draws from religion ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Religion)), folk tales, heroic sagas, popular novels, and theatre. The Theatre Toone in Brussels still presents a Passion of Christ play. German puppet theatre used the Faust legend, French puppet theatre used the Temptation of St. Anthony, and Sicilian and Liege theatres used chivalric themes from Ariosto.

In the 19th century, traveling marionette theatres in England and the United States performed popular plays such as East Lynne and Uncle Tom's Cabin. In Asia, religious and legendary sources remained central, especially the Hindu epics. The repertoire therefore ranges from sacred drama to melodrama, comedy, satire, and heroic adventure.

The puppet is especially suited to stylization. It can exaggerate movement, reduce character to a powerful type, or perform impossible actions without breaking theatrical belief. Because the puppet is not a living actor, audiences accept symbolic violence, supernatural events, and rapid transformations more easily.

## Key Events & Developments

- 5th century BC: European written evidence for puppet shows appears, including references connected with Xenophon.
- The late 18th century: Local European comic heroes such as Guignol, Kasperl, and Jan Klaassen spread beyond the older Pulcinella pattern.
- 1914: Richard Teschner's rod-puppet work, including figures such as Faun and Nymph, showed the artistic reach of the form.
- The 1930s: Sergey Obraztsov's Moscow theatre helped make rod puppets central to large state-supported puppet stages.
- After World War II: Eastern European tours by the Moscow theatre encouraged new rod-puppet theatres.

## Interesting Facts

- A puppet is defined by human manipulation, so a clockwork automaton is excluded even if it looks theatrical.
- Glove puppets are quick and portable, while rod puppets are expressive but often need several manipulators.
- Punch, Guignol, Kasperl, and similar figures are related in comic function but are distinct national types.
- Shadow puppetry connects Greek, Turkish, Indian, Indonesian, and other traditions through silhouette performance.
