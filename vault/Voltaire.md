## Introduction

Voltaire was the pseudonym of Francois-Marie Arouet, born in [[Paris]] on November 21, 1694 and dead there on May 30, 1778. He was one of the greatest French writers and became famous worldwide as a crusader against tyranny, bigotry, and cruelty. His wit, satire, and critical intelligence promoted an ideal of progress that remained influential across nations.

His long life stretched from the last years of French classicism to the eve of the revolutionary era. He helped shape European civilization through plays, poems, histories, philosophical tales, polemics, and public campaigns. Although much of his vast output is no longer read, his contes and letters remain central monuments of French literature.

## Heritage and Youth

Voltaire came from a middle-class background. He lost his mother young, disliked his father Francois Arouet and brother Armand, and attached himself to his freethinking godfather, the Abbe de Chateauneuf. At the Jesuit college of Louis-le-Grand, he learned literature, theatre, and classical taste, but religious instruction strengthened his skepticism.

He witnessed the troubled last years of Louis XIV, including military disasters and religious persecution. He admired enlightened royal power as a possible agent of progress, but he rejected the study of law and chose literature. His wit made him famous in Parisian society after Louis XIV's death, but mocking the regent, the Duc d'Orleans, led to banishment and nearly a year in the Bastille in 1717.

In 1718, after the success of his tragedy Oedipe ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oedipus)), he adopted the name Voltaire. He wanted to become the great epic poet France lacked and wrote the Henriade, inspired by Henry IV and the ideal of religious tolerance. Early literary success brought him court approval and a pension.

## England and the Philosophical Turn

Voltaire's quarrel with the Chevalier de Rohan changed his life. After being beaten, imprisoned, and sent from France in 1726, he spent more than two years in England. There he learned English, met writers such as Alexander Pope, Jonathan Swift, and William Congreve, and studied the thought of John Locke, Isaac Newton, and English religious dissenters such as the Quakers.

England convinced him that liberty of discussion, commerce, and scientific inquiry could strengthen a nation. He admired English institutions while disliking partisan violence. He also discovered Shakespearean theatre, whose energy affected him even when he considered it barbarous by French classical standards.

After returning to France around 1728-29, he presented England as a model. His Lettres philosophiques ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Letters_on_the_English)) of 1734 praised religious toleration, Locke's empiricism, Newtonian science, commerce, English politics, and Shakespeare. The book attacked French religious and political orthodoxy and helped define the direction of the Enlightenment ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Age_of_Enlightenment)).

## Cirey, History, and Science

The scandal over Lettres philosophiques ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Letters_on_the_English)) forced Voltaire to take refuge at Cirey with Emilie du Chatelet. Their life together was luxurious, scholarly, and intellectually intense. She drew him toward science and metaphysics, while he popularized Newtonian physics in Elements de la philosophie de Newton in 1738.

At Cirey, Voltaire expanded his historical work. His Histoire de Charles XII contrasted destructive military glory with the civilizing achievements of Peter the Great. He later developed Le Siecle de Louis XIV, the Essai sur les moeurs, and histories of Russia and Louis XV. He wanted history to include arts, sciences, manners, and civilization, not only battles and rulers.

His plays also continued. Zaire was a major success; Alzire contrasted humanitarian civilization with brute conquest; Mahomet was banned after success because of its dangerous religious implications. Court favour came and went, and his relationship with Mme du Chatelet remained the deepest intellectual partnership of his life until her death in childbirth in 1749.

## Tales, Exile, and Ferney

After disappointments in theatre and court life, Voltaire found a form suited to his ironic intelligence: the philosophical tale. Zadig, Micromegas, Memnon, and related stories used fantasy and satire to question providence, human pride, and philosophical optimism. In 1758, he wrote Candide, his most famous work, in which disasters undermine the claim that this is the best of all possible worlds and end in the practical maxim of cultivating one's garden.

Voltaire accepted the invitation of Frederick II of Prussia in 1750, but the Berlin period collapsed after quarrels, especially with Pierre-Louis de Maupertuis. He left Prussia in 1753, was detained at Frankfurt, and could not return freely to Paris. He later settled near [[Geneva]], then bought Ferney on the French-Swiss border in 1758.

At Ferney he became patriarch, estate builder, host, correspondent, and activist. He promoted agriculture, local industry, workers' interests, and freer trade around Gex and Geneva. Visitors such as James Boswell, Giacomo Casanova, Edward Gibbon, and leading philosophes came to him, while he corresponded with rulers including Frederick II and Catherine II.

His central enemy was l'infame, by which he meant organized religious intolerance, especially when joined to judicial cruelty and state power. He defended simple theism and civil authority against ecclesiastical oppression. His interventions in the cases of Jean Calas and the Chevalier de La Barre made him a public champion of tolerance, legal reform, and opposition to torture and fanaticism.

## Final Years and Assessment

Voltaire returned triumphantly to [[Paris]] in 1778 after 28 years away. Crowds and institutions celebrated him; at a performance of Irene, he was crowned in his box. The excitement damaged his health, and he died on May 30, 1778. His body was quickly buried at the Abbey of Scellieres, and his remains were transferred to the Pantheon during the Revolution in July 1791.

Voltaire's reputation has always provoked strong reactions. His enemies blamed him for the Revolution, while later anticlerical, liberal, middle-class, and scientific readers reclaimed him. His epic poems, much verse, and most plays faded, but his tales, letters, and polemical legacy endured.

His lasting lesson was clarity joined to generosity. He taught readers to think sharply, resist cruelty, value toleration, and distrust empty metaphysics. His mind was skeptical and combative, but also committed to human progress.

## Key Events & Developments

- 1694: Voltaire was born in [[Paris]].
- 1717: He was imprisoned in the Bastille after mocking the regent.
- 1718: Oedipe ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oedipus)) succeeded, and he adopted the name Voltaire.
- 1726-28: Exile in England reshaped his views on liberty, science, and toleration.
- 1734: Lettres philosophiques ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Letters_on_the_English)) provoked scandal in France.
- 1738: Elements de la philosophie de Newton popularized Newtonian science.
- 1749: Emilie du Chatelet died, deeply affecting Voltaire.
- 1758: He wrote Candide and acquired Ferney.
- 1762: The execution of Jean Calas led Voltaire into a famous campaign for judicial redress.
- 1778: He returned to Paris, was publicly celebrated, and died.
- 1791: His remains were transferred to the Pantheon.

## Key People

- Voltaire ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voltaire)): French writer, philosophe, satirist, historian, and campaigner for tolerance.
- Emilie du Chatelet ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emilie_du_Chatelet)): Scientist, translator, and Voltaire's intellectual partner at Cirey.
- John Locke ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Locke)): English philosopher whose empiricism influenced Voltaire.
- Isaac Newton ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isaac_Newton)): Scientist whose physics Voltaire helped popularize in France.
- Frederick II: Prussian ruler, correspondent, patron, and later adversary of Voltaire.
- [[Jean-Jacques Rousseau]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jean-Jacques_Rousseau)): Philosopher whose views helped mark a break with Voltaire's rationalist Enlightenment.
- Jean Calas ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jean_Calas)): Protestant victim of judicial and religious persecution whose case Voltaire championed.
- Chevalier de La Barre ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chevalier_de_La_Barre)): Young man executed for religious offenses, becoming another symbol of intolerance.
- Gustave Lanson ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gustave_Lanson)): Scholar who later helped renew serious study of Voltaire.
