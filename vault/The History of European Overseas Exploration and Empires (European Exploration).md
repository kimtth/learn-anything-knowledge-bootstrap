European exploration ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Age_of_Discovery)) is the investigation of regions of Earth by Europeans for scientific, commercial, religious, military, political, and strategic purposes, especially from the 15th century onward.

Motives for exploration included curiosity, trade, religion, security, political power, and imperial competition. Different motives could operate at the same time: rulers, merchants, missionaries, sailors, soldiers, and scholars often wanted different things from the same voyage.

The history of exploration has three broad phases: exploration of the Old World ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_World)) around the Mediterranean and Asia; the Age of Discovery, when Europeans sought sea routes to Cathay and found the Americas; and the mapping of the modern world through relationships between Old and New Worlds and exploration of continental interiors.

## Old World Exploration

Early Western geographical knowledge expanded from the river-valley worlds of Babylonia and Egypt to the Mediterranean world of Greece and Rome, whose hinterlands stretched from the Sahara to the Gobi and from the Atlantic to the Indian Ocean.

Trade across Asia, Africa, and Europe encouraged early exploration. Coastlines of the [[Indian Ocean]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_Ocean)), Red Sea, Persian Gulf, and surrounding regions became known through merchants, sailors, conquerors, and state-sponsored expeditions.

Herodotus recorded a story that Pharaoh Necho II sent Phoenicians around Africa, returning through the Pillars of Hercules after three years. Herodotus doubted some details, such as the sun appearing on the sailors' right, but accepted the possibility of Africa being surrounded by sea.

About 510 BCE, Darius the Great sent Scylax of Caria to explore the Indus. Scylax travelled to the Kabul River, reached the Indus, followed it to the sea, sailed west, explored the Red Sea, and reached Arsinoe near modern Suez.

[[Alexander the Great]]'s campaigns were also military explorations. His armies passed through Babylonia, Persia, Central Asia, the Hindu Kush, and regions new to Greek geography.

## Marco Polo and Asian Geography

Marco Polo's travels to China from 1271 to 1295 deeply affected European imagination. Travelling with his father and uncle, he crossed Persia, the Pamirs, the Tarim Basin, and the Gobi to reach Cambaluc, the Mongol capital.

Marco Polo served the Great Khan, travelled through Chinese provinces, ports, and remote regions, and described roads, postal stations, cities, markets, administration, and wealth. His account made Cathay, Quinsay, and Cipango vivid to European readers.

His book promised that Asia held enormous commercial reward. Columbus owned and annotated a Latin edition, and later explorers tried to identify new discoveries with places Marco Polo described.

Together, Ptolemy and Marco Polo shaped the Age of Discovery: Ptolemy suggested the way west to Asia might be short, while Polo suggested the reward would be great.

## The Age of Discovery

From the mid-15th to mid-16th century, Europeans sought new sea routes rather than new lands. Mongol fragmentation made overland travel less secure, Ottoman power blocked old eastern outlets, and Atlantic nations were ready for overseas trade and adventure.

Henry the Navigator of Portugal began the search for a sea route east by sailing south along Africa. His motives included curiosity, navigation, ship design, crusading hopes, and diverting gold and ivory trade from Sahara routes to Portugal.

Portuguese expeditions explored the African coast. Dinis Dias reached the Senegal in 1445, Alvise Ca' da Mosto reached Gambia and the Cape Verde Islands in 1455-56, and later sailors pushed farther south toward the Cape of Good Hope.

The Portuguese route around Africa eventually connected Europe to the [[Indian Ocean]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_Ocean)), while Spanish voyages sought a westward route to Asia. Columbus's voyages revealed lands Europeans gradually recognized as a New World.

## Magellan and Circumnavigation

![[attachments/the_history_of_european_overseas_exploration_and_empires_european_exploration_002.webp|420]]

*Early map of the Strait of Magellan from 1520*

Ferdinand Magellan, familiar with the eastern route to Asia, believed a shorter western route existed. In 1519 he sailed from Sevilla with five ships under Spanish sponsorship.

Magellan crossed to Brazil, explored the Rio de la Plata, moved down Patagonia, wintered at Port Julian, and on October 21, 1520, found the strait now bearing his name.

The Strait of Magellan was long, tortuous, deep, rock-walled, foggy, and stormy. Three ships passed through its 325-mile length and emerged into an ocean Magellan named the Pacific.

The Pacific proved vast. For 14 weeks the ships crossed without land, short of food and water, before reaching Guam and then the Philippines, where Magellan was killed in a local conflict.

Survivors reached the Moluccas. One ship, the Vittoria, under Juan Sebastian del Cano, crossed the Indian Ocean, rounded the Cape of Good Hope, and reached Sevilla in 1522 with 17 European survivors and spice cargo that paid for the expedition.

The voyage destroyed two illusions: that the American barrier had an easy passage and that Asia lay near once it was crossed. It also completed the first circumnavigation of the globe.

## Africa

African exploration turned on river systems. Europeans debated the direction and outlet of the Niger, wondering whether it connected to the Senegal, Gambia, Congo, or Nile.

Mungo Park, sent by the African Association, reached the Niger in 1796 and saw it flowing east. On a later attempt to follow it to the sea, he drowned near Bussa.

In 1830, Richard Lander travelled from the Bight of Benin to Bussa and navigated the Niger to its mouth, showing that it drained through the oil rivers into the Gulf of Guinea.

David Livingstone explored southern and central Africa, crossing the Kalahari, finding Lake Ngami, reaching the Zambezi, crossing from the Zambezi basin to Luanda, returning to the falls he named Victoria Falls, and following the river to the east coast.

Livingstone's later journeys explored the Shire, Rovuma, and Lake Nyasa, combining missionary aims, anti-slavery concerns, geographical curiosity, and imperial interest.

## Australia

The central question in Australia was whether the interior contained an inland sea or a desert. Early settlers on the east coast struggled against the barrier of the Blue Mountains.

In 1813, Gregory Blaxland crossed the Blue Mountains by following ridges rather than valleys. Beyond the range, rivers flowed in puzzling ways, encouraging inland-sea theories.

Charles Sturt resolved the question in 1829-30 by travelling the Murrumbidgee into the Murray and identifying the Darling's relation to it. He found that the Murray drained to Encounter Bay and that Australia's interior was not an inland sea but a vast desert.

Edward John Eyre crossed along the south coast from Adelaide to Albany in 1840-41, and Robert Burke and William John Wills crossed from Melbourne toward the Gulf of Carpentaria, revealing Australia's scale and harsh interior.

## Polar Regions

Polar exploration belonged mainly to the first half of the 20th century. Scientific curiosity was a major motive, though national rivalry and prestige also mattered.

Arctic and Antarctic expeditions tested [[Navigation|navigation]], survival, mapping, meteorology, magnetism, geology, and human endurance. They extended the modern map into the last great blank areas of European geographical knowledge.

## Interesting Facts

- Magellan named the Pacific because it seemed calm after the dangerous strait.
- Vittoria's spice cargo paid for Magellan's entire expedition despite huge loss of life.
- Marco Polo's book helped persuade explorers that Asia's wealth justified dangerous voyages.
- [[Australia]]'s interior was long imagined as an inland sea before explorers showed it was largely desert.
- [[Exploration]] often mixed curiosity with trade, religion, war, and imperial ambition.
