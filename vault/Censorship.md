# Censorship

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> Censorship is the suppression or regulation of expression in the name of public order, morality, religion, or political authority. Its history spans ancient civic and Confucian controls, Christian orthodoxy and printing, and modern arguments over press freedom, individuality, and self-government.

## Introduction

Censorship is the changing, suppression, or prohibition of speech or writing judged to be subversive of the common good. The English term traces to the office of censor established in [[Rome]] in 443 BCE, an officer who conducted the census and regulated the morals of the citizens. Once honourable, censorship is now generally treated as a relic of a more oppressive age.

The central concern is not only what is suppressed, but why societies think suppression is justified. The discussion begins with Protagoras (c. 490–420 BCE), whose admission of agnosticism in Concerning the Gods scandalized his fellow Greeks and would have drawn social or criminal sanctions across the ancient world. The modern shift—from "the law forbids whatever it does not permit" to "one may do whatever is not forbidden"—reflects what Leo Strauss called the quarrel between the ancients and the moderns over the status of individuality. The contemporary repudiation of censorship rests on the dignity of the individual and the requirements of self-government.

## Ancient Greece and Rome

In the classical world, citizens were formed in accordance with the character and needs of the regime. The contrast runs between tough, uncommunicative Sparta and pleasure-loving Corinth, while strong-minded individuals still emerge in [[Homer]], Plutarch, and Tacitus. Each city-state promoted its own worship: respecting the city's gods was a privilege of citizenship, and refusing to conform outwardly brought legal hardship.

The force of religious opinion appears in prosecutions for impiety and in cities refusing public business when the religious calendar or auspices forbade it. The secrecy of the mysteries was so complete that no record survives of their content, and a poem by Archilochus (7th century BCE) provoked outrage in Sparta for violating the proprieties.

## Biblical and Christian foundations

For pre-Christian writers, saying everything one thought was potentially licentious, and restraint was a social duty. Christian writers reversed this, demanding the saying of everything as the indispensable witness of faith. Scriptural authority—"the truth will make you free" (John 8:32)—became central to later arguments against censorship, while encouraging an individuality that eventually turned against organized religion itself.

## Ancient China

China was a civilization whose education system culminated in rigorous selection of administrators by examination, producing very strong controls over thought. The teaching of [[Confucius and Confucianism (Confucianism)|Confucius]] (551–479 BCE) emphasized deference to authority and elders, caution in speech, and respect for ritual; it was contrary to good taste to speak openly of a government's faults. The wholesale destruction of books in 231 BCE showed how far rulers could go in stamping out unwanted doctrine.

The Confucian mode revived afterward to dominate Chinese life for almost two millennia, with character shaped by mastering orthodox texts and control of information lodged in the authorities. Yet even [[Confucius and Confucianism (Confucianism)|Confucius]] warned that "oppressive government is fiercer than a tiger" and that a country is ruined when people are discouraged from opposing a ruler's bad words (Analects 13:5).

Christian Europe developed its own major censorship systems, above all the Index Librorum Prohibitorum of the Roman Catholic Church, whose origins reach back to the 5th century CE and which kept official sanction into the 20th century. The most spectacular silencing was the restriction placed on [[Galileo]] in 1633. The spread of [[Printing, Typography, and Photoengraving (Printing)|printing]] made dissent far easier to circulate, sharpening the church's defense of canonical texts.

A precise orthodoxy led to creeds: the Nicene Creed (325 CE) was devised to fend off heresy but, after a unilateral Western change in wording, contributed to the 1054 schism between Eastern Orthodoxy and Roman Catholicism. The persecutions of witches, ranging across Europe from the 14th to the 18th century, showed how hard it could be to separate religious from nonreligious motives.

## England and the United States

![[attachments/censorship_003.webp|420]]

*United States Bill of Rights manuscript page*

The English tradition developed a strong doctrine against prior restraint: Blackstone's Commentaries defined liberty of the press as laying no previous restraints on publication, while leaving authors answerable for improper or illegal matter after the fact. The First Amendment, ratified in 1791, barred Congress from abridging freedom of speech or of the press, modeling free speech on British parliamentary privilege. The case of John Peter Zenger (1735) had already established that truth was a defense in seditious-libel prosecutions.

Strict limits followed. The Sedition Act of 1798 lapsed after two years and was later repudiated; Thomas Jefferson's First Inaugural (1801) urged that error of opinion be tolerated where reason is free to combat it. In Schenck v. United States (1919), Oliver Wendell Holmes Jr. articulated the "clear and present danger" test; Fred M. Vinson extended it in Dennis v. United States (1951), upholding Communist Party convictions.

## Character and freedom

The wider issue extends beyond legal doctrine: modern free-speech arguments largely derive from [[John Stuart Mill]]'s On Liberty (1859). Jamie Kalven observed that being badly edited is as close as most American writers come to censorship, while Mill himself recognized that a people must be trained properly to use the liberty he advocated.

Censorship thus depends on the kind of citizens a society wants to form and the conditions under which liberty can be sustained. Tocqueville, in Democracy in America (1835–40), warned that "nothing is harder than freedom's apprenticeship," and Laurence Berns reframed the dilemma of the trial of [[Socrates]]: whether philosophy is inherently subversive, or whether a good society is impossible without freedom to philosophize.

## Conclusion

Censorship cannot be understood as a simple yes-or-no policy. It is tied to religion, politics, education, public morality, and the balance between collective order and individual freedom.

## Key Events & Developments

- 443 BCE: The Roman censor office gives the word its English root.
- Classical antiquity: City-states regulate speech and worship.
- 231 BCE: Qin book destruction becomes a major symbol of suppression.
- 325 CE onward: Christian orthodoxy is formalized in creeds and later the Index.
- 1633: Galileo is restricted by the Catholic Church.
- 1791: The U.S. First Amendment protects speech and press.
- 20th century: Modern free-speech doctrine develops through major court cases.
