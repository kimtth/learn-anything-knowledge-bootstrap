# Insects

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> Insects are the largest class of arthropods, recognized by a segmented body with head, thorax, abdomen, jointed legs, exoskeleton, antennae, and often wings. Their small size, rapid reproduction, diverse diets, and adaptability make them dominant across land and freshwater habitats and extraordinarily species-rich.

## Introduction

Insects ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Insect)) are members of the largest class in the phylum Arthropoda. They have segmented bodies, jointed legs, and external skeletons. Adults are distinguished by a three-part body: a head with mouthparts, eyes, and one pair of antennae; a three-segmented thorax usually bearing three pairs of legs and one or two pairs of wings; and a segmented abdomen containing digestive, excretory, and reproductive organs.

Insect success is extraordinary. About 1 million species have been described, roughly three-fourths of all described animal species, and entomologists estimate many more remain unknown. Insects dominate land habitats because they are small, reproduce rapidly, exploit many foods, and adapt to nearly every land and freshwater environment where food exists.

Popular attention often focuses on pests and disease carriers such as bedbugs, houseflies, moths, Japanese beetles, aphids, mosquitoes, fleas, horseflies, and hornets. Yet many insects pollinate plants, produce useful materials, control other insects, decompose organic matter, build soil, feed animals, and serve as models for genetics, physiology, ecology, population biology, and environmental monitoring.

## Diversity and Importance

Insects ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Insect)) range from nearly inert parasites to fast aerial hunters, swift tiger beetles, swimming diving beetles, elaborate nest builders, and social species with colonies of millions. Some adult mayflies live less than two hours, while queen termites may live up to 50 years. Some species show extreme sexual dimorphism, with males and females so different that one sex gives few clues to the other.

Abundance can be immense. Rich moist soil in the north temperate zone may contain 500 to 2,000 insects per square yard, or roughly 4 million insects per moist acre. Insects are especially diverse in the tropics and occupy deserts, jungles, glacial fields, mountain streams, ponds, hot springs, brackish waters, seawater surfaces, and even crude-petroleum pools where some fly larvae eat trapped insects.

Ecological roles include decomposition, carrion breakdown, soil formation, pollination, predation, scavenging, and nutrient cycling. Flesh-fly and blowfly maggots help consume bacteria and carrion, while moths and beetles later break down hair and feathers. Insects and flowers have evolved together, with many flowering plants depending on insects for pollination.

Commercial products include honey, silk, beeswax, lac wax, dyes, pigments, and shellac-like lac products. Cochineal insects on Mexican cacti produce carmine dye used in foods, makeup, drugs, and textiles. Insects are also protein sources for many peoples, including grasshoppers, termite swarms, and palm weevil grubs.

Damage and disease make insect control necessary. Crop pests feed on leaves, stems, fruit, roots, and stored products, or transmit bacterial, viral, and fungal diseases to plants. Locusts may shift from solitary forms to migratory swarms that destroy crops over vast regions. Insects injure humans and livestock through bites, stings, irritation, myiasis, sheep-strike, and vector-borne diseases such as malaria, relapsing fever, leishmaniasis, sleeping sickness, plague, typhus, dengue, yellow fever, and encephalitis.

Biological control can suppress pests by introducing predators, parasites, or pathogens from a pest's original range. The cottony cushion scale threatened California citrus in 1886, but the Australian vedalia beetle rapidly controlled it. In eastern Canada during the early 1940s, a viral disease controlled the European spruce sawfly and encouraged interest in insect pathogens as pest-management tools.

## Development and Reproduction

Most insects begin as fertilized eggs. Eggshells often contain respiratory openings, and some species protect eggs in packets such as cockroach and mantid oothecae. Eggs may survive drought or winter through dormancy or diapause. Larvae hatch by biting, cutting with spines, or forcing open escape caps through pressure from swallowed air, muscles, or expandable head structures.

[[Biological Growth and Development (Growth)|Growth]] occurs by molting because the cuticle cannot expand once formed. New larger cuticles develop beneath old ones, and old cuticles are shed in ecdysis. Primitive wingless insects have little change during growth, while grasshoppers, true bugs, and aphids show simple metamorphosis. Insects with complete metamorphosis have larval, pupal, and adult stages, allowing larvae and adults to evolve different forms and habits.

Adult life is usually oriented toward reproduction. Males may find females by sight, swarming, light signals, songs, flight sounds, or pheromones. Fireflies and glowworms use luminous organs, crickets and grasshoppers chirp, male mosquitoes respond to female wing tones, and silkworm or gypsy moth females release extremely powerful scents. Nutrition, temperature, juvenile hormone, and neurosecretory hormones influence egg production and reproductive diapause.

## Senses and Behaviour

![[attachments/insects_001.webp|420]]

*Variegated grasshopper (Zonocerus variegatus)*

Insect senses are highly developed. Tactile hairs on antennae, palps, legs, tarsi, and body surfaces detect contact and body position. Campaniform organs in the cuticle detect strain, helping wings and halteres control flight and equilibrium. Hearing organs occur on mosquito antennae, cricket legs, grasshopper and moth abdomens, and other structures.

Chemical perception occurs through sensilla used for taste and smell. Honeybees are extremely sensitive to queen substance, while male silkworm moths respond to tiny traces of female pheromone. Vision includes simple eyes and compound eyes; many insects see ultraviolet patterns invisible to humans, and flowers often exploit these patterns to guide pollinators.

Instinctive behaviour ranges from simple reflexes to complex sequences such as courtship, hunting, nesting, provisioning, and social organization. Leaf-cutter bees follow a rigid nest-building sequence using cut leaves, pollen, honey, and eggs. Honeybees show more flexible colony behaviour, learning, task switching, and communication through dances.

## Ecology and Adaptation

Terrestrial insects feed on nearly every kind of organic matter and face hazards from temperature extremes and desiccation. Hot conditions drive insects to shade or positions that reduce exposed surface area. Cool conditions lead butterflies to bask like solar collectors and moths to shiver flight muscles. The optimal muscle temperature for flight is about 38 to 40 C.

Cold hardiness may involve tolerating some ice formation or preventing freezing. Many insects accumulate glycerol as antifreeze and supercool body fluids below the normal freezing point of water. Water conservation depends on waxy cuticle layers, respiratory adaptations, and waste-handling mechanisms.

Defenses include camouflage, disruptive markings, chemical defense, warning coloration, and mimicry. Predators learn to avoid conspicuous distasteful or dangerous insects, while harmless species may gain protection by resembling them. Population regulation depends on competition, weather, food, predators, parasites, disease, migration, and balanced polymorphism.

## Body Structure

The cuticle covers the insect and serves as both waterproof barrier and external skeleton. It contains chitin and sclerotin, with an outer wax layer to reduce evaporation. Soft-bodied larvae may use hydrostatic pressure for support, while hard-bodied insects have sclerites joined by flexible membranes. Cuticle colours may come from pigments or from physical interference in scales and surface structures.

The head consists of fused segments bearing antennae, eyes, and mouthparts. Chewing mouthparts include labrum, mandibles, maxillae, labium, and sometimes hypopharynx. These parts are extensively modified for biting, sucking, piercing, lapping, sponging, or nectar feeding, as seen in horseflies, mosquitoes, houseflies, butterflies, and many other groups.

The thorax has prothorax, mesothorax, and metathorax, each with legs and often with wings on the last two segments. Legs include coxa, trochanter, femur, tibia, and tarsus, and may be modified for running, jumping, burrowing, grasping, swimming, or clinging. Wings are cuticular sacs stiffened by veins carrying tracheae, nerves, and blood; beetle forewings become protective elytra.

The abdomen has up to 11 segments, though fusion often reduces the number. Appendages are usually absent except in caterpillar prolegs and reproductive structures. External genitalia help males clasp females and females manipulate eggs during oviposition. Cerci in crickets and cockroaches carry sensory functions.

Internal systems include digestive, excretory, reproductive, respiratory, circulatory, nervous, and endocrine structures. Insects need water, salts, essential amino acids, and often symbiotic microorganisms when diets are restricted. Mycetocytes can house microorganisms that supply vitamins or nutrients and pass through eggs to the next generation.

Eyes are simple or compound. Ocelli detect light, darkness, and movement crudely. Compound eyes consist of many ommatidia; houseflies have about 4,000 facets, and dragonflies may have up to 28,000. The image is an erect mosaic whose resolution improves with facet number.

## Evolution and Classification

Insect wings probably evolved once among the Pterygota. Fossil insects near the end of the Carboniferous already had fully developed wings. Hypotheses propose origins from thoracic gliding planes or from movable gill-like outgrowths. Paleoptera such as mayflies and dragonflies cannot fold wings flat, while Neoptera generally have a wing-flexing mechanism.

Metamorphosis likely evolved as larvae and adults adopted different ways of life. As larval and adult characters became genetically more independent, each stage could specialize. Extreme differences favoured the evolution of a pupal stage. Insect evolution also paralleled flowering plants, with mutual changes in nectar, pollen, flower colour, scent, and insect mouthparts.

Classification ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taxonomy)) uses head, mouthparts, antennae, thorax, legs, wings, abdomen, genitalia, bristles, sensory organs, venation, metamorphosis, larvae, and pupae. Class Insecta includes wingless Apterygota and winged or secondarily wingless Pterygota. Major groups include mayflies, dragonflies, cockroaches, mantids, grasshoppers, lice, thrips, true bugs, beetles, flies, butterflies, moths, bees, ants, wasps, fleas, caddisflies, and many smaller orders.

## Core Equations

Biological systems are often summarized with growth, heredity, and energy-transfer relations:

$$\frac{dN}{dt}=rN\left(1-\frac{N}{K}\right)$$

The logistic-growth equation models population growth that slows as population size N approaches carrying capacity K.

$$p^2+2pq+q^2=1$$

Hardy-Weinberg proportions describe expected genotype frequencies when allele frequencies are p and q under idealized assumptions.

$$\Delta G=\Delta G^\circ+RT\ln Q$$

This equation adjusts standard free-energy change for actual concentrations or pressures through the reaction quotient.

## Key Events & Developments

- Carboniferous insects: Fossil winged insects appear with wings already fully developed near the end of the Carboniferous.
- Flowering-plant coevolution: Insects and angiosperms evolved pollination, nectar, scent, colour, and mouthpart specializations together.
- California biological control: The vedalia beetle controlled cottony cushion scale after the citrus threat of 1886.
- Sawfly disease control: A viral disease controlled European spruce sawfly in eastern Canada in the early 1940s.
