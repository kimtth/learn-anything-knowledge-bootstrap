# Argentina

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> Argentina is a large South American republic whose Andes, Pampas, Patagonia, river basins, and Atlantic coast support sharply contrasting environments and an agrarian-industrial economy. Independence, federal conflict, European immigration, Peronism, military dictatorship, the Falklands War, and democratic recovery have shaped its modern political and cultural identity.

## Introduction

Argentina ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Argentina)) is a large country in [[South America]] covering most of the southern part of the continent. The world's eighth largest country, it occupies an area more extensive than [[Mexico]] and the U.S. state of Texas combined, encompassing plains, deserts, tundra, forests, tall mountains, rivers, and some 2,900 miles of Atlantic coastline, and it claims part of [[Antarctica]] and the British-ruled Falkland Islands ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Falkland_Islands)) (Islas Malvinas). Its name comes from the Latin word for silver, argentum, though livestock and cereals, not minerals, once made it one of the world's wealthiest nations.

After three centuries of Spanish rule, Argentina ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Argentina)) declared independence in 1816, and its nationalists helped revolutions elsewhere, prompting Jorge Luis Borges to call the continent's independence largely an Argentine enterprise. Torn between centralists in [[Buenos Aires]] and provincial autonomists, the country leaned toward caudillo (strongman) rule, most famously under Juan Peron. A military dictatorship in the 1970s made thousands of dissidents disappear, ending in the disastrous Falklands War of 1982; defeat restored democracy, which has endured despite repeated economic crises.

## Major Regions

Shaped like an inverted triangle, Argentina ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Argentina)) is about 880 miles across at its widest and stretches 2,360 miles from the subtropical north to the subantarctic south, bounded by [[Chile]], [[Bolivia]], [[Paraguay]], [[Brazil]], and [[Uruguay]]. Its geography falls into four major regions: the Andes, the North, the Pampas ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pampas)), and Patagonia ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Patagonia)). The Andes run some 2,300 miles along the western edge and include Aconcagua (22,831 feet), the highest mountain in South America.

The North divides into the dry Gran Chaco ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gran_Chaco)), with some of Latin America's hottest weather, and Mesopotamia ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mesopotamia%2C_Argentina)), the riverine zone between the Parana and [[Uruguay]] rivers. The centrally located Pampas ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pampas)) are grasslands split into the arid Dry Pampa and the more humid eastern Humid Pampa. To the south, Patagonia ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Patagonia)) is a cold, windy region of plateaus and cliffs extending some 1,200 miles from the Colorado River to Tierra del Fuego.

## Drainage, Soils, and Climate

![[attachments/argentina_002.webp|420]]

*Iguazu Falls in Argentina*

Argentina's most important river system is the Paraguay-Parana-Rio de la Plata basin, which drains some 1.6 million square miles across northern Argentina, [[Paraguay]], eastern [[Bolivia]], [[Uruguay]], and much of [[Brazil]]. The Rio de la Plata—the River of Silver—is the estuary formed where the Parana and Uruguay meet, and just above their junction the Iguazu River plunges over the Brazilian escarpment to create the spectacular Iguazu Falls. Lake Mar Chiquita, the country's largest lake, is salty and has no outlet.

Argentina lies almost entirely in the temperate zone of the Southern Hemisphere, unlike the tropics to the north. Conditions range from subtropical heat in Formosa and Misiones to tundra and polar climates in the high Andes and Tierra del Fuego. Rainfall is heaviest in the northeast and lightest in the far northwest and southern Patagonia ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Patagonia)), and violent windstorms called pamperos, bringing thunder and hail, are common. Soils range from saline puna formations to the dark, humus-rich earth of the fertile Pampas.

## Vegetation and Wildlife

![[attachments/argentina_001.webp|420]]

*Location of Argentina on a globe*

Natural environments vary sharply across the country. The northwestern puna carries dwarf shrubs and bunchgrass and is the land of the guanaco and its relatives the llama, alpaca, and vicuna, while forested Andean slopes rise to subtropical rainforest and cloud forest. The Gran Chaco ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gran_Chaco)) holds thorn forest, algaroba (carob), and the tannin-rich quebracho, with rich wildlife including deer, peccaries, jaguars, pumas, armadillos, and the flightless rheas.

In Mesopotamia ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mesopotamia%2C_Argentina)), wax palms, gallery forests, and Parana pines shelter monkeys, tapirs, toucans, and hummingbirds. In the Pampas ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pampas)), crops and vast herds of cattle and horses have replaced much native flora, leaving the prized but commercially useless ombu tree, rheas, hares, and the burrowing viscacha; guanacos vanished from the plains after the Conquest of the Desert. Patagonia ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Patagonia)) ranges from Andean forest to steppe and shrub desert, home to guanacos, eagles, pumas, and the Patagonian cavy.

## People and Ethnic Composition

Heavy immigration, especially from [[Spain]] and [[Italy]], produced a population that is almost entirely of European ancestry. In colonial times the Spanish met the town-dwelling Diaguita of the Northwest, the semiagricultural Guarani of Mesopotamia ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mesopotamia%2C_Argentina)), the Querandi around [[Buenos Aires]], the Tehuelche of Patagonia, and the Ona of Tierra del Fuego; most were conquered, exterminated, or driven away. By 1810 Argentina had more than 400,000 people, perhaps 30 percent of them Indian and 10 percent of African origin.

A great wave of European immigration after the mid-1800s molded the country's modern ethnic character, pushing aside or absorbing the Indians and mestizos and apparently absorbing the black and mulatto populations entirely. The population grew 20-fold from the 1.8 million counted in the first census of 1869, rising rapidly into the early 20th century before birth rates and immigration fell, leaving Argentina with some of South America's lowest growth rates and highly uneven, city-centred settlement.

## Historical Development of Settlement

Different regions were settled in different ways. Spaniards arriving overland from [[Peru]] and [[Bolivia]] made their first permanent settlement at Santiago del Estero in 1553, soon founding San Miguel de Tucuman (1565), Cordoba (1573), and—from Chile—Mendoza and San Juan in the 1560s, chiefly to supply the silver mines of Potosi. Along the rivers of Mesopotamia ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mesopotamia%2C_Argentina)), Corrientes was founded in 1588, and Jesuit missions (reducciones) made northern Mesopotamia, later named Misiones, a great centre of colonization.

The Pampas ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pampas)) developed through cattle ranching and the rise of the estancia, vast tracts worked by half-wild herds and managed by gauchos. In Patagonia ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Patagonia)), European settlement came only after the Conquest of the Desert crushed Indian resistance in 1879, bringing Argentine, Chilean, Welsh, Scottish, and English settlers; Welsh and English are still spoken there, and Ushuaia became the world's southernmost city.

## Economy

Argentina's economy is one of the stronger in the region, now led by services and manufacturing ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manufacturing)), though agribusiness and ranching dominated for much of the 19th and 20th centuries. It still produces more grain than any other Latin American country and is second in cattle only to [[Brazil]]. After the farming colony of Esperanza was founded in 1856, the base of agriculture shifted from livestock to wheat, corn (maize), and flax across the Pampas ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pampas)).

The perfection of refrigeration after 1876 let frigorificos (meat-packing plants) ship fresh beef to Europe, and by the early 20th century Argentina was the world's leading exporter of corn, flax, and meat. The [[Great Depression]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Depression)) of the 1930s cut foreign trade so badly that between 1930 and 1980 the country fell from being one of the world's wealthiest to ranking among less-developed nations, and the late 20th and early 21st centuries brought high inflation, unemployment, and a major financial crisis.

## Society, Education, and Daily Life

Argentina has a highly literate population and an extensive, mostly free education system. The National University of Cordoba, founded in 1613, is the oldest, and the University of Buenos Aires, founded in 1821, is the largest. Public health and social welfare expanded greatly during the first presidency of Juan Peron (1946-55), when a social security system was set up, though housing shortages and shantytowns remained serious in the cities, where average life expectancy now exceeds 75 years.

Daily life in the cities resembles that of southern Europe, with shops closing for a long midday break, suppers taken after 9:00 PM, and lively cafe and nightclub culture. Beef is central: Argentines eat more per person than any nation except [[Uruguay]], usually grilled on the parrilla. The herbal drink mate, sipped from a gourd, and prized red wine from Mendoza round out a diet where New and Old World cultures meet.

## Culture and Identity

Argentine culture is a lively blend of European inheritance and local innovation. Its two most powerful national symbols are the tango, the sensual dance born in the dockside districts of [[Buenos Aires]], and the gaucho, the mestizo ranch hand who holds a place in South American lore much like the cowboy in the United States; once scorned, he became the central emblem of national identity through literature.

Jorge Luis Borges, whose metaphysical stories evoke both the capital's back alleys and the endless Pampas, is the country's best-known literary figure, and [[Buenos Aires]]—often called the Paris of South America—holds the historic Colon Theatre and the National Library, founded in 1810. Sport is central too: football is the most popular game, and Argentina's preeminence in polo reflects its hardy horses and gaucho-trained riders.

## Political History

European explorers revealed Argentina's Atlantic outline in the early 16th century: Juan Diaz de Solis entered the Rio de la Plata in 1516 before being killed, Magellan arrived in 1520, and Sebastian Cabot reached the Parana in 1526 and sent home reports of silver. The independence movement began when British attacks on [[Buenos Aires]] were repelled in 1806-07, and on May 25, 1810, an open cabildo set up an autonomous government; full independence was declared at San Miguel de Tucuman on July 9, 1816.

After independence, conflict between centralists in Buenos Aires and provincial autonomists shaped the new country, and caudillos dominated. Juan Manuel de Rosas built a formidable dictatorship enforced by the secret police known as the Mazorca, and in 1833 Britain seized the Falkland Islands ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Falkland_Islands)). In the 20th century Juan Peron and Peronism became decisive forces, combining nationalism, labour appeal, and mass politics.

Peronism endured because it was more than one leader's rule. It linked industrial workers, unions, social welfare, economic nationalism, charismatic leadership, and the symbolic appeal of Eva Peron. Supporters saw it as social justice and national dignity; opponents saw authoritarianism, patronage, inflationary policy, and attacks on liberal institutions. That double legacy made Peronism a recurring language of Argentine politics, capable of appearing in left, right, labour, populist, and nationalist forms.

## Dictatorship, Falklands War, and Democracy

One of the darkest chapters was the military dictatorship of 1976-83. A junta under Jorge Rafael Videla closed Congress, banned unions, and launched the Dirty War, in which an estimated 10,000 to 30,000 people were imprisoned, tortured, or disappeared. The Mothers of the Plaza de Mayo drew international attention through weekly vigils, and the human-rights activist Adolfo Perez Esquivel won the Nobel Peace Prize in 1980.

Facing a collapsing economy, Leopoldo Galtieri invaded the Falkland Islands ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Falkland_Islands)) in 1982, and defeat by British forces brought down the regime. Raul Alfonsin won the election of October 30, 1983, restored democracy, and prosecuted junta members, five of whom—including Videla—were convicted in 1985. Later governments still struggled with military legacies, inflation, and heavy debt, leaving a country that remains democratic but economically unsettled.

## Conclusion

Argentina ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Argentina)) is a country of large spaces, strong regional contrasts, and a history marked by both promise and crisis. Its wealth in land and resources helped make it one of Latin America's most important states, but recurring political conflict and economic instability repeatedly interrupted that potential.

Even so, Argentina has a distinctive cultural voice and deep historical influence. Geography, immigration, ranching, urban culture, Peronism, dictatorship, and democracy together form one long story of power, identity, and change.
