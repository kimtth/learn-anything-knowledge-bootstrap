## Introduction

Brussels ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brussels)) is the capital of Belgium and the seat of major European Union institutions. It is a bilingual, international city that is both the administrative heart of Belgium and a global centre of diplomacy and business.

The city is also a global meeting point. Its role as host to the EU and NATO gives it a political importance that is unusual for a city of its size, while its status as a financial and commercial centre makes it a practical hub as well as a symbolic one.

## Landscape and City Form

![[attachments/brussels_001.webp|420]]

*Cinquantenaire arch in Brussels*

Brussels lies in a valley on the Senne River and developed around a medieval core surrounded by successive rings of walls and later boulevards. The old lower town contrasts with the more governmental and elite upper town, both of which remain visible in the city’s layout.

Brussels developed from a medieval core and then spread outward through walls, boulevards, and later suburban growth. The original pentagonal form of the old town still influences the modern street pattern, even though the city has spread far beyond it.

The lower town preserves the older commercial core, including the Grand' Place and the streets around the former market area. The upper town, by contrast, became the district of government buildings, palaces, and elite residences, so the city still displays a historic division between trade below and authority above.
Brussels sits within a wider regional landscape. It acts as a transport and economic link between the coastal lowlands, the river valleys, and the inland uplands, which helped the city grow into a regional crossroads.

## Population and Language

The city’s population has grown through waves of migration from Flanders, Wallonia, southern Europe, North Africa, Turkey, and elsewhere in Europe. French became the dominant language over time, but Brussels remains officially bilingual, with English now widely used as well.

The city’s population has grown through repeated migration from Flanders, Wallonia, southern Europe, North Africa, Turkey, and other parts of Europe. This changed not only the size of Brussels but also its social composition, because different migrant groups tended to fill different occupations and neighbourhoods.

French became dominant over time, even though the city was historically Flemish. Social prestige, urban employment, and administrative life helped French become the main public language, while Brussels remained officially bilingual and increasingly international in everyday practice.

English has also become important, especially among European officials, business workers, and educated residents. The result is a city in which language is closely tied to class, migration, and politics, not simply to geography.

## Economy and Transport

Brussels is Belgium’s financial, commercial, and service centre. Government institutions, international organizations, law, finance, insurance, consulting, and real estate are central, while manufacturing has shrunk and the city has become increasingly postindustrial.

The decline of older industry did not reduce Brussels’s importance. Instead, its economy shifted toward administration and high-level services, especially those connected with the EU and the international workforce that the city attracts.

The city is also a transport hub, with dense rail connections, highways, tunnels, and airport access linking it to Belgium and the wider European region. That transport role supports commuter traffic, business travel, and the circulation of goods and people across the metropolis.

## Government and European Role

![[attachments/brussels_002.webp|420]]

*Berlaymont building of the European Commission in Brussels*

Brussels ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brussels)) is the capital of the Brussels-Capital Region and a major site of Belgian federal government. Belgium’s federal structure gives the city a special constitutional position, since it is both a region in its own right and a capital for the Flemish- and French-language communities.

Brussels also hosts the European Commission, the Council of the European Union, committee sessions of the European Parliament, and NATO headquarters. This gives it a role that goes beyond national administration and makes it a key centre for European decision-making, diplomacy, and policy coordination.

At the same time, the city’s nineteen communes keep a large degree of local autonomy. That arrangement helps explain why Brussels is politically important but administratively fragmented.

## Culture and History

Brussels grew from its medieval origins through Burgundian prosperity, Habsburg rule, revolutionary turmoil, Belgian independence, urban modernization, world wars, and the rise of the European Communities. Each phase left a visible mark on the city, especially in the surviving civic architecture, squares, museums, and religious buildings, and on its cosmopolitan identity.

The late medieval and late 19th-century periods were the two great moments of cultural flowering. Art Nouveau architecture, the Royal Palace, La Monnaie, the Palace of Fine Arts, and the city’s major academies and libraries are all signs of a dense cultural life.

Brussels’s history in the 20th century was one of tension and reinvention. War, language conflict, suburbanization, and the growth of European institutions all altered the city, but they also strengthened its role as a cosmopolitan capital.

## Conclusion

Brussels is a city shaped by language politics, international institutions, and urban change. Its significance lies in being both a national capital and a European capital, while also remaining a place where older local identities and newer international ones coexist uneasily.

A deeper point is that Brussels cannot be understood through a single lens. It is at once a medieval market city, a federal capital, an EU command centre, and a multilingual metropolis whose identity keeps changing.
