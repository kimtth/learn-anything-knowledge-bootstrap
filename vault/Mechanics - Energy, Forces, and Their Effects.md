# Mechanics - Energy, Forces, and Their Effects

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> Mechanics studies bodies at rest and in motion under forces, organizing ideas of mass, inertia, equilibrium, gravitation, work, energy, and momentum. Its vector methods and Newtonian laws provide a foundational framework for explaining motion and physical interaction.

## Introduction

![[attachments/mechanics_001.png|420]]

*Pulley force diagram*

Mechanics studies the motion of bodies under forces, including the special case of rest. It first asks what forces bodies exert on one another, then asks how bodies move under those forces. This makes gravitation, electricity, magnetism, equilibrium, and motion part of one connected subject.

## History

![[attachments/mechanics_002.webp|420]]

*Newton cradle*

Classical mechanics grew from the scientific revolution begun by Nicolaus Copernicus in 1543 and completed in major form by Isaac Newton's Principia about 150 years later. Copernicus moved astronomy away from Earth-centered calculation, [[Galileo]] developed inertia and falling-body ideas, and Newton joined celestial and terrestrial motion under universal laws.

## Vectors

![[attachments/mechanics_003.png|420]]

*Mechanics energy diagram*

Vectors describe quantities with both magnitude and direction, such as displacement, velocity, acceleration, force, momentum, and angular momentum. Cartesian coordinates can describe the same motion differently for different observers, but vector equations keep the physical relation independent of the chosen axes.

## Vector Addition

Vector addition ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euclidean_vector)) uses a geometric rule: vectors can be placed head to tail, and the sum runs from the start of the first to the end of the last. Some directed operations, such as finite rotations about different axes, do not behave as true vectors because their order can change the result.

## Scalar Multiplication

A scalar can multiply a vector without changing its direction unless the scalar is negative. A displacement can equal velocity multiplied by time, so a vector quantity and a scalar quantity can combine to produce another vector with appropriate units.

## Dot and Cross Products

The dot product forms a scalar and becomes zero when two vectors are perpendicular. The cross product forms a new vector perpendicular to the original pair, with direction given by the right-hand rule and magnitude based on the sine of the angle between them.

## Derivative of a Vector

The derivative of a vector measures its rate of change in direct analogy with scalar differentiation. Subtracting nearby vector values and dividing by a small time interval gives another vector, which is essential for velocity and acceleration.

## Vectors in Mechanics

Newtonian mechanics uses vectors to connect motion, force, and equilibrium. Forces can change the vector state of a body, while balanced forces allow rest or uniform motion to continue.

## Newton's Laws of Motion

[[Newton]]'s three laws say that bodies keep rest or straight uniform motion unless acted on by force, that change of motion follows the impressed force, and that interactions come in equal and opposite action-reaction pairs. These laws of motion make force the cause of acceleration rather than a requirement for continued motion.

## Force and Mass

The compact form F = ma expresses Newton's second law for constant mass and became one of physics' central equations. Newton's third law prevents an isolated two-body interaction from creating a net force on the whole pair, because each force acts on a different body in an equal and opposite direction.

## The Principle of Inertia

[[Galileo]]'s principle of inertia helped explain why Earth's rotation and orbital motion are not directly felt. People and objects share Earth's motion, so their natural tendency to keep that motion makes Earth seem at rest to observers on it.

## Falling Bodies

Near Earth's surface, falling bodies follow equations of motion using height, speed, acceleration, and the acceleration of gravity, about 9.8 metres per second squared. A body raised above the surface has gravitational potential energy, which can change into kinetic energy as it falls.

## Equilibrium

A mass attached to springs can rest in equilibrium when spring forces balance. If displaced, the springs act to pull it back toward the equilibrium position.

## Hooke's Law

Hooke's law states that spring force is proportional to displacement and directed opposite to it. A stiffer spring has a larger constant and produces stronger restoring acceleration for the same displacement.

## Simple Harmonic Motion

Simple harmonic motion ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simple_harmonic_motion)) describes repeated oscillation between equal positive and negative displacements. The period is the time for one complete cycle, and the frequency is the number of cycles per second.

## Stable Equilibrium

Stable mechanical equilibrium appears when a slight disturbance creates a restoring force. A mass in a bowl or a spring-supported body oscillates around the stable point instead of moving away from it.

## Dissipative Forces

Dissipative forces such as friction and viscosity remove mechanical energy from motion. For slowly moving bodies, these forces can often be treated as proportional to speed, making oscillations decay while keeping the same basic restoring behavior.

## Damped Oscillation

Damped oscillation occurs when resistance reduces amplitude over time. Critical damping stops oscillation as quickly as possible without repeated motion, as in automobile shock absorbers that reduce bouncing after springs are disturbed.

## Projectile Motion

Projectile motion under ideal gravity follows a parabolic path when air resistance is ignored. This model shows core principles clearly, though real cannonballs or baseballs can deviate because drag and other forces matter.

## The Pendulum

[[Galileo]]'s pendulum idea connects gravity, circular arcs, and oscillation. For small swings, a pendulum follows the same mathematical pattern as a harmonic oscillator, with a period depending mainly on length and gravity rather than mass.

## Pendulum as Harmonic Motion

A pendulum moving through a small arc behaves like simple harmonic motion. The angular displacement plays the same role that linear displacement plays for a spring, so the same cosine-type solution describes the motion.

## Uniform Circular Motion

Uniform circular motion can be described by x and y components that each oscillate harmonically. The two components are out of phase, so one reaches maximum value when the other passes through zero.

## Average Velocity

The average velocity of a particle moving on a circle is a vector based on displacement over a time interval. As the interval shrinks, this average approaches the instantaneous velocity.

## Instantaneous Velocity

Instantaneous velocity in circular motion is tangent to the circle. Its magnitude can stay constant even though its direction changes at every moment.

## Velocity and the Radius

Vector subtraction of nearby radius vectors shows why circular velocity lies perpendicular to the radius. The tangent direction emerges from the limit as the time interval becomes very small.

## Acceleration in Circular Motion

Even at constant speed, circular motion has acceleration because velocity direction changes. This acceleration points inward toward the centre of the circle.

## Centripetal Acceleration

The inward centripetal acceleration is opposite the radius vector and perpendicular to the instantaneous velocity. Its magnitude depends on speed and radius.

## Universal Gravitation

Universal gravitation describes attraction between bodies such as the Sun and Earth. The force depends on both masses, their separation, and the gravitational constant, and it acts along the line joining their centers.

## Orbital Speed

For circular orbits, orbital speed depends on distance from the Sun and the Sun's mass, not on the planet's own mass. A planet is a freely falling body whose inward acceleration continually bends its path into an orbit.

## Angular Momentum

Angular momentum ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Angular_momentum)) combines position and momentum through a cross product. Its rate of change is connected to torque, so angular momentum is conserved when no external torque acts.

## Centre of Mass

The centre of mass ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Center_of_mass)) of the Earth-Sun system lies very near the Sun because the Sun is enormously more massive. Choosing the center of mass as the origin simplifies two-body motion.

## Many-Body Centre of Mass

For any many-body system, the centre of mass ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Center_of_mass)) is a weighted position based on all masses. The same definition applies to stars in a galaxy, atoms in a rigid body, or selected pieces of a structure.

## Conservation of Momentum

Conservation of momentum follows from Newton's second law when no external force acts. Internal forces cancel in action-reaction pairs, so the total momentum of an isolated system remains constant.

## Collisions

A collision is an encounter that changes the motion of at least one body. The interacting forces may be gravitational, electromagnetic, or microscopic forces that appear macroscopically as contact, and total momentum remains central to the analysis.

## Two-Particle Collisions

In a two-particle collision, momentum conservation constrains the outgoing momenta as a vector triangle. The condition limits possible results but does not alone determine every final direction and speed.

## Kinetic Energy in Collisions

Kinetic energy is not always fully preserved as kinetic energy after a collision. Microscopic bodies can gain internal energy, so outgoing particles may carry less kinetic energy than the incident particle had.

## The Centre-of-Mass Frame

The centre-of-mass frame is the frame in which total momentum is zero. It often makes collision geometry simpler because all motion is measured relative to the system's own mass center.

## Coupled Oscillators

Coupled oscillators show that even two equal masses joined by springs can exchange motion in surprising ways. Energy can pass from one mass to another through the spring between them.

## Normal Modes

Normal modes are special patterns in which all parts of a coupled system oscillate together at one frequency. A chain of masses can form half-sine, full-sine, and higher patterns depending on the mode.

## Static Equilibrium

A body in static equilibrium must have no net force. Equal and opposite forces can balance translation when they act along the same line.

## Torque

Equal and opposite forces can still create torque ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Torque)) if they act along different lines. Then the body may not accelerate linearly but can begin to rotate.

## Statics

Statics studies bodies and structures in equilibrium, requiring both zero net force and zero net torque. This explains why forces must be arranged carefully in beams, supports, and buildings.

## Compression

Compression occurs when equal and opposite forces push inward on a body. Materials and structures respond differently depending on whether they are compressed or stretched.

## Tension

Tension occurs when forces pull outward, and some materials that resist compression poorly handle tension well, or the reverse. Cables, strings, stone, brick, concrete, and arches show why engineers must match material strength to force type.

## Supports and Geometry

A supported body can place members under tension or compression depending on geometry. The force in supports can become very large when their angle is too shallow.

## Stone and Spans

Ancient Greek stone temples show the limits of horizontal stone slabs. Stone can be strong in compression yet weak across long unsupported spans.

## Support Angles

Small structural angles can make support forces intolerably large. This is why a mass cannot safely hang from thin horizontal members that can carry only limited tension or compression.

## Structural Stability

Structural stability depends on how forces change when a body is slightly disturbed. Engineers and architects must account for such perturbations, not only for perfect balance.

## Rotational Inertia

A rigid body rotating about a fixed axis resists changes because of rotational inertia. The resistance depends on both mass and how that mass is distributed around the axis.

## Angular Momentum of a Rigid Body

The total angular momentum of a rigid body is found by summing contributions from all its small mass elements. Each contribution depends on mass, speed, and distance from the rotation axis.

## Moment of Inertia

The moment of inertia is the rotational analogue of mass. It remains constant for a rigid body about a fixed axis, allowing torque, angular acceleration, and angular momentum to be related in forms parallel to Newton's second law.

## Rolling Motion

Rolling motion depends on friction between the body and the surface. Too little friction allows sliding, while enough static friction allows rolling without slipping.

## The Point of Contact

In rolling without slipping, the point of contact is instantaneously at rest relative to the surface. Because static friction acts at that point, it need not do work, so mechanical energy can be conserved.

## Gyroscopic Precession

Gyroscopic precession occurs when torque changes the direction of angular momentum rather than simply tipping a spinning body downward.

## Nutation

The spinning body may show nutation, an up-and-down oscillation caused when the center of mass overshoots a stable precession path. Friction can damp this nodding and leave steadier precession.

## The Spinning Top

A spinning top first stays nearly vertical through conservation of angular momentum. As friction slows it and the axis tilts, gravity produces torque that makes the top precess.

## Earth as a Gyroscope

The [[Earth - Its Properties, Composition, and Structure (Earth)|Earth]] behaves like a gyroscope because it spins daily. Since Earth bulges at the Equator, the Sun's gravity can exert torque on the bulge and slowly change the direction of Earth's axis.

## Precession of the Equinoxes

The precession of the equinoxes comes from the small persistent torque on Earth's equatorial bulge. Earth's axis precesses about once every 25,800 years, shifting the equinox points known since antiquity.

## Lagrangian and Hamiltonian Mechanics

Generalized coordinates and Lagrange's equations reduce complicated coupled mechanical problems to independent equations when suitable coordinates are chosen. Hamilton's equations go further by using generalized momentum, giving another powerful formulation of dynamics.

## Core Equations

Classical mechanics relates force, motion, energy, and momentum:

$$\vec{F}=m\vec{a}$$

This is Newton's second law: force is the product of mass and acceleration, so a larger mass or a faster change in motion requires a larger net force.

$$K=\frac{1}{2}mv^2$$

Kinetic energy increases with mass and with the square of speed, which is why faster motion rapidly raises impact energy.

$$p=mv$$

Momentum combines mass and velocity, making it useful for tracking motion before and after collisions or interactions.

## Key Events & Developments

- 1543: Nicolaus Copernicus published work that helped begin the scientific revolution in celestial motion.
- About 150 years later: Isaac Newton's Principia unified key principles of classical mechanics.
- 25,800 years: Earth's axis completes one precessional cycle caused by torque on its equatorial bulge.

## Key People

- Nicolaus Copernicus ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nicolaus_Copernicus)): Reframed planetary motion and helped launch the scientific revolution.
- [[Galileo]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Galileo_Galilei)): Used inertia and pendulum motion to clarify why Earth's motion is not directly felt.
- Isaac Newton ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isaac_Newton)): Reduced mechanics to laws of motion and linked force with acceleration.
- [[Kepler]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kepler)): His third law is connected with the circular-orbit relation between planetary speed and distance.

## Interesting Facts

- A planet in orbit is a freely falling body whose path keeps curving around the Sun.
- Friction can allow rolling without doing work when the contact point is instantaneously at rest.
- A gyroscope responds to torque by precessing rather than simply falling in the torque direction.
