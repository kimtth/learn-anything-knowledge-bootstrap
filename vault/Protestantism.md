# Protestantism

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> Protestantism is the diverse Christian movement that emerged from the sixteenth-century Reformation against medieval Roman Catholic doctrine and practice. The source follows Lutheran, Reformed, Anabaptist, Puritan, Pietist, Methodist, evangelical, fundamentalist, and Pentecostal developments alongside their political and social settings.

## Introduction

Protestantism ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Protestantism)) is the Christian movement that began in northern Europe in the early 16th century as a reaction against medieval Roman Catholic doctrines and practices. Along with Roman Catholicism and Eastern Orthodoxy, it became one of the three major forces in Christianity.

The name Protestant ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Protestantism)) first appeared at the Diet of Speyer in 1529. After Emperor Charles V rescinded earlier permission for rulers to decide whether to enforce the Edict of Worms, 14 free cities and six Lutheran princes protested, saying they must obey God rather than Caesar.

The label gradually applied to those who followed the Reformation. In Germany many preferred evangelicals, in France Huguenots, and in Switzerland, Holland, England, and Scotland many preferred Reformed. In the 16th century, Protestantism mainly referred to Lutheran and Reformed schools, but it later included many denominations and movements.

## Late Medieval Background

The Protestant Reformation ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reformation)) arose from a late medieval church already full of reform efforts, criticism, devotion, and conflict. Catholic and Protestant historians often distorted this background: Catholics emphasized reforms before Luther, while Protestants portrayed the late medieval church as corrupt to justify the break.

Critics attacked abuses by popes, clergy, monks, and nuns. Pope Innocent VIII and Pope Alexander VI became symbols of scandal, while papal wars, artistic patronage, and financial demands angered many believers during a period of rising nationalism.

Christian humanists such as Johannes Reuchlin and Desiderius Erasmus promoted Greek, Hebrew, biblical learning, and criticism of superstition. Erasmus stressed the philosophy of Christ and the Bible, causing later critics to say that he laid the egg Luther hatched.

Yet early 16th-century Europe was not simply ready to abandon Catholicism. Most believers remained loyal to the church and saw it as the vehicle of salvation, even while they wanted reform of clergy, relics, pilgrimages, indulgences, and popular religious abuses.

## Luther and the Continental Reformation

*Portrait of Martin Luther by the workshop of Lucas Cranach the Elder*

Martin Luther believed he differed from earlier reformers because he attacked doctrine, not only the conduct of churchmen. He rejected the idea that sinners could resolve guilt by confession, penance, absolution, indulgences, and good works. For him, the whole human being was sick and needed God's grace.

The indulgence system rested on the treasury of merit of Christ and the saints and was used to raise money for crusades, cathedrals, hospitals, bridges, and St. Peter's. In Luther's time, indulgence preaching promised release from purgatory and seemed to invade the sacrament of penance.

On October 31, 1517, Luther launched the Ninety-five Theses against abuses connected with Albert and Tetzel. The theses attacked financial abuse, papal claims over purgatory, and the idea of a treasury of merits. Luther's crucial claim was that the true treasury of the church was the gospel.

When the papacy called his position heretical, [[Luther]] denied the infallibility of popes and councils and declared Scripture the only basis of authority. Support came from evangelical reform circles, Erasmus's appeal for a fair hearing, and political conditions that prevented Rome from quickly silencing him.

## Reformed and Radical Movements

The Swiss Reformation included Huldrych Zwingli and later John Calvin. Their followers preferred the name Reformed and became influential in Switzerland, Holland, England, Scotland, and parts of France.

The Anabaptists were more radical. They restricted biblicism to the New Testament and upheld separation of church and state, voluntary church membership, and religious liberty. They rejected infant baptism because they saw baptism as the sign of adult spiritual rebirth.

Anabaptists also rejected oaths and violence, believing saints should withdraw from the wicked world. Catholics and Protestants saw them as dangerous to social order, and the Diet of Speyer in 1529 imposed death penalties. Felix Manz was drowned in Zurich in 1527.

The Munster episode in 1534 damaged Anabaptist reputation when apocalyptic radicals led by John of Leiden seized the city, took up arms, and restored polygamy. Later, more peaceful Anabaptist groups continued under persecution.

## Puritanism

Puritanism emerged in Elizabethan England from disputes over clerical vestments, liturgy, and church government. Elizabeth demanded uniformity, and those who refused prescribed garb were mocked as Puritans or precisians.

A second controversy concerned church government. Thomas Cartwright argued in 1570 for presbyterian government by local councils of clergy and laity rather than bishops and archbishops. Separatists such as Robert Browne went further, forming voluntary congregations outside the parish system.

Most Puritan leaders rejected Separatism and sought a church within the church. They organized prophesyings, public meetings of clergy and laity to discuss Scripture, which Archbishop Edmund Grindal favoured for education but Elizabeth viewed as politically threatening.

Under James I, Puritan hopes rose but were frustrated. At the 1604 Hampton Court Conference, James opposed Presbyterianism with the phrase no bishop, no king. His policies, canons against Nonconformists, and Book of Sports deepened conflict.

Some Puritans left England. A Scrooby congregation fled to Holland and then sailed on the Mayflower to found Plymouth Colony in 1620. Others stayed, conformed minimally, or suffered deprivation.

Under Charles I, anti-Puritan liturgy became linked with anti-Calvinist Arminian theology. William Laud, archbishop of Canterbury from 1633, promoted Arminians and tried to impose a liturgy in Scotland, provoking rebellion and helping create the crisis that led to civil war.

## Restoration and New England Puritanism

After Cromwell's death, some Puritans supported restoring Charles II in hope of compromise. Instead, the Act of Uniformity of 1662 forced about 2,000 Puritan ministers from their positions. The Conventicle Act and Five Mile Act created the Great Persecution and pushed Puritanism into Nonconformity.

In Virginia, Puritan influence existed before the Mayflower. Sir Thomas Dale interpreted colonial survival and military success as signs of God's favour. Yet Virginia later aligned more closely with the English establishment after becoming a royal colony in 1624.

The Massachusetts Bay Colony gave Puritans their greatest opportunity. John Winthrop described it as a covenant with God. John Cotton shaped a middle way between Separatism and Presbyterianism, allowing only those who declared a work of grace to become church members.

Massachusetts linked church and commonwealth. Only the elect could vote and rule, biblical law ordered church and state, and dissenters such as Roger Williams, Anne Hutchinson, Mary Dyer, and other Quakers faced punishment or banishment.

## Protestant Renewal and Pietism

The Thirty Years' War intensified desire for spiritual renewal. Central Europe suffered economic collapse, burned churches, closed schools, and moral deterioration. Renewal drew on mystical traditions from Johannes Tauler, Thomas a Kempis, Caspar Schwenckfeld, Valentin Weigel, and Jakob Bohme.

Catholic powers also tried to recover Protestant territories. Louis XIV revoked the Edict of Nantes in 1685, expelling many Huguenots to England, Holland, and Germany. Some French Protestants, including the Camisards, resisted, while others survived secretly until revival after the French Revolution.

Pietism grew from Reform Orthodoxy and Lutheran renewal. Johann Arndt's Four Books on True Christianity emphasized repentance, regeneration, and new life, making him a father of Pietism. Lutheran hymnody also contributed to spiritual renewal.

Philipp Jakob Spener became the central figure. In Frankfurt he held catechism classes and collegia pietatis, devotional meetings for Bible study and conversation. His Pia Desideria of 1675 proposed wide Bible reading, priesthood of believers, active love, charitable controversy, pastoral education reform, and preaching for inner piety.

Spener's ideas threatened clergy and professors, and some conventicles became separatist. He responded by defending the church while urging renewal. Pietism then spread through Halle, Moravian communities, Scandinavia, North America, missions, and evangelical awakenings.

## Moravians, Methodism, and Great Awakening

Count Zinzendorf shaped the Moravian Church at Herrnhut and later worked in western Germany, England, and North America. Moravian congregations influenced John Wesley, whose Aldersgate experience in 1738 occurred while hearing Luther's Preface to Romans read in a Moravian setting.

Methodism, led by John Wesley, resembled German Pietism in its stress on conversion, disciplined devotion, lay preaching, and evangelistic travel. Wesley did not intend separation from the Church of England, but the parish system could not contain his free evangelism. The first conference of Methodist preachers met in 1744.

Methodism succeeded in industrial parishes, rural districts, Wales, Yorkshire, Cornwall, and later America. It revived doctrines of grace and justification, moral earnestness, hymn singing, and free-church patterns.

The Great Awakening in the American colonies drew on Pietist, Wesleyan, and Calvinist currents. Jonathan Edwards's Northampton revival of 1734-35 and George Whitefield's preaching helped spread conversions. The movement encouraged independent Bible-centred Baptist groups and gave evangelicalism frontier energy.

## Rationalism and Nineteenth-Century Protestantism

Rationalism grew from confidence in reason, science, and figures such as Isaac Newton. Cambridge Platonists, Christian Wolff, English Deists, and other thinkers pushed theologians to defend Christianity through reason and philosophy. Bishop Joseph Butler offered a major response to Deism.

In Germany, a reaction against Enlightenment rationalism appeared in Romanticism ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Romanticism)), Idealism, renewed Pietism, and neoconfessional Lutheranism. Schools included Repristination Theology, Neo-Lutheranism, and the Erlangen school.

Nineteenth-century immigration carried German and Scandinavian Protestant forms to [[North America]]. Old Lutherans, Saxons, Norwegians, Danes, and Swedes brought confessional, pietist, and Haugean traditions, shaping American Lutheran bodies such as the Buffalo and Missouri synods.

The expansion of Protestantism also depended on toleration and the secular state. After the French Revolution, old church-state monopolies weakened. Protestant evangelists could work in more places, especially in colonial Africa and India, while churches had to redefine their role in secular society.

## Mainstream Protestantism and Modern Movements

[[World Wars (World War I)|World War I]] weakened European confidence and Christian prestige. The atheist Russian government after 1917 increased pressure on Christianity, and European Protestant churches lost members and influence. In Germany, Nazi rule created new challenges after Hitler's rise in 1933.

Many Protestant leaders first welcomed Nazi conservatism, but conflict grew over baptized Jews and the German Christians' Aryanized Christianity. The Confessing Church, led by Martin Niemoller and others, defended traditional doctrine. Dietrich Bonhoeffer and other leaders paid with their lives for resistance.

Pentecostalism grew from Wesleyan Holiness movements in the United States, appearing in Topeka in 1901 and Los Angeles in 1906. It stressed Spirit baptism, speaking in tongues, prophecy, interpretation, and healing. It spread strongly in the Caribbean, Latin America, and sub-Saharan Africa.

Fundamentalism combined premillennialism with biblical inerrancy and took its name from The Fundamentals, published in 1910-15. It became militant in U.S. denominational conflicts around 1919-20 and fought evolution and higher criticism, symbolized by the Scopes trial of 1925.

Evangelicalism, represented by Billy Graham and Christianity Today, shared some doctrines with Fundamentalism but was more culturally engaged, ecumenical, and institution-building. Evangelicals entered liberal arts education, social programs, politics, and international conferences.
