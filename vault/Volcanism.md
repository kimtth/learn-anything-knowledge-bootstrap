Volcanism ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Volcanism)), also called vulcanism, includes the movement of molten rock, gases, hot water, ash, and rock fragments from Earth’s interior toward or onto its surface. It includes volcanic eruptions, lava flows, ash clouds, geysers, fumaroles, and the long-term construction of volcanic landscapes. A volcano is not simply a mountain: it is part of a plumbing system through which magma, gas, and heat travel from depth to the surface.

Volcanism has shaped Earth’s crust, atmosphere, oceans, soils, and life for billions of years. It also occurs beyond Earth. Lava plains and volcanic features exist on Mercury, Venus, Mars, and the Moon, while Io, a moon of Jupiter, is the most volcanically active known body in the solar system. Studying volcanism therefore helps explain both immediate natural hazards and the evolution of planets.

## Magma and Eruption

Magma is molten or partly molten rock beneath the surface. It contains liquid rock, crystals, and dissolved gases such as water vapour, carbon dioxide, and sulfur compounds. Magma forms when rock melts because of increased temperature, reduced pressure, or the addition of water and other volatile substances. As magma rises, pressure falls and dissolved gases can escape as bubbles, much as gas escapes from a carbonated drink when its container is opened.

Whether an eruption is gentle or explosive depends largely on viscosity, the resistance of magma to flow, and on its gas content. Hot, low-silica basaltic magma is relatively fluid, allowing gas to escape and lava to flow. Cooler, silica-rich magma is more viscous, trapping gases until pressure builds and fragments the magma into ash, pumice, and other pyroclastic material. Volcanic behaviour is therefore a result of material properties and pressure, not simply the size of the volcano.

## Plate Tectonic Settings

Most terrestrial volcanism is connected to [[Plate Tectonics|plate tectonics]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plate_tectonics)). At convergent boundaries, one plate descends beneath another in a subduction zone. Water and other materials carried downward help melt parts of the mantle above the sinking plate. The resulting magma rises and forms chains of volcanoes called island arcs or continental volcanic arcs. Many volcanoes around the Pacific Ring of Fire formed in this setting, and their gas-rich magmas can produce highly explosive eruptions.

At divergent boundaries, plates move apart and mantle rock rises. Decompression allows some of that rock to melt, producing basaltic magma that creates new oceanic crust along mid-ocean ridges. Most of this activity occurs underwater. Where ridge systems rise above sea level, as in [[Iceland]], people can observe fissures, lava fields, hot springs, and geysers directly. Continental rifts, such as the East African Rift, are another setting where stretching crust permits magma to rise.

Some volcanoes form within a plate rather than at a boundary. A hot spot is a long-lived region of unusually hot rising mantle material or other localized melting process. As a plate moves across it, successive volcanoes can form a chain. The Hawaiian Islands illustrate this pattern: the youngest active volcanoes lie over the hot spot, while older islands and seamounts extend away in the direction of plate movement.

## Volcano Forms and Products

Volcano shape reflects the way material erupts. Shield volcanoes are broad and gently sloping because repeated fluid lava flows spread far from their vents. Stratovolcanoes, also called composite volcanoes, are steeper cones built from alternating lava, ash, and pyroclastic deposits. Cinder cones are smaller accumulations of loose volcanic fragments around a vent. Large explosive eruptions can empty a magma reservoir enough for the ground above to collapse, forming a broad depression called a caldera.

Volcanoes produce several kinds of material. Lava is magma that reaches the surface. Ash consists of tiny sharp fragments of rock and glass, not soft burned material. Larger fragments include lapilli, bombs, and blocks. Pyroclastic flows are fast, hot, ground-hugging mixtures of gas, ash, and rock that can be among the most destructive volcanic hazards. Volcanic gases can create acid rain, poor air quality, and, in some places, dangerous accumulations of carbon dioxide.

## Hazards and Monitoring

Volcanic hazards include lava flows, ash fall, pyroclastic flows, lahars, landslides, toxic gases, earthquakes, and tsunamis. Lahars are fast flows of water, ash, rock, and debris that travel down valleys, often after heavy rain or rapid melting of snow and ice. They can affect communities far from the volcano itself. Ash can damage crops, contaminate water, collapse roofs, disrupt machinery, and threaten aircraft because volcanic particles can melt inside jet engines.

Volcanologists cannot reliably predict the exact time and size of every eruption, but they can monitor change. Seismometers detect earthquakes caused by moving magma, ground-deformation instruments measure swelling or cracking, gas sensors track changes in emissions, and satellites observe heat and surface motion. Monitoring helps officials issue warnings, define evacuation zones, and communicate uncertainty. Effective risk reduction also requires land-use planning, public education, emergency routes, and attention to communities that may have fewer resources to evacuate or rebuild.

## Benefits and Long-Term Effects

Volcanism also creates benefits. Volcanic ash and weathered lava can produce fertile soils, supporting intensive farming in many volcanic regions. Geothermal heat can provide electricity or heating. Volcanic rocks supply building materials, metals, sulfur, pumice, and industrial minerals. Dramatic landscapes attract tourism and support scientific research.

At a planetary scale, volcanism releases gases that can help build atmospheres and recycle material between Earth’s interior and surface. Very large eruptions can temporarily cool climate by placing sulfur-rich particles high in the atmosphere, where they reflect some sunlight. Over much longer periods, volcanic carbon dioxide contributes to the carbon cycle. These effects show why volcanism must be understood as both a hazard and a fundamental Earth system.

## Key Concepts

- Magma: molten or partly molten rock below the surface.
- Lava: magma that erupts onto the surface.
- Viscosity: resistance to flow; it strongly affects eruption style.
- Pyroclastic flow: a fast, hot current of gas, ash, and volcanic fragments.
- Caldera: a large depression formed when land collapses after magma is removed.
- Geothermal energy: usable heat from Earth’s interior.

## Key Events & Developments

- [[Plate Tectonics|Plate tectonics]] explained why many volcanoes form at subduction zones, rifts, and mid-ocean ridges.
- 1991: the eruption of Mount Pinatubo showed how a major eruption can temporarily influence global climate.
- Modern era: satellite and ground monitoring improved eruption warnings and hazard mapping.

## Key People

- Volcanologists: study magma, eruptions, hazards, and the physical signals of volcanic unrest.
- Alfred Wegener: proposed continental drift, an important predecessor to plate-tectonic theory.
- Emergency managers: translate volcanic monitoring into evacuation, communication, and recovery plans.

## Interesting Facts

- Volcanic ash consists of sharp rock and glass fragments rather than soft material left by fire.
- Shield volcanoes can be very large because fluid lava spreads far from the vent.
- Ice shelves can affect sea-level rise indirectly by slowing glaciers that flow from land into the sea.
