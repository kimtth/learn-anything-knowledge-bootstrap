## Introduction

Tokyo-Yokohama Metropolitan Area ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greater_Tokyo_Area)), commonly called Greater Tokyo, is Japan's largest urban complex. It lies along the northern and western shores of Tokyo Bay on Honshu's Pacific coast.

At its centre is Tokyo ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tokyo)), Japan's capital and largest city. The wider complex includes Tokyo metropolis and the neighbouring prefectures of Saitama, Chiba, and Kanagawa, though definitions vary because many commuters live beyond them.

The former city of Tokyo became part of the larger Tokyo metropolis in 1943. The 23 wards form the city proper, while the metropolis also includes western mountains, the Izu Islands, and the Bonin Islands.

Important neighbouring cities include Yokohama, Japan's second largest city, industrial Kawasaki between Tokyo and Yokohama, and industrial Chiba on the bay's northeastern coast.

Old Edo occupied alluvial and reclaimed land near the Sumida River and hills to the west. Its site commanded approaches to the Kanto Plain, Japan's largest plain.

The palace stands between the flat Low City and the more stable hilly Uptown. Eastern districts have been poorer, more crowded, geologically unstable, and vulnerable to earthquake, fire, and bombing.

The Tone, Tama, Sumida, Edo, and Arakawa systems shaped the region's settlement, flood control, and boundaries. Diversions and drainage channels were built to reduce flooding.

## Climate and Green Space

Tokyo has a humid temperate climate with hot, sticky summers, brisk winters, rainy periods, and typhoons in some years. Winter is the sunniest and clearest season.

Seasonal flowers are central to urban culture. Cherry blossoms, plums, peonies, azaleas, wisteria, irises, camellias, and chrysanthemums draw crowds to parks, temples, shrines, and riverbanks.

Despite dense development, Tokyo has many parks and green places. Ueno Park and the Sumida embankment remain famous for cherry blossoms, while Meiji Shrine, Nezu Shrine, and other sites are known for flowers.

## Urban Layout

*Night in the Greater Tokyo Area ISS054*

Greater Tokyo is not one simple centre but a system of centres and satellites. Marunouchi is the entrepreneurial heart, Kasumigaseki is the government centre, and Nihombashi remains historically important for finance.

Ginza is a famous retail and entertainment district, while Shinjuku is one of the largest retail, entertainment, and railway centres in Japan. Other satellite centres compete strongly for people, work, shopping, and nightlife.

Yokohama, Kawasaki, Chiba, and many suburban cities form an extended urban region. The area mixes offices, factories, ports, housing, commuter suburbs, old neighbourhoods, landfill, and coastal industry.

## People and Society

![[attachments/tokyo_yokohama_metropolitan_area_002.webp|420]]

*Historical population of Tokyo-to, greater Tokyo, and Tokyo ku-area, 1890-2010*

Greater Tokyo is striking for its [[Population|population]]. The four main prefectures contain about one-fourth of Japan's people, and Tokyo's wards alone hold millions.

The region has long attracted young people, students, office workers, commuters, and migrants from elsewhere in Japan. Pedestrian crowds are a major feature of the city's vitality.

Housing is difficult because of land prices. Many people live in small apartments, condominiums, public housing blocks, or distant suburbs, with long daily commutes.

## Economy

Tokyo became Japan's leading industrial centre after World War II. Heavy industry is concentrated in Chiba, Kawasaki, and Yokohama, while Tokyo proper leans more toward light industry, publishing, electronics, management, and services.

The region concentrates finance ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Finance)) and corporate management. Marunouchi is the preferred corporate location, while Nihombashi contains the Bank of Japan and the Tokyo Stock Exchange.

During the 1980s, Tokyo became a global financial centre during the bubble economy. Land speculation became extreme before prices fell in the early 1990s.

## Transportation

Tokyo is Japan's greatest transport hub. The first Japanese railway linked Tokyo and Yokohama in 1872, and rail remains the region's essential commuter system.

Major stations include Tokyo ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tokyo)), Ueno, and Shinjuku. Shinjuku handles enormous commuter flows from western suburbs and is among the busiest stations in the world.

Narita handles much international air travel, while Haneda is closer to central Tokyo and serves domestic and some international flights. Yokohama remains the region's major port, with Chiba, Kawasaki, and Tokyo also important.

Roads and expressways are extensive but strained by traffic. A dense subway and commuter rail network provides the main alternative to cars.

## Government and Services

Tokyo metropolis is governed by a metropolitan assembly and elected governor. The 23 wards have elected councils and ward heads but limited authority.

Water supply, sewage, garbage, and land use are major urban problems. Edo had aqueducts but no sewers; modern Tokyo has expanded water systems, sewerage, and waste disposal under heavy pressure.

## Culture

Tokyo dominates Japanese culture ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Culture)). It contains many universities, learned societies, research institutes, libraries, publishing houses, museums, theatres, concert halls, writers, journalists, and opinion makers.

Major institutions include the Tokyo National Museum, National Diet Library, National Theatre, Tokyo Metropolitan Art Museum, and cultural sites around Ueno and government districts.

The city offers unusual variety: old and new, East and West, traditional festivals, high culture, popular entertainment, shopping districts, temples, shrines, parks, and modern media.

## History

Edo began as a small fortified settlement near an estuary. It became important after Tokugawa Ieyasu took possession in 1590 and made it the seat of his government in 1603.

During the Tokugawa period, Edo grew rapidly and may have become the world's largest city in the 18th century. Mercantile culture, kabuki, printing, and popular entertainment flourished.

In 1868, the emperor moved from Kyoto, Edo became Tokyo, meaning Eastern Capital, and the city became Japan's imperial capital.

The 1923 earthquake and the 1945 air raids devastated eastern Tokyo. Postwar rebuilding produced vast modern development, while many older wooden neighbourhoods disappeared.

Yokohama was badly damaged by earthquake and bombing but retained more visible traces of its Meiji port history. Kawasaki and Chiba developed as industrial coastal cities.

## Key Events & Developments

- 1603: Tokugawa Ieyasu made Edo the seat of shogunal government.
- 1868: Edo was renamed Tokyo when the imperial capital moved there.
- 1872: Japan's first railway linked Tokyo and Yokohama.
- 1923: the Great Kanto earthquake devastated much of Tokyo and Yokohama.
- 1943: Tokyo city and prefecture were merged into Tokyo metropolis.
- 1945: wartime bombing destroyed large areas of Tokyo and Yokohama.
