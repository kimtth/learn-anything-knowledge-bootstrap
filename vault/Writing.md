## Introduction

![[attachments/writing_001.webp|420]]

*Proto-cuneiform tablet*

Writing ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Writing)) is human communication through visible marks that conventionally represent some level of language. It does not represent thought directly. It represents language, and language has several levels: sentences, words, morphemes, syllables, phonemes, and even smaller sound features. The long history of writing is partly the history of discovering these levels and finding ways to mark them efficiently.

Literacy ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Literacy)) means more than knowing letters. It means competence in a writing system and in the special social uses that written language serves. Written language is visible and durable, while speech disappears as it is spoken. Yet writing is not just speech written down. Literary, scientific, legal, and administrative writing often use forms of language that are not normal in speech.

## Writing as a System of Visible Language

Written language ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Writing)) is a system for symbolizing the symbols of spoken language. A reader normally must know the language behind the script, because the marks depend on that language's structure. Written and spoken forms can also develop different styles and functions, so a community may have more than one written dialect.

Ferdinand de Saussure and Leonard Bloomfield treated writing mainly as transcription in the earlier 20th century, which made its importance seem smaller than it was. Later scholars, including Milman Parry, Marshall McLuhan, Eric Havelock, Jack Goody, and Walter Ong, studied writing as a separate medium with social and conceptual effects.

## Visible Signs, Pictures, and Language Structure

Visible signs such as gestures, pictures, trademarks, and toilet-door signs can communicate, but they are not all writing. A raised hand or a wink is visible communication, but it does not transcribe language. Pictures can show events, but they become close to writing only when they are conventional signs for specific linguistic meanings.

Andre Martinet described language as having double articulation: structures of meaning and structures of sound. Meaning can be divided into morphemes, words, clauses, and larger discourse forms such as stories or poems. Sound can be divided into phonemes, distinctive features, consonants, vowels, and syllables. Writing systems may represent any of these levels.

Pictorial signs work well only when possible meanings are few. A figure on a washroom door, the human figure on the Pioneer spacecraft plaque, an inverted horse and rider near a dangerous trail, or a cattle brand can be read because the reader already has a narrow set of possible meanings. They do not express pure thought; they express meanings through familiar conventions.

Numerals also seem to cross language boundaries, but they are better understood as an orthography for number meanings. The symbol 2 can be read as two, deux, or zwei, depending on language. It represents a linguistic meaning in a special written form and also supports operations such as carrying and borrowing.

## Writing, Speech, and Culture

Writing ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Writing)) should not be confused with language itself. People sometimes think sounds are made of letters because the written word is so familiar. Even [[Aristotle and Aristotelianism|Aristotle]] used one term, gramma, for units of speech and writing. Modern analysis separates language competence from knowledge of a writing system.

Literacy ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Literacy)) has also been overvalued when it is equated with civilization. Since the 18th century, Europeans often connected literacy with moral and cultural superiority, especially during colonization. Anthropology and literary studies later showed that nonliterate societies can have complex languages, rich oral traditions, and strong social and moral orders.

Milman Parry showed in the 1920s that the Homeric poems came from an oral tradition rather than from literate composition. Writing preserved the poems, but oral poets created them. This helped weaken the old division between literate civilization and supposedly primitive nonliterate life.

## The Functions of Writing

Writing systems ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Writing_system)) spread because they preserve language and information across time and space. Their use depends on both the nature of the script and the cultural practices of the society that adopts it. Isak Dinesen described how written news had special authority among Kikuyu readers in Out of Africa, while Albert Lord argued that written storytelling can gradually replace oral narrative song when readers begin to seek entertainment and instruction in books.

Harold Innis divided writing systems into those that bind through time and those that bind across space. Stone hieroglyphs and clay cuneiform preserve records for later generations. Portable papyrus helps carry messages over distance. These uses support archival, communicative, and mnemonic functions.

Archival writing preserves political, religious, scientific, literary, and commercial records. Communicative writing sends messages through letters, newspapers, encyclicals, and similar forms. Mnemonic writing serves private memory, as with notches on a counting stick or pebbles in a sack. A private mnemonic system need only be readable by its maker, but communicative and archival systems must be conventional enough for other readers, including readers who do not know the writer.

## Types of Writing Systems

![[attachments/writing_002.webp|420]]

*Egyptian hieroglyphs*

Orthography, or script, is a set of visible characters related to language structure. A logographic system represents words or morphemes. A syllabic system represents syllables. A consonantal system represents consonant values while leaving vowels mostly unmarked. An alphabet represents phonemes. A featural system represents articulatory features below the phoneme.

Logographic writing, as in Chinese, uses a graph for a meaningful unit. This makes homophones visually distinct, but it requires many characters because languages have many words and morphemes. Written English has more than 500,000 recorded words, showing why a purely logographic system can demand a very large memory load.

Syllabaries give one symbol to each syllable. Since syllables are easy to hear, early sound-based systems were often syllabic. Linear B ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_B)), a Mycenaean script from about 1400 BC, represented syllables such as pa and per with separate graphs. Syllabaries need hundreds of signs and still leave ambiguity, because readers often need context to know the intended word.

Consonantal scripts mark consonants but not vowels. This is highly ambiguous for a language like English, where pat, pet, pit, pot, and put are different words. It is less ambiguous in Hebrew and Arabic, where consonantal roots often carry the main lexical meaning and vowels mark grammar such as tense or aspect.

Alphabetic systems analyze syllables into consonants and vowels. A small set of letters can generate many syllables: 21 consonants and 5 vowels can make 105 simple consonant-vowel syllables and more than 2,000 consonant-vowel-consonant syllables. Alphabets work especially well for languages where sound differences strongly mark word differences.

Featural systems go below phonemes and represent how sounds are made. Hangul ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hangul)), traditionally created by King Sejong in the 15th century, uses signs whose shapes relate to articulatory features. Pitman shorthand, invented in Britain in the 19th century, also uses featural principles. Geoffrey Sampson praised Hangul as one of humanity's major intellectual achievements.

No orthography is pure. Chinese uses both meaning-based characters and sound-based aids. Alphabets preserve meaningful units too, as in the common spelling of photo in photograph and photography despite pronunciation changes. English also distinguishes homophones visually, as in meat, meet, and mete. Practical writing balances sound, meaning, recognition speed, and cultural use.

## Development of Writing Systems

Writing ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Writing)) is a recent technology compared with speech or signed language, and it must be taught to each generation. Older histories often treated writing as a steady march toward the Greek alphabet. Eric Havelock and Ignace Gelb emphasized the alphabet's economy and accuracy, but later views stress that a script's value depends on the language and functions it serves.

The alphabet was invented only once and then borrowed widely. It was a major intellectual step because it analyzed syllables into smaller sound units and allowed readers to decode unfamiliar words. Yet this was not equally useful for all languages. It fit Greek and other Indo-European languages much better than Chinese, Japanese, or Semitic languages.

Writing history has at least two major independent lines: the Sumerian line and the Chinese line. Western scripts can be traced to Sumer, but Asian scripts do not need to be explained as borrowings from Sumer. Different languages and societies produced different solutions to the problem of representing language visibly.

## Sumerian Writing

Sumerian script is the earliest known writing system and later developed into cuneiform. Its early stages are reconstructed from fragmentary evidence. Denise Schmandt-Besserat, building on Pierre Amiet's hypothesis, connected writing to clay tokens used for bookkeeping in lower Mesopotamia, now southern [[Iraq]].

Clay tokens date as early as 8000 BC, when agricultural life was replacing hunting and gathering. By about 3500 BC, many token shapes represented goods such as grain, sheep, cattle, jars, or animals. Tokens placed inside clay envelopes may have recorded debts or shipments. Marks on the outside made it unnecessary to open the envelope, and then the envelope itself could become a flat tablet.

Archaic Sumerian tablets from about 3100 BC used roughly 1,200 characters for numerals, objects, and personal names. The system served economic and administrative needs. When a blunt stylus replaced a pointed one, the signs became less pictorial and more wedge-shaped, giving cuneiform its name from the Latin cuneus, meaning wedge.

Phonographic writing appeared when signs began to stand for sounds as well as meanings. A sign for water could also represent a suffix that sounded like the word for water. This helped write abstract or hard-to-picture forms. In the 3rd millennium BC, Akkadians adopted and expanded the script, and Assyrians and Babylonians produced much of what is now called Akkadian cuneiform.

## Alphabetic Systems

![[attachments/writing_003.webp|420]]

*Phoenician alphabet chart*

Linear B ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_B)) was the first writing system consistently based on sound structure. It was a Mycenaean Greek syllabary from about 1400 BC, deciphered in 1952 by Michael Ventris. It was systematic but incomplete for Greek because Greek has many syllables beyond simple consonant-vowel forms, so readers often needed context.

The Semitic alphabet was created sometime in the 2nd millennium BC, probably in the northern Fertile Crescent. It used 22 ordered signs, beginning alef, bet, and gimel. Its acrophonic principle came from Egyptian hieroglyphic: a picture could represent the first sound of the word naming the pictured thing.

Semitic scripts marked consonants and left vowels unrepresented. In roots such as ktb, vowel changes could mark grammatical meaning while the consonants carried the lexical idea of writing. Because this suited Semitic languages, leaving vowels out was not simply a defect. Later systems such as Hebrew added vowel aids, including matres lectionis and pointing, especially for sacred texts, beginners, and poetry.

The Greek alphabet emerged around 1000-900 BC when Greeks adapted Semitic letters to a language that needed written vowels. Greek used Semitic signs for sounds absent in Greek to represent vowels. This was important because Greek words could begin with vowels, consist of vowels, or rely on vowels for lexical contrasts.

Romans borrowed the Greek alphabet and developed the Roman, or Latin, alphabet. Learned Latin remained the language of state and scholarship in [[Europe]] until the end of the Middle Ages. For English, the differentiation of all 26 letters was completed only in the 19th century.

Alphabetic development shows progress in analyzing language, but not a simple ladder of superiority. Each advance often came from adapting a script to a language for which it was not originally suited. Children learning alphabetic writing may pass through similar stages: first linking word length to object size, then writing words as sounds, then marking consonants, and only later grasping vowels.

## Chinese Writing and Its Derivatives

Chinese writing ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Written_Chinese)) developed at about the same time as the Semitic alphabet but followed a different path because Chinese has clearly separated syllables, each usually tied to a morpheme. Since Chinese is isolating rather than inflected, a separate character for each morpheme made sense even though it required many graphs.

Chinese script is mainly logographic. Early picture signs became complex characters that often combined meaning and sound. By 1400 BC, the system had about 2,500 to 3,000 characters, many still readable later. The full morpheme-graph system grew to about 40,000 characters, while a literate Chinese reader might need about 4,000.

Chinese orthography solved ambiguity in speech by separating homophones visually. Attempts to simplify the system can bring ambiguity back and make reading harder. The script also remained intelligible through major changes in spoken Chinese, showing the strength of representing morphemes rather than only sounds.

## Japanese Writing

[[Japan]] came into contact with Chinese culture during the Han dynasty and began writing Japanese in the 5th century AD using Chinese models. The fit was difficult because Japanese words often have several syllables and Japanese is inflected, unlike monosyllabic, isolating Chinese.

Japanese writing ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Japanese_writing_system)) became mixed. Kun writing used Chinese characters for Japanese words of similar meaning and also used characters for sound values. In the 8th century, man'yogana applied the phonographic principle more systematically, but its combination with kun writing and many homophones made single correct readings difficult.

Kana developed in the 9th or 10th century. Hiragana used simplified cursive forms of Chinese characters, while katakana used selected parts of them. Full Chinese characters are kanji. Modern Japanese combines kanji, hiragana, and katakana because kana alone is ambiguous and kanji alone is visually complex.

Japanese reform in 1946 limited common kanji to 1,850, later expanded to 1,945 in 1981. Even with reform, written Japanese remains hard to read without context because of homophony. The syllable kan can mean many different things, including sweet, print, view, slow, tube, Chinese, or Korean.

## Korean Writing

[[Korea]] was strongly influenced by Chinese institutions and culture, and until the 20th century Chinese was the normal written medium. From about the 6th century, Chinese script was adapted to Korean, creating problems like those in Japanese because Korean structure differed from Chinese.

Hangul ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hangul)) was the major Korean breakthrough. Invented by King Sejong in 1446, it used about 24 letters whose shapes were systematically related to phonetic features. Unlike borrowed Chinese orthography, Hangul was a native featural system and one of the clearest examples of a script designed around speech structure.

Writing systems ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Writing_system)) generally began with motivated pictures of objects, then became general orthographies by representing sound patterns. Western scripts moved farthest toward phonographic representation, from syllables to consonants to full vowels. Eastern scripts preserved the logographic principle. Korean was exceptional because it adopted a practical featural design.

## Literacy: The Uses of Writing

![[attachments/writing_004.webp|420]]

*Gutenberg Bible*

Literacy ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Literacy)) is competence in reading and writing, but its history depends on who can use a script and what the script can do. Scripts grow with new uses, and new writing systems make wider uses possible. Two important dimensions are learnability and expressive power, and they often pull in opposite directions.

Learnability is ease of acquisition. Environmental signs and small logographic sets are easy, and children may learn them almost automatically. Syllabaries such as Cree syllabary or the Liberian Vai syllabary can be learned quickly. Alphabets and consonantal scripts usually need years of schooling. Full Chinese or mixed Japanese systems require memorizing thousands of characters.

Expressive power is the ability to represent the full meanings of a language clearly. Easy pictorial systems express only limited meanings. Syllabaries are often ambiguous. Alphabets and consonantal systems can express a wide range of lexical and grammatical meanings, though not intonation. Logographic systems achieve explicitness by adding characters, trading easy learning for precise reading.

Democratization of writing depends partly on script design but also on materials, instruction, and social need. Alphabets have often been linked to ordinary people's access to literacy, but even in literate societies, specialized materials in science, government, law, and literature remain the domain of people with more education.

Writing functions expanded with cities, bureaucracy, science, law, and literature. Early tokens served bookkeeping, and early Sumerian writing served administration. More complete scripts made histories, edicts, treaties, scientific works, and literary texts possible because readers separated by time and place could read them more consistently.

Eric Havelock, Marshall McLuhan, and Walter Ong argued that alphabetic literacy and the decline of orality helped shape Western cultural development, including the Renaissance. Alphabetic literacy also encouraged a sharp distinction between reading and interpretation. Writers tried to reduce variant interpretations through grammar, fixed terms, punctuation, and literal meaning, especially in science and philosophy.

Literate societies changed because writing made statements repeatable, portable, and durable. Readers could scan, compare, and interpret texts. Writers could plan words, make lists and tables, and create indexes. Writing separated message from author and context, supported bureaucracy and law, replaced some face-to-face governance with written procedures, and helped turn scribes into authors with individual authority.

## Literacy and Schooling

Oral language is learned naturally, but literacy usually depends on teaching. Broad literacy depends heavily on schools. In Sumer and [[China]], schools developed alongside full writing systems, first to teach adults and later children. Modern technological societies depend on schools for high literacy.

Schooling ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/School)) alone does not create a literate society. Literacy levels reflect the uses of writing in the larger culture. When compulsory schooling spread in Britain, [[Europe]], and America in the 19th century, it grew in a setting where much of the population already used writing for diaries, notes, letters, and records.

Literacy ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Literacy)) is not merely decoding marks into sounds. It includes reading, writing, comprehension, interpretation, specialized vocabulary, specialized grammar, and knowledge of genres such as description, explanation, argument, instructions, narrative, and exposition. These forms often require years of schooling and can later shape speech itself, making it possible to speak in a written style.

European literacy grew during the Middle Ages as written texts replaced oral language and ritual in property deeds, servant indentures, trial evidence, and saints' lives. The Protestant Reformation and modern science strengthened literacy because both valued private reading, verification, and interpretation of written texts. Printing with movable type and vernacular translations increased the number of texts and readers.

Functional literacy is difficult to define. In 17th-century [[Sweden]], a person could be counted literate and allowed to marry if able to read parts of the catechism and sign the church registry. During [[World Wars (World War II)|World War II]], the United States Army used about fifth-grade achievement as a minimum. By 1966, the [[United States of America (United States)|United States]] Adult Education Act treated secondary-school completion as functional literacy.

Environmental literacy, or lay literacy, means practical competence in a written environment: signs, labels, trademarks, headlines, scores, ballots, checks, and notes. Brian Stock used the term textual communities for medieval groups organized around interpreters of religious texts. Even people who do not read much can understand that texts are permanent, authoritative, consultable, and interpretable.

Elite literacy is advanced competence in fields such as science, law, and literature. It requires specialized vocabulary and grammar and may more than double a person's reading vocabulary compared with ordinary speaking vocabulary. High literacy is not only writing; it can also reshape spoken language by bringing written-style argument, explanation, and precision into speech.

## Key Events & Developments

- 8000 BC: Clay tokens in lower Mesopotamia began serving simple bookkeeping needs.
- 3500 BC: More elaborate token systems and clay envelopes reflected growing urban exchange.
- 3100 BC: Sumerian tablets showed a logographic system tied to the Sumerian language.
- the 3rd millennium BC: Akkadians adopted Sumerian script and expanded its sound-based use.
- 1400 BC: Linear B ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_B)) represented Mycenaean Greek syllables, and Chinese writing had thousands of characters.
- 1000-900 BC: Greeks adapted Semitic consonantal signs to create a full vowel-marking alphabet.
- 1446: King Sejong introduced Hangul ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hangul)), a featural Korean writing system.
- the 19th century: Compulsory schooling spread literacy to much of western Europe and America.

## Interesting Facts
