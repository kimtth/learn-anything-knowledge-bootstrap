# Rio de Janeiro

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> Rio de Janeiro is Brazil's historic Atlantic port and former national capital, shaped by Guanabara Bay, mountains, beaches, neighbourhood contrasts, and a layered colonial, imperial, national, and cultural history. Its distinctive landscape frames urban growth, tourism, architecture, social inequality, and Brazilian identity.

## Introduction

Rio de Janeiro, in full Cidade de Sao Sebastiao do Rio de Janeiro, is a city and port on the Atlantic Ocean in southeastern Brazil. It is the capital of the state of Rio de Janeiro and remains one of the world's most recognizable urban symbols of Brazil, even though Brazil's national capital moved to Brasilia in 1960.

The city's name came from Portuguese navigators who arrived on January 1, 1502 and mistook Guanabara Bay for a river mouth. The future town was founded in 1565 and named for St. Sebastian and King Dom Sebastiao of Portugal. Rio became colonial capital in 1763 and capital of independent Brazil from 1822 to 1960.

Rio differs strongly from Brasilia and [[Sao Paulo]]. Brasilia is a planned interior capital dating from the 1960s, and Sao Paulo is a huge commercial and industrial centre. Rio is older, coastal, mountainous, scenic, and culturally distinctive, with urban life shaped by beaches, bays, hills, tunnels, favelas, and dramatic landmarks.

## Landscape

![[attachments/rio_de_janeiro_002.webp|420]]

*Guanabara Bay viewed from Sugarloaf Mountain*

Rio de Janeiro lies near the Tropic of Capricorn on an east-west stretch of Brazil's Atlantic coast, so much of the city faces south. Its original site was Guanabara Bay, whose entrance is marked by Sugar Loaf Mountain. The old core, or Centre, lies on plains along the bay's western shore.

The North Zone spreads northwest across plains, hills, and rocky mountains. The South Zone reaches the ocean beaches but is cut off from the Centre and North Zone by coastal mountains. The West Zone, long separated by mountains, became more accessible through roads and tunnels by the late 20th century.

## Climate

Greater Rio has a tropical, hot, humid climate, but ocean winds, mountains, and cold fronts from Antarctica create variation. Sea breezes moderate coastal temperatures. Summer showers can cause floods and landslides, especially where slopes and urban settlement are vulnerable.

Mountain barriers increase rainfall by trapping humid Atlantic winds. Jardim Botanico receives more than 63 inches, or 1,600 mm, annually. Winter from June to September is usually pleasant and less rainy, while summer from December to March is hotter and wetter. The average annual temperature is about 73 F, or 23 C.

## Centre

The Centre, or Cidade, corresponds roughly to the old city, though little colonial fabric survives because of repeated modernization. Hills were levelled, lagoons and swamps filled, streets widened, older buildings demolished, and the port rebuilt in 1907.

The Centre contains mixed architecture ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Architecture)) from many eras. The Municipal Theatre resembles the Paris Opera House. The Ministry of Education building of 1936, associated with Le Corbusier and Brazilian architects, represents Modernismo. Older low houses, pre-1940s buildings, postwar towers, and skyscrapers stand close together.

Praca 15 de Novembro shows this mixture clearly. It is bordered by colonial and religious buildings such as the Carmo Convent and church, as well as modern structures. The plaza reflects Rio's layered history as colonial city, imperial capital, national capital, and state capital.

## South Zone

![[attachments/rio_de_janeiro_001.webp|420]]

*Christ the Redeemer statue overlooking Rio de Janeiro*

The South Zone contains many of Rio's most famous views and neighbourhoods. Santa Teresa, reached by winding streets and trolley, overlooks older districts. Laranjeiras Palace, Guanabara Palace, Catete Palace, Flamengo beach, Botafogo Bay, Urca Hill, and Sugar Loaf shape a dense scenic landscape.

Mount Corcovado, rising to 2,310 feet or 704 metres, is crowned by the statue of Christ the Redeemer and reached by funicular railway or road. Nearby districts connect mountains, lagoon, beaches, museums, former ambassadorial residences, and parks into one of the city's most iconic urban settings.

The South Zone also includes Leblon, Ipanema, Copacabana, Leme, Lagoa, Jardim Botanico, and Gavea. These districts include affluent housing, luxury hotels, beaches, sidewalk cafes, universities, and cultural sites. Their shape is strongly determined by mountains, tunnels, lagoons, and the oceanfront.

## North Zone

![[attachments/rio_de_janeiro_003.webp|420]]

*Dense favela housing on a Rio de Janeiro hillside*

The North Zone is close to the Centre and South Zone but separated by mountain ridges, making travel difficult. Tijuca is a major middle-class district and connects to the subway. Nearby districts include Andarai, Grajau, Vila Isabel, Maracana, Rio Comprido, Catumbi, and Estacio.

Tijuca National Park occupies much of the rocky backbone between districts and offers waterfalls and views over the city. Maracana is home to the famed stadium. Sao Cristovao contains Quinta da Boa Vista, the National Museum, the zoo, and former imperial palace landscapes.

Industrial and transport corridors extend north through Avenida Brasil, Governador Island, the international airport, university sites, bridges to Niteroi, and large flatland favelas. Many other favelas occupy steep hillsides, reflecting how topography and poverty interact in Rio's urban form.

## West Zone

During the 1970s and 1980s, Rio expanded rapidly westward along the coast. Sao Conrado and Barra da Tijuca attracted upwardly mobile professionals seeking suburban-style luxury. Shopping malls, a convention centre, an autodrome, gated towers, schools, clubs, and boutiques supported this growth.

The Yellow Line Highway, opened in the late 1990s, improved access to inland areas beyond Tijuca by cutting tunnels through mountains. Growth spread toward Jacarepagua, Santa Cruz, Campo Grande, Guaratiba, and Sepetiba, extending the metropolitan region farther west as well as north.

## Suburbs

Rio's suburbs extend north within the municipality and into neighbouring municipalities. Since the 1950s, suburbs grew rapidly through migration from the interior of Rio de Janeiro state and other Brazilian states. Most suburban residents are poor, though each suburb includes areas of relative prosperity.

Government housing programs in the 1960s tried to move favela residents from central and North Zone areas to suburbs, but many resisted leaving places near work. Later programs focused more on improving favelas themselves. Major suburbs include Nova Iguacu, Sao Joao de Meriti, Belford Roxo, Duque de Caxias, Nilopolis, Petropolis, and Niteroi.

## People

Rio's inhabitants, called Cariocas, reflect Brazil's ethnic diversity. They include people of European, African, and mixed ancestry. Terms such as pardo and preto are used in Brazil, partly by skin colour and partly by self-identification. Many Brazilians of colour identify as pardos.

Cariocas are mainly Roman Catholic, though many also practice Umbanda. People of European ancestry are concentrated in affluent South Zone districts, Tijuca, and western coastal areas. Pardos and pretos are more concentrated in North Zone districts, northern suburbs, and favelas.

Rio's population grew heavily through domestic migration. Foreign immigration declined after government restrictions in the 1930s. By 1960, almost half the city population consisted of Brazilian migrants, especially from Rio de Janeiro, Minas Gerais, and Espirito Santo states. Portuguese, Italian, and Spanish immigrants were the largest foreign-born groups.

## Economy

Rio de Janeiro has a diverse economy with employment in industry, manufacturing, commerce, finance, trade, tourism, entertainment, education, communications, and other services. It remains Brazil's second-largest manufacturing centre after Sao Paulo and a major national service centre.

## Manufacturing

Greater Rio's manufacturing ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manufacturing)) includes metallurgy, engineering, printing, publishing, electronics, computers, shipyard materials, clothing, footwear, textiles, minerals, food, beverages, chemicals, and pharmaceuticals. Construction employs many unskilled workers and is supported by second-home building in the metropolitan area.

Oil and natural gas from fields off the northern coast of Rio de Janeiro state are major assets. The state encourages industrial districts with infrastructure and special land conditions. Corporate headquarters, Petrobras, the National Economic and Social Development Bank, export-import companies, financial markets, and banking also keep Rio economically important.

## Transportation

Greater Rio is a major [[Transportation|transportation]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transport)) centre. Galeao-Antonio Carlos Jobim airport handles domestic and international flights, while Santos Dumont serves important domestic routes. The port of Rio is among Brazil's leading ports by tonnage and has a large market area.

Surface transport depends heavily on bus, truck, and automobile routes. Rail links connect Rio with Sao Paulo and Belo Horizonte but have limited daily service. The subway began construction in 1972, opened its first station in 1979, and helps reduce traffic, though it is less extensive than systems in Sao Paulo or major Northern Hemisphere cities.

## Government and Municipal Services

The municipality is governed by a mayor, or prefeito, assisted by secretaries. Since 1984, the mayor has been popularly elected to a four-year term. The Municipal Chamber holds legislative power, and other municipalities in Greater Rio follow similar patterns.

Rio's mayor and the state governor are often political rivals because the city contains much of the state's population and wealth. Both state and municipal governments depend heavily on federal funding, so relations with Brasilia and the president matter. Urban services struggle to keep up with rapid growth, especially in suburbs and favelas.

## Health, Education, and Culture

Rio has a generally healthful climate, but favelas face disease risks from poor sanitation, inadequate diet, and limited health facilities. The metropolitan region still has one of Brazil's better ratios of people to hospital beds and doctors.

[[Education]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Education)) includes municipal primary schools, state secondary schools, and many universities. Major institutions include the Federal University of Brazil, Pontifical Catholic University, Fluminense Federal University, Candido Mendes University, Estacio de Sa University, and the State University of Rio de Janeiro.

Rio is a major cultural capital. Institutions include the Brazilian Academy of Letters, Brazilian Academy of Sciences, National Museum of Fine Arts, National Museum, National Historical Museum, Museum of Modern Art, Indian Museum, National Library, and Municipal Theatre. Music, theatre, dance, museums, libraries, and Carnival all reinforce Rio's cultural identity.

## History

French traders seeking brazilwood founded La France Antarctique in Guanabara Bay before the Portuguese secured the area. In 1565, Estacio de Sa, nephew of Mem de Sa, occupied the plain between Dog Face Hill, Sugar Loaf, and Urca, laying Rio's foundation. After fighting in 1565-67, the French were expelled and Estacio de Sa was killed.

In 1568, the settlement was laid out as a fortified town on Castle Hill. Sugarcane plantations became the main source of income, and in 1660 Rio became seat of government for Brazil's southern captaincies. Gold and diamond mining in the 18th century brought migration, wealth, and population growth.

Rio became colonial capital in 1763 and later capital of independent Brazil. In the mid-20th century, immigration and natural increase pushed metropolitan growth beyond older limits. The city proper exceeded 3.3 million by 1960, while suburban population more than tripled between 1940 and 1960.

After the capital moved to Brasilia in 1960, Rio's growth slowed, but suburbs continued expanding. By the early 21st century, the metropolitan population exceeded 11 million. Real estate speculation, automobiles, high-rise building, and favela expansion changed the city, pushing poorer residents toward peripheries and hillsides.

In 2009, the International Olympic Committee chose Rio de Janeiro to host the 2016 Summer Games, making it the first South American city selected for the Olympics.
