# Pre-Columbian Civilizations

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> Pre-Columbian civilizations were the complex agricultural societies of Mesoamerica and the Andes before Spanish conquest, including Olmec, Maya, Zapotec, Teotihuacan, Aztec, and Inca traditions. Their cities, states, monuments, calendars, writing, art, and religious systems grew from regional ecologies, farming, exchange, and political authority.

## Introduction

![[attachments/pre_columbian_003.webp|420]]

*Machu Picchu*

Pre-Columbian civilizations ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pre-Columbian_era)) were the American Indian cultures that developed in Meso-America and the Andean region before Spanish exploration and conquest in the 16th century. They ranked with the early civilizations of Egypt, Mesopotamia, and China because they produced kingdoms, empires, cities, monuments, writing, metallurgy, art, complex religion, and cycles of growth and decline.

The roots of these civilizations lay in native agriculture. Early plant cultivation began perhaps around 7000 BC, but permanent village farming appeared much later. By about 1500 BC, sedentary villages in Meso-America grew corn, beans, squash, chili peppers, and cotton, while people wove cloth, made pottery, and practiced Neolithic crafts.

These beginnings were slow and experimental. Domestication did not instantly create civilization; centuries of selecting plants, learning soils, and adjusting settlement patterns came first. Once farming villages became stable, craft production, ritual structures, and local leadership could grow on a more dependable food base.

After early village life, social complexity appeared quickly. Between about 1200 and 900 BC, large earthen pyramids, platforms, and monumental stone sculpture emerged in the southern Gulf coast region of Mexico. These works, now called Olmec, likely represented chiefs or rulers and marked the rise of more stratified political and religious life.

## Meso-American Civilization

![[attachments/pre_columbian_001.webp|420]]

*Teotihuacan pyramid*

Meso-America includes much of Mexico and Central America, especially all of Guatemala and Belize and parts of Honduras and El Salvador. It contains two major ecological zones: highlands and lowlands. Highland valleys and basins supported permanent agriculture, irrigation, and chinampas, while lowland farming often used shifting cultivation.

The northern and southeastern borders were cultural rather than simple political lines. Desert, highland basin, volcanic cordillera, Gulf lowland, Pacific slope, and Maya lowland environments all shaped settlement differently. Because agriculture worked differently in each zone, civilization grew through regional specialization as well as shared crops, calendars, ritual forms, and exchange.

The most important crops were corn, beans, and squashes, though cotton, chili peppers, and many other plants mattered. The contrast between highland and lowland environments shaped settlement, farming, political organization, and later civilization. Highland sites often became dense urban centres, while lowland Maya civilization developed ceremonial and political centres in tropical forest settings.

## Early Formative Period

The Early Formative period, roughly 1500-900 BC, saw the spread of settled village life. In the Pacific coasts of Chiapas and Guatemala, the Barra, Ocos, and Cuadros phases show small villages, corn farming, pottery, shellfish, fish, turtles, and thatched houses. Ocos pottery was technically and artistically developed.

Tiny handmade clay figurines, often nude females, suggest fertility concerns. Earthen mounds may have supported perishable buildings and perhaps early temples. These signs point to growing prosperity and the first social differentiation, especially where favourable lagoon-estuary environments supported larger, stable communities.

The Olmec civilization was the major development of this period. San Lorenzo and other Gulf coast centres produced colossal sculpture, were-jaguar imagery, pottery figurines, and a wide exchange network. Exotic materials such as obsidian, iron ore, serpentine, and fine stones show that Olmec elites controlled long-distance luxury trade.

San Lorenzo's stone monuments required great labour because the region lacked metal tools and local basalt sources. Artisans carved in the round by pounding and pecking, yet produced technically powerful heads, thrones, and were-jaguar figures. The absence of jade before about 900 BC also helps distinguish early Olmec exchange from later luxury networks.

Olmec influence reached the highlands. San Lorenzo-type ceramics and figurines appeared in burials in the Valley of Mexico and Morelos. The Olmec seem to have sent groups into highland regions to protect or manage trade routes, spreading religious and artistic ideas across much of Meso-America.

## Middle and Late Formative Periods

The Middle Formative period, about 900-300 BC, was marked by pottery horizons and rising regionalism. Hard white pottery with incised lines and solid figurines with large staring eyes spread widely. Olmec presence remained visible, but local traditions became stronger as populations filled villages, hamlets, and small towns.

La Venta became the leading Olmec centre between about 800 and 400 BC. It had a 100-foot mound, plazas, long mounds, a basalt-column enclosure, buried offerings, and monuments. Its ceremonial layout suggests rulers, tombs, religion, and political display, even though its island setting was difficult to reach.

At Monte Alban, near Oaxaca, one hill was leveled to become the Zapotec capital. The danzante reliefs likely show dead or sacrificed enemies, and some carry calendrical or hieroglyphic signs. This indicates that the Middle Formative elite at Monte Alban were among the first in Meso-America to develop writing and a written calendar.

The Late Formative period, about 300 BC-AD 100, transformed earlier traditions. Olmec civilization in southeastern Meso-America moved toward early lowland Maya forms, and urban culture appeared at Teotihuacan in the Valley of Mexico. Temple-pyramids, elite burials, complex pottery, and regional cultures became common.

Temple-pyramids likely developed from older house and platform traditions. Because many Meso-American peoples buried the dead beneath house floors and abandoned those dwellings, elite lineages could enlarge a house-platform into a funerary and ceremonial monument. A ruler, an ancestor, and a patron deity could then be honoured together at the centre of settlement.

## Early Classic Period and Teotihuacan

![[attachments/pre_columbian_002.webp|420]]

*Maya stela*

The Classic period is often defined through Maya dated monuments, but for much of Meso-America the Early Classic was the real golden age. Teotihuacan and lowland Maya civilization were the two leading cultures. One was urban and expansionist; the other was less urban but intellectually and artistically extraordinary.

Teotihuacan was probably the largest New World city before the Spaniards. By the late 6th century AD it covered about eight square miles and may have housed more than 150,000 people. Its grid plan, Avenue of the Dead, Pyramid of the Sun, Pyramid of the Moon, and planned quarters show massive state power and urban design.

The city's orientation and planning suggest organized construction from an early stage. Major avenues were laid out near the beginning of the Christian Era, and the Pyramid of the Sun rose in great stages facing the Avenue of the Dead. Such planning required labour coordination, food supply, authority, and ceremonial vision on a huge scale.

Teotihuacan could not have depended only on its own valley for food. Its scale implies broad economic control, trade, and tribute. Its art and architecture influenced many regions, including Cholula, Monte Alban, and Maya areas. The talud-tablero style became a major architectural marker of its sphere.

Cholula was another major centre, famous for the largest single structure in pre-conquest Mexico. Its pyramid was built in four construction stages and used Teotihuacan architectural ideas. Its similarities with Teotihuacan suggest that it may have been a sister city, an allied centre, or part of the same cultural and political sphere.

By the time Spaniards saw Cholula, the ancient pyramid looked like a natural hill. Archaeology revealed superimposed adobe structures with Teotihuacan-style talud-tablero forms and painted decoration. Its scale, pottery, and figurines show that Early Classic highland networks were not confined to a single capital.

Classic Central Veracruz developed a distinctive ball game culture. The game used a solid rubber ball struck mainly with the hip, and players wore protective belts, pads, and gear. Stone yokes, hachas, and palmas are linked to ritual equipment, ball courts, and perhaps sacrifice after games.

Southern Veracruz sites such as Cerro de las Mesas mixed Olmec traditions with Teotihuacan and Maya traits. Stelae, slab-legged tripod pottery, hollow deity figures, and a cache of about 800 jade objects show continuity, exchange, and hybrid artistry. Remojadas figurines, including smiling figures, became famous burial goods.

## Classic Monte Alban and the Maya

Classic Monte Alban, about AD 250-700, was the major Zapotec centre in Oaxaca. Its platforms, stairways, ball courts, tombs, murals, gray ware pottery, and evidence of Teotihuacan influence show a powerful local civilization that developed without suffering the same early disruptions as Teotihuacan and the Maya.

The lowland Maya Classic is divided into Tzakol and Tepeu phases. Early monuments such as the Leiden Plate and Tikal Stela 29 show the developed Maya calendar, royal imagery, and narrative art. Maya centres such as Tikal, Uaxactun, and Holmul had temples, palaces, plazas, white stucco surfaces, and corbel-vaulted rooms.

Late Classic Maya sites raise debate over whether they were cities or ceremonial centres. Tikal had about 3,000 structures within six square miles, and perhaps 10,000-11,000 people in the central survey area, with many more tied to its wider territory. Settlement was often dispersed in household groups around open spaces, zones, and districts rather than planned streets.

Maya achievements in mathematics and astronomy were among the greatest in the ancient world. They used positional numeration and zero, calculated the solar year and Venus cycle with great accuracy, and created eclipse tables. Their calendar included the 260-day tzolkin, the 365-day haab, and Long Count cycles from a zero date in 3114 BC.

The sacred calendar worked by combining 13 numbers with 20 named days, while the solar calendar used 18 months of 20 days plus five unlucky days. A 52-year cycle linked the two shorter counts, but the Long Count allowed rulers and priests to anchor events across far longer spans of historical and cosmic time.

The Long Count made historical time visible on monuments. A Maya date could record baktuns, katuns, tuns, uinals, and days from the zero point, then link that count with sacred and solar calendars. This let rulers place accessions, wars, rituals, and dedications inside a cosmic chronology.

## Maya Religion and Society

Classic Maya religion included gods later known from Postclassic codices. Itzamna was the supreme creator, linked to fire and the hearth. The Feathered Serpent, known as Kukulcan among the Maya, appears in Classic reliefs. Bolon Tzacab was linked to royal descent and often shown as a sceptre.

The Maya paid great attention to royal ancestors. Tomb pottery and reliefs described the underworld and the soul's dangerous journey. Human sacrifice existed, especially of captives and nobles, though it was not as large in scale as among the Aztecs. Bloodletting through ears, tongue, or genitals was a major offering.

Maya sources include archaeological remains, hieroglyphic inscriptions, codices, native books in Latin script, Spanish accounts, and modern ethnology. The Dresden, Madrid, Paris, and Grolier codices preserve astronomy, divination, and ritual. The Books of Chilam Balam and the Popol Vuh preserve myth, history, prophecy, and cosmology.

These sources differ sharply in date and purpose. Stone monuments and tomb objects come from ancient ceremonial settings, while Spanish-era writings often preserve Postclassic or colonial memory. Missionary descriptions, native chronicles, hieroglyphic texts, codices, and modern Maya traditions must therefore be compared carefully rather than treated as one identical record.

Maya creation stories describe earlier worlds destroyed before the present one. People were made first of earth, then wood, and finally corn gruel. The universe had 13 heavens above and nine underworlds below. The ceiba tree, world directions, colours, Bacabs, Sun, Moon, and divinized time cycles formed an elaborate cosmology.

Time itself had divine force in Maya thought. Priests used past cycles to interpret future dangers, especially katun endings, and inscriptions could project calculations over enormous intervals. This made astronomy, mathematics, myth, and rulership mutually reinforcing rather than separate fields.

Classic Maya cosmology joined elite speculation with older nature worship. Corn and rain gods remained central to agricultural life, while priestly knowledge added heavenly bodies, time periods, world directions, and layered heavens and underworlds. Religion therefore linked peasant fertility concerns with royal and astronomical ideology.

Maya rites included incense, balche drink, bloodletting, fasting, abstinence, animal and human sacrifice, dances, New Year ceremonies, calendar rituals, ball games, and phallic rites in some regions. Sorcerers and medicine men used prayers, chants, magic formulas, medicinal herbs, bleeding, and prophecy.

Maya society was deeply stratified. Lavish elite tombs contrast with simple peasant graves. Rulers and nobles were believed to be separate from commoners and descended from gods. Warfare was common, often to gain captives for slavery and sacrifice, and rulers combined sacred ancestry with political power.

## Postclassic Meso-America

The Postclassic period began about AD 900 and ended with the Spanish conquest in the early 16th century. It was once contrasted sharply with the Classic as more militaristic, urban, secular, imperial, and artistically declining. Later research complicates that view because Classic Teotihuacan and Monte Alban already had cities, states, militarism, and large-scale organization.

The Toltec are central to Postclassic tradition. Many later dynasties claimed descent from them, treating them as first civilizers, city builders, and originators of craft and astrological knowledge. The capital Tollan, or Tula, is usually identified with the archaeological site near modern Tula, though its scale does not match the legends of a vast empire.

Postclassic histories often begin with migration legends. Groups described themselves as wandering from the north, passing through trials, and finally gaining a homeland and dynastic success. The word Chichimeca could refer to true northern hunters and gatherers, but it was also applied to rustic agricultural peoples whose movement into older civilized areas became part of political memory.

Such legends were political tools as well as memories. Dynasties used Toltec descent, migration stories, omens, and divine sponsorship to explain why they deserved power. The same accounts can preserve real movements of people while reshaping them into origin stories of election, hardship, conquest, and legitimacy.

Tula had strong Quetzalcoatl symbolism and a population estimated at 30,000-60,000. Its art and architecture influenced central Mexico and Yucatan, but it was smaller than Teotihuacan and later Tenochtitlan. Its relationship with Chichen Itza remains debated because Chichen's public architecture was often more monumental.

Chichen Itza in northern Yucatan shows close Toltec-style features: serpent columns, colonnaded halls, warrior imagery, skulls, felines, heart-eating birds, and structures resembling Tula forms. A likely explanation is an independent Toltec state in Yucatan, larger in area and population than Tula's central Mexican core.

The Postclassic unity of Meso-America was not a simple empire. It included shared styles, military symbols, trade, religious borrowings, and claims of Toltec descent, but local states still followed their own histories. Cholula, Xochicalco, Tula, Chichen Itza, and later Tenochtitlan show overlapping rather than identical political worlds.

## Aztec Culture

At Spanish contact, the dominant Meso-American power was the Aztec. Knowledge comes from conquistador accounts, missionary writings, Spanish-language histories, codices, Nahuatl texts, tribute records, and archaeology. These sources must be used carefully because many were shaped by conquest, Christian hostility to native religion, and postconquest change.

The sources nevertheless are unusually rich. Conquistadors described markets, temples, and warfare from direct observation, missionaries learned Nahuatl and analyzed ritual, and native scribes preserved tribute, history, herbs, calendars, and dynastic memory in pictorial and alphabetic forms. Their biases are real, but together they make Aztec society unusually visible.

The Aztec homeland was the Basin of Mexico, a high mountain-rimmed basin with lakes, frost risks, uneven rainfall, and limited level land. Despite these limits, by 1519 it supported perhaps 1,000,000 to 1,500,000 people through intensive farming, including chinampas, terracing, irrigation, fertilization, and careful soil management.

Tenochtitlan was a huge metropolis covering more than five square miles. Built on islands in Lake Texcoco, it expanded by chinampa construction and consolidation. With Tlatelolco and nearby settlements, it formed a vast urban and agricultural complex. Its population in 1519 was about 400,000, the densest concentration in Meso-American history.

The Aztec market at Tlatelolco reportedly drew 60,000 buyers and sellers on major market days. Canoe traffic moved goods across the lake. Craft guilds, merchants, priests, warriors, administrators, and specialist producers show a highly integrated economy supported by tribute, trade, and intensive agriculture.

Aztec religion viewed the present world as the fifth sun. Four earlier worlds had been destroyed by jaguars, wind, fire rain, and flood. The Aztec saw themselves as the People of the Sun, responsible for feeding the sun with blood and hearts so the universe would continue.

Aztec cosmology divided the earth into four directions plus the centre, with colours, gods, and day signs. There were 13 heavens and nine underworld levels. Deities included Tlaloc, Chalchiuhtlicue, Coatlicue, Xipe Totec, Huitzilopochtli, Tezcatlipoca, Quetzalcoatl, and many fertility, rain, earth, war, and death gods.

The tonalpohualli, a 260-day ritual calendar, governed divination and daily life. Priests interpreted day signs and number combinations for birth, marriage, travel, rulership, and other actions. Aztec worldview gave humans a small place in a cosmic order ruled by destiny, gods, omens, sacrifice, and warfare.

Aztec ritual specialists interpreted the calendar and managed ceremonies for rulers and commoners. Their work connected births, marriages, trade journeys, warfare, building, confession, and sacrifice with divine forces. The same system that organized private fate also justified imperial warfare and the collection of tribute and captives.

## Andean Civilization

Andean civilization developed in western South America for millennia before the Spanish invasion of Peru in 1532. It included coastal deserts, high mountains, valleys, puna grasslands, and Amazonian edges. Although no modern state covers all former Inca territory, Tawantinsuyu once united parts of Ecuador, Peru, Bolivia, Chile, and Argentina.

The Andes encouraged ecological complementarity, also called the vertical archipelago. Political centres were often high in the mountains, while related communities lived days away in lower corn fields, coca gardens, fishing settlements, forests, or coastal enclaves. This dispersed pattern let groups control resources from several ecological tiers.

Outlier communities were often multiethnic. Different political centres shared settlements of salt miners, fishers, coca cultivators, timber cutters, honey gatherers, and other specialists. The system reduced risk, expanded storage, and made possible dense populations and large polities such as Chavin, Huari, Tiwanaku, Cuzco, and the Inca.

The vertical archipelago was more than seasonal migration. Some outliers were too distant for ordinary back-and-forth movement, so they were permanent colonies tied to a highland political centre. This arrangement let one ethnic group combine puna herds, maize fields, coca gardens, timber, honey, fish, and seaweed without depending on a single fragile environment.

## Pre-Inca Andes

The Late Preceramic period had human occupation by 3500 BC, with coastal peoples cultivating beans, squash, cotton, and chili peppers while fishing with nets and hooks. Some groups used lomas vegetation in winter and coastal camps in summer; later they moved toward permanent fishing villages.

The Initial Period, Early Horizon, Early Intermediate Period, Middle Horizon, Late Intermediate Period, and Late Horizon are archaeological divisions used because ancient names are often unknown. These periods track changes in coastal and highland societies, temple centres, ceramics, textiles, metallurgy, and regional political power.

Andean archaeology shows repeated interaction between desert coast and high mountains. Coastal peoples preserved rich remains in dry conditions, while highland groups controlled puna herds, tubers, grains, and ritual centres. Political power rose in alternating waves, not as one continuous empire.

Chavin, Huari, and Tiwanaku were major highland and regional centres before the Inca. Coastal cultures also achieved artistic and technological sophistication. Across Andean history, local hegemony rose and fell, while long-distance exchange and ecological complementarity linked coast, highlands, and eastern slopes.

The archaeological periods used for the Andes are modern tools rather than ancient names. Late Preceramic, Initial Period, Early Horizon, Early Intermediate, Middle Horizon, Late Intermediate, and Late Horizon mark shifts in pottery, temples, textiles, metallurgy, settlement, and political reach. They help compare coast and highlands even when native names are lost.

The Late Intermediate Period saw strong regional societies before Inca expansion. Coastal valleys and highland polities built traditions in ceramics, textiles, irrigation, settlement, fortification, and warfare. These were complex worlds that the Inca later had to conquer, ally with, or administer.

The Chincha illustrate coastal specialization in this period. Spanish reports describe many households of high-seas fishers, sailors, and long-distance traders who maintained exchange northward toward warmer waters. Demand for Spondylus shell among highland peoples tied maritime skill to rainmaking, ritual, and interregional value.

## The Inca

The Inca of Cuzco were latecomers to much of the area they ruled. Their empire, Tawantinsuyu, expanded rapidly and incorporated many ethnic groups whose resentment later helped the Spanish. Its recent expansion made consolidation incomplete by 1532.

Inca expansion depended on Cuzco as a highland centre and on older Andean habits of storage, labour service, and regional lordship. The empire could move goods and people across deserts, valleys, and mountains because it organized existing local production rather than replacing every community with a uniform imperial pattern.

Inca administration relied on roads, storage, labour obligations, state warehouses, local lords, and quipu record keeping. Don Francisco Cusichaq of Xauxa later testified that his bookkeepers recorded what the Spanish received from Xauxa warehouses and what was stolen, showing how Andean record systems continued into the early colonial period.

The empire's scale was extraordinary but recent. By 1532 it ruled millions of people speaking many languages across territory from Ecuador through Peru and Bolivia into Chile and Argentina. A century earlier, Inca power had been largely confined near Cuzco, so conquest created both imperial capacity and many unresolved resentments.

The Inca state did not erase older Andean patterns. It built on highland centres, vertical ecological control, local ethnic authority, and stored production. Its success depended on mobilizing labour and resources across difficult terrain, from Pacific coast to high puna and beyond.

Because Tawantinsuyu expanded rapidly, many incorporated groups were recently conquered when the Spanish arrived. Local rivalries, resentments among ethnic lords, and the difficulty of controlling distant ecological zones weakened imperial cohesion and gave the invaders political openings.

The Spanish conquest therefore met powerful states, but also societies whose recent expansion had left unresolved local tensions and fragile alliances.

Pre-Columbian civilizations were not isolated curiosities but complex regional systems. Mesoamerica, the Andes, the Caribbean, Amazonia, and North American cultural regions developed different combinations of farming, trade, ritual, settlement, hierarchy, and political power. Maize, potatoes, beans, squash, cotton, cacao, llamas, roads, markets, calendars, and tribute systems all supported distinct social worlds.

Urbanism took several forms. Teotihuacan, Maya cities, Tenochtitlan, Monte Alban, Chan Chan, Cuzco, and Andean ceremonial centres were not simply large settlements; they organized labour, ritual, politics, craft production, storage, exchange, and sacred geography. Their plazas, pyramids, temples, palaces, ball courts, roads, and terraces shaped public life and state authority.

Religion and power were closely linked. Rulers claimed legitimacy through descent, conquest, ritual performance, calendars, sacrifice, ancestor cults, mountain or sun symbolism, and control of sacred centres. Priests, diviners, scribes, astronomers, and specialists helped connect political authority to cosmic order.

The diversity of writing and record keeping matters. Mesoamerican scripts, Maya glyphs, Mixtec pictorial manuscripts, Aztec codices, Andean quipu, oral history, monuments, and colonial-era testimonies preserve different kinds of memory. Some systems recorded phonetic language; others encoded numbers, genealogies, tribute, ritual, or political events through images and knots.

Agriculture required technical skill. Terracing, irrigation, raised fields, chinampas, hillside farming, storage systems, and crop selection allowed dense populations in environments ranging from tropical lowlands to high mountains and dry coasts. These techniques show that political power rested on environmental knowledge as much as on warfare.

The Spanish conquest transformed these civilizations but did not instantly erase them. Indigenous communities survived through language, tribute obligations, local offices, religion, landholding, craft traditions, and adaptation to colonial rule. Pre-Columbian history therefore continues into colonial and modern Indigenous life, not only into ruins and artifacts.

## Environment, Power, and Cultural Continuity

Pre-Columbian civilizations ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pre-Columbian_era)) were the many societies of the Americas before sustained European conquest and colonization. They ranged from small mobile communities to large cities and empires. Their histories cannot be reduced to a few famous names because North, Central, and South America contained thousands of languages, environments, political systems, and artistic traditions. Coastal fisheries, forests, deserts, high mountains, river valleys, and tropical lowlands each encouraged different ways of producing food and organizing society.

Maize agriculture was especially important in Mesoamerica, where people developed farming systems based on maize, beans, squash, chilies, cacao, and other crops. In the Andes, communities cultivated potatoes, quinoa, maize, and many high-altitude plants while raising llamas and alpacas. Terracing, irrigation, raised fields, chinampas, storage systems, and selective breeding show that agricultural knowledge was sophisticated and adapted to demanding environments.

The Maya developed city-states, writing, mathematics, astronomy, monumental architecture, and complex calendars across parts of present-day Mexico and Central America. Teotihuacan became one of the largest cities in the ancient world and influenced a wide region. The Aztec-led Mexica empire used alliance, tribute, warfare, markets, and religious ceremony to govern central Mexico. These societies changed over time through drought, migration, war, trade, internal conflict, and new political alliances.

In the Andes, the Inca Empire connected diverse regions through roads, bridges, storehouses, labour obligations, administrative centres, and terrace agriculture. The Incas did not use alphabetic writing like the Maya, but they kept information through quipu—knotted cords used for counting and administration. Earlier Andean societies, including Chavín, Moche, Nazca, Tiwanaku, and Wari, developed distinctive art, religion, engineering, and political systems long before Inca expansion.

European arrival brought disease, warfare, forced labour, mission systems, and colonial rule that caused catastrophic population loss and political disruption. Yet Indigenous continuity did not end. Native peoples preserved languages, farming, art, ceremonies, kinship systems, and claims to land while adapting under colonial and modern conditions. Pre-Columbian history is therefore not only a prelude to conquest; it is part of the continuing history of Indigenous peoples throughout the Americas.

## Key Events & Developments

- about 7000 BC: Early Americans experimented with plant cultivation.
- 1500 BC: Sedentary village farming existed in Meso-America.
- 1200-900 BC: Olmec monuments and pyramids signaled new social complexity.
- 800-400 BC: La Venta was the leading Olmec centre.
- AD 100-600: Teotihuacan dominated Early Classic highland Meso-America.
- AD 600-900: Lowland Maya civilization reached major intellectual and artistic heights.
- AD 900-1519: The Postclassic included Toltec and Aztec powers.
- 1532: Spanish forces entered the Inca world in Peru.
