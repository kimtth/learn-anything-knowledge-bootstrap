## Introduction

![[attachments/mental_disorders_002.webp|420]]

*Brain illustration*

Mental disorders ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mental_disorder)) are illnesses with important psychological or behavioral signs that cause distress or impair daily functioning. They receive increased attention because physical diseases have become more controllable, mental illness remains common, and the public expects medical and mental health systems to reduce suffering and disability.

No single definition is universally satisfactory. Culture affects what counts as abnormal, a narrow definition would require structural or biochemical brain disease, and an overly broad one would treat almost any lack of mental well-being as illness. Clinical judgment therefore combines distress, impairment, context, and evidence.

The field covers both consequences and treatment. Mental disorders can disable people, disrupt families, and require coordinated medical, psychological, and social care. Their study therefore includes symptoms, diagnosis, causes, classification, epidemiology, individual syndromes, and the historical shift from confinement toward therapy and community care.

## Classification

[[Diagnosis and Therapeutics (Diagnosis)|Diagnosis]] identifies mental illness through symptoms, history, interviews, observation, psychological tests, and sometimes physical or neurological examination. Mental health practitioners use diagnosis to choose treatment, communicate with colleagues, predict course, and support research, but classification is difficult because many syndromes overlap and causes are often uncertain.

The diagnostic process also depends on the patient's personal history, current situation, interaction with the practitioner, and observable behavior. A preliminary assessment may later change as symptoms evolve or as medical causes are excluded, so classification is a working clinical tool rather than a final explanation of cause.

## Classification Systems

Two main classification systems organize mental disorders: the World Health Organization's ICD and the American Psychiatric Association's DSM. The ICD is widely used for international and administrative purposes, while the DSM introduced detailed diagnostic criteria and was revised repeatedly from 1952 to DSM-5 in 2013.

The DSM's detailed criteria improved consistency, but its broad categories and limited use of biological markers remain controversial. Its abandonment of the older psychosis-neurosis framework also created a gap between formal classification and terms still widely used in clinical speech.

## Psychoses

Psychoses are severe disorders marked by delusions, hallucinations, disordered thought, and impaired judgment or insight. Affected people may be unable to function in everyday life and may not recognize that their perceptions conflict with reality. Older distinctions separated organic psychoses from functional psychoses.

The severe disturbance in thought, emotion, and behavior makes psychosis different from ordinary distress. Subjective experience can become detached from objective reality, and the person may be confused by the outside world while still feeling intense distress.

## Neuroses

![[attachments/mental_disorders_003.webp|420]]

*Portrait of Sigmund Freud*

Neuroses are less severe than psychoses but can still seriously impair life through anxiety, depression, and exaggerated defensive reactions. Reality testing and personality remain mostly intact, and patients usually know they are ill and want recovery, giving them better prospects than many psychotic patients.

Neurotic symptoms may resemble everyday coping mechanisms, but they become unusually intense, prolonged, or disabling. The difference is not that anxiety or sadness is unknown in ordinary life, but that the defensive reaction becomes poorly matched to the situation.

## Epidemiology

Epidemiology studies how mental disorders are distributed across populations. Prevalence counts existing cases, incidence counts new cases, and social or cultural context helps explain patterns. Schizophrenia, for example, has a lifetime risk of about 1 percent across many different cultures.

Treatment statistics can understate true prevalence because many affected people never seek professional help. Surveys are also shaped by diagnostic definitions, interviews, and cultural expectations, making long-term changes in rates difficult to prove even when some disorders appear more common.

## Genetics

Genetic research compares illness rates in relatives, twins, and families. First-degree relatives share about 50 percent of genetic material, so higher illness rates among them suggest inherited risk. Twin studies compare identical and fraternal twins to separate genetic influence from shared environment.

## Biochemistry

Biochemical theories seek chemical differences in the brain, but direct study of living human brain chemistry is difficult. Blood, urine, cerebrospinal fluid, animal models, and postmortem tissue can be misleading, and abnormalities may be causes, effects, or side results rather than simple explanations.

Psychiatric drugs nevertheless support the importance of neurotransmitters. Antidepressant, antipsychotic, and antianxiety drugs alter the amount, action, or breakdown of messengers such as norepinephrine, dopamine, acetylcholine, and serotonin across synaptic gaps between neurons.

## Neuropathology

Neuropathology studies brain tissue to explain mental symptoms. The discovery that general paresis was neurosyphilis caused by Treponema pallidum showed how brain disease could produce psychiatric illness. Dementias such as Alzheimer disease and arteriosclerosis also revealed links between brain pathology and mental disorder.

## Psychodynamic Theory

Sigmund Freud's psychodynamic theory explained neurotic symptoms as products of unconscious conflict among motives, wishes, drives, and feelings. The unconscious was treated as a store of threatening memories and impulses that remain active even when pushed out of awareness, producing anxiety and symptoms through defenses.

## Schizophrenia

Schizophrenia ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Schizophrenia)) was named by Eugen Bleuler in 1911 and replaced Emil Kraepelin's dementia praecox. It includes varied symptoms, so experts may disagree on defining features. Annual prevalence is about two to four cases per 1,000 people, and lifetime risk is about 1 percent.

Symptoms of schizophrenia may include hallucinations, delusions, disorganized thinking, emotional disturbance, and social or occupational impairment. Because the syndrome is broad, clinical agreement often depends on diagnostic criteria rather than one single symptom.

## Etiology

The etiology of schizophrenia includes genetic risk, stressful life events, possible birth-related brain damage, neurological signs, viral hypotheses, and biochemical factors. Early 21st-century studies found higher risk among children born to fathers older than 50, while stress can trigger onset or relapse.

Treatment commonly combines medication with supportive therapy. Atypical antipsychotics such as clozapine, risperidone, and olanzapine can reduce delusions, hallucinations, thought disorder, agitation, and violent behaviour, and maintenance treatment lowers relapse risk.

## Hypochondriasis

Hypochondriasis is unrealistic preoccupation with bodily signs and fear of serious disease. Normal sensations or minor symptoms are interpreted as dangerous, and reassurance after physical examination may not persuade the person. It can occur with anxiety, depression, or schizophrenia.

## Depersonalization

Depersonalization makes the self or body feel unreal, distant, altered, or dreamlike. Derealization may make external objects feel unreal too. It often appears with anxiety, phobic, or depressive symptoms, especially in younger women, and organic conditions such as temporal lobe epilepsy must be excluded.

## Paraphilias

Paraphilias are recurrent sexually arousing fantasies, urges, or behaviors that last at least six months and cause distress or impairment. Examples include fetishism, transvestism, pedophilia, exhibitionism, and voyeurism, each defined by the object, situation, or behavior tied to arousal.

## Eating Disorders

Eating disorders include anorexia nervosa, which usually begins in late adolescence and is far more common in girls. It involves failure to maintain normal weight, intense fear of weight gain, distorted body perception, and sometimes amenorrhea. Medical complications can be life-threatening.

Anorexia nervosa involves body image, fear, and behavior rather than appetite alone. Weight loss of at least 15 percent below ideal body weight and disturbed perception of shape or weight are central clinical signs.

## Childhood Disorders

Childhood psychiatric disorders include stereotyped movement disorders, tics, Tourette syndrome, stuttering, enuresis, encopresis, sleepwalking, and night terror. These symptoms are not always signs of emotional disturbance, but they can become clinically important when persistent or impairing.

Child assessment depends heavily on observed behaviour because children may not be able to describe feelings clearly. Family problems, especially parent-child difficulties, can contribute to symptoms, but isolated tics, enuresis, or sleepwalking do not automatically prove a broader mental illness.

## Ancient Explanations

Ancient mental illness explanations often invoked gods, demons, or punishment, but some Greek physicians treated disorders as natural diseases. Hippocrates emphasized physiology, observation, and brain pathology, while later Greek and Roman writers recommended quiet settings, work, and drugs.

This early naturalistic medical view mattered because it opposed punishment, banishment, or exorcism as the only responses. It treated disturbed behavior as something to observe, classify, and care for, even though later periods repeatedly returned to supernatural explanations.

## Medieval Care

During the Middle Ages, demonology and witchcraft explanations returned in Europe, though religious orders sometimes cared for the mentally ill. From the Islamic world and later Europe came hospitals and custodial care, but treatment often mixed protection, confinement, and harsh ideas about madness.

## Humane Reform

Reformers promoted humane treatment in the 18th and 19th centuries. William Tuke founded the York Retreat in 1796, Vincenzo Chiarugi described humane hospital care in 1788, and Dorothea Dix exposed inhumane American mental hospitals in the mid-19th century, helping spread reforms.

## Deinstitutionalization

Deinstitutionalization ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deinstitutionalisation)) reduced long-term mental hospital populations after about 1950. England and Wales fell from nearly 150,000 hospitalized patients in 1954 to 75,000 in 1980, while the United States fell from just under 560,000 in 1955 to just over 130,000 in 1980. Policy shifted toward community care, though community resources were often uneven.

## Psychosurgery

Psychosurgery destroys selected brain cells or fibers to relieve severe psychiatric symptoms. Classical prefrontal lobotomy is no longer used because it often caused epilepsy, apathy, passivity, reduced initiative, and lasting personality change. Later operations became more restricted and precise.

The distinction between psychosurgery and ordinary brain surgery is important: removing a tumor that causes psychiatric symptoms is not psychosurgery. Psychosurgery was aimed directly at symptoms such as chronic self-destruction, delusion, agitation, or violence when other approaches had failed.

## Modern Psychotherapy

Carl Rogers emphasized empathy, warmth, and a nonjudgmental therapeutic relationship, reflecting humanistic psychology's belief in personal growth. Existential and Gestalt therapies focused on meaning, authentic encounter, and present experience, while other treatments used behavioral, cognitive, family, group, drug, and biological approaches.

Modern psychotherapy therefore includes several assumptions about change. Some approaches explore unconscious conflict, some reshape behavior or thinking, some work with families or groups, and humanistic approaches rely on the therapeutic relationship as a setting where patients can discover and use their own capacity for growth.

Behaviour therapy grew from learning theory associated with Pavlov, Watson, Skinner, and later clinical work. It treats maladaptive habits as learned responses that can be weakened while adaptive habits are built, especially in phobias where avoidance responses become the treatment target.

## Core Equations

Health and behavioural sciences often quantify risk, dose, change over time, and population variation:

$$C(t)=C_0e^{-kt}$$

This models concentration falling exponentially over time, often used for clearance, decay, or elimination processes.

$$z=\frac{x-\mu}{\sigma}$$

A z-score tells how many standard deviations a value lies above or below the mean.

$$\text{risk}=\frac{\text{cases}}{\text{population}}$$

## Key Events & Developments

- 1911: Eugen Bleuler introduced the term schizophrenia.
- 1952: The DSM began its modern series of diagnostic manuals.
- 1955-1980: U.S. mental hospital populations dropped sharply during deinstitutionalization.
- 2013: DSM-5 was presented.
