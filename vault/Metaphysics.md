# Metaphysics

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> Metaphysics studies the most general nature and structure of reality, asking what it means for things to exist and how concepts such as substance, mind, matter, space, time, causation, and God fit together. Its tradition runs from Aristotle through medieval and modern systems to contemporary analysis.

## Introduction

![[attachments/metaphysics_001.webp|420]]

*Bust of Aristotle*

Metaphysics ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metaphysics)) is the philosophical study of the real nature of things: the meaning, structure, and principles of whatever exists. Although often treated as overly abstract, metaphysicians present it as the most fundamental inquiry because it asks about reality as a whole. The term originally meant what came after physics in the ordering of Aristotle's writings, but it came to name inquiry into being, substance, God, mind, matter, space, time, causation, and the conditions of experience.

Its scope differs from ordinary science because it asks about the assumptions behind all particular inquiries. A science may study living things, motion, or society, while metaphysics asks what it means for anything to exist, what categories are needed to speak of reality, and whether common-sense belief, scientific description, and religious or moral claims can fit into one view of the world.

Aristotle's distinction between second philosophy and first philosophy set a lasting pattern. Natural philosophy studies changing things in the sensible world; first philosophy studies being as such and the unmoved or most real substance on which nature depends. Later metaphysics repeatedly returned to this double task: explaining ordinary reality while asking whether ordinary reality points beyond itself.

## Common Sense

G.E. Moore is linked with a modern analytic resistance to speculative metaphysics. Common-sense philosophers used ordinary certainties to challenge sweeping claims that denied familiar things such as bodies, time, or external objects.

## Philosophical Analysis

Philosophical analysis ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philosophical_analysis)) often opposed itself to metaphysics by treating metaphysical systems as speculative world theories. Yet analysis still raises metaphysical questions when it asks what kinds of things exist, what concepts presuppose, and how different parts of knowledge depend on deeper categories.

The analytic objection was not merely stylistic. It challenged the metaphysician's claim to reveal hidden facts about reality as a whole. Still, questions about common sense ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Common_sense)), external objects, other minds, and universals kept returning, showing that analysis could criticize metaphysics without escaping its basic problems.

## Forms

![[attachments/metaphysics_002.webp|420]]

*School of Athens with Plato and Aristotle*

Early Greek philosophers asked what truly exists. Pythagoras answered with number and intelligible structure, while [[Plato and Platonism (Plato)|Plato]] developed the theory of Forms: stable realities grasped by intellect rather than sense. This shifted metaphysics from material stuff toward structure, essence, and intelligibility.

The Forms also carried a moral and epistemological purpose. They explained how knowledge can be more stable than opinion and how values such as goodness or justice can be more than changing social habits. The sensible world was real enough for daily life, but it was not the final object of philosophical understanding.

Platonism gained force from the experience that familiar things appear unstable, mixed, and contradictory. A beautiful object can also lack beauty in some respect, while the Form of Beauty is beautiful without qualification. This made metaphysical knowledge a discipline of conversion: the thinker must move from changing appearances and inherited opinions toward intelligible structures that make truth and value possible.

## The Existence of God

St. Anselm stands for the classical question of God's existence. In metaphysics, God is treated as the perfect being or most real of all things, and arguments try to show whether such a being must be recognized beyond ordinary objects of experience.

## The Ontological Argument

The ontological argument associated with Anselm is an a priori argument: it reasons from the idea of a perfect being to existence. Later philosophers disputed whether existence can be established by pure reasoning, but the argument became a central model of metaphysical proof.

Other arguments for God used premises drawn from the world rather than from definition alone. Aquinas argued from motion, contingency, and order: changing things require a first mover, dependent beings require a necessary source, and the coordination of nature points to an ordering cause. These arguments made theology a central metaphysical test case because they connect finite experience with claims about necessary being.

## Phenomena and Noumena

Immanuel Kant distinguished things in themselves, or noumena, from appearances, or phenomena. Phenomena are empirically real but transcendentally ideal, meaning that human experience is real for us while shaped by the mind's forms and categories. Kant denied knowledge of supersensible things while preserving ideas of God, freedom, and immortality as necessary beliefs.

Kant's critical philosophy changed the problem of metaphysics from describing objects beyond experience to explaining how experience itself is possible. Space, time, causality, substance, and unity are not simply copied from things as they are in themselves; they are conditions under which a world can appear to human knowers.

## Time and Duration

Henri Bergson challenged ordinary and scientific accounts of time by emphasizing duration and flow. The issue shows how metaphysics questions central concepts such as time, change, and continuity even when common sense and analysis warn against rejecting ordinary thought too quickly.

## Concrete Universals

Georg Wilhelm Friedrich Hegel treated human institutions, sciences, and history as embodiments of mind or spirit. He argued that such realities require concepts richer than isolated particulars, using concrete universals to understand forms of life that develop through reason.

## Platonism

[[Plato and Platonism (Platonism)|Platonism]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Platonism)) separates the changing world of sense from an unseen world of true realities grasped by intellect. It treats ordinary belief as unstable and often contradictory, while philosophy seeks knowledge of Forms, values, and eternal structures that make sensible things intelligible.

This two-world view gives philosophy a corrective role. The ordinary person may take appearances and inherited values for granted, but the philosopher examines their foundations, exposes contradictions, and turns toward realities that do not depend on shifting perception.

## Aristotelianism

[[Aristotelianism]] rejects a simple separation between Forms and sensible things. [[Aristotle and Aristotelianism|Aristotle]] placed form in matter and tried to understand the here and now through substance, categories, causation, and purpose, though he also allowed pure form in God and astral intelligences.

Aristotle's categories made metaphysics more descriptive of the world of experience. Substance, quality, quantity, relation, place, time, and other categories offered a way to sort what is said of things and what belongs to things themselves.

His account of substance also made explanation teleological. A thing is not merely matter pushed from behind; it is form embodied in matter and moving from potentiality toward actuality. Causes therefore include purpose and structure as well as efficient force. That made Aristotelian metaphysics attractive to later thinkers who wanted nature to be intelligible without being separated from sensible things.

## Thomism

[[Thomas Aquinas and Thomism (Thomism)|Thomism]] joined Christian revelation with Aristotelian philosophy. Thomas Aquinas developed a Christian metaphysics that used reason to discuss creation, God, being, soul, and purpose while still treating revealed doctrine as authoritative.

Christian metaphysics had to explain both the dependence of the world on God and the intelligibility of created nature. Thomism made room for philosophical arguments while refusing to reduce Christian truth to unaided reason alone.

## Cartesianism

Rene Descartes built metaphysics during rapid scientific advance. He separated thinking substance from extended substance and treated physical things as defined by primary qualities such as shape, size, mass, and motion rather than color or smell. This dualism made mind-body interaction a central problem.

Cartesian dualism also reflected the new mechanical science. Bodies could be explained by size, motion, and impact, while conscious thinking seemed to require a different kind of substance. The strength of this separation created the difficulty of explaining how bodily events and mental acts affect one another.

## After Descartes

Later Cartesians struggled to explain how mind and matter could form one universe. Some made their unity depend on God's continuing action. Kant offered another dual standpoint: human actions can be viewed theoretically as events in nature and practically as acts of agents.

## Idealism

Idealism responds to mind-matter dualism by reducing matter to mind or treating reality as dependent on consciousness, spirit, or experience. Its forms range from Kant's transcendental idealism to Hegel's absolute idealism, all seeking unity where dualism leaves division.

In idealist systems, mind is not merely one object among others. It is the condition, structure, or active principle through which objects are knowable or real. This lets idealists account for values, meanings, and institutions without treating them as accidental additions to matter.

Idealism also has different forms. Berkeley treated material objects as experiences in minds, while Hegel treated spirit as the reality through which nature, history, society, and thought become intelligible. The difference matters because Hegel did not simply deny material things; he argued that they must be understood within a rational and historical whole.

## Materialism

Materialism responds in the opposite direction, treating matter as the only substantial existent. It must explain thoughts, numbers, institutions, and values as dependent on or reducible to material reality, often by using category distinctions inherited from Aristotle.

Materialist reduction faces the problem of explaining things that do not look material in the ordinary sense. A thought, number, law, or university may exist, but materialism must show how each depends on physical processes, social arrangements, or material bodies rather than on a separate mental or spiritual substance.

## Metaphysical Argument

Metaphysical argument often begins with a granted proposition, shows that it implies another, and then accepts the consequence. The necessity lies in the valid form of inference, not always in the first premise itself, so metaphysics depends on both vision and logical discipline.

## Origin

A metaphysic begins as an imaginative view of the world and becomes a conceptual system. A thinker may see reality as mechanism, divine creation, mind, matter, process, or experience, but must then clarify concepts, defend arguments, and connect the vision with facts and ordinary knowledge.

This makes metaphysical method neither mere poetry nor ordinary experiment. It starts from a vision of the whole, but it must be tested by coherence, by its ability to make sense of experience, and by its power to illuminate problems that specialized sciences leave presupposed.

## Hume

David Hume gave early powerful criticism of metaphysics. He argued that simple ideas derive from impressions and that complex ideas are built from simpler ones, leaving no room for innate ideas independent of experience.

Hume's empiricism made experience the origin and limit of meaningful ideas. If a supposed idea cannot be traced to impressions, it becomes suspect; this placed pressure on metaphysical ideas of necessary connection, enduring substance, and a self known apart from experience.

His critique of causality was especially damaging to older metaphysics. Causal reasoning lets people move beyond memory and present sensation, but Hume found no internal tie between cause and effect, only repeated temporal sequence and expectation. If necessary connection is not given in experience, then metaphysical claims about hidden powers, substances, and divine causes lose their usual support.

## Ideas and Facts

In A Treatise of Human Nature and An Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding, Hume distinguished relations of ideas from matters of fact. Sense experience supplies basic concepts, while factual claims are contingent and cannot be known by pure intellect alone.

## Necessity and Contingency

Relations of ideas, such as logical and mathematical truths, are necessary, while matters of fact depend on experience and could have been otherwise. Hume used this distinction to attack unsupported claims about substance, causation, self, and God.

## Kant

Kant answered Hume by limiting metaphysics and transforming it into critical philosophy. Knowledge of supersensible things is impossible, but inquiry into the conditions that make experience possible can be scientific. The categories structure experience, yet things as they are in themselves remain unknowable.

Kant also left a difficult boundary. God, freedom, and immortality cannot be known as objects, but practical reason still requires them as beliefs connected with morality. Later metaphysics repeatedly returned to this tension between limiting knowledge and preserving meaningful commitments.

Kant's distinction between sensibility and understanding sharpened that boundary. Intuitions supply particular objects, while concepts such as substance and cause organize them. Intuitions without concepts are blind, but concepts without intuitions are empty. Metaphysics could become rigorous only by explaining this cooperation instead of pretending that pure concepts alone reveal supersensible objects.

## Logical Positivists

Logical Positivists ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logical_positivism)) attacked metaphysics in the 20th century, influenced by Auguste Comte and the Vienna Circle. They held that meaningful statements are either analytic truths or empirically verifiable claims, leaving traditional metaphysical assertions meaningless rather than false.

Their anti-metaphysical program was both philosophical and cultural. It aimed to defend rational thought, science, and clarity against what they regarded as obscurity. The price was a narrow account of meaning that later critics questioned.

## Comte's Three Stages

Auguste Comte treated metaphysics as a superseded stage between primitive superstition and modern science. Logical Positivists adapted this suspicion in a post-World War I German-speaking context that they saw as threatened by obscurantism.

## The Verification Principle

The verification principle made logic and mathematics meaningful as analytic, and science meaningful as factual. Claims that could not be tested, observed, or reduced to such forms were rejected as empty, including many traditional statements about the supersensible.

## Metaphysics After Kant

William Henry Walsh is associated with later discussion of contemporary metaphysical tendencies. After Kant, metaphysics could continue as inquiry into the categorial structures that make experience and knowledge possible, not only as speculation about hidden worlds.

## John Dewey

John Dewey began within a Hegelian setting and later reshaped those themes through pragmatism. His early work on Kant and method helped bring together Kant's concern with conditions of experience and Hegel's concern with the historical and social character of reason.

## Pragmatist Metaphysics

Dewey's pragmatism treated concepts as tools within experience rather than as mirrors of a fixed independent order. Contemporary American metaphysics often kept Kant's insight that experience is structured while resisting a sharp split between phenomenal experience and unknowable reality.

## William James

William James sought to protect psychology as a science while exploring mind, matter, experience, and knowledge. His pragmatism and radical empiricism challenged simple dualism by asking how relations, purposes, and experienced continuities belong within the real world.

James's pragmatism judged ideas partly by their role in experience and conduct. Instead of asking only whether a metaphysical theory copies an independent reality, he asked what difference the theory makes in thought, inquiry, and life.

## Edmund Husserl and Phenomenology

Edmund Husserl used phenomenology to suspend ordinary judgments of existence and study how things appear with meaning in consciousness. He did not deny the world but bracketed assumptions to uncover necessary structures behind physical and psychological sciences.

Husserl's method of bracketing aimed to free philosophy from unexamined natural assumptions. By reflecting on intentionality, or consciousness directed toward objects, phenomenology tried to show how objectivity, meaning, and scientific concepts arise from lived experience.

## The Existentialists

Existentialists disputed the status of the lived world, or Lebenswelt, and asked whether philosophy can make all its presuppositions transparent. Thinkers after Husserl focused on historical meaning, ordinary experience, human existence, freedom, anxiety, and the grounding of theoretical science in lived life.

## Whitehead

Alfred North Whitehead developed a process metaphysics in which reality consists of events or occasions rather than static bits of matter. His idea of prehension described how each actual entity takes account of the wider universe, making relation and becoming basic features of reality.

Whitehead's process philosophy rejected the idea that nature is fully captured by point-instants and distributions of matter. Enduring objects are patterns of events, and each occasion has a unity formed through responsiveness to others, usually without consciousness but still relational in structure.

## Key Events & Developments

- Late 6th century BC: Pythagoras treated number and structure as what is most real.
- 1739-1740: David Hume's Treatise sharpened empiricist criticism of metaphysics.
- 1748: Hume's Enquiry restated the distinction between relations of ideas and matters of fact.
- 20th century: Logical Positivism, phenomenology, pragmatism, existentialism, and process philosophy reshaped metaphysical debate.
