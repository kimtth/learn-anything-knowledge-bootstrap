# Jackson Pollock

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> Jackson Pollock was an American painter whose drip and poured canvases became central to abstract expressionism. His methods challenged conventional composition and helped shift postwar art's international center toward the United States while provoking debate about gesture, process, and artistic meaning.

## Introduction

Jackson Pollock, born January 28, 1912, in Cody, Wyoming, and died August 11, 1956, in East Hampton, New York, was an American painter and a leading figure of Abstract Expressionism. He became famous for poured and drip painting, a radical method linked to action painting and to free, gestural movement across the canvas.

Pollock's importance rests on more than technique. Among artists of his generation he was respected for a deeply personal and uncompromising commitment to painting. His work helped make an American painter recognizable, during life and afterward, as a peer of major 20th-century European modernists.

## Life and Formation

![[attachments/jackson_pollock_001.webp|420]]

*Jackson Pollock*

Pollock was the youngest of five sons of Stella May McClure and LeRoy Pollock. His family left Wyoming when he was still an infant and moved repeatedly through California and Arizona before settling in Los Angeles in 1928.

Frederick John de St. Vrain Schwankovsky influenced Pollock at Manual Arts High School by giving him basic art training, introducing advanced European modernism, and encouraging interest in theosophy. Pollock also encountered the ideas of Jiddu Krishnamurti, preparing his later openness to Carl Jung's theories of unconscious imagery.

Thomas Hart Benton taught Pollock at the Art Students League in New York after Pollock moved there in 1930. Pollock studied drawing, painting, and composition with Benton for about two and a half years, then lived in poverty with his brothers while continuing to develop his own direction.

## Art, Reception, and Legacy

Pollock's mature art fused physical action, unconscious imagery, and personal authenticity. He believed art came from the unconscious and treated himself as the essential subject of his painting. His poured technique became famous, but its novelty often obscured the psychological seriousness behind it.

Critical reaction ranged from Clement Greenberg's support in the 1940s to Time magazine's mocking phrase Jack the Dripper shortly before Pollock's death. Fame did not bring financial security; he never sold a painting for more than $10,000 during his lifetime.

Pollock's international standing grew through showings at the Venice Biennales of 1948, 1950, and 1956, and a Paris one-man show in 1952. After his death, happenings, Pop art, Op art, Colour Field painting, and other movements looked back to him as a model of fused art and life.
