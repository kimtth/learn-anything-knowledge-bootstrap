# Hydrosphere

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> The hydrosphere is the discontinuous layer of water at and near Earth's surface, comprising oceans, ice, lakes, streams, groundwater, soil moisture, and atmospheric vapor. The hydrologic cycle links these reservoirs to climate, erosion, biological production, chemistry, human use, and pollution.

## Introduction

The hydrosphere is the discontinuous layer of water at or near [[Earth - Its Properties, Composition, and Structure (Earth)|Earth]]'s surface. It includes oceans, lakes, streams, glaciers, groundwater in soil and rock, and atmospheric water vapour. About 1.4 billion cubic kilometres, or 326 million cubic miles, of liquid and frozen water form this layer.

The organizing idea is the hydrologic cycle ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water_cycle)), a system of reservoirs, transfer processes, phase changes, and rates of movement. These pathways extend upward about 15 km into the atmosphere and downward about 5 km into the crust. The cycle links water quantity, chemistry, climate, erosion, biological production, human use, and pollution.

## Distribution and Quantity of Earth's Waters

![[attachments/hydrosphere_003.webp|420]]

*World water distribution*

Most water in the hydrosphere is ocean water, including water trapped in sediment pore spaces. Smaller reservoirs, such as atmosphere, rivers, lakes, soil moisture, groundwater, ice, and living organisms, hold much less water but can be environmentally powerful because they circulate quickly or interact closely with life.

Atmospheric water totals about 0.013 x 10^6 cubic kilometres, only about 0.001 percent of surface water, but it turns over rapidly and drives weather. Ice holds a little more than 1 percent of present water and may have held 3 percent or more during Pleistocene glaciations. Soil moisture and biosphere water are tiny fractions, yet soil evaporation and plant transpiration make them important parts of the cycle.

## Biogeochemical Properties of the Hydrosphere

Natural waters are never pure H2O. They contain dissolved gases, salts, suspended particles, organic matter, nutrients, trace metals, and microorganisms. Their composition depends on rain, sea spray, atmospheric pollution, rock weathering, biological production, evaporation, redox reactions, and human activity.

The chemistry of waters is therefore a record of movement through the environment. River water carries weathering products from continents to oceans. Groundwater reflects the minerals it has touched underground. Lake water records climate, basin shape, evaporation, organisms, and waste input. Ice preserves gases and particles from past atmospheres.

## Rainwater

About 110,300 cubic kilometres of rain falls on land each year, and atmospheric water turns over every 9.6 days. Rainwater contains dissolved gases and salts, fine particles, organic substances, and bacteria from oceans, soils, fertilizers, air pollution, and fossil-fuel burning.

The salt in coastal and island rain comes largely from sea aerosol. Bursting bubbles and breaking waves inject tiny salt-bearing droplets into the air; the water evaporates and leaves particles that winds can carry far inland. Marine aerosol may provide large shares of river sodium, chlorine, potassium, and sulfate, showing that atmospheric transport is a major part of global element cycles.

Average rainwater has a pH near 5.7 under equilibrium with atmospheric carbon dioxide. Industrial regions add sulfur and nitrogen gases. Sulfur dioxide reacts with hydroxyl radicals and hydrogen peroxide to form sulfuric acid, while nitric oxide and nitrogen dioxide form nitric acid. These reactions create acid rain, especially in the northeastern [[United States of America (United States)|United States]], southeastern [[Canada]], and western [[Europe]].

## River and Ocean Waters

Seawater contains about 3.5 percent dissolved salts, while average river water contains only about 0.012 percent, or 120 ppm. Ocean water is also about 2.75 percent denser than typical river water. In seawater, sodium and chlorine dominate the 35 parts per thousand salinity, with magnesium, sulfate, calcium, potassium, carbonate, and bicarbonate making up most of the rest.

Only eight elements, including oxygen, sulfur, chlorine, sodium, magnesium, calcium, potassium, and carbon, make up about 99 percent of seawater. Nutrients such as phosphorus, nitrogen, and silicon, plus trace elements such as iron, cobalt, and copper, strongly regulate ocean productivity. River water is instead dominated by bicarbonate, calcium, sulfate, and dissolved silicic acid.

Seawater is not simply concentrated river water. Each element has its own sources, sinks, and reactions. Biological skeleton formation removes calcium and silica, clays exchange sodium and calcium, and iron is removed by precipitation as hydroxides, iron-rich clays, and hydrated oxides.

## Water-Rock Reactions and River Chemistry

River and lake chemistry is controlled mainly by carbon dioxide-rich rain and soil water attacking minerals. Soil gases may contain 10 to 40 times more carbon dioxide than open air, and roots can create acidic microenvironments with pH values near 2. This acidified water dissolves and alters rocks as it moves through soil and groundwater.

Calcite dissolution in limestones releases calcium and bicarbonate. Higher carbon dioxide and temperature can increase dissolution, so waters in carbon dioxide-rich soils may carry far more calcium than waters in equilibrium with the free atmosphere. Insoluble residues such as quartz, clay minerals, and iron oxides become soils or suspended loads in streams.

Incongruent weathering changes one mineral into another while releasing solutes. Potassium feldspar can react with carbon dioxide-charged water to form kaolinite, bicarbonate, potassium, and dissolved silica. Globally, river dissolved load comes from salts, sulfates, carbonates, and silicate weathering, while large solid and organic carbon fluxes move from land to ocean.

## Lake Waters and Biological Cycling

[[Lakes]] hold only a small part of the hydrosphere, but they are important freshwater reservoirs and are sensitive to human use. Their salinity may remain low in leached, humid regions, rise through rock and soil salts, or become high in closed basins where evaporation concentrates dissolved material.

Biology strongly shapes lake chemistry. In stratified lakes, photosynthesis in the epilimnion removes carbon, nitrate, phosphate, and silica and can supersaturate surface waters with oxygen. Organic matter that sinks into the hypolimnion decomposes there, releasing nitrogen and phosphorus while consuming oxygen.

Deep waters may also gain dissolved silica and inorganic carbon when carbonate and siliceous skeletons dissolve. Human nutrient input from sewage, agriculture, and industry can accelerate these natural processes, producing eutrophication and oxygen-poor bottom waters.

## Groundwaters

Groundwater composition depends on dissolution, hydrolysis, precipitation, adsorption, ion exchange, oxidation-reduction, gas exchange, and biological processes. The strongest control on major dissolved constituents is reaction with rock minerals along underground flow paths.

Limestone groundwater is typically rich in calcium and bicarbonate because calcite dissolves easily. Waters from shale with carbonate and dispersed salts can be highly saline. Waters from schist or other silicate rocks may have lower salinity because silicate minerals react slowly. Granite groundwater may contain calcium and sodium from plagioclase feldspar.

The most mobile groundwater elements are calcium, sodium, and magnesium. Silicon and potassium have intermediate mobility, while aluminum and iron are mostly locked in solids. Human contamination can add fertilizers, pesticides, radioactive wastes, industrial chemicals, household materials, mine refuse, petroleum, and sewage.

## Ice

[[Ice and Ice Formations (Ice)|Ice]] is almost pure solid water, but it traps particles and gases in bubbles. Because successive ice layers preserve these materials, continental ice sheets record atmospheric and environmental change through time.

Younger glacial ice shows increased lead from tetraethyl lead released by gasoline burning. Air bubbles in ice also show that atmospheric carbon dioxide and methane have risen because of human activity. Present atmospheric carbon dioxide is about 25 percent higher, and methane about 167 percent higher, than their values 200 years ago.

## The Hydrologic Cycle

Each year about 496,000 cubic kilometres of water evaporates from land and ocean surfaces, remains in the atmosphere for about 10 days, and returns as rain or snow. Evaporation uses about half the solar radiation received at Earth's surface. About one-third of precipitation falling on land returns to the oceans mainly by rivers, while direct groundwater discharge supplies only about 0.6 percent of total oceanward discharge.

Residence time differs widely among reservoirs. Ocean water has a residence time near 37,000 years, reflecting the immense ocean volume. Atmospheric water vapour has a residence time near 10 days. Lakes, rivers, ice, and groundwater fall between these extremes and vary strongly by place.

Global precipitation depends on atmospheric water vapour and rising air. Net precipitation is high near the equator and in belts of prevailing westerly storms. Evaporation exceeds precipitation in subtropical stable-air zones and near the poles, though polar ice sheets can still grow where evaporation is very low.

## Water Vapour and Precipitation

Atmospheric water vapour is tiny in mass but crucial for Earth's radiation balance and for the upward branch of the hydrologic cycle. At a mean surface temperature of 15 C, pure water has an equilibrium vapour pressure of about 0.017 atmosphere. Salts lower vapour pressure, and a saturated sodium chloride solution has a vapour pressure about 22 percent below pure water.

Relative humidity compares actual water-vapour pressure with saturation pressure at the same temperature. Mean atmospheric relative humidity is low, though the troposphere averages about 50 percent. Water vapour decreases rapidly with altitude, and above 15 km it is less than 0.1 percent of the surface value.

Condensation forms droplets, ice crystals, clouds, rain, snow, and hail. Sea salt and dust particles serve as condensation nuclei. Tiny cloud particles grow into falling raindrops or snowflakes, while hailstones form through stronger growth processes in storm clouds.

## Distribution of Precipitation and Soil Moisture

Precipitation may evaporate before reaching the ground, be intercepted by vegetation, remain stored as snow, infiltrate soil, be detained in depressions, or become overland flow. Infiltration is high after dry spells but often declines as soil pores clog and clay particles swell.

Soil water returns to the atmosphere through evaporation and plant transpiration, together called evapotranspiration. Potential evapotranspiration depends mainly on available heat, while actual evapotranspiration cannot normally exceed precipitation unless irrigation supplies extra water. Soil-moisture balance tracks precipitation, evaporation, runoff, percolation, and storage.

## Groundwaters and River Runoff

Water percolates through the vadose zone to the groundwater table and the saturated phreatic zone. Henri Darcy's 1856 experiments produced Darcy's law for flow through porous media. Permeability depends on porosity, grain size and shape, and cementation.

Groundwater supplies about 30 percent of global river runoff and sustains base flow between storms. Streamflow also includes subsurface storm flow and overland flow. Horton overland flow occurs when rainfall exceeds saturated hydraulic conductivity; Dunne overland flow occurs when shallow water tables or impeding layers saturate soil from below.

Human activity has increased natural river input of major dissolved constituents to the oceans by more than 10 percent, with sodium, chlorine, and sulfate increases possibly reaching 30 percent. In the [[United States of America (United States)|United States]], water use equals about one-third of total runoff, and some southwestern supplies have been exhausted without replacement.

## Origin and Evolution of the Hydrosphere

[[Earth - Its Properties, Composition, and Structure (Earth)|Earth]] is about 4.6 billion years old, but the oldest known rocks, about 3.8 billion years old, already show deposition in water. There is no direct record of surface water between those dates. Early water likely came from volatile-rich accreting material and later outgassing during core formation and crustal cooling.

The earliest atmosphere and ocean may have formed as hot volatiles cooled. Below the critical temperature of water, 374 C, and later below 100 C, water condensed while acid gases reacted with crustal minerals. One model gives an early ocean rich in hydrochloric acid and carbon dioxide, which would leach silica and cations from igneous crust and leave aluminous clays.

By the Early Archean, about 3.8 to 3.4 billion years ago, surface temperatures were probably below 100 C, and cyanobacteria older than 3 billion years show that acid volatiles had been neutralized enough for life-supporting waters. Early ocean volume and salinity may even have been close to modern values if volatile proportions stayed broadly constant.

## The Transitional and Modern Hydrosphere

From about 3.8 billion to 1 to 2 billion years ago, oxygen levels in the atmosphere and ocean were far lower than today. Ferrous iron was important, and middle Precambrian iron formations contain siderite, greenalite, chert, and pyrite. These minerals point to higher carbon dioxide pressure, lower pH, more calcium, and silica-rich seawater.

Modern hydrosphere chemistry likely developed about 1.5 to 2 billion years ago. Calcium sulfate deposits show sulfur gases had been neutralized to sulfate, and ferric oxides show free oxygen was available. Carbon and sulfur isotope records suggest long-term biological cycling, including bacterial sulfate reduction as early as 2.7 billion years ago.

The modern ocean is a dynamic quasi-steady-state chemical system, not a simple salt accumulator. Present ocean salts could be supplied by rivers in about 12 million years, and dissolved silica could double in only 20,000 years, so outputs must balance inputs over relevant times. A remaining sodium imbalance may reflect uncertain pore-water fluxes or submarine weathering.

Atmosphere-hydrosphere chemistry continued changing after oxygen rose and carbon dioxide declined. Land plants in the Devonian may have increased oxygen and lowered carbon dioxide. Ice records show carbon dioxide near 180 ppmv during ice ages and about 280 ppmv during the last interglacial around 120,000 years ago. Events near the end of the Cretaceous may have caused cooling, acid rain, metal fluxes, and anoxic waters.

## Impact of Human Activities on the Hydrosphere

Human society has changed the hydrosphere through toxic chemicals, radioactive substances, industrial wastes, fertilizers, herbicides, pesticides, petroleum, sewage, and thermal pollution. Fresh waters are especially vulnerable because people use them for households, farms, industry, transportation, and waste disposal.

Three major human-impact problems receive special attention: eutrophication, acid rain, and the buildup of greenhouse gases. Each changes water chemistry, biological production, or the hydrologic cycle itself.

## Eutrophication

Oligotrophic waters are nutrient-poor and have low biological production. Eutrophic waters are rich in nitrogen and phosphorus, support heavy plankton growth, become murky, and may lose oxygen at depth. Natural eutrophication occurs as aquatic systems gradually fill with organic matter.

Cultural eutrophication ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eutrophication)) is caused by human nutrient and organic inputs from sewage, agriculture, and industry. Natural eutrophic lakes may produce about 75 to 250 grams of carbon per square metre per year, while culturally eutrophic lakes may produce up to 750 grams. Stratified bottom waters can fall below about 1 mg/L oxygen, changing biological and chemical processes.

The process can sometimes be reversed if nutrient input stops, but it has already affected rivers, lakes, estuaries, and coastal seas. River organic matter entering oceans is now about twice the prehuman level, and nitrogen and phosphorus fluxes have more than doubled. In the North Sea, silica deficiency and excess nitrogen and phosphorus can reduce diatoms and favour cyanobacteria.

## Acid Rain

Acid rain ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acid_rain)) is precipitation below pH 5.7 caused by gases beyond carbon dioxide, chiefly sulfur dioxide and nitrogen oxides from fossil-fuel burning. These gases form sulfuric and nitric acids, and wet and dry deposition carry hydrogen ions, sulfate, and nitrate into soils and waters.

Lakes in crystalline-rock terrains are especially vulnerable because they have little buffering capacity. Acidified soil water can mobilize aluminum, which harms aquatic life. In the eastern [[United States of America (United States)|United States]] and western [[Europe]], acid rain has been linked to lake acidification, fish loss, and forest damage.

## Buildup of Greenhouse Gases

Atmospheric carbon dioxide near 350 ppmv is about 25 percent above late-1700s values, based on ice bubbles and continuous Mauna Loa measurements since 1958. Fossil-fuel burning, wood burning, and deforestation, including slash-and-burn in the Amazon River basin, add greenhouse gases.

The ocean now absorbs about 2,340 million tons of carbon per year, but rising carbon dioxide can change surface-ocean pH and dissolved inorganic carbon. If carbon dioxide doubles late-1700s levels by 2030 to 2050, projected warming of 1.5 to 5 C could increase evaporation and precipitation by 4 to 7 percent, dry some midlatitude summers, intensify water shortages, and increase high-latitude runoff.

Sea level may rise about 0.7 metre over the next century through melting and thermal expansion. Collapse of the West Antarctic ice sheet could eventually raise sea level by 5 to 6 metres, and melting all glacial ice would raise it about 56 metres. Even a 1 metre rise would almost completely inundate coastal [[Bangladesh]].

- 3.8 billion years ago: The oldest known rocks show deposition in water.
- 3.8 to 3.4 billion years ago: Early Archean waters were probably cooler than 100 C and increasingly neutralized.
