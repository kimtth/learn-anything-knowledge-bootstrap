# Atmosphere

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> The atmosphere is the evolving gaseous envelope surrounding Earth and other worlds, in which radiation, heat transfer, water, aerosols, chemical cycles, and circulation regulate weather and climate. Its layered structure—from the weather-bearing troposphere to the ionosphere and exosphere—links surface processes, life, air quality, and planetary space.

## Introduction

The atmosphere ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atmosphere)) is the envelope of gases and aerosols surrounding a planet. On Earth it extends from land, water, and ice surfaces outward into space, thinning with height because gravity pulls its contents inward most strongly near the surface. Earth's atmosphere is special because it has been able to retain water in solid, liquid, and gaseous forms, a condition essential to life. Earth's current atmosphere likely developed gradually from volcanic outgassing and the activities of living organisms rather than remaining in its original primordial form.

## Radiation and Energy Balance

![[atmosphere_002.webp|420]]

*Earth energy budget and greenhouse effect diagram*

The atmosphere and surface are heated and cooled through [[Electromagnetic Radiation|electromagnetic radiation]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electromagnetic_radiation)). Incoming solar energy is mainly shortwave radiation ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shortwave_radiation_%28optics%29)), while radiation emitted by Earth and the atmosphere is mainly longwave, or infrared, radiation.

Some incoming radiation is absorbed by atmospheric gases, some is scattered, and some is reflected by clouds or the surface. This balance of absorption, reflection, and emission is fundamental to atmospheric temperature and to the maintenance of climate.

## Conduction and Convection

Heat moves not only by radiation but also by conduction and convection. Conduction transfers heat through direct contact and is strongly affected by the thermal properties of surface materials such as soil, concrete, or water.

Convection, or turbulent vertical mixing, is especially important in the atmosphere. It occurs when surface heating makes lower air rise or when wind shear stirs the air mechanically. These processes redistribute heat, moisture, and momentum and are central to weather and boundary-layer behaviour.

## Chemical Budgets and Atmospheric Cycles

Major atmospheric chemical budgets include those of sulfur and carbon. Sulfur enters the atmosphere through weathering, volcanic emissions, and human activities, especially fossil-fuel combustion, and can return to the surface as sulfuric acid in the problem known as acid rain.

Carbon appears mainly as carbon dioxide, which is essential to climate and life. Through [[Photosynthesis|photosynthesis]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Photosynthesis)), plants remove CO2 and release oxygen, while respiration, decay, weathering, and volcanism restore CO2 to the atmosphere. These chemical cycles are fundamental to Earth's environmental balance.

## Vertical Structure

![[atmosphere_001.webp|420]]

*Atmospheric layers, temperature, and emission sources diagram*

The atmosphere is divided compositionally into the homosphere, where major gases are well mixed, and the heterosphere, where lighter and heavier gases separate more clearly with altitude. The transition between them is the turbopause.

It is also divided thermally into layers defined by temperature changes with height. The lowest is the troposphere, where temperature usually decreases upward and where most clouds and ordinary weather occur.

## Troposphere and Boundary Layer

The troposphere is the lowest atmospheric layer and the main domain of weather. Its lower portion, the planetary boundary layer, is directly influenced by Earth's surface through turbulence, moisture exchange, temperature contrasts, and friction.

By day over land, this layer often becomes deep because solar heating drives convective mixing. At night, cooling near the surface can create inversions and suppress turbulence. Daily atmospheric behaviour begins from these surface interactions.

## Upper Layers: Thermosphere, Ionosphere, and Exosphere

Higher in the atmosphere, temperatures in the thermosphere can become extremely high, especially during active solar periods. Within it lies the ionosphere, a zone rich in charged particles created by solar ultraviolet radiation.

The ionosphere is especially important because it can reflect radio waves and is closely tied to solar activity. Above still higher levels, the exosphere is so thin that particles rarely collide and may escape to space entirely. This region is critical to whether a planet can retain an atmosphere over geologic time.

## Horizontal Structure and Solar Heating

The horizontal structure of the atmosphere arises from the uneven distribution of solar heating. Earth's tilted axis and elliptical orbit produce seasonal contrasts, while the difference between tropical and polar heating drives large-scale circulation.

These contrasts help create cold high-latitude winter air, warm tropical air, and the pressure systems and circulation cells that shape global climate. The movement of energy from warmer to colder regions is therefore one of the atmosphere's central tasks.

## Condensation and Clouds

Cloud formation depends on aerosols that act as cloud condensation nuclei or ice nuclei. Without such particles, spontaneous phase changes of water vapour would require unrealistically high levels of supersaturation.

Aerosols make condensation and deposition much easier and therefore play an essential role in cloud development. Their concentration varies between oceanic, continental, and polluted air masses.

## Precipitation Processes

Precipitation processes divide into warm-cloud and ice-based types. In warm clouds, droplets grow first by condensation and then by collision-coalescence, eventually becoming large enough to fall as rain.

This explanation shows how precipitation emerges from countless microscopic droplets or crystals interacting in clouds. These growth processes are key to understanding rain formation.

## Planetary Atmospheres

Earth's atmosphere can be compared with those of other planets and some moons. Venus, Mars, and the giant planets all have significant atmospheres, while Mercury has almost none. Titan, a moon of Saturn, is singled out as having a thick atmosphere.

These comparisons show how atmospheres differ according to gravity, composition, temperature, solar distance, volcanic history, and interaction with the solar wind. Venus is extremely hot and carbon-dioxide rich, Mars is thin and cold, and the giant planets display powerful bands, storms, and high-speed atmospheric motions.

## Conclusion

The atmosphere ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atmosphere)) is a dynamic system that links radiation, chemistry, heat transfer, water, circulation, and planetary history. On Earth it is the medium through which weather occurs, climate is regulated, and life is sustained.

At the same time, the atmosphere is shown to be part of a broader planetary context. By comparing Earth with other worlds, we see that atmospheres are not fixed shells but evolving systems shaped by physics, chemistry, and time.
