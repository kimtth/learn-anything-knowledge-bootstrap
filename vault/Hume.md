## Introduction

![[attachments/hume_001.webp|420]]

*David Hume portrait by Allan Ramsay*

David Hume, born in [[Edinburgh]] in 1711 and dead there in 1776, was a Scottish philosopher, historian, economist, and essayist known for empiricism and skepticism. He treated philosophy as an experimental science of human nature ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Human_nature)), borrowing the inductive spirit of Sir Isaac Newton and building on John Locke. His strongest conclusion was that knowledge cannot pass beyond experience.

## Early life and works

Hume was the younger son of Joseph Hume of Ninewells and Catherine Falconer, daughter of Sir David Falconer. His father died early, and he entered Edinburgh University at about 12. He rejected law, read widely, and after a nervous breakdown ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mental_disorder)) in 1729 slowly recovered.

In 1734, after a failed trial in commerce at Bristol, Hume went to [[France]] and spent much of three years at La Fleche. There he wrote A Treatise of Human Nature ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A_Treatise_of_Human_Nature)), a three-book system on understanding, the passions, and morals. It explained ideas, space, time, causality, sense testimony, emotional life, and moral approval as parts of a single study of the mind.

The Treatise ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Treatise)) was Hume's fullest philosophical work, but he later rejected it as youthful. Its structure was uneven, key terms were ambiguous, and parts were extreme. Still, Book I became central for later philosophers because it gave his deepest account of knowledge and causality.

## First publications and wandering years

Hume returned to England in 1737. The first two books of the Treatise ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Treatise)) appeared in 1739, and the third in 1740, but the poor reception discouraged him. Essays, Moral and Political did better in 1741-42, yet his attempt to gain the moral philosophy chair at [[Edinburgh]] in 1744 failed after charges of heresy and atheism.

During the next years Hume earned money through scattered posts: tutor to the marquess of Annandale, secretary to Gen. James St. Clair during the failed Brittany expedition of 1746, and secretary on an embassy to [[Vienna]] and Turin in 1748-49. In 1748 he recast Book I of the Treatise ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Treatise)) as the work later called An Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/An_Enquiry_Concerning_Human_Understanding)), including On Miracles.

## Causality and knowledge

Hume divided thought into relations of ideas and matters of fact. Relations of ideas, such as mathematical truths, can be demonstrated because denying them creates contradiction. Matters of fact cannot be demonstrated, because their opposites remain conceivable; fire might not burn, and lead might not be heavy, as far as logic alone is concerned.

His account of causality follows from this distinction. People suppose that causes contain a necessary connection with effects, but the senses reveal only repeated conjunction: one kind of event regularly follows another. Habit forms from that repetition, and its felt pressure becomes the source of the idea of necessary connection.

## Belief

For Hume, belief is a lively idea, not a rational proof added to an idea. A falling glass makes a person expect breaking; wet ground makes a person believe rain has fallen. In such cases, an impression transfers vividness to an associated idea through habit.

Hume did not deny that people believe in causal order or in the future resembling the past. He held that such natural beliefs are unavoidable and necessary for survival. His skeptical point was narrower: neither reason nor observation can prove them, even though human nature forces belief in them.

## Morals and historical writing

In An Enquiry Concerning the Principles of Morals, Hume made sympathy the basis of social life and moral judgment. Morality rests on sentiment rather than knowledge. Qualities are approved because they are useful or agreeable to their possessor or to others, and people naturally share one another's joy and grief.

This view made altruism central and shows why Hume was not merely a detached scoffer at duty. His skepticism attacked excessive moral theorizing, not ordinary moral commitment. From 1751 to 1763, he lived mostly in [[Edinburgh]], became librarian to the Faculty of Advocates, and used its library to write the successful History of England ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_History_of_England_%28Hume%29)).

## Later public life and final years

Hume served in [[Paris]] from 1763 to 1766, where the circle around Denis Diderot and d'Alembert admired him. He helped [[Jean-Jacques Rousseau]] seek refuge in England, but Rousseau suspected treachery, causing a public dispute. In 1769 Hume settled in [[Edinburgh]], revised his writings, prepared his autobiography, left Dialogues Concerning Natural Religion for posthumous publication, and died in 1776.

## Assessment

As a writer, Hume had a clear, smooth, restrained style that suited scientific prose. As a historian, his History of England ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_History_of_England_%28Hume%29)) was fuller, more impartial, and more readable than its predecessors; it connected events into causal patterns and included intellectual life as well as kings and statesmen.

As an economist, Hume helped prepare classical economics by arguing that wealth lies in commodities rather than money, that money supply should relate to goods, that low interest signals active trade, and that no nation prospers by exporting only for bullion. As a philosopher, he renewed ancient skepticism by questioning necessary causal links, influencing 19th-century positivists and 20th-century analytic philosophers interested in naturalism.

## Major Works

Hume's main philosophical, religious, political, and moral works include A Treatise of Human Nature ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A_Treatise_of_Human_Nature)), An Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/An_Enquiry_Concerning_Human_Understanding)), Four Dissertations, Dialogues Concerning Natural Religion, Essays, Moral and Political, An Enquiry Concerning the Principles of Morals, and Political Discourses.

## History

Hume's historical writings include The History of Great Britain, The History of England Under the House of Tudor, and The History of England from the Invasion of Julius Caesar to the Accession of Henry VII. Other works include the Rousseau dispute narrative and his autobiography.

- 1763-66: Hume won fame in [[Paris]] and became involved in the Rousseau dispute.
- 1776: Hume died in [[Edinburgh]].

## Key People

- David Hume ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Hume)): Scottish thinker known for empiricism, skepticism, moral sentiment, history, and economics.
- Sir Isaac Newton ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sir_Isaac_Newton)): Model for Hume's experimental study of human nature ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Human_nature)).
- John Locke ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Locke)): Epistemological predecessor important to Hume's focus on experience.
- [[Adam Smith]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adam_Smith)): Friend and literary executor who praised Hume's wisdom and virtue.
- [[Jean-Jacques Rousseau]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jean-Jacques_Rousseau)): Writer helped by Hume, then involved in a bitter public dispute with him.

## Interesting Facts

- Hume later rejected the Treatise ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Treatise)), though Book I became his most studied philosophical text.
- Hume's causality theory says necessity is felt as habit, not observed in events.
- Hume believed people must rely on natural beliefs even when reason cannot prove them.
- Hume's History of England ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_History_of_England_%28Hume%29)) helped establish readable causal narrative for general readers.
- Hume never married and spent his final years revising his writings in [[Edinburgh]].
