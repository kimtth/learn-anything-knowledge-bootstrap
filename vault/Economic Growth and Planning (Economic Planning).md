# Economic Growth and Planning (Economic Planning)

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> Economic planning coordinates production, investment, resource allocation, and development goals through public decisions or institutional plans. It has been used in varied market and socialist systems.

## Introduction

Economic planning ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economic_planning)) is the process in which central governments make or influence major economic decisions. It is different from laissez-faire, where the economy is left mostly to market forces. By the late 1960s, most countries used some kind of national economic plan. Yet in the 1980s, confidence in planning weakened. Developed market economies had slower growth and higher unemployment, developing countries saw inefficient state bureaucracies and weak public enterprises, and the Soviet bloc showed waste, rigidity, and poor adaptation.

## The nature of economic planning

The idea of central economic planning grew partly from criticism of capitalism as disorderly and selfish. Marxist critics attacked the market system, but they gave little practical detail about how a socialist economy should actually be run after capitalism ended. When the new Soviet government took control of the means of production in 1917, it had no ready blueprint. Its planning methods developed through emergency, experiment, and repeated change.

## Economic planning in communist countries

Communist economic planning in the Soviet Union and many other communist countries was shaped by the Soviet effort to industrialize in the 1920s and the 1930s. The Bolsheviks had taken power without a clear operating plan for the economy. Karl Marx had suggested that a socialist society would organize production for the common good, but he did not explain how ministries, enterprises, prices, wages, or supplies would function day by day.

## Planning in the early U.S.S.R.

The Russian Revolution of October 1917 was followed by economic breakdown, civil war, inflation, and refusal of cooperation by many officials and managers. The Bolsheviks reacted by placing industry and trade under state control, nationalizing economic property including land, banning private enterprise, and demanding that peasants deliver food surpluses to state agencies. Money lost value, and the economy moved into the emergency system later called War Communism ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/War_communism)).

## The controversies of the 1920s

In 1921, [[Lenin]] introduced the New Economic Policy ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Economic_Policy)), or NEP. Small private manufacturing, private trade, and free sale of peasant surpluses became legal again. The state kept the commanding heights, such as large industry, foreign trade, banking, and transport. The state sector was still run through trusts and state-appointed managers under V.S.N.Kh., but enterprises had more autonomy than they would later have under the command system.

## The First Five-Year Plan

The First Five-Year Plan, launched for 1928-1932, demanded huge investment in heavy industry. Steel output, for example, was planned to more than double by 1932. As the plan began, the planning machinery was remade and a command economy gradually took shape. Industrial enterprises no longer decided inputs and outputs by negotiation with one another. The planners set these decisions, and enterprises were expected to follow administrative instructions.

## The structure of the Soviet planning system

The new tasks of forced industrialization required a new Soviet planning system ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economy_of_the_Soviet_Union)). In 1932, three People's Commissariats for heavy, light, and timber industries replaced V.S.N.Kh.. These bodies were later divided further, and by 1939 Soviet industry was run by 21 People's Commissariats. In 1946, commissariats were renamed ministries. Each ministry controlled a branch of industry, directly or through republic-level ministries.

## The work of Gosplan

The task of Gosplan was to turn political objectives into a consistent set of plan targets. The plan had to balance production with supply, investment with available capital goods, and foreign trade with domestic resource needs. Exports drained resources, while imports supplied needed goods. Planners used material balances, which compared expected supply and demand for important commodities, and revised the plan until a general balance seemed possible.

## The Gorbachev reform agenda

Low growth, shortages, and corruption alarmed the Soviet leadership in the late 1970s and the early 1980s. Earlier reforms in 1965 and 1974 were criticized as inconsistent and halfhearted. Under Mikhail Gorbachev, the reform program of 1987-90 aimed at a much deeper change. The goal was to let enterprise managers decide much of the product mix by negotiation with customers or wholesale trade bodies, rather than by direct planner command.

## Soviet agricultural planning

Soviet agricultural planning had a difficult history because industrialization received priority. Under [[Stalin]], agriculture was treated mainly as a source of cheap food and raw materials for cities. In 1930-35, peasants were forced into collective farms, or kolkhozy. State farms, run more like factories with wage labour, were preferred in Soviet ideology but remained less important until after 1954.

## Planning in other communist countries

Other communist countries copied the Soviet planning system ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economy_of_the_Soviet_Union)) in great detail until 1956. After that, the choices of each party leadership mattered more. Yugoslavia in the 1960s and [[China]] in the 1980s decentralized control over important sectors and introduced stronger individual incentives. The Soviet satellites in eastern Europe kept more rigid controls until 1989-90, when Soviet political and military control weakened and most of them began moving toward market economies.

## Poland

[[Poland]] had serious economic problems because of unsound policies in the 1970s. Domestic imbalances and foreign debt helped create the political-economic crisis of 1980-81. Martial law in 1981 made it possible to raise consumer prices sharply, and the regime then adopted reforms meant to strengthen the market mechanism. These reforms were slowed by shortages and imbalances inherited from earlier policies.

## Czechoslovakia

Czechoslovakia was reforming its centralized economic system in 1968, but fears of deeper political change led to Soviet military intervention. This stopped the economic reform process. After the events of 1989-90, Czechoslovakia moved in the same broad direction as Poland. It reduced state subsidies, removed many price controls, and legalized or encouraged private ownership of industrial firms, commercial enterprises, and farmland.

## East Germany

East Germany organized industrial planning through monopolistic cartels called Kombinate. These bodies had considerable autonomy in meeting domestic and export needs. The system may have worked more efficiently than most Soviet-bloc systems because of German organizational traditions and work discipline. Even so, it remained very inefficient by the standards of western European market economies.

## Romania

Romania kept Stalinist economic and political methods more fully than any other Soviet-bloc country into the 1970s and the 1980s. Bad economic policies produced a long crisis and severe shortages, especially of energy and food. Widespread deprivation helped cause the popular uprising of 1989, which overthrew Nicolae Ceausescu.

## Hungary

In 1968, [[Hungary]] adopted a system of market socialism. Enterprise managers gained broad freedom to decide production programs, and central planners no longer set compulsory production targets in the old way. Some prices stayed controlled, while others were freed. Enterprises also gained some freedom to trade abroad, and reforms tried to connect Hungarian prices with world market prices.

## Yugoslavia

Yugoslavia developed its own form of socialist planning after breaking with Moscow in 1948. It abandoned agricultural collectivization in the early 1950s. State-owned enterprises were controlled by workers' councils, which decided production programs according to profitability, while prices were subject to negotiation. Investment came partly from enterprise profits, partly from central planners, and partly from bank credit.

## China

[[China]] first followed the Soviet planning pattern. In 1958, the Great Leap Forward tried to speed progress by moving rural labour into manufacturing. It failed disastrously, and Chinese leaders had to create planning methods suited to a very large country with poor communications and a low level of development. The Cultural Revolution and the death of [[Mao Zedong]] were followed by a major policy shift.

## Assessment of Soviet-type planning

Soviet-type planning was built for the special conditions of a poor country trying to industrialize quickly. Its emphasis on heavy industry came partly from the logic of industrialization, because steel and machinery could support further growth, and partly from concern for military strength. The system made forced saving and investment easier and allowed rapid growth of basic industries, but it neglected citizens' basic consumer needs for many years.

## Economic planning in noncommunist countries

Economic planning in noncommunist countries had a different meaning from Soviet command planning. Since World War II, many developed noncommunist countries used explicit economic plans, including Belgium, Canada, Finland, France, Germany, Ireland, Italy, Japan, the Netherlands, New Zealand, Norway, Portugal, Spain, Sweden, and the United Kingdom. These plans were strongest as policy tools in the 1960s and the 1970s.

## Planning in developed countries: origins and objectives

In developed noncommunist countries, planning was pragmatic rather than doctrinal. It grew from reconstruction needs, balance of payments problems, dissatisfaction with economic performance, regional inequality, and the desire to coordinate public and private action. The main spring of growth remained the private sector, but governments tried to set a more coherent framework for national choices.

## Origins of planning

Before World War II, there was no serious attempt at economic planning outside the Soviet Union. During the Great Depression of the 1930s, many governments intervened strongly, but mainly through emergency economic warfare: protection for domestic producers, tolerance of cartels, higher government spending, relief, and armaments. After the war, intervention became more positive and more connected to reconstruction and modernization.

## Objectives of planning

Developed-country plans usually set target rates of growth and showed how available goods and services might be allocated among private consumption, public consumption, social investment, productive investment, stocks, and exports. The plan had to balance total demand with probable total supply, while also allowing for the desired balance of payments position. It therefore served as a way to test whether national goals were consistent with one another.

## Stages of planning in developed countries

One merit of national planning was that it made choices public and encouraged debate. In [[France]], Parliament was consulted on the broad outline of the five-year plan, often through several alternative balanced sets of objectives. These alternatives could show, for example, that more social spending might mean slower growth of after-tax personal income, or that faster growth required more adaptation by producers and consumers.

## Assessment of planning in developed countries

By the 1970s and the 1980s, planning in developed countries became more flexible and selective. The general view was that government should create conditions for growth, including competition, rather than control private firms in detail during peacetime. Detailed controls were seen as reducing efficiency. Planning therefore shifted away from elaborate compulsory targets and toward broader frameworks, better information, and selected public responsibilities.

## Planning in developing countries: approaches

Since World War II, governments in developing countries have commonly published development plans. These were usually medium-term plans, often for about five years. The period was meant to be long enough for projects that crossed several budget years, but short enough to allow regular assessment across a series of plans. Development plans tried to promote growth by collecting information about the economy, raising investment, breaking production bottlenecks, and improving coordination among sectors.

## Difficulties in development planning

The practical record of development planning in developing countries was often disappointing. Plans frequently failed at the first major hurdle: matching the investment resources required for target growth with the investment resources actually available. Governments often began with a politically acceptable growth target and assumed resources would somehow appear. Realistic planning would have begun from available resources and then estimated the maximum possible growth rate.

## Key Events & Developments

- 1917: The Bolsheviks seized power in Russia and soon faced economic collapse, civil war, inflation, and the problem of running a state-owned economy without a clear plan.
- 1921: [[Lenin]] introduced the New Economic Policy ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Economic_Policy)), allowing small private manufacturing, private trade, and sale of peasant surpluses while the state kept major sectors.
- 1926-28: Soviet planners debated genetic planning versus teleological planning, and the radical industrializing approach gained victory with Stalin's support.
- 1928-1932: The First Five-Year Plan launched forced industrialization, built the command economy, and prioritized heavy industry over consumer goods.
- 1932: The Soviet planning system was reorganized when People's Commissariats replaced V.S.N.Kh. and branch ministries became central to industrial control.
- 1956: After this point, communist countries began to vary more in their use of the Soviet model, although eastern European satellites kept rigid controls for decades.
- 1968: [[Hungary]] adopted market socialism, while Czechoslovakia's reform process was stopped by Soviet military intervention.
- 1987-90: Mikhail Gorbachev pursued radical Soviet reforms that tried to combine planning with market mechanisms but weakened central control without creating a stable new system.
- 1989-91: Communist regimes in eastern Europe and the Soviet Union collapsed or lost control, and many states began painful transitions away from central planning.
- the 1960s and the 1970s: Noncommunist developed-country planning reached its height, before governments later reduced ambitions and relied more on market forces.

## Key People

- N.I. Bukharin: A Bolshevik leader who expressed the view in 1920 that conscious planning would replace market categories such as price, value, and profit.
- Vladimir Lenin ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vladimir_Lenin)): The Bolshevik leader who introduced the New Economic Policy ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Economic_Policy)) in 1921 after War Communism failed as a general economic system.
- Joseph Stalin ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph_Stalin)): The Soviet leader whose support helped the teleological planning school win and whose rule forced rapid industrialization and collectivization.
- Nikita Khrushchev ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nikita_Khrushchev)): A Soviet leader who changed agricultural policy in the late 1950s and the early 1960s through better prices and major investment.
- Leonid Brezhnev ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leonid_Brezhnev)): A Soviet leader who continued large agricultural investment and higher farm prices in the 1960s and the 1970s, though results remained weak.
- Mikhail Gorbachev ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mikhail_Gorbachev)): The Soviet leader whose reform program in 1987-90 tried to reduce direct planning and give enterprises more market-like freedom.
- Nicolae Ceausescu ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nicolae_Ceau%C8%99escu)): Romania's longtime leader, overthrown in 1989 after deprivation and shortages helped spark a popular uprising.
- [[Mao Zedong]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mao_Zedong)): The Chinese leader whose era included the Great Leap Forward and the Cultural Revolution, after which China moved toward major reforms.
- Deng Xiaoping ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deng_Xiaoping)): The Chinese reform leader who came to power in the late 1970s and expanded market forces, decollectivized agriculture, and legalized small private activity.

## Interesting Facts

- War Communism ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/War_communism)) looked like centralized planning on paper, but it mainly helped concentrate resources for civil war and failed badly as a normal economic system.
- In 1924, only 35 percent of Soviet national income and just 1.5 percent of agricultural production were socialized, showing how incomplete early Soviet planning still was.
- Gosplan used material balances to compare expected supply and demand, but the huge number of products and instructions made perfect coordination impossible.
- Soviet targets measured in tons could encourage factories to make products unnecessarily heavy, while targets in rubles could reward the use of costly materials.
- Private plots in the Soviet Union produced 25 percent of all agricultural output as late as 1986, despite the official importance of collective and state farms.
- East Germany's industry seemed relatively efficient inside the Soviet bloc, but after unification in 1990 many firms could not survive without subsidies.
- [[Japan]] achieved exceptional postwar growth without using detailed output targets in its multiyear plans.
- In developing countries, development plans often became more elaborate on paper even when the statistical data behind them were unreliable or conjectural.
- Planning works best when it matches incentives, information, administrative capacity, and realistic resource limits.
