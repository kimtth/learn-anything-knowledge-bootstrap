# Afghanistan

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> Afghanistan is a landlocked, multiethnic country at the crossroads of South and Central Asia. Its mountainous terrain, linguistic and religious diversity, and strategic routes have shaped its history, economy, and political life.

## Introduction

Afghanistan is a landlocked multiethnic country located in the heart of south-central [[Asia]], lying along important trade routes connecting southern and eastern Asia to [[Europe]] and the Middle East. For millennia great armies have tried to subdue it, leaving ruined monuments behind, but its deserts, mountains, and fiercely independent peoples defeated many empire builders, so the land long endured as a patchwork of ethnic factions rather than a unified nation.

## Land and Geographic Regions

![[attachments/afghanistan_001.webp|420]]

*Location of Afghanistan on a globe*

Afghanistan is completely landlocked—the nearest coast lies along the Arabian Sea about 300 miles to the south—and remains one of the most poorly surveyed areas of the world. It is bounded by [[Pakistan]] to the east and south, [[Iran]] to the west, and the Central Asian states of Turkmenistan, Uzbekistan, and Tajikistan to the north, touching [[China]] at the end of the narrow Wakhan Corridor ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wakhan_Corridor)) in the extreme northeast.

## The Central Highlands, Northern Plains, and Southwestern Plateau

The central highlands, actually part of the Himalayan chain ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Himalayas)), include the main Hindu Kush ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hindu_Kush)) range, encompassing an area of about 160,000 square miles with deep, narrow valleys and lofty mountains, some peaks rising above 21,000 feet. High passes between 12,000 and 15,000 feet are of great strategic importance, including the Shebar Pass northwest of Kabul and the storied Khyber Pass, which leads to the Indian subcontinent on the [[Pakistan]] border southeast of Kabul.

## Drainage Systems and Rivers

Practically the entire drainage system of Afghanistan is enclosed within the country, only the eastern rivers reaching the sea; the Kabul River flows into the Indus River in [[Pakistan]]. Most other rivers rise in the central highlands and end in inland lakes or vanish in the deserts.

## Climate and Precipitation

In general, Afghanistan has extremely cold winters and hot summers, typical of a semiarid steppe climate. The northeastern mountains are subarctic, while areas on the [[Pakistan]] border feel the Indian monsoons from July to September; temperatures range from 120 °F recorded at Jalalabad to lows near −15 °F in the high mountains.

## Ethnic Groups and Population

No national census has been conducted in Afghanistan since a partial count in 1979, and years of war and population dislocation have made an accurate ethnic count impossible. Rough estimates show that the Pashtuns—whose two largest tribal groups are the Durrani and Ghilzay—make up about two-fifths of the population.

## Languages

The people of Afghanistan form a complex mosaic of ethnic and linguistic groups. Pashto and Persian (Dari), both Indo-European languages, are the official languages; more than two-fifths of the population speak Pashto and about half speak some dialect of Persian, and bilingualism is common, so language does not always match ethnic group.

## Religion

Virtually all the people of Afghanistan are Muslims, of whom some three-fourths are Sunnites of the Hanafi branch. The rest, especially the Hazara and Kizilbash, follow Ithna Ashari or Ismaili Shiite Islam, and Sufism is widely practiced.

## Major Cities and Urban Settlement

The most heavily populated part of the country is between the cities of Kabul and Charikar, with other concentrations near Jalalabad, in the Herat oasis, and in the Qonduz valley. Most towns have grown along the ring road that runs from Kabul to Kandahar, Herat, and Mazar-e Sharif.

## Economic Overview and Soviet Legacy

When Afghanistan began planning economic development with Soviet assistance in the mid-1950s, it lacked the institutions and technical skills for modern economic activity and stood at a much lower stage of development than its neighbours. Between 1956 and 1979 growth was guided by several five- and seven-year plans and supported by heavy foreign aid, mainly from the Soviet Union and the United States, which covered more than four-fifths of government investment.

## Minerals and Energy Resources

Afghanistan bears significant mineral deposits: high-grade iron ore at Hajji Gak, copper at Aynak, and uranium near Khvajah Rawash, all close to Kabul. Other deposits include lead, zinc, chrome ore, gold, and the semiprecious stone lapis lazuli in Badakhshan, whose taxation helped finance anti-Taliban forces during the civil war.

## Manufacturing and Traditional Handicrafts

In peaceful times, manufacturing is based mainly on agricultural and pastoral raw materials. The most important is the cotton textile industry, and the country also produces rayon, cement, sugar, vegetable oil, soap, shoes, and woolen textiles. A natural-gas–based fertilizer plant operates at Mazar-e Sharif and a cement factory at Pol-e Khomri.

## Banking, Currency, and Finance

The Bank of Afghanistan, the largest bank, became the centre of the formal banking system and formerly played an important role in determining and implementing financial policies, though private money traders have traditionally provided most commercial banking services. The currency, the afghani, suffered rampant inflation from the 1990s, so precious metals and gems became common for large transactions.

## Trade Patterns and Exports

Total annual imports have customarily exceeded exports. Before the fall of the communist regime, about two-thirds of exports went to the former Soviet republics, with the rest going mainly to the [[United Kingdom]] and [[Germany]], and the principal export was natural gas.

## Prehistory and Early History

Paleolithic (Old Stone Age) peoples probably roamed Afghanistan as early as 100,000 years ago. The earliest definite evidence of human occupation was a transitional Neanderthal skull fragment with Mousterian tools found in the cave of Darra-i-Kur in Badakhshan, dating to about 30,000 years ago, while caves near Aq Kupruk yielded an early Neolithic culture based on domesticated animals around 9000–6000 BCE.

## The Achaemenian and Greek Period

In the 6th century BCE, the Achaemenian ruler Cyrus II established his authority over the area, and Darius I consolidated rule through satrapies such as Aria (Herat), Bactria (Balkh), and Arachosia (Kandahar). [[Alexander the Great]] overthrew the Achaemenids and conquered most of these provinces before leaving for [[India]] in 327 BCE.

## The Kushan Empire

About 135 BCE, a loose confederation of five Central Asian nomadic tribes known as the Yuezhi wrested Bactria from the Bactrian Greeks and united under the Kushan. Kushan power peaked in the 2nd century CE under King Kaniska, whose empire stretched from Mathura in north-central India beyond Bactria to the frontiers of [[China]].

## The Sasanids, Hephthalites, and Early Islamic Period

Persian Sasanids established control over parts of Afghanistan, including Bagram, in 241 CE. In 400 the Hephthalites took control, only to be defeated in 565 by a coalition of Sasanids and Western Turks, while Chinese Buddhist pilgrims such as Xuanzang continued to travel and record the region's religious centres.

## The Tahirids, Saffarids, Samanids, and Ghaznavid Dynasty

One of the earliest Islamic dynasties was the Tahirids of Khorasan, who won virtual independence from the Abbasid Caliphate in 820. They were succeeded by the Saffarids of Sistan in 867, and local princes became feudatories of the powerful Samanids of Bukhara; from 872 to 999 Bukhara, Samarkand, and Balkh enjoyed a golden age.

## The Ghurid Dynasty and Khwarezm-Shah

Mahmud's descendants continued to rule until 1150, when Ala al-Din Husayn of Ghur sacked Ghazna and drove the last Ghaznavid into [[India]]. His nephew, Muhammad of Ghur, first invaded India in 1175, and after his death in 1206 his general Qutb al-Din Aybak became the sultan of [[Delhi]].

## The Mongol Invasion and Aftermath

[[Genghis Khan]] invaded the eastern part of the Khwarezm-Shah empire in 1219. After the death of his grandson at Bamiyan, the enraged khan ordered that no living being there be spared, and Bamiyan was utterly destroyed; he then defeated Jalal al-Din, who made a final stand near the Indus in 1221.

## The Safavid, Mughal, and Hotaki Periods

Early in the 16th century, the Turkic Uzbeks rose to power in [[Central Asia]] under Muhammad Shaybani, who took Herat in 1507. Meanwhile Babur, a descendant of [[Genghis Khan]] and Timur, made Kabul his capital in 1504 and in 1526 marched on [[Delhi]] to found the Mughal Empire, which controlled eastern Afghanistan into the 19th century.

## The Durrani Dynasty

After Nadir Shah was assassinated in 1747, the commander of his Afghan bodyguard, Ahmad Khan Abdali, was elected shah at Kandahar by a tribal council, taking the title Durr-i Durran (Pearl of Pearls). As Ahmad Shah Durrani he extended Afghan control from Meshed to Kashmir and [[Delhi]], creating the second greatest Muslim empire of the later 18th century, surpassed in size only by the Ottomans.

## Modern Afghanistan and British Conflicts

Abd al-Rahman Khan (1880-1901) returned from exile in [[Central Asia]] and proclaimed himself emir of Kabul in 1880, after Britain's costly involvement in the Anglo-Afghan wars. During his reign the boundaries of modern Afghanistan were drawn by Britain and [[Russia]], including the Durand Line of 1893, which divided responsibility between British India and the Afghan kingdom.

## Zahir Shah's Reign and Soviet Influence

Mohammad Zahir Shah (1933-1973) reigned for four decades, beginning with cautious national consolidation and neutrality through [[World Wars (World War II)|World War II]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_War_II)). After the independence of [[Pakistan]] in 1947, the dispute over Pashtunistan—the status of Pashtuns on the Pakistani side of the Durand Line—shaped Afghan politics.

## Soviet Occupation and Civil War

The Soviet invasion of Afghanistan in 1979 led to a prolonged occupation and devastating civil war, as mujahideen insurgents resisted the Soviet-backed communist government. After the Soviet withdrawal, the regime of Najibullah held out until it was ousted in April 1992, soon after the breakup of the Soviet Union.

## The Taliban Emergence and Rule

Partly in response to lawlessness and population exhaustion from civil war, the Taliban (Persian: Students) emerged in fall 1994. Its spiritual and political leader was the former mujahideen fighter Mullah Mohammad Omar, and the movement drew recruits from madrasah (religious school) students in [[Pakistan]] and the southern province of Kandahar.

## Post-Taliban Reconstruction and Democracy

The Taliban regime collapsed in December 2001 in the wake of a sustained U.S.-dominated military campaign aimed at the Taliban and al-Qaeda fighters. Anti-Taliban forces then agreed to a transitional administration that led to a new constitution, and a Loya Jirga approved that constitution in January 2004, calling for a directly elected president and a two-chamber legislature.

## Ongoing Challenges and Violence

Ongoing violence throughout 2005 increased steeply at year's end and worsened considerably in subsequent years as instability and warfare spread, with frequent clashes between the U.S.-led coalition and the Taliban in the eastern and southern provinces. In July 2006 NATO ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NATO)) troops replaced the U.S.-led coalition in the south and by October had taken command of military operations across the whole country.

## Health, Education, and Infrastructure

Based on infant mortality and life expectancy levels, Afghanistan has one of the least-developed health care systems in the world, a situation made worse by thousands of land mines and unexploded ordnance left across the countryside. Being landlocked, the country depends on its neighbours for transit, lacks railways, and relies on roads as the mainstay of its transport system.

## Cultural Heritage and Traditions

![[attachments/afghanistan_002.webp|420]]

*Bamiyan Buddha niche in Afghanistan*

Afghanistan has a rich cultural heritage covering more than 5,000 years and absorbing elements from many cultures, especially those of [[Iran]] (Persia) and [[India]], with traces of Greek culture from the Hellenistic Age. This blend flourished under the Mughal emperors, when Kabul and Herat became important centres of art and learning, though near-total isolation between the 16th and early 20th centuries slowed new work in art, literature, and architecture.

- 327 BCE: [[Alexander the Great]] conquers most of the Afghan satrapies before marching to [[India]].

- 1996: The Taliban capture Kabul and impose a theocratic regime.

## Interesting Facts

- Afghanistan is a landlocked crossroads connecting [[Central Asia]], South Asia, and the Middle East, with the nearest coast some 300 miles away.
- The giant [[Buddha and Buddhism (Buddha)|Buddha]] statues of Bamiyan, up to 175 feet tall and carved in the 4th–5th centuries CE, were destroyed by the Taliban in 2001.
- The lapis lazuli of Badakhshan was exported to Mesopotamia and [[Egypt]] in the Bronze Age and later helped fund anti-Taliban forces.
- The rivalry of Britain and [[Russia]] over the country was famously named the Great Game by Rudyard Kipling.
