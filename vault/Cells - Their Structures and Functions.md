# Cells - Their Structures and Functions

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> Cells are membrane-bounded, self-maintaining units that organize metabolism, energy conversion, genetic information, transport, communication, division, and differentiation. Comparing prokaryotic and eukaryotic cells reveals how organelles, membranes, cytoskeleton, and gene regulation link molecular processes to tissues, development, disease, and evolution.

## Introduction

A cell is the basic membrane-bound unit of life and the building block of all living organisms. Some cells, like bacteria and yeast, are complete organisms by themselves, while others specialize and cooperate to form tissues and organs in multicellular life.

Cells are both self-contained chemical systems and parts of larger organisms. A cell can metabolize nutrients, make molecules, store and use energy, and divide to produce offspring, yet in animals and plants it also depends on communication and cooperation with surrounding cells.

Cells are remarkably small. The smallest known cells are tiny bacteria called mycoplasmas; some are spheres only about 0.3 micrometre across, with a mass equal to that of 8,000,000,000 hydrogen atoms. A typical human cell has a mass about 400,000 times larger, yet is still only about 20 micrometres across, so it would take roughly 10,000 human cells to cover the head of a pin. Despite this tiny size, the cytosol of a single cell holds more than 10,000 different kinds of molecules engaged in biosynthesis.

## Nature and Function of Cells

![[attachments/cells_their_structures_and_functions_001.webp|420]]

*Diagram of a typical animal cell with labeled organelles*

The cell is enclosed by a plasma membrane that regulates what enters and leaves. Inside are organelles and cytoplasm, where metabolism, synthesis, energy production, and cellular organization take place. The nucleus stores genetic information, mitochondria and chloroplasts handle energy conversion, and many other structures support cell function.

Cells fall into two great classes: eukaryotic cells, which contain membrane-bound organelles, and prokaryotic cells, which are smaller and simpler. That distinction runs through all of cell biology, because cell structure is tied to how energy, heredity, and division are managed.

The cell also works as a biochemical factory. Proteins, nucleic acids, lipids, carbohydrates, ions, and water interact through enzyme-controlled reactions. These reactions do not occur randomly; they are organized in pathways, membranes, compartments, and feedback systems that keep the cell alive.

Cell size is limited by surface area, volume, and transport. A cell must exchange materials through its membrane while supporting internal chemistry. This explains why cells are microscopic and why large organisms are made of many specialized cells rather than a few enormous ones.

## Cell Membrane and Transport

![[attachments/cells_their_structures_and_functions_002.webp|420]]

*Detailed diagram of the cell membrane lipid bilayer with proteins*

The cell membrane is a thin, selective barrier made of lipids and proteins. Typically between 4 and 10 nanometres thick, its main task is to keep essential cell contents in while letting nutrients in and waste out. Much of what is known about membranes comes from studies of human red blood cells (erythrocytes), which are easy to obtain and lack internal organelles. Membranes busy with metabolism carry more protein: the mitochondrial membrane is as much as 75 percent protein, while the insulating Schwann-cell membrane has as little as 20 percent. Membrane structure rests on the lipid bilayer, membrane proteins, and the chemical properties that control permeability.

The bilayer is built from two kinds of lipid, phospholipids and sterols such as cholesterol. When dry phospholipids are placed in water they spontaneously form globular liposomes whose walls are double layers, with water-repellent tails turned inward and water-attracted heads facing outward; this self-assembled bilayer is the most stable arrangement a phospholipid-water mixture can take. Membrane proteins are either loosely held extrinsic proteins or intrinsic proteins that penetrate and bind tightly within the bilayer.

Transport across the membrane includes permeation, counter-transport, co-transport, active transport, endocytosis, and exocytosis. These systems allow cells to absorb nutrients, control ions, export products, and maintain internal conditions that would otherwise collapse. Permeation is simple diffusion through the bilayer, and its speed follows the partition coefficient, a measure of how well a substance dissolves in oils: proteins and sugar polymers do not permeate at all, while water and alcohols cross in under a second.

Carrier systems move whole groups of solutes. In counter-transport (antiport), one substance rides in down its gradient while another is pushed out; a well-studied pump expels one calcium ion for every two or three sodium ions taken in, keeping calcium low. In co-transport (symport), an ion and another molecule bind together, so that, for example, the bacterium Escherichia coli drags lactose inward with hydrogen ions until lactose is a thousand times more concentrated inside than outside. The sodium-potassium pump is the main example of primary active transport.

Membrane proteins make selective transport possible. Channels, carriers, pumps, and receptors allow ions and molecules to move in controlled ways. Some movement follows concentration gradients, while active transport uses energy to move substances against those gradients.

Endocytosis and exocytosis show that the membrane is dynamic. Cells can engulf particles, take in extracellular fluid, recycle membrane, and secrete proteins or other products. There are three kinds of endocytosis: pinocytosis, which draws in small fluid-filled vesicles; phagocytosis, which engulfs larger solid matter; and receptor-mediated endocytosis, the route by which cholesterol enters cells. In exocytosis, a nerve impulse can trigger secretory vesicles to fuse with the membrane and release neurotransmitters into the synaptic cleft. These processes connect the plasma membrane with internal organelles and with communication outside the cell.

## Energy and Metabolism

Cells obtain energy from photosynthesis or from the breakdown of food molecules into ATP ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adenosine_triphosphate)), the universal energy carrier, which is made from adenosine diphosphate (ADP) and inorganic phosphate. Glycolysis, the tricarboxylic acid cycle, and oxidative phosphorylation are the major steps in ATP production, especially in mitochondria. Glycolysis is a chain of nine enzymatic reactions that splits the six-carbon sugar glucose into two molecules of three-carbon pyruvic acid and captures the energy of each glucose in just two ATP. The eight-step tricarboxylic acid cycle then strips carbon away as carbon dioxide and hands high-energy hydrogen atoms to carrier molecules.

Photosynthetic cells use chloroplasts to convert sunlight, carbon dioxide, and water into carbohydrates. In both cases, cellular life depends on highly organized chemical pathways that transform energy while preserving order.

ATP ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adenosine_triphosphate)) production is linked to electron transfer and membrane gradients. In mitochondria, electrons pass through respiratory chains, and the energy released helps pump protons across membranes. ATP synthase then uses that gradient to make ATP. Chloroplasts use a related principle during photosynthesis, but the initial energy comes from light. The electron-transport chain is built from three large protein complexes embedded in the inner membrane: NADH dehydrogenase passes electrons to the lipid-soluble carrier ubiquinone, then to the b-c2 complex, then to cytochrome c, and finally to cytochrome oxidase, which hands four electrons to oxygen to make water. The proton gradient this builds drives the knob-and-stalk enzyme F1F0 ATPase, which is reversible and can either make ATP or pump protons.

Metabolism includes both breakdown and construction. Catabolic pathways release energy from sugars, fats, and other molecules, while anabolic pathways build proteins, nucleic acids, membranes, and cell walls. The cell is a system that balances energy harvest, biosynthesis, and waste removal.

## Internal Membranes and Organelles

Eukaryotic cells contain internal membranes that compartmentalize functions. The endoplasmic reticulum synthesizes proteins and lipids, the Golgi apparatus modifies and sorts them, lysosomes digest unwanted material, and peroxisomes and glyoxysomes handle oxidative reactions. Prokaryotic cells are only one to five micrometres long and have just a single membrane, whereas in a eukaryotic cell the plasma membrane makes up 10 percent or less of the total membrane. Lysosomes hold hydrolytic enzymes that work best at about pH 5 and leave behind undigested debris called a residual body. Peroxisomes, roughly 0.5 to 1 micrometre across, generate hydrogen peroxide and then destroy it with the enzyme catalase, while the plant glyoxysome runs the glyoxylate cycle that turns fat into carbohydrate.

The nucleus manages DNA and RNA expression, while mitochondria and chloroplasts are semi-independent energy organelles with their own genetic material. Compartmentalization is a defining feature of the eukaryotic cell.

The endoplasmic reticulum is divided into rough and smooth regions. First noticed in stained cells in the late 19th century and named only in the 1940s after the electron microscope revealed it, it can make up more than half of all the membrane in a cell. Rough ER is associated with ribosomes and protein synthesis, while smooth ER is important in lipid synthesis, detoxification, and calcium storage. The Golgi apparatus receives, modifies, sorts, and ships cellular products, marking lysosomal enzymes with phosphate groups so they are routed to lysosomes rather than secreted.

Mitochondria and chloroplasts likely have an endosymbiotic origin. Their double membranes, own DNA, and bacterial-like features suggest that they began as once-independent organisms incorporated into ancestral eukaryotic cells. Mitochondria are elongated bodies about one micrometre long whose inner membrane is folded into ridges called cristae that greatly enlarge its surface; chloroplasts instead enclose stacks of flattened sacs called thylakoids. The fluid-filled interior is called the matrix in mitochondria and the stroma in chloroplasts.

The nucleus is more than a storage compartment. It contains chromatin, chromosomes, nucleoli, and nuclear pores that regulate traffic between nucleus and cytoplasm. DNA is copied before division, but it is also transcribed into RNA so that genetic information can be used to make proteins.

Ribosomes translate RNA into proteins. Free ribosomes make many cytoplasmic proteins, while ribosomes attached to the rough ER make proteins destined for membranes, secretion, or lysosomes. This gives the cell a directional flow of information and material: DNA to RNA to protein, and ER to Golgi to membrane or other destinations.

## Cytoskeleton and Cell Movement

The cytoskeleton is the filament network that gives the cell its shape and supports movement. It has four major filament types: actin filaments, microtubules, intermediate filaments, and septins. Actin filaments and microtubules constantly assemble and disassemble, intermediate filaments are stabler reinforcing structures, and septins take part in cell division. Each actin subunit faces the same way, so an actin filament is polar, with a barbed and a pointed end.

Microtubules are especially important for cilia, flagella, the centrosome, and the mitotic spindle. These structures show how cells move, divide, and organize their internal contents with great precision.

Actin filaments are central to cell crawling, muscle contraction, cell shape, and cytokinesis. Microtubules act as tracks for intracellular transport and as structural supports for cilia and flagella. Intermediate filaments provide tensile strength, especially in cells exposed to mechanical stress.

Molecular motors make the cytoskeleton active. Kinesin, dynein, and myosin convert chemical energy into movement, carrying vesicles, separating chromosomes, beating cilia, and contracting muscle. The cell interior is therefore organized motion rather than static architecture.

## Cell Communication and Matrix

Cells do not operate alone. They are embedded in an extracellular matrix and communicate with one another through receptors, adhesion molecules, and signaling molecules. These interactions allow cells to recognize neighbours, build tissues, and respond to chemical messages.

Plant cells rely on cell walls, plasmodesmata, and oligosaccharins, showing that plants use a different but equally complex system of support and communication. In both animals and plants, the environment of the cell is not empty space but an active regulatory field.

Animal cells use extracellular matrix proteins such as collagen, elastin, fibronectin, and proteoglycans to build tissues. Cells attach to this matrix through receptors and adhesion molecules, and those attachments influence survival, migration, differentiation, and shape.

Cell signaling can be local or long-distance. Hormones, neurotransmitters, growth factors, and surface-bound signals bind to receptors and activate internal pathways. These pathways may change enzyme activity, gene expression, cytoskeletal organization, secretion, or cell division.

Cell junctions help organize tissues. Tight junctions seal spaces between cells, gap junctions allow communication, and anchoring junctions hold cells together or attach them to matrix. These systems show how multicellular life depends on cooperation among cells.

## Division, Growth, and Differentiation

Cell division is essential for reproduction in unicellular organisms and for growth and repair in multicellular ones. Mitosis, meiosis, the cell cycle, and the regulation of proliferation are central to life, development, and heredity.

Cell differentiation makes specialized tissues possible. Different cell types arise by differential gene expression, tissues are organized in specific ways, and the whole organism depends on balancing growth, renewal, and functional specialization.

The cell cycle is regulated so that DNA is copied before division and damaged cells do not divide unchecked. Checkpoints coordinate growth, DNA replication, chromosome separation, and cytokinesis. Failures in these controls can lead to developmental defects or cancer.

In multicellular organisms, differentiation depends on patterns of gene expression rather than different genetic contents in each cell. Muscle cells, nerve cells, epithelial cells, and blood cells can contain the same genome while using different parts of it. This allows specialization without losing hereditary unity.

Cell division is also linked to heredity. Mitosis produces genetically similar daughter cells for growth and repair, while meiosis produces gametes with reduced chromosome number and genetic recombination. These processes connect cell biology to reproduction and evolution.

Growth control is essential to multicellular life. Cells must divide when tissues need renewal, stop when space or signals require restraint, and sometimes die by programmed mechanisms. This balance allows development and repair, while its failure can produce uncontrolled growth.

Differentiation also depends on communication between cells. Signals from neighbouring cells, hormones, extracellular matrix, and developmental gradients can turn genes on or off. Thus the identity of a cell is shaped by both inherited information and its position in a tissue or organism.

## Evolution and History of Cell Theory

The story of cells extends from primitive RNA-based systems to modern cells, and the history of cell theory runs from Hooke and Leeuwenhoek to Schleiden, Schwann, and Virchow. The discovery of the nucleus, chromosomes, and cell division established that cells come from preexisting cells and that biology must be understood at the cellular level.

The final message is that cells are not only the units of life but also the key to understanding development, disease, inheritance, and the organization of organisms as a whole.

The evolutionary story begins with the possibility of early RNA-based systems, then moves toward cells with membranes, metabolic networks, and genetic replication. RNA can copy itself even though it is single-stranded, and some RNA molecules, the ribozymes, fold into shapes that let them act as catalysts, while messenger RNA carries templates that ribosomes translate into proteins. The 1953 discovery of the DNA double helix by James Watson and Francis Crick later anchored this molecular view. Prokaryotic cells appeared before eukaryotic cells, and later endosymbiosis helped create more complex internal organization.

The history of cell theory shows how instruments changed knowledge. Improved microscopes made it possible to see cells, nuclei, chromosomes, organelles, and eventually molecular structures. Modern cell biology is a union of microscopy, chemistry, genetics, physiology, and evolutionary theory.

Cell biology is also linked to disease. Defects in membranes, organelles, enzymes, DNA repair, cell-cycle control, or signaling can produce inherited disorders, cancer, immune problems, and developmental abnormalities. Because every tissue is cellular, disease often begins as a disturbance of cellular structure or function.

Modern cell research uses electron microscopy, cell culture, biochemical fractionation, fluorescent tags, genetic engineering, and molecular sequencing. These methods let scientists connect visible structures with molecules and explain how cells work as living systems.

A larger conclusion is that cell theory unifies biology. It explains why bacteria, plants, animals, fungi, and protists can be studied through shared principles of membranes, metabolism, genetic information, and reproduction, even though their forms and life histories differ greatly.

Cells also provide the bridge from molecules to organisms. DNA, proteins, membranes, and energy pathways become life only when organized into a bounded, self-maintaining, responsive system. That is why the cell is the basic unit of structure, function, heredity, and evolution.

## Key Events & Developments

- 17th century: Hooke and Leeuwenhoek help open cell study.
- 1830s: The nucleus and cell theory begin to take shape.
- 1855: Virchow states that all cells come from cells.
- Late 19th century: Cell chemistry and division are more fully understood.
- 20th century: Molecular biology reveals DNA, RNA, and organelle function.
