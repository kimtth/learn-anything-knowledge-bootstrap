## Introduction

![[attachments/transcaucasia_001.png|420]]

*Map of the Caucasus region*

Transcaucasia ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_Caucasus)), also called Southern Caucasia, is the region south of the Caucasus Mountains. It includes Georgia, Azerbaijan, and Armenia.

The region lies between [[Russia]], the Caspian Sea, Iran, Turkey, and the Black Sea. It forms part of the broad Caucasian isthmus where Europe and Asia meet.

Together the three states cover about 71,850 square miles. The area is small but densely populated and historically important as a route between the Middle East, Europe, and the Eurasian interior.

## Names and Historical Position

Caucasia includes both Transcaucasia and Ciscaucasia, or Northern Caucasia. The name Caucasus comes through Greek and Latin forms and may trace to an old Hittite name for people near the Black Sea.

Ancient Greeks placed Prometheus's sufferings in the Caucasus and connected Colchis with the Golden Fleece. These myths reflect the region's early importance in Mediterranean imagination.

The Caucasus also served as a route for cultural diffusion from the Fertile Crescent northward. Over centuries, migrations, invasions, trade, and mountain refuge created great ethnic and cultural diversity.

## Mountains and Lowlands

The Caucasus system has two main ranges: the Greater Caucasus in the north and the Lesser Caucasus in the south. The Greater Caucasus forms Transcaucasia's northern boundary.

The Greater Caucasus stretches about 750 miles from near the Black Sea to the Caspian. Its highest peak is Mount Elbrus at 18,510 feet, and many central peaks rise above 16,000 feet.

South of the Greater Caucasus are major lowlands: the Kolkhida plain on the Black Sea side and the Kura-Aras Lowland in the central-eastern depression.

The Lesser Caucasus, Talish Mountains, and Armenian Highland create a complex landscape of uplands, plateaus, volcanic massifs, valleys, basins, and mountain chains.

## Geology and Water

Much of Caucasia formed from the Alpine geosyncline, a great structural downwarp linked to the younger mountains of Europe. The region remains tectonically unstable.

The Greater Caucasus includes ancient crystalline rocks, Jurassic schists and sandstones, younger limestones, and extinct volcanoes such as Mount Elbrus. Earthquakes can be locally destructive.

Major rivers include the Kura and Aras flowing toward the Caspian, and the Rioni and Inguri flowing toward the Black Sea. Flooding often follows spring snowmelt.

Lake Sevan in the eastern Lesser Caucasus is the largest lake of Transcaucasia. More than 2,000 glaciers occur in the Greater Caucasus, though they cover only a small part of the range.

## Climate and Natural Life

The Greater Caucasus separates temperate and subtropical influences and blocks cold northern air. Climate varies sharply with altitude, exposure, and distance from the sea.

Kolkhida has a humid subtropical climate, while the Kura-Aras Lowland is dry subtropical. Mountain zones become cooler, wetter, and alpine, with snow on the highest crests.

Vegetation ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vegetation)) and agriculture vary with climate: humid lowlands support tea, citrus, bamboo, and other subtropical crops, while drier areas depend on irrigation.

## People and Settlement

![[attachments/transcaucasia_002.png|420]]

*Old town of Tbilisi*

Settlement is uneven. Dense rural populations occur along the Black Sea coast, in the Rioni valley, smaller cultivated valleys, and foothills. Alpine regions and arid Caspian lowlands are sparse.

Urban dwellers form a large share of the population. Baku, Tbilisi, and Yerevan each have populations well above one million.

The peoples of Transcaucasia reflect long contact among [[Europe]], Asia, the Middle East, and the steppe. Iranian languages, Christianity, Islam, Russian influence, and local mountain cultures all shaped the region.

Stronger neighbours repeatedly pressured smaller ethnic groups, and many preserved themselves by taking refuge in mountain ravines and isolated valleys.

## Economy and Resources

Transcaucasia has important natural resources. Oil and natural gas occur around the Greater Caucasus, the Kura-Aras Lowland, and beneath the Caspian Sea.

Metallic ores include iron near Daskasan, copper and molybdenum in uplands, manganese near Chiatura, and other ores in the Greater Caucasus. Building stones and mineral springs are also important.

Agriculture includes tea, citrus, tung trees, bamboo, tobacco, corn, grapes, fruits, cotton, rice, alfalfa, mulberry trees for silk, and livestock in higher elevations.

Industry produces steel, locomotives, vehicles, rubber, chemicals, cement, hard metals, textiles, footwear, food, tobacco products, and electronics. Hydroelectric power is strongly developed.

Tourism is important because of Black Sea and Caspian beaches, mineral springs, mild climates, mountain scenery, skiing, mountaineering, and architectural villages such as those in Svanetia.

## Transportation

Mountains make [[Transportation|transportation]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transport)) difficult. Railways ring the main ranges and cross the Caucasian isthmus, but no railway crosses the Greater Caucasus.

Branch rail lines enter many valleys and connect with Iran and Turkey. Highways carry most passengers and goods, while oil and natural-gas pipelines cross the region.

Only the lower reaches of the Kura and Rioni rivers are navigable, so river transport is limited.

## Key Events & Developments

![[attachments/transcaucasia_003.webp|420]]

*Armenian monastery in the Caucasus*

- 2nd millennium BCE: Transcaucasia became an ancient centre of bronze working.
- 5th century BCE: Herodotus described peoples of the region.
- Pre-modern centuries: Scythians, Alani, Huns, Khazars, Arabs, Seljuqs, and Mongols passed through or invaded.
- 16th century onward: Russian cultural and political influence spread southward into Caucasia.

## Interesting Facts

- Lake Sevan is the largest lake in Transcaucasia.
- Mineral springs made the Caucasus a famous health and resort region in the former Soviet world.
