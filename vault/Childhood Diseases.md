# Childhood Diseases

> [!abstract] Reference navigation

## Introduction

Childhood diseases are illnesses, impairments, or abnormal conditions that affect infants, children, and adolescents, spanning the age range from the fetus through adolescence. Childhood is a period of rapid growth and environmental change, so disease in children must be understood differently from disease in adults. Some disorders, such as precocious puberty, are unique to childhood; others, such as acute nephritis, are common in children but rare in adults; and some adult conditions, including essential hypertension and gout, are infrequent in children.

Pediatric care must deal not only with illness but with the prevention and treatment of congenital anomalies, nutritional disorders, infections, accidents, and abuse. Infectious disease remains a major cause of childhood death in many settings, and most of the common communicable diseases, such as measles, chicken pox, and mumps, are encountered in childhood, while nutrition and injury are especially important in public health.

Age changes both risk and diagnosis. Newborns may show serious illness through nonspecific signs such as poor feeding or lethargy, while older children can describe pain, weakness, or breathing trouble more clearly. Growth, immunity, vaccination status, nutrition, family environment, and school exposure all affect which diseases appear and how they should be treated.

The newborn infant is vulnerable to common infections as well as to organisms that are especially dangerous in early life, such as Escherichia coli, Staphylococcus aureus, and group B hemolytic streptococci. Infection may be acquired before birth, during delivery, or later in the nursery. Serious infections in infancy include pneumonia, meningitis, and septicemia, and they may present with very few signs beyond poor feeding, lethargy, pallor, or mild fever.

Because newborn resistance is poor, early diagnosis and treatment are important even when infection is only suspected. Congenital defects of each part of the immune system can also leave infants unable to fight infection. In some forms of agammaglobulinemia, a lack of antibodies in the blood, untreated patients may die from overwhelming infection in infancy or early childhood.

Infection control is a central part of pediatrics. The evidence of infection may be maternal, hospital-based, or environmental, and the infant’s symptoms may be nonspecific. This means physicians must judge risk from age, birth history, feeding, temperature, activity, and exposure rather than waiting for dramatic signs.

## Respiratory disorders

Respiratory problems are common in the newborn period. The most severe is respiratory distress syndrome, also called hyaline membrane disease, which occurs in 0.5 to 1 percent of all deliveries and is especially common in premature infants, infants of diabetic mothers, or after cesarean delivery; rarely it strikes full-term infants with no apparent cause. Affected infants breathe rapidly and shallowly and exchange gases poorly; without expert treatment they may die within hours or develop permanent complications.

Respiratory distress is an example of a disease in which immaturity, birth circumstances, and the physical transition to independent breathing all matter. Such cases show the need for intensive pediatric management in the earliest stages of life.

## Malnutrition

Malnutrition means disease caused by an improper diet. In developed countries the most common form is obesity, which contributes to poor health throughout life. In poorer countries, by contrast, undernutrition and deficiency disease remain major problems. Inadequate food intake leads to wasting, stunted growth, pallor, fatigue, and increased susceptibility to infection.

Kwashiorkor, a severe protein deficiency disorder, affects mainly children between six months and five years old, often after weaning from breast milk to a starchy diet, and is prevalent in parts of [[Africa]], [[Asia]], and Latin America. Children with kwashiorkor are small, edematous, and often have enlarged livers, altered skin pigmentation, and sparse reddish hair, and lasting effects on intellectual function are a major concern. Vitamin deficiency diseases also matter: rickets from lack of vitamin D damages the epiphyseal cartilage and bone growth, scurvy from lack of vitamin C causes bleeding and bone disease, and pellagra results from a deficiency of niacin.

[[Nutrition]] is also linked to immunity and development. A poorly nourished child is more vulnerable to infection, and infection can worsen malnutrition by reducing appetite and increasing bodily demands. This cycle explains why childhood disease, diet, poverty, and public health are closely connected.

## Accidents

In developed countries, accidents cause more childhood death and disability than disease for children other than infants. Road traffic accidents account for nearly half of accidental deaths, usually with the child as a pedestrian or cyclist, while burns and falls in the home make up about another quarter. Boys are more at risk than girls, particularly in poor inner-city families, and children suffer serious burns more easily than adults because their thin skin is more liable to full-thickness damage.

Accidental poisoning is especially common in two- to four-year-olds who explore the world by putting objects in their mouths. Most such poisonings involve household fluids or medicines, and medicinal drugs are far more likely to cause illness than household products or berries, though severe illness is uncommon because children usually swallow only a tiny amount. Low-level lead exposure, often from atmospheric pollution or leaded gasoline, is also a continued concern because of its possible effects on behavior and intelligence.

This makes accident prevention a pediatric task involving safer roads, safer homes, medicine storage, burn prevention, and reduced exposure to environmental poisons.

## Child abuse and neglect

Child abuse includes physical injury, emotional abuse, sexual abuse, deliberate poisoning, and the fabrication of illness by parents or caregivers, known as Munchausen syndrome by proxy. Children under two are especially vulnerable to direct physical abuse. Abuse and neglect can leave lasting physical and psychological damage and are major pediatric and social concerns.

Neglect is a medical issue as well as a social one. Failure to provide food, protection, supervision, or needed treatment can damage growth and development even without a single violent injury. Pediatric care therefore includes recognizing family risk, characteristic injury patterns, and emotional harm before lasting damage occurs.

## Conclusion

Childhood disease is a field shaped by development, vulnerability, and environment. Many illnesses of childhood resemble adult disorders, but the timing of growth, the immaturity of body systems, and the child’s dependence on adults create distinctive patterns of disease and prevention. Because a child passes through infancy, childhood, and adolescence almost as a series of related individuals, both diagnosis and treatment must be matched to each stage of growth.

## Key Events & Developments

- Infancy through adolescence: Childhood diseases are defined across the full pediatric age span.
- Newborn infections: Serious infections may be acquired before, during, or after birth.
- Respiratory distress syndrome: A major newborn respiratory disorder, especially in premature infants.
- Kwashiorkor: Severe protein malnutrition typically appears after weaning.
- Accidents: A leading cause of death and disability in children in developed countries.
- Child abuse recognition: Abuse and neglect are treated as major pediatric concerns.

## Interesting Facts

- Some diseases common in adults, such as gout and essential hypertension, are uncommon in children.
- Congenital immune defects can make ordinary infections fatal in infancy.
- Two- to four-year-olds are especially prone to accidental poisoning because they explore orally.
- Low-level lead exposure remains a concern because of possible effects on development.
