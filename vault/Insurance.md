# Insurance

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> Insurance transfers specified economic losses from an individual or organization to an insurer in exchange for a premium, replacing uncertainty with greater predictability. It relies on the law of large numbers and on insurable risks involving many similar exposures, accidental loss, and limits on simultaneous catastrophe.

## Introduction

Insurance ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Insurance)) is a system in which an insurer, for a premium or other consideration, promises to reimburse the insured or provide services if specified accidental events cause losses during a stated period. Its main function is to replace uncertainty about the economic cost of loss with greater certainty. Insurance is therefore a method of coping with risk.

The law of large numbers ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Law_of_large_numbers)) underlies insurance. In large homogeneous populations, the frequency and severity of events such as deaths, accidents, fires, or injuries can be predicted more accurately than in individual cases. From the insurer's standpoint, insurable risks must involve numerous similar exposures, limited chance of simultaneous catastrophic destruction, accidental loss beyond the insured's control, and measurable occurrence and amount.

The insured's viewpoint also matters. A risk is worth insuring only if the probability is not so high that premiums become excessive and if the possible loss would cause financial hardship. Property loss, death, illness, injury, and legal liability are usually insurable. Market price changes, ordinary business competition, war, currency debasement, and political risks are often uninsurable privately, though governments may insure some of them.

## Property Insurance

Property insurance ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Property_insurance)) covers loss or damage to homes, businesses, ships, cargo, and transported goods. Homeowner policies may reimburse on replacement-cost or actual-cash-value bases. Replacement-cost recovery avoids depreciation if coverage reaches a required percentage, often 80 percent, of replacement value. If coverage falls short, a coinsurance clause can reduce recovery.

Homeowner conditions require prompt written notice, proof of loss, cooperation with the insurer, advance premium payment, and honesty about material facts. Policies may protect mortgagee interests, allow cancellation on notice, and give the insurer subrogation rights against liable third parties. Exclusions commonly address vacancy, freezing, war, neglect, flood, earth movement, or other risks unless separately covered.

Business property insurance often uses building and personal property coverage. A single policy may insure buildings, fixtures, machinery, equipment, business personal property, property of others in the insured's custody, newly acquired property, off-premises property, valuable papers, records, fences, signs, and antennas. Coverage may be scheduled by item or written more broadly.

Ocean marine insurance covers ships, cargo, freight, liability, and voyage risks. Traditional clauses list perils of the sea, fire, pirates, enemies, jettison, barratry, arrests, restraints, and other maritime hazards, though courts usually interpret coverage by the named perils rather than as unlimited all-risk insurance. Running-down clauses cover collision liability, while protection and indemnity clauses cover other liabilities.

Marine warranties may be express or implied. Express warranties include free-of-capture-and-seizure exclusions for war and exclusions for strikes, riots, and civil commotion. Implied warranties concern seaworthiness, lawful voyage, and deviation from the intended course. Inland marine insurance adapts transportation coverage to goods moving by land, typically on named-peril terms.

## Liability, Automobile, and Workers' Compensation

Liability insurance protects against legal responsibility for injury or property damage to others. Personal liability coverage is often part of homeowner insurance and may include medical payments to guests regardless of negligence. Professional liability policies address claims against physicians, lawyers, architects, engineers, and other professionals, often with special rules because reputation and multiple claimants may be involved.

Automobile insurance is one of the largest property-liability lines, especially in the United States. It commonly covers liability, collision damage to the vehicle, other physical damage known as comprehensive loss, and medical expenses for the driver, passengers, and others. Coverage often extends to anyone driving with the owner's permission.

Workers' compensation replaces tort suits in many employment injuries by providing statutory benefits for job-related accidents and illnesses. U.S. reforms after early-1970s studies broadened coverage, raised benefits to about or above two-thirds of average weekly wages, and added claims involving back pain, stress, and heart conditions traceable to work. Costs rose as coverage expanded and litigation increased.

## Credit, Suretyship, and Bonds

Credit insurance covers risks created by widespread use of credit, including bad debts from insolvency, death, disability, bank failure, unpaid home-loan installments, export credit losses, war, currency restrictions, import-license cancellation, and political causes. Merchandise credit insurance protects sellers whose business depends on a few buyers whose insolvency could threaten the seller's financial stability.

Suretyship differs from ordinary insurance because a bond has three parties: the principal, the obligee, and the surety. Fidelity bonds protect employers from dishonest employees, filling gaps left by theft insurance. Surety bonds cover dishonesty or failure to perform by independent or semi-independent principals such as contractors or public officials. Unlike ordinary insurance, the surety may seek reimbursement from the bonded principal.

## Life and Health Insurance

Life insurance is both protection and a major savings institution in wealthy countries. In the United States in 1990, nearly $9.4 trillion of life insurance was in force, and more than 2,200 life insurers held nearly $1.4 trillion in assets. Life insurance is less common in poorer countries but has grown with income and financial development.

Life contracts include term, whole life, and universal life. Term insurance protects for a fixed period and ends without cash value. Whole life runs for the insured's lifetime and accumulates cash value. Universal life, introduced in the United States in 1979, lets the owner vary premiums and death benefits while the insurer deducts charges and credits interest.

Health insurance varies among countries. Some systems provide government medical services; others reimburse private care; many mix public and private insurance. The United States has government medical services, Medicare for people 65 and older under 1965 Social Security amendments, and extensive private group coverage through employers.

Health-policy types include hospitalization, surgical, regular medical, major medical, disability income, dental, and long-term care. Major medical policies usually use deductibles and coinsurance rather than many small limits. Disability income policies pay periodic benefits when illness or accident prevents work, but definitions of disability vary. Renewability is critical because cancelable or nonrenewable coverage can fail exactly when chronic illness appears.

Private health-insurance problems include restricted coverage, overutilization, duplicate policies, coverage exceeding actual loss, rising medical costs, and adverse selection by people most likely to need benefits. Underwriting may reduce abuse but can deny protection to people who need it most.

Group insurance has ancient roots in burial societies, guild funds, fraternal associations, and religious plans. In the 20th century, private insurers wrote large amounts of group life, health, and annuity coverage. In 1990, more than 95 percent of the U.S. industrial labour force was covered by employer group life and health plans, driven partly by labour-union bargaining.

## Reinsurance, Law, and Regulation

Reinsurance divides risk among insurers so each bears less exposure. Treaties specify how premiums and losses are shared. Pro rata treaties divide premiums and losses by percentages, while excess-of-loss treaties leave the originating insurer responsible up to a stated amount and reinsurers responsible above it. Facultative reinsurance covers individual risks. Reinsurance stabilizes profits, smooths loss ratios, reduces capital needs, and lets insurers shed unwanted risk segments.

Insurance regulation is extensive worldwide. Germany's Federal Insurance Supervisory Authority tightly regulates premiums, reserves, and investments. The United Kingdom historically gave insurers more freedom and focused on final business results. U.S. regulation is largely state-based, while other countries mix central and local controls. Regulation seeks solvency, fair rates, policyholder protection, market stability, and reliable claims payment.

Contract law rests on representations, warranties, material facts, subrogation, indemnity, and insurable interest. Misrepresentation or concealment of material facts may let the insurer void a contract. Subrogation lets insurers recover from liable third parties after paying a loss. Indemnity prevents recovery beyond actual cash loss. Insurable interest prevents people from wagering on losses that would not personally harm them.

Modern English insurance advanced after the Great Fire of London in 1666. Fire insurers appeared after 1711, including fraudulent bubble-era schemes but also durable firms such as London Assurance Corporation and Royal Exchange Assurance Corporation. Lloyd's of London began as a 17th-century coffeehouse where merchants, bankers, and underwriters gathered for marine insurance information and later became a major insurance market and reinsurer.

American insurance began with Benjamin Franklin's Philadelphia Contributionship in 1752 and the Presbyterian Ministers' Fund in 1759. Many early property and life insurers failed because of speculation, poor management, weak statistics, inadequate reserves, exaggerated advertising, and disasters such as the Great Chicago Fire of 1871 and the San Francisco earthquake and fire of 1906. After 1910, U.S. life insurance grew steadily.

Russian and Eastern European insurance changed sharply with politics. Russia nationalized insurance after the 1917 Revolution, with Gosstrakh handling domestic insurance and Ingosstrakh foreign risks. Perestroika after 1985 and the breakup of the Soviet Union in 1991 encouraged private insurers. Eastern European systems moved from state monopolies toward varied and more decentralized arrangements.

Japanese insurance combines private enterprise with government programs for crop, livestock, forest fire, fishery, export credit, accident and health, installment credit, and social security. Rapid post-[[World Wars (World War II)|World War II]] industrialization brought strong growth, and toward the late 20th century Japan led the world in life insurance in force and ranked second after the United States in total premiums.

## Key Events & Developments

- Great Fire of London: The 1666 disaster stimulated English fire insurance.
- Lloyd's of London: A 17th-century coffeehouse grew into a major insurance market.
- Philadelphia Contributionship: Benjamin Franklin organized the first American insurance company in 1752.
- Presbyterian Ministers' Fund: Founded in 1759 as the first life insurer in the American colonies.
- Medicare: U.S. Social Security amendments of 1965 created federal health insurance for older citizens.
- Universal life: Introduced in the United States in 1979.
- Soviet transition: Private insurers emerged after perestroika and the 1991 Soviet breakup.

## Key People

- Benjamin Franklin ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Benjamin_Franklin)): Organizer of the Philadelphia Contributionship.
- Edward Lloyd: Coffeehouse owner whose shipping information business became associated with Lloyd's of London.
- Harry S. Truman ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harry_S._Truman)) and Lyndon B. Johnson ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lyndon_B._Johnson)): Figures connected with U.S. Medicare's enactment in 1965.
- Insurance actuaries: Specialists who use statistics, mortality, loss data, and probability to price and reserve insurance.
