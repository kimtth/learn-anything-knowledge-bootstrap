# Information Systems

## Introduction

An information system is an integrated set of components for collecting, storing, processing, and delivering data, information, knowledge, and digital products. Businesses use information systems to run operations, manage finances and human resources, reach customers, coordinate suppliers, and compete. Governments use them to provide services, while individuals use Internet-based systems for social life, study, shopping, banking, entertainment, and communication.

Historical information technologies changed what societies could record and process. Johannes Gutenberg's mid-15th-century printing press expanded knowledge dissemination, and Blaise Pascal's 17th-century mechanical calculator helped automate calculation. Herman Hollerith's census tabulator processed the 1890 U.S. census and helped inspire computerized information systems. UNIVAC I was installed at the U.S. Bureau of the Census in 1951 and at General Electric in 1954.

Modern development accelerated with personal computers from the late 1970s, networked computing, the Internet, the Web, mobile devices, cloud services, open-source software, and embedded sensors. Information systems became central infrastructure for organizations and daily life, while also creating security, privacy, access, and ethical problems.

## Components

![[attachments/information_systems_002.png|420]]

*English information system diagram showing user, purpose, processing, data, and output*

Information-system components include hardware, software, telecommunications, databases, data warehouses, people, and procedures. Hardware, software, and telecommunications together form information technology, or IT. The components matter only as an organized whole: data must be captured, processed, moved, interpreted, secured, and used by people following defined procedures.

Computer hardware ranges from smartphones and personal computers to distributed corporate systems and powerful parallel-processing servers in data centres. Peripheral equipment includes magnetic or solid-state storage, input-output devices, and networking gear. Hardware costs have fallen while speed and storage have grown, but power use and environmental effects remain concerns.

Computer software divides into system software and application software. Operating systems manage hardware, files, programs, and user interaction, usually through graphical interfaces. Applications handle specific user tasks, from spreadsheets and word processing to package-routing systems, industry-specific platforms, in-house applications, outsourced systems, Web-delivered software-as-a-service, proprietary products, and open-source software.

[[Telecommunications Systems (Telecommunication)|Telecommunications]] connect systems and transmit information through wired and wireless media. Coaxial cable, fibre optics, microwaves, and radio waves support local, metropolitan, wide-area, mobile, and global networking. LANs link a site, MANs cover dense urban areas, WANs connect distributed centres, and the Internet is a network of networks. Intranets serve internal users, while extranets and VPNs connect business partners securely.

Databases store interrelated records so individual records or groups can be retrieved by criteria, as with employee files or product catalogs. Data warehouses store archival data collected over time for analysis, marketing, customer service, product development, and strategic decisions. Credit-card purchases, Web transactions, and operational events can all become part of such collections.

Human resources and procedures are essential. Technical roles include development and operations managers, business analysts, systems analysts, designers, database administrators, programmers, security specialists, and computer operators. All workers need training to use systems, and procedures define how systems are operated, maintained, authorized, documented, and audited.

## Organizational Uses

Information systems ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Information_system)) support operations, knowledge work, management, and strategy. Older functional systems for marketing, production, finance, or human resources have often been replaced or supplemented by cross-functional systems that follow the organization's real processes and value chains more closely.

Transaction processing systems record and support everyday operations through which products are designed, marketed, produced, delivered, billed, and serviced. In large organizations, integrated enterprise systems and enterprise resource planning systems join sales, production, finance, and human resources. These systems can connect orders, just-in-time production, warehouses, suppliers, financial accounts, billing, and supply-chain partners.

Management reporting systems provide routine information about past and present activities for managers, especially first-level supervisors. To avoid overload, reports may be sent only on request or when exceptional conditions occur. Decision support systems and business intelligence systems help analyze data, test what-if scenarios, model outcomes, and find patterns in large data pools.

Knowledge management uses systems to preserve, share, and apply what organizations know. Collaboration platforms, document bases, intranets, analytics tools, and search systems help workers locate expertise, reuse documents, coordinate projects, and turn operational data into decisions.

## Development, Management, and Security

Information systems development involves identifying organizational needs, analyzing processes, designing data and applications, building or acquiring software, testing, implementation, training, and maintenance. Organizations may use formal methodologies, agile approaches, packaged applications, outsourcing, cloud services, or internal development depending on risk, scale, and strategy.

Information systems management aligns IT with organizational goals. Strategic systems can reshape competition, customer relationships, supply chains, and business models. Because systems can become central to execution, senior executives and sometimes boards of directors must govern architecture, investment, risk, and value creation.

Security and control protect the integrity and safety of system resources and activities. As systems became connected to the global Internet and embedded in business, government, finance, health care, air traffic control, and everyday infrastructure, their failure became a societal risk. Threats require access controls, countermeasures, backups, audits, risk assessment, monitoring, and organizational discipline.

## Economy and Society

Information systems ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Information_system)) helped globalize business together with transportation infrastructure. When paired with organizational change and complementary resources, they have raised productivity in many industries. Electronic commerce moved transactions among firms and individuals to the Internet and Web, creating auction firms, search engines, social networks, online games, and other new models.

Digital work can be moved to lower-cost locations because data, information, and knowledge travel electronically. This global redistribution created efficiencies and entrepreneurial opportunities but did not uniformly improve job markets. Broad deployment also intensified debates about privacy, property rights, free speech, universal access, information accuracy, quality of life, and cyberethics.

## Key Events & Developments

- Printing press: Johannes Gutenberg's mid-15th-century technology expanded recorded knowledge.
- Mechanical calculation: Blaise Pascal's 17th-century calculator automated arithmetic.
- Census automation: Herman Hollerith's tabulator processed the 1890 U.S. census.
- UNIVAC I: Installed at the U.S. Bureau of the Census in 1951 and General Electric in 1954.
- Personal computers: From the late 1970s, PCs brought information-system benefits to individuals and small organizations.
- Internet systems: Web-based systems enabled electronic commerce, social networking, and global digital services.
