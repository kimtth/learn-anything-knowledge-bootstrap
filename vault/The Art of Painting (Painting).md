## Introduction

Painting ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Painting)) is the expression of ideas and emotions through a two-dimensional visual language. Its elements include shape, line, colour, tone, and texture, which can suggest volume, space, movement, and light on a flat surface.

Painters combine visual elements into expressive patterns to represent real or supernatural subjects, interpret narratives, or create abstract relationships. Medium, support, scale, form, and technique all shape the final image.

Painting forms include murals, easel pictures, panels, miniatures, manuscript illumination, scrolls, screens, fans, panoramas, collages, and modern mixed media. Media include tempera, fresco, oil, acrylic, watercolour, ink, gouache, encaustic, casein, pastel, lacquer, sand, and synthetic paints.

Earlier painters were often treated as skilled artisans serving tribes, religions, guilds, courts, and states. In Asia and Renaissance Europe, the idea of the fine artist grew, and painters gained more status, signed works, chose designs, and negotiated personally with patrons.

In the 19th century, many Western painters lost secure patronage and created independent exhibitions, touring shows, or commercial relationships. This helped form the modern image of the artist as an independent creator.

## Line, Shape, and Mass

![[attachments/the_art_of_painting_painting_001.webp|420]]

*Bison cave painting from Altamira showing prehistoric line and pigment*

Line is one of painting's oldest and most intuitive conventions. Children's drawings and prehistoric rock paintings show how simple linear images can be widely understood.

Line can describe volume, weight, position, light, texture, movement, and emotional direction. Thick and thin, broken and continuous, sinuous and jagged lines create contrast and repetition.

Japanese ink painting shows the power of calligraphic line, where economy, vitality, and brush mastery give the image life. Lines may be actual contours or implied by edges, axes, alignments, and repeated visual directions.

Shape and mass include all areas of colour, tone, and texture. Their arrangement creates balance, rhythm, focus, tension, and spatial relationships across the picture surface.

## Colour, Tone, and Texture

[[Colour]] may be decorative, descriptive, symbolic, or the main expressive element. Its main attributes are hue, tone, and intensity. Red, yellow, and blue are subtractive pigment primaries; mixtures create secondary and tertiary hues.

Local hues are the expected colours of things, such as grass green. Impressionists showed that light and atmosphere alter local colour, but many representational traditions use local hues for clarity, symbolism, and design.

Tone is the relative lightness or darkness of colour. Low-key paintings use darker ranges, while high-key works use pale ranges. Painters adjust tonal systems because pigments cannot match nature's full range of light and shadow.

Complementary colours intensify one another through simultaneous contrast, described by Michel-Eugene Chevreul. Warm colours often appear to advance, while cool colours recede, a device used to suggest depth.

Texture includes tactile surface effects and visual patterns made by lines, colours, tones, and shapes. Pointillism creates granular texture, while decorative textures may imitate leaves, snow, birds, geometry, textiles, or ornamental patterns.

## Volume, Space, and Framing

Painting can create perceptual space, like a fixed window view, or conceptual space, where objects are shown from the viewpoint that best expresses their meaning. Children and many traditional artists often use conceptual scale and space.

Western painting since the Renaissance often used linear perspective, where objects shrink and parallel lines converge toward the horizon. Other traditions used stacked space, shifting viewpoints, symbolic scale, and polydimensional arrangements.

Frames shape how pictures are read. Deep ornate frames support the illusion of a window view, while many modern works avoid frames to stress flatness, objecthood, or forms advancing toward the viewer.

## Mediums and Pigments

Mediums technically bind and carry pigment, but the word also covers paints, tools, supports, surfaces, and techniques. Pigments come from natural and artificial sources, including charcoal, bone black, earths, minerals, and synthetic colours.

Rare pigments such as cinnabar, lapis lazuli, and malachite were once expensive and reserved for focal or symbolic areas. Trade and synthetic substitutes expanded painters' colour ranges.

Tempera mixes dry pigment with an emulsion and water. Egg tempera, especially yolk-based, is durable, quick drying, and capable of transparent or opaque effects. It was used in ancient murals, icons, altarpieces, manuscripts, and panels.

Fresco is painting on walls or ceilings. Buon fresco applies pigment to wet plaster, while fresco secco applies lime-resistant colours to a prepared dry or damp surface. Cave painting, Egyptian murals, Renaissance walls, and Italian frescoes show the range of mural traditions.

Sgraffito scratches through coloured plaster layers to reveal colours below. It has been used on exterior walls, folk buildings, and modern surfaces enriched with textures and mosaic materials.

## Oil, Watercolour, Ink, and Gouache

![[attachments/the_art_of_painting_painting_002.webp|420]]

*Jan van Eyck's Arnolfini Portrait, an early Netherlandish oil painting*

Oil paint mixes pigment with drying oils such as linseed oil. It became a major European fine-art medium in the 15th century through painters such as Giovanni Bellini and Hubert and Jan van Eyck, allowing glazes, subtle tones, and rich surfaces.

Oil techniques include glazing, which lays transparent colour over other paint, and scumbling, which drags opaque pale pigment over darker areas. Oils can be used on canvas, panel, metal, stone, paper, and prepared boards.

Watercolour uses pigment with gum arabic and water, usually on paper. The English method builds transparent washes, leaves white paper for highlights, and creates atmosphere through wet staining and sharp accents on dry paper.

Ink is traditional in China and Japan, where carbon ink is ground and brushed on silk or paper. East Asian brushwork is tied to calligraphy, Zen, Confucian thought, and spiritual interpretation of nature.

Gouache is opaque watercolour, quick drying and matte. It is useful for crisp lines, preparatory studies, miniatures, design work, Islamic and Indian painting, and modern artists seeking flat, vivid colour.

## Other Media

Encaustic fixes pigment with heated wax and may have been practiced in Egypt by about 3000 BCE. Coptic mummy portraits show its translucent qualities, and modern artists such as Jasper Johns used it for maps, targets, and flags.

Casein uses pigments bound with milk curd or cheese glue and lime. It dries quickly, works on rigid surfaces, can create impasto, and has been used from ancient Rome to modern artists.

Acrylic resin emulsion challenged oil's dominance by combining quick drying, durability, water thinning, elasticity, and intense colours. It suited Op art, Minimalism, Photo-realism, and hard-edge painting.

Pastel uses nearly pure pigment bound lightly into sticks. It gives direct colour but smudges easily. Oil pastels are harder and more permanent because pigment is mixed with oils and waxes.

Other media include glass painting, ivory miniatures, lacquer, sand painting, silhouettes, collage, frottage, mechanical media, holograms, video, performance, conceptual proposals, and mixed media.

## Mural, Miniature, Manuscript, and Screen Forms

Mural painting uses architectural space. Early murals often respected wall flatness, while Renaissance painters such as Giotto, Masaccio, Fra Angelico, Piero della Francesca, Michelangelo, and Raphael extended interiors with perspective, foreshortening, and trompe l'oeil architecture.

Miniature painting developed in manuscripts, portrait keepsakes, Persian and Indian books, and ivory portraits. European miniatures declined after photography, while Asian miniature traditions lasted longer.

Manuscript illumination includes Egyptian Book of the Dead papyri, Classical scrolls, Aztec maps, Mayan and Chinese codices, Celtic initials, Gothic books, Islamic and Indian miniatures, and William Blake's hand-coloured engravings.

Screens, fans, scrolls, and panoramas expanded painting beyond fixed rectangular panels. Panoramas simulated wide views and optical entertainment, while Asian scrolls and screens joined painting with poetry, calligraphy, architecture, and ceremony.

## Modern Forms

Modern painting challenged the idea that painting only creates illusions on flat supports. Relief painting, shaped canvases, attached objects, neon, mirrors, kinetic panels, conceptual documents, happenings, and performance blurred painting with sculpture, theatre, film, and design.

Artists such as Robert Rauschenberg, Jim Dine, Frank Stella, Kenneth Noland, Jean Arp, Ben Nicholson, Richard Smith, and Bridget Riley changed painting's support, surface, and relation to the viewer.

Conceptual art questioned whether a painting needed a conventional visual object at all. Instructions, proposals, or documented ideas could replace the finished painted surface.

## Imagery and Subject Matter

Early painting subjects were often prescribed by religious, tribal, dynastic, or court authority. Renaissance Europe gradually increased the artist's freedom to choose themes, styles, and interpretations.

Devotional painting presents relations between humans and the divine. Christian, Buddhist, and other religious images could inspire awe, fear, faith, salvation, meditation, or ritual devotion.

Narrative painting tells stories from scripture, mythology, literature, history, allegory, and folklore. Renaissance religious narratives turned sacred events into believable human worlds, while artists such as Bruegel and Bosch filled narratives with moral and symbolic meanings.

Portraiture balances likeness, status, psychology, decoration, and patron demands. Egyptian sarcophagi, Roman mummy portraits, Raphael, Rubens, Velazquez, Rembrandt, Goya, Courbet, van Gogh, Holbein, Matisse, Modigliani, Francis Bacon, Lucian Freud, Chuck Close, and Alex Katz show very different approaches.

Genre painting depicts everyday life: work, play, domestic scenes, markets, cafes, music halls, festivals, peasants, servants, and urban life. Dutch 17th-century painters, Chardin, Millet, Daumier, Courbet, Degas, Toulouse-Lautrec, Bonnard, Vuillard, Ashcan painters, and Social Realists all used it.

Landscape painting became an independent Western subject in the 17th century, though East Asia had long valued it spiritually. Landscape ranges from idealized Poussin and Claude to Dutch realism, Impressionism, van Gogh, Cezanne, Turner, Constable, Kokoschka, Magritte, and Diebenkorn.

Still life focuses on objects, often with symbolic meanings: bread, wine, skulls, flowers, fruits, candles, hourglasses, and food. It became important in northern Europe and later in modern experiments with structure, colour, and perception.

## Symbolism

Many cultures developed iconographic systems prescribing painting's site, form, subject, medium, and imagery. Byzantine murals, Buddhist images, Christian Trinity formats, mandalas, Egyptian tomb paintings, and Indigenous sand paintings all used symbolic systems.

Eastern symbolism often works through mood, nature, calligraphic movement, spiritual harmony, and poetic association. Indian painting uses sensuous symbols and colour moods; Chinese and Japanese painting may turn brushwork itself into a sign of spiritual empathy with nature.

Western symbolism is often more literary and codified. Saints, martyrs, virtues, vices, animals, colours, gestures, and objects can identify doctrines or narratives precisely. Yet meanings vary: dragons, snakes, birds, and colours can signify different things in different cultures.

## Key Events & Developments

- 20000-15000 BCE: Cave murals used earth pigments, charcoal, and rock surfaces.
- 15th century: Oil painting became a major European fine-art medium.
- Renaissance: Perspective, fresco, patronage, and artistic status changed Western painting.
- 19th century: Independent exhibitions and modern markets altered painters' patronage.
- 20th century: Acrylics, collage, shaped canvases, conceptual art, performance, and media technology expanded painting.
