# Ice and Ice Formations (Ice)

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> Ice is solid water whose hydrogen-bonded molecular structure produces distinctive crystalline, thermal, and physical behavior. Its formation and transformation under different pressures and temperatures explain the properties of natural ice, vapor, liquid water, and the many ice bodies that affect Earth systems.

## Introduction

Ice ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ice)) is solid water formed when vapour or liquid water freezes below 0 C, or 32 F. Vapour forms frost and snowflakes; liquid water forms river ice, sea ice, hail, and artificial ice. Natural forms include the ice sheets of [[Antarctica]] and Greenland, Arctic ice caps, mountain glaciers, permafrost ground ice, icebergs, pack ice, and winter lake or river ice.

## Structure

![[attachments/ice_001.webp|420]]

*Macro photograph of a snowflake ice crystal*

Water is unusual because the H2O molecule has two hydrogen atoms and one oxygen atom bent at about 105 degrees. Lone-pair orbitals attract nearby hydrogen atoms, forming weak but directional hydrogen bonds ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrogen_bond)) that shape the differences among vapour, liquid water, and ice.

In liquid water, hydrogen bonds ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrogen_bond)) break and reform in polymer-like chains; in vapour, molecules are mostly independent. In ice, molecules form an ordered open lattice, making ice less dense than liquid water: density falls from about 1,000 to 917 kilograms per cubic metre. This is why ice floats.

The common ice crystal near standard pressure has open hexagonal rings of oxygen atoms stacked in planes. Layers can glide, but hydrogen atoms must rearrange between oxygen atoms, sometimes creating charged lattice sites. Temperature-sensitive rearrangement links mechanical, thermal, and electrical properties.

## Properties

Mechanically, ice first deforms elastically, then creeps plastically under long shear stress. This creep is central to glacier flow and involves intracrystalline gliding, recrystallization, and movement of lattice defects called dislocations.

Thermally, the heat of fusion of water is 334 kilojoules per kilogram. Hydrostatic pressure lowers melting point by 0.0074 C per bar, so a 300-metre glacier at melting temperature has basal ice about 0.25 C colder than surface ice.

Optically, pure ice is transparent, while bubbles make it cloudy. It is weakly birefringent and appears blue or green in glacier crevasses because red wavelengths are absorbed. Snow is highly reflective, glacier ice less so, and dry polar ice is transparent enough to radio waves for radar thickness measurement.
