## Introduction

Islam ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islam)) is a major world religion proclaimed by [[Muhammad and the Religion of Islam (Muhammad)|Muhammad]] in [[Arabia]] in the 7th century CE. The Arabic word islam means surrender, expressing the core idea that a Muslim submits to the will of Allah, the one creator, sustainer, and restorer of the world. God's will is made known through the Quran ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quran)), revealed to Muhammad, who is understood as the final prophet completing the line of Adam, Noah, Abraham, Moses, Solomon, and Jesus.

Islam combines strict monotheism, shared ritual practice, scripture, law, community identity, and social ethics. Muhammad's early followers developed strong bonds through persecution in Mecca, and the religion spread rapidly through the Middle East, Africa, Europe, India, Southeast Asia, and China. By the early 21st century, Muslims numbered more than 1.5 billion, linked by faith and the idea of one community despite sectarian and cultural diversity.

## Hadith, Consensus, and Legal Reasoning

The Hadith ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hadith)) preserve reports of Muhammad's words and deeds and became essential to Sunnah, the Prophet's normative example. Six collections compiled in the 9th century CE became authoritative for Sunnis, while Shiites developed four canonical collections of their own. The doctrine of ijma, or consensus, arose in the 8th century CE to stabilize legal thought and community practice.

Ijtihad, or exertion of reasoning, addressed new legal and doctrinal problems. Early ijtihad produced many opinions, but Sunni law increasingly replaced it with qiyas, strict analogy from Quran and Hadith, while consensus closed many questions. Shiism preserved a stronger place for ijtihad, and reformers from the 18th to 20th centuries revived calls for renewed reasoning.

## God

The Quranic doctrine of God ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/God)) is uncompromisingly monotheistic. God has no partner, equal, incarnation, or intermediary. He creates by command, sustains the universe, orders all things justly, and surrounds creation with mercy. He is majestic and sovereign but also near to human beings, responding to distress and guiding humanity on the straight path.

Quranic monotheism answered pagan Arabian belief in blind fate by replacing impersonal destiny with a powerful, provident, merciful God. The Quran rejects idolatry and the divinities associated with sanctuaries such as the Kabah in Mecca. The order of the universe, its design, and its purposeful arrangement serve as signs of God's unity and lordship.

## Humanity

According to the Quran ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quran)), God created humans from clay and jinn from fire. Humans are God's vicegerents on earth and bear the trust of responsibility that the rest of creation refused. Adam's disobedience is accepted, but Islam does not treat it as original sin; God forgave Adam. Human beings have dignity, freedom, and purpose, but they are also frail, proud, rebellious, and tempted to forget creaturely limits.

The cardinal human sin is pride, because it treats the self as sufficient and so associates humanity with God's exclusive status. Nature is made serviceable to humans, but humans are created to serve and obey God. This tension between noble station and moral weakness makes guidance, revelation, accountability, and repentance central.

## Prophecy

Prophets are human messengers chosen by God. The Quran requires recognition of all prophets, though some, including Abraham, Noah, Moses, and Jesus, are especially steadfast. Prophets are vindicated by God: Abraham is saved from fire, Noah from the Deluge, Moses from Pharaoh, and Jesus from crucifixion. They are not divine but are perfected human recipients of revelation.

[[Muhammad and the Religion of Islam (Muhammad)|Muhammad]] is the last and greatest prophet because earlier messages are consummated in him. Gabriel brought the Quran to his heart, and revelation came with overwhelming weight and certainty. The Quran describes itself as divine speech from a heavenly source, not poetry, soothsaying, or invention.

## The Five Pillars

The Five Pillars of Islam give Muslim life a shared ritual grammar. The first pillar is the profession of faith, or shahadah, affirming that there is no god but God and that [[Muhammad and the Religion of Islam (Muhammad)|Muhammad]] is God's messenger. Prayer, almsgiving, fasting, and pilgrimage then translate belief into repeated action. Zakat, the required giving of alms, connects worship to social obligation by treating wealth as accountable before God and by supporting the needy and the community.

## Prayer

Prayer ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prayer)) is the second pillar of Islam and consists of five daily canonical prayers: before sunrise, after noon, late afternoon, after sunset, and before sleep. Ablutions prepare the worshipper, the muezzin calls people to prayer, and the imam leads the congregation toward Mecca. Each prayer includes standing recitation, bowing, and prostration in units called rakah.

Friday congregational prayer replaces the noon prayer and includes a khutbah, or sermon, with Quranic recitation and moral, social, or political instruction. Night prayers are encouraged, especially during Ramadan, when tarawih prayers are performed. In strict doctrine, even the sick must pray, though posture may be adapted.

## Fasting

Fasting in Ramadan is the fourth pillar. From daybreak to sunset, eating, drinking, and smoking are forbidden. Ramadan is sacred because the Quran was revealed in that month, especially on the Night of Power, usually observed on the night of 26-27 Ramadan. Travelers and the sick may postpone fasting, while the elderly and incurably sick may feed a poor person instead if able.

## Hajj

The hajj ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hajj)) is the fifth pillar and is required once in a lifetime for Muslims who can afford it and provide for their families during absence. Pilgrims enter ihram, wear simple seamless garments, avoid certain acts, circle the Kabah seven times, touch or kiss the Black Stone, move between Safa and Marwah, go to Mina and Arafat, spend a night at Muzdalifah, and offer sacrifice before leaving ihram.

## Islamic Thought

Islamic theology, or kalam, and philosophy, or falsafah, developed as rational disciplines. Theology defended Islamic principles, while philosophy pursued Greek and Hellenistic sciences and reason. Theologians and philosophers occupied a middle position between strict traditionalists and thinkers whose reasoning seemed to leave the community. In practice, declaring a professing Muslim an unbeliever remained difficult except on fundamental matters such as God, revelation, and judgment.

## Theology and Sectarianism

Sectarian disputes began after [[Muhammad and the Religion of Islam (Muhammad)|Muhammad]]'s death. Sunnis held that he designated no successor, and the community chose Abu Bakr as caliph before the Prophet's burial. Shiites held that Muhammad had designated Ali ibn Abi Talib, his son-in-law and husband of Fatimah. Ali eventually became fourth caliph in 656, but dispute over rightful leadership created the major Sunni-Shiite division.

## The Kharijites

Kharijites emerged after rebellion against the third caliph, Uthman, and later opposed Ali because he submitted his claim to arbitration. They believed grave sinners who did not repent ceased to be Muslim, and they made righteous works part of faith. Their militant interpretation of enjoining good and forbidding evil justified rebellion, and their intense idealism made them hostile to most established authority.

## The Mutazilah

The Mutazilah arose as rational theologians shaped by disputes over faith, works, free choice, God's justice, and contact with Greek philosophy and other religions. They taught that works are part of faith, that grave sinners occupy a middle position unless they repent, and that human beings freely choose moral acts. Divine predestination of human actions, they argued, would contradict God's justice and human responsibility.

The Mutazilah trusted human reason to know good and evil independently of revelation, though revelation confirmed reason. Their concern for divine unity led them to reject any similarity between God and created things. They defended Islam through rational argument but eventually provoked Sunni reaction.

## Sunnism

Sunnism formed as a traditional majority reaction against sectarian extremes and Mutazilite rationalism. Sunnah means a well-trodden path, and in this context it became the way of the consolidated majority, or al-jamaah. Hadith emphasizing the community's majority strengthened Sunni identity and transformed community consensus into a mark of protected truth.

Sunni theologians rejected the Mutazilite idea of two independent actors, God and humanity. Al-Ashari taught that God creates human acts and humans acquire them, while al-Maturidi allowed humans to be real actors without being creators. They also differed on reason's ability to know good and evil apart from revelation, but both became central representatives of Sunni theology.

## Shiism

Shiism began as loyalty to Ali and the demand that leadership return to the Prophet's family. Over time, political legitimism developed into a theology of the imam as infallible interpreter, bearer of esoteric knowledge, and metaphysical guide. Twelver Shiites recognize 12 imams, the last of whom disappeared in the 9th century and will return to fill the world with truth and justice. In his absence, Shiite mujtahids interpret law and doctrine.

## Ismailis

Ismailis are a major branch of Shiism that upheld the claim of Ismail rather than Musa as seventh imam. Some treated Ismail as the final imam, while most continued the imamate through his descendants. Ismaili teaching spread from North Africa to Sind in the 9th century, and the Fatimid dynasty built a powerful empire in Egypt. Later Ismailis divided into Nizaris, led by the Aga Khan, and Mustalis, centred in Mumbai and Yemen.

## Sufism

Sufism, Islamic mysticism, arose from ascetic protest against worldliness and externalist religion. Sufis emphasized moral motivation, contrition, the state of the heart, inward purification, spiritual knowledge, and the experience of divine nearness. Their practices and interpretations sometimes conflicted with legalist and theological forms of Islam but also became deeply embedded in Muslim devotional life.

## The Ahmadiyyah

The Ahmadiyyah began in Punjab in the late 19th century with Mirza Ghulam Ahmad, who claimed inspired status and sought to defend and synthesize religion under Islam. He adopted views that Jesus died naturally and that jihad by the sword had been replaced by jihad of the pen. A succession split in 1914 produced Lahore and Qadiani branches. Because the main group treated Ghulam Ahmad as a prophet, many Muslims opposed it, and Pakistan imposed restrictions in 1974 and 1984.

## Islamic Philosophy

Islamic philosophy ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islamic_philosophy)) developed from Greek, Pahlavi, Sanskrit, and scientific translations, especially by the middle of the 9th century. Unlike theology, it did not confine itself to the Muslim community; Muslims, Christians, and Jews participated in Arabic philosophical culture. It assumed that unaided reason could reach truth and that truth properly understood would not contradict Islam.

## The Oriental Philosophy

Al-Farabi helped make philosophy powerful enough to shape later Islamic thought, especially through Avicenna. Avicenna distinguished essence and existence and argued that existence cannot be inferred simply from essence. He adapted Aristotelian and Neoplatonic ideas to questions of creation, forms, immortality, revelation, and divine law, influencing theology, mysticism, and Persian poetry in Eastern Islam.

## Philosophy

Western Islamic philosophy reached a major form in Averroes, or Ibn Rushd, a 12th-century judge and philosopher. He argued that divine law seeks the happiness of all and requires belief and worship from all, while those capable of demonstrative knowledge have a legal obligation to pursue philosophy. He held that theologians should not confuse the masses with speculative interpretations and that philosophy must guide higher interpretation.

## Theology

Averroes criticized al-Ghazali's attack on philosophers as philosophically incompetent and legally harmful. He also criticized al-Farabi and Avicenna when they accommodated theologians too much. For the masses, Quran, Hadith, and consensus provide sufficient conviction; for philosophers, demonstration is necessary; theology occupies an intermediate place and must remain under philosophy and divine law. His commentaries on Aristotle strongly influenced medieval and Renaissance Europe.

## Later Philosophy and Modernism

Later Islamic philosophy ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islamic_philosophy)) was challenged by resurgent traditionalists such as Ibn Taymiyyah, who called for return to the pious ancestors. Yet philosophy reappeared in new forms through synthesis with theology and mysticism, especially after al-Ghazali's program. Modernists later admired Mutazilite reason, human freedom, responsibility, traditionalist devotion to early Islam, and Ashari defense of the community. They argued that true Islam supports critical thought and engagement with modern science, philosophy, and social-political ideas.

## Social and Ethical Principles

Islamic social ethics encourage marriage and treat celibacy as exceptional. The Quran permits polygamy up to four wives only with justice, while also warning that true justice among wives is difficult. Divorce was traditionally vested mainly in husbands, though women could seek divorce through courts. Chastity is central, adultery is severely punished, false accusation is also punished, and the Quran describes spouses as garments for one another, joined by love and mercy.

## Education

Muslim [[Education|education]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Education)) began in the 8th century to transmit the Quran and Sunnah. The search for knowledge produced sciences of tradition, history, literature, law, Hadith, Quran commentary, and theology. Greek philosophy, medicine, and mathematics generated lay knowledge and rationalist challenge. The madrasah system later separated religious and secular sciences, preserving law and theology but weakening intellectual exchange over time.

## Music

Orthodox Islam initially forbade instrumental music, while Quran recitation developed into the sophisticated art of tajwid. Courts patronized music influenced by Persian and Greek forms. Al-Farabi was credited with constructing an organ-like instrument, and Amir Khosrow synthesized Indian and Persian music. Sufis adopted vocal and instrumental sama for spiritual practice, and Jalal al-Din al-Rumi interpreted music as a sign of divine longing.

## Literature

Islamic [[The Art of Literature (Literature)|literature]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Literature)) restricted drama and pure fiction because representation and invention raised religious concerns, but story literature survived through works such as The Thousand and One Nights and Kalilah wa Dimnah. Hyperbole became a device for imaginative escape without literal falsehood. Poetry, especially lyric, panegyric, satire, and mystical poetry, allowed ambiguity between earthly and spiritual love and sometimes veiled criticism of orthodoxy.

## Architecture

Islamic architecture, especially mosque architecture, is the most important expression of Islamic art. The mosque's spaciousness and arabesque design externalize divine unity and infinity. Early monuments include the mosque of Amr in Egypt and the Dome of the Rock in Jerusalem, completed in 691. Regional forms developed in Damascus, Cordoba, Granada, Persia, Turkey, and elsewhere, using mihrabs, arcades, domes, eyvans, glazed faience, and monumental space.

## Islamic Myth and Legend

Islam's strict monotheism limited mythology, but Quranic stories were elaborated by commentators, storytellers, mystics, poets, and popular preachers. Jewish, Christian, Hellenistic, and ancient Middle Eastern materials entered Muslim legend. Stories about Muhammad and his family inspired the masses, while mystics spiritualized Quranic words and developed layered meanings that sometimes became quasi-mythological.

## Cosmogony and Eschatology

Islamic cosmogony teaches creation from nothing by God's command kun, Be. Angels were made from light, Adam from clay, and Iblis refused to prostrate before Adam and was cursed. God made a pre-eternal covenant with Adam's descendants, asking, Am I not your Lord? Mystical poetry often treats this covenant as the origin of love, fate, and human relation to God. Angels and jinn appear in cosmology, folklore, and legend.
