## Introduction

![[attachments/sweden_001.webp|420]]

*Relief map of Sweden*

Sweden is a northern European country on the Scandinavian Peninsula. Its name comes from the Svear, or Suiones, mentioned by Tacitus in AD 98. Stockholm has been the permanent capital since 1523.

The country occupies most of the peninsula it shares with [[Norway]]. Its land slopes from mountains along the Norwegian frontier toward the Baltic Sea. Sweden is geologically old and stable, but glaciers shaped its soils, lakes, ridges, and rocky coast. More than 1,300 miles of coastline include many islands and archipelagoes.

Sweden has a favourable [[Climate and Weather (Climate)|climate]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Climate)) for its northern latitude because Atlantic winds and the North Atlantic Current moderate temperatures. It has a continuous history as a sovereign state of about 1,000 years, though its borders changed often until 1809.

Modern Sweden is a constitutional monarchy ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constitutional_monarchy)) with parliamentary democracy dating from 1917. It developed from a poor agricultural society into a wealthy postindustrial welfare state with high life expectancy, advanced social services, strong education, and a long tradition of neutrality and international mediation.

## Land

Sweden borders [[Finland]] in the northeast, Norway in the west, the Gulf of Bothnia and Baltic Sea in the east, and Denmark across the Oresund in the south. It stretches about 1,000 miles north to south and is traditionally divided into Norrland, Svealand, and Gotaland.

Norrland is the largest and most sparsely populated region, with mountains, forests, river valleys, and large lakes. The Kolen, or Scandinavian, Mountains mark much of the Norwegian border. Kebnekaise and Sarek are among the highest northern peaks.

Svealand includes lowlands in the east and highlands in the west, while Gotaland includes the wooded Smaland highlands and the rich agricultural plains of Skane. Skane is the oldest settled and most densely populated agricultural region and resembles Denmark more than northern Sweden.

Swedish [[Rivers|rivers]] mostly begin in northern mountains and flow southeast to the Gulf of Bothnia or Baltic Sea. They provide hydroelectric power except in protected far-northern areas. Major lakes include Vaner, Vatter, and Malar.

Swedish [[Soils|soils]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soil)) are mainly glacial till. Till from old granite and gneiss bedrock is often poor and acidic, while clayey till and uplifted sea-bottom clays in southern and central Sweden form fertile agricultural soils. Marshland and peat cover about one-fifth of the country.

## Climate and Conservation

About 15 percent of Sweden lies north of the Arctic Circle. Northern areas have midnight sun in summer and long winter darkness, while Stockholm still has only a few hours of semidarkness in early summer and short daylight in December.

The climate reflects alternating Atlantic and continental influences. The north has long snowy winters and severe cold, while southern winters are milder and less regular. Spring arrives much earlier in Skane than in northern Norrland.

Sweden has been a leader in [[Conservation of Natural Resources (Conservation)|conservation]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conservation_biology)). Sarek National Park, established in 1909, was Europe's first national park. A modern environmental protection act followed in 1969, and many national parks and reserves have been created. Serious problems remain, including acidified lakes, threatened groundwater, and pollution in the Baltic, Kattegat, and Skagerrak.

## People

Sweden was long ethnically, linguistically, and religiously homogeneous, but postwar immigration changed this pattern. Many immigrants came from Nordic neighbours, and later from wider Europe and beyond. Nearly one in five Swedes has at least one foreign-born parent.

Indigenous and minority groups include Sami people in the northern interior and Finnish-speaking communities near the Finnish border. The Sami historically hunted, fished, and developed reindeer herding, though many now also work in other occupations.

Swedish is the national language and the mother tongue of most people. It is a North Germanic language related to Danish, Norwegian, Icelandic, and Faroese. Recognized national minority languages include Sami, Finnish, Meankieli, Romani, Yiddish, and sign language, and immigration has brought about 200 languages into the country.

Pre-Christian religion centred on Norse gods such as Odin, Thor, Freyr, and Freyja. Christianity spread in the 11th century, and Lutheranism became dominant after the Reformation. The Church of Sweden was the state church until 2000. Immigration increased Roman Catholic, Orthodox, and Muslim communities, while Judaism has been present since 1776.

Most Swedes live in the southern third of the country and in towns. Stockholm, Gothenburg, and Malmo dominate urban life, while northern Sweden remains more sparsely populated because of climate, terrain, and economic history.

## Economy

![[attachments/sweden_003.webp|420]]

*Kiruna iron ore mine landscape*

Sweden has one of the world's high per capita national incomes, but also high taxes. Private enterprise is important and export-oriented, yet taxation funds education, health care, child care, pensions, sick pay, and other social transfers. Roughly three-fifths of GDP has passed through the public sector when transfers are included.

Exports equal about one-third of GDP, making Sweden dependent on free international trade. Sweden joined the European Union in 1995 but retained the krona instead of adopting the euro. The Riksbank sets monetary policy and price stability.

Agriculture ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agriculture)) uses less than one-tenth of land. The growing season ranges from about 240 days in the south to 120 days in the north. Southern Sweden grows wheat, barley, sugar beets, oilseeds, potatoes, and vegetables, while northern farms emphasize hay and potatoes. Animal agriculture, especially dairy, is important.

[[Forestry and Wood Production (Forestry)|Forestry]] is central because forests cover much of Sweden. Timber, pulp, paper, boards, prefabricated houses, and furniture are major products. Fishing declined in relative importance, though coastal and inland fisheries remain part of the economy.

## Manufacturing, Trade, and Services

Swedish manufacturing ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manufacturing)) is export-oriented and produces much export income. Forest products, iron and steel, engineering, automobiles, aerospace, electronics, communications equipment, petrochemicals, pharmaceuticals, biotechnology, glassware, metal processing, and plastics all matter.

Engineering is the largest manufacturing field. Volvo and Saab gave Sweden strong automotive reputations, while Stockholm and Vasteras became centres for electrical, electronic, and communications industries. The iron and steel industry remains linked to Bergslagen and coastal plants at Oxelosund and Lulea.

Swedish trade ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trade)) shifted from raw materials and semimanufactures toward finished goods such as cars, telecommunications equipment, hydroelectric equipment, high-technology products, chemicals, and biotechnology. Major export markets include Germany, the United Kingdom, the United States, Norway, Finland, and Denmark.

The service sector ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tertiary_sector)) employs more than one-third of workers and became the main growth area after industrial employment peaked around 1960. Business services, technology consulting, tourism, administration, education, health, and child care are major parts of employment.

## Government and Justice

Sweden is a constitutional monarchy ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constitutional_monarchy)) with parliamentary government. Parliamentarism became established after the 1917 election, when the king had to accept a Liberal-Social Democratic government. Later reforms expanded suffrage, including women's suffrage and more equal electoral rules.

The Swedish legal tradition is shaped by the 1734 National Law Code, though little original text remains. Courts include district courts, courts of appeal, and the Supreme Court. District courts use legally trained judges and lay assessors in many serious criminal and family cases.

Administrative decisions can be appealed through administrative authorities and courts. Legal aid is available, fines are linked to daily income, and offenders under 18 are imprisoned only in exceptional cases.

## Security, Health, and Welfare

Sweden has not been occupied since the 16th century and has not been at war since 1814. It avoided military alliances through neutrality until joining the EU in 1995, while maintaining army, navy, and air-force capacity. Conscription ended in 2010.

Sweden strongly supports the [[United Nations]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_Nations)), peacekeeping, disarmament, nonproliferation, arms control, and bans on antipersonnel mines. Its national police service includes security, criminal investigation, forensic, and county police bodies.

High taxes fund broad welfare programs. National health insurance covers residents, infant mortality is low, life expectancy is high, and primary health centres are widespread. Benefits include paid parental leave, child allowances, study support, accident insurance, unemployment support, job retraining, sheltered employment, and relocation grants.

The Swedish welfare state is also a model of social citizenship. Its policies connect employment, taxation, family life, public health, education, housing, and gender equality rather than treating them as separate issues. Paid parental leave, child care, active labour-market policy, and support for education helped make high female labour-force participation compatible with family policy. The model depends on trust, administrative capacity, and high taxes, which also made it vulnerable to debate during economic downturns and immigration-driven social change.

Housing policy after World War II improved standards through subsidies, rent control, municipal housing companies, and the Million Home Program. Economic crisis in the 1990s led to reduced subsidies and new housing-policy problems, followed by later efforts to stimulate construction and support renters.

## Education and Culture

![[attachments/sweden_002.webp|420]]

*Stockholm old town skyline*

Swedish [[Education|education]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Education)) is mostly public and free. Municipalities run primary and secondary schools, while the state oversees universities and colleges. Comprehensive school is compulsory for nine years, from ages 7 to 16, and includes free lunches, transport, and materials.

Upper secondary school offers theoretical and vocational programs, and many young people continue to higher education. Academic freedom is protected, preschool is widely available, and independent schools exist alongside municipal schools.

Swedish festivals include spring bonfires, Midsummer Eve, Advent, St. Lucia's Day, Christmas, Easter, and Whitsuntide. Food traditions include smorgasbord, pickled herring, crispbread, lingonberries, preserved dairy foods, lussekatter, ginger biscuits, Christmas ham, and glogg.

Swedish arts include music, literature, theatre, cinema, design, sculpture, and painting. Important figures include J.H. Roman, Franz Berwald, Birgit Cullberg, Victor Sjostrom, Ingmar Bergman, Greta Garbo, Ingrid Bergman, Max von Sydow, Sven Nykvist, Carl Larsson, Anders Zorn, Bruno Liljefors, Carl Milles, Astrid Lindgren, Stieg Larsson, and Henning Mankell.

## Early History and Viking Age

The last ice sheet retreated from southern Sweden about 14,000 years ago. The first traces of human life date to about 9000 BC near Malmo. Later Stone Age communities hunted, fished, farmed, raised cattle, used flint tools, and developed trade links with central Europe.

During the Bronze Age, rock carvings, weapons, ornaments, cremation burials, and long-distance trade show complex society. Around the beginning of the Viking Age, independent tribes lived in what became Sweden: the Svear around Uppsala and the Gotar in Ostergotland and Vastergotland.

Swedish Vikings dominated trade routes through western Russia and Ukraine, controlling river routes to the Black Sea, Constantinople, the Caspian, and the East. They traded slaves, furs, and silver and founded Birka, often considered Sweden's oldest town.

## Christianization and Medieval Sweden

The Frankish monk Ansgar tried to Christianize Sweden in 830 at Birka, but lasting success came later. Vastergotland accepted Christianity in the 11th century, while Uppsala remained a pagan stronghold until its temple was destroyed near the end of the century.

Olof Skotkonung, baptized at Skara about 1000, supported Christianity. Civil wars and throne disputes continued until a more stable monarchy emerged in the 13th century. Debate continues over whether Sweden formed from the Lake Malar region under the Svear or from Gotaland areas.

Sweden entered the Kalmar Union with Denmark and Norway, but Danish and German influence in administration, taxation, and church affairs provoked conflict. Engelbrekt Engelbrektsson led a major rebellion in 1434. Charles Knutsson and later Sten Sture figures represented antiunion resistance.

The 1520 Stockholm Bloodbath, ordered after Christian II took Stockholm, executed many Sture supporters. Gustav Vasa escaped, led rebellion from Dalarna, became regent in 1521, and was elected king in 1523, dissolving the Kalmar Union.

## Vasa Kings and the Age of Greatness

Gustav Vasa restored royal power, built central administration, reduced dependence on Lubeck, ended Hanseatic privileges, stabilized finances, and led the Swedish Reformation with support from figures such as Olaus Petri.

His successors pursued Baltic power. Erik XIV gained Estonia but fought Denmark, Lubeck, and Poland. John III, Charles IX, and later rulers faced struggles over religion, aristocratic power, and Baltic trade.

Under Gustav II Adolf, Sweden became a major European power. Administration was professionalized, a standing army organized, and mobile artillery improved. Sweden gained Ingria and Kexholm from Russia, Livonia and Baltic customs rights from Poland, and entered the Thirty Years' War in 1630.

Gustav II Adolf defeated imperial forces at Breitenfeld in 1631 and died at Lutzen in 1632. Chancellor Axel Oxenstierna led the regency for Queen Christina. Sweden emerged as the dominant Baltic power and gained territories through war and diplomacy.

Queen Christina abdicated in 1654, converted to Catholicism, and moved to Rome. Charles X Gustav defeated Denmark and gained Skane, Halland, Blekinge, and Bohuslan in the 1658 Peace of Roskilde, creating much of Sweden's modern southern and western border.

## From Absolutism to Parliamentary Change

Continuous warfare strengthened nobles but strained the crown. Charles XI reduced noble holdings in the 1680s, restored crown finances, and created Carolingian absolutism, a system geared toward military readiness.

Charles XII inherited enemies in Denmark, Poland, and Russia. Sweden's great-power policy failed during the Great Northern War, and after his era the Age of Freedom shifted authority from king to estates and Diet. Parties called Hats and Caps competed, foreign subsidies influenced politics, and wars with Russia and Prussia weakened Sweden.

Gustav III later restored stronger royal authority, but after Sweden's defeats in the Napoleonic era Gustav IV Adolf was deposed in 1809. Sweden surrendered Finland and the Aland Islands to Russia at Fredrikshamn and adopted a new constitution based on separation of powers.

## Nineteenth and Twentieth Centuries

Jean-Baptiste Bernadotte, chosen crown prince in 1810, became Charles XIV John. Instead of trying to recover Finland, he allied with Russia and Britain against Napoleon and gained Norway from Denmark through the Treaty of Kiel. Sweden's union with Norway lasted until 1905.

From 1815 to 1900, Sweden's population more than doubled despite mass emigration, mainly to North America. Agriculture was modernized by enclosure, villages were broken up, industry grew from timber and iron, and the late 19th century brought industrialization, party formation, labour politics, liberalism, conservatism, and Social Democracy.

Sweden stayed neutral in [[World Wars (World War I)|World War I]] but suffered trade disruption and food shortages. The 1917 election forced parliamentary government, and reforms introduced broader suffrage. In the 1930s, Social Democrat Per Albin Hansson used public works and agricultural support to respond to the Great Depression, helping build the welfare-state model.

During and after [[World Wars (World War II)|World War II]], Sweden remained outside war, expanded industry, strengthened social policy, and became known for neutrality, prosperity, and welfare. Later decades brought EU membership, immigration debates, economic crisis in the 1990s, globalization, and competition over the future of welfare programs.

In 2006, the Moderate-led centre-right alliance under Fredrik Reinfeldt defeated the Social Democrats. The 2008-09 global crisis hit Sweden sharply, but stimulus and recovery helped the government win again in 2010, when the anti-immigrant Sweden Democrats entered parliament.
