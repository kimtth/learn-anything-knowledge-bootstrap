# Muhammad and the Religion of Islam (Muhammad)

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> Muhammad was the Meccan and Medinan prophet who founded Islam and is understood by Muslims as God's final prophet. This account approaches his life through the Quran, Hadith, Sunnah, and early biographies while explaining the Hijrah, community formation, and historiographical difficulties surrounding his career.

## Introduction

Muhammad, in full Abu al-Qasim Muhammad ibn Abd Allah ibn Abd al-Muttalib ibn Hashim, was born in 570 in Mecca and died on June 8, 632, in Medina. He founded Islam and is accepted by Muslims as the last prophet of God. His life is difficult to write because he has been revered by Muslims, attacked in long Western polemical traditions, and interpreted by modern scholars through many different assumptions.

The main sources for Muhammad are the Quran, Hadith, Sunnah, and early biographies, or sirah. The Quran is understood by Muslims as God's word rather than Muhammad's, but it reveals essential aspects of his mission. Early biography goes back to Ibn Ishaq, preserved through Ibn Hisham and other compilers, while Hadith and Sunnah record sayings and actions that became models for Muslim life.

## Named: The Divine Attributes of God, 2000)

Islamic tradition says that signs of prophethood were recognized in Muhammad before his mission, including a mole understood by some as a prophetic mark. The Christian monk Bahira is said to have recognized this sign when Muhammad traveled with Abu Talib toward Syria. At age 25, Muhammad married Khadijah bint Khuwaylid, a wealthy Meccan woman whose business affairs he had managed. She was 15 years older, bore his children, and remained his only wife until her death.

Khadijah bore two sons who died young and four daughters: Zaynab, Ruqayyah, Umm Kulthum, and Fatimah. Fatimah later became especially important because Shiite imams and sayyids or sharifs trace descent from her and Ali. Muhammad's reputation in Mecca rested on trustworthiness, piety, and judgment, illustrated by the story in which he settled a tribal dispute over placing the Black Stone in the rebuilt Kabah by having representatives lift it together on his cloak.

Before revelation, Muhammad often withdrew for prayer and meditation outside Mecca. Islamic tradition describes early visions as like the breaking of dawn. These experiences prepared for his prophetic vocation and show his deep religious seriousness before public proclamation.

## The Hijrah and Medina

*Decorated door at Al-Masjid an-Nabawi in Medina*

On September 25, 622, Muhammad completed the Hijrah ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hijrah)) from Mecca to Yathrib, which became Medina, the City of the Prophet. This migration established Islam as a religious and social order and became the starting point for the Islamic calendar. The caliph Umar I later fixed the calendar's beginning at the first day of Muharram corresponding to July 16, 622.

Muhammad first reached Quba, where he ordered Islam's first mosque to be built, then entered Medina. Because every family wanted to host him, he let his camel choose the place to stop. There a mosque, later the Mosque of the Prophet, was built beside his dwelling, and his tomb remains there. In Medina, the community included Meccan emigrants, or muhajirun, and Medinan helpers, or ansar.

Medina required political and religious integration. Muhammad united the Aws and Khazraj tribes with the emigrants into an Islamic society, while relations with Jewish groups worsened as most did not accept him as prophet. The Medinan period transformed Muhammad's mission from persecuted preaching into community building, law, worship, conflict, diplomacy, and governance.

## The Sunnah and Hadith

The Sunnah ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sunnah)), Muhammad's deeds and model, and Hadith, his sayings, are after the Quran the most important Islamic sources. They shape law, worship, art, economics, family life, metaphysics, and personal conduct. The Sunnah covers daily actions such as greeting with al-salamu alaykum, entering a mosque, hygiene, marriage, burial, prayer, fasting, and pilgrimage, as well as inner spiritual matters.

Muhammad's sayings were preserved orally and in writing on materials such as parchment, papyrus, and camel shoulder bones. In the 8th and 9th centuries, scholars collected Hadith using rigorous criteria for chains of transmission, or isnad. These collections became foundational for Sunni and Shiite traditions and allowed Muslims to model ordinary and sacred life on the Prophet's example.

## Assessment

During 23 years of prophethood, Muhammad transmitted the Quran and his understanding of divine revelation, which became the foundation of Quranic interpretation. He established Sunnah and Hadith, taught disciples, formed a new religious and spiritual community, and created the institutional means for Islamic continuity.

Muhammad also unified Arabia in a sociopolitical structure based on the Quran and formed what the source describes as an empire of faith in the hearts and minds of his followers. His community carried his message far beyond Arabia, making his historical influence one of the most profound and enduring in world history.

## Key Events & Developments

- 570: Muhammad was born in Mecca.
- 595: Muhammad married Khadijah.
- 622: The Hijrah ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hijrah)) to Medina established the Islamic community and calendar.
- 632: Muhammad died in Medina.

## Key People

- Muhammad ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muhammad)): Founder of Islam and final prophet in Muslim belief.
- Khadijah bint Khuwaylid ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Khadijah_bint_Khuwaylid)): Muhammad's first wife and early supporter.
- Fatimah ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fatimah)): Muhammad's daughter, central to later Shiite lineage and piety.
- Ali ibn Abi Talib ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ali_ibn_Abi_Talib)): Muhammad's kinsman and son-in-law, linked to Fatimah's descendants.
- Abu Talib: Muhammad's uncle and guardian in youth.
- Umar I ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Umar)): Caliph who established the Islamic calendar system.
