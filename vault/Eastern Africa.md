# Eastern Africa

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> Eastern Africa is a diverse region extending across highlands, rift valleys, coasts, and inland lakes. Its environments, languages, trade networks, and political histories connect Africa with the Indian Ocean world.

## Introduction

Eastern Africa ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/East_Africa)) is described as the eastern part of sub-Saharan Africa made up of two traditional regions. East Africa ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/East_Africa)) includes Kenya, Tanzania, and Uganda, while the Horn of Africa ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Horn_of_Africa)) includes Somalia, Djibouti, Eritrea, and Ethiopia. The region is mostly high plateau country, with especially high areas in Ethiopia and Kenya that rise to about 6,500 to 10,000 feet. Its surface is shaped by the East African Rift System ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/East_African_Rift)), including the Great Rift Valley from the Red Sea and Gulf of Aden through Ethiopia, Kenya, and Tanzania, and the Western Rift Valley along Uganda and Tanzania. Between them lies a broad plateau that includes Lake Victoria, while Kilimanjaro in northeastern Tanzania is the highest mountain in Africa.

## East Africa

![[attachments/eastern_africa_002.webp|420]]

*LocationEasternAfrica*

East Africa's history begins with the coast until 1856. The earliest written descriptions of the East African coast come from the Periplus Maris Erythraei, probably written by a Greek merchant in Egypt in the second half of the 1st century CE, and from Ptolemy's Guide to Geography, whose East African material probably reflects geographic knowledge available at Byzantium around 400. These works show that the coast was already part of a wider Red Sea and Indian Ocean trading world.

## Azania

From about 700, Arab traders seem to have preferred the wetter coast south of modern Somalia because it had offshore islands and better landing places. They used the monsoon winds, sailing south with the northeast monsoon and returning with the southwest monsoon. They called this coast Azania ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Azania)), or the Land of Zanj, meaning the land of Black peoples, and this name remained important until about the 10th century.

## Northeastern Bantu

The spread of some Bantu peoples to the northern East African coast during the 1st millennium CE is linked with the remembered settlement area of Shungwaya north of the Tana River. Shungwaya probably flourished between the 12th and 15th centuries, before being invaded by Cushitic-speaking Oromo from the Horn of Africa. The origins of the Kamba and Kikuyu remain uncertain, but their ancestors may have left Shungwaya before the Oromo attack.

## The Luo and Maasai

In the 19th century, earlier Luo movement from west to east was followed by new movements from east to west. The Lango expanded southward and westward toward Lake Kyoga and the Victoria Nile, where they met the Acholi. The Teso and Kumam also moved west and south. Around Lake Kyoga, a strong trading network developed, showing that migration and trade were linked rather than separate processes.

## The Horn of Africa

The history of the Horn of Africa ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Horn_of_Africa)) is dominated by Ethiopia and by repeated struggles between Muslim and other herders and Christian farmers over resources and living space. Christians mostly spoke Semitic languages, while Muslims mostly spoke Cushitic languages, though both language groups came from the Afro-Asiatic stock. Visible differences in language, livelihood, and religion often became reasons for war.

## Aksum

The Ethiopian empire of Aksum entered recorded history at the end of the 1st century CE as a trading state known around the Red Sea. Its people spoke Geez, a Semitic language, and mostly worshipped Middle Eastern gods, though some African deities survived. Its port of Adulis received textiles, glassware, tools, jewelry, copper, iron, and steel, and exported ivory, tortoiseshell, rhinoceros horn, gold, silver, slaves, frankincense, and myrrh.

## The Somali

The Somali, a Cushitic people, separated from the Oromo in what is now north-central Kenya. Their livelihood depended on the one-humped Arabian camel, sheep, and goats. During the first centuries CE, they migrated southeast, followed the Tana River to the Indian Ocean, and then turned north to people the whole Somali peninsula.

## The Solomonids

A Christian state led by Semitized Agew reappeared in the 12th century. This Zagwe dynasty was replaced in the late 13th century by the Solomonid dynasty, which claimed descent from King Solomon and the Queen of Sheba. That claimed genealogy gave legitimacy and continuity to Ethiopian national history. In the 14th and 15th centuries, Solomonid monarchs expanded south and east.

## Abyssinia

After the Oromo advances, the Christians retreated into Abyssinia, a defensible and socially cohesive highland unit. It included mostly Christian, Semitic-speaking peoples in most of Eritrea, Tigray, and Gonder, plus parts of Gojam, Shewa, and Welo. For two centuries, Abyssinia marked Ethiopia's limits, though not its wider influence, because the Christian highlands still received trade from the hinterland on its way to the Red Sea and Nile valley.

## Pan-Somalism

The Italians left Somaliland with towns, communications, and administrative infrastructure, and the southern centres became incubators of pan-Somali ideas. These ideas quickly reached northern Somalis under British rule. On May 13, 1943, the Somali Youth Club formed in Mogadishu. It promoted Somali unity beyond clan divisions and soon included religious leaders, the gendarmerie, and junior administrators. By 1947, when it became the Somali Youth League, much of Somaliland's educated class supported pan-Somalism.

## Key Events & Developments

- 1st century CE: The Periplus Maris Erythraei described northern Somali and East African coastal trade, including cloth, grain, oil, copper, tin, gums, tortoiseshell, ivory, and enslaved people.
- 700: Arab traders increasingly favoured the coast south of Somalia, using monsoon winds and calling the region Azania ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Azania)) or the Land of Zanj.
- 1107: A Kufic inscription at Zanzibar recorded the building of a mosque, showing that substantial Muslim settlement existed there.
- 13th century: Kilwa rose through the Sofala gold trade, building major stone architecture such as Husuni Kubwa.
- 1498: Vasco da Gama's arrival began Portuguese intervention on the East African coast.
- 1593: The Portuguese began building Fort Jesus at Mombasa, making the city the centre of their coastal control.
- 1698: Omani forces under Sayf ibn Sultan captured Fort Jesus, ending Portuguese dominance at Mombasa.
- 1837: Sayyid Said took control of Mombasa, making the East African coast subject again to one outside power.
- 1840: Sayyid Said moved his capital from Muscat to Zanzibar, confirming Zanzibar as the coast's main commercial centre.
- 1856: Sayyid Said died, and Zanzibar stood as the main centre from which trade and inland caravans spread.
- 1886: The Anglo-German Agreement divided East African influence between Britain in the north and Germany in the south.
- 1961-1964: Tanganyika became independent, merged with Zanzibar, and later took the name Tanzania.
- 321: Emperor Ezana and the Aksumite court converted to Christianity, helping make Christianity central to Ethiopian state history.
- 1543: Ahmad Gran was killed at Weyna Dega, ending the Muslim war against Christian Ethiopia.
- 1896: Ethiopia defeated Italy at Adwa, and Italy later recognized Ethiopian independence in the Treaty of Addis Ababa.
- 1935-1936: Italy invaded Ethiopia from Eritrea and Somalia, used modern warfare, and proclaimed an East African empire.
- 1952: The United Nations approved the federation of Eritrea with Ethiopia.
- 1960: Somalia became independent and promoted Somalia irredenta, including Djibouti, northern Kenya, and the Ogaden.
- 1974: The Dergue deposed Haile Selassie and began a socialist military state in Ethiopia.
- 1977-1978: Somalia invaded the Ogaden, but Ethiopia, aided by the Soviet Union, Cuba, North Korea, and Yemen, regained control.
- 1991-1993: Mengistu and Siyaad Barre fell, Somalia fragmented, and Eritrea moved from de facto EPLF rule to independence.

## Key People

- Vasco da Gama ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vasco_da_Gama)): Portuguese commander whose arrival in 1498 began Portugal's violent entry into East African coastal politics and trade.
- Abu al-Mawahib: Kilwa ruler whose reign brought control of the Sofala gold trade and major architectural growth.
- Sayf ibn Sultan: Omani ruler whose forces captured Fort Jesus in 1698 and ended Portuguese control at Mombasa.
- Ali ibn Uthman al-Mazrui: Mazrui leader who overthrew an Omani force at Mombasa in 1746 and helped build Mazrui power.
- Sayyid Said ibn Sultan ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sayyid_Said_ibn_Sultan)): Omani ruler who made Zanzibar the centre of East African coastal power and trade.
- Mbatian ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mbatian)): Maasai laibon who became famous during the 19th-century Maasai conflicts over cattle and grazing lands.
- Mirambo ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mirambo)): Nyamwezi warrior chief who built a powerful personal rulership between 1870 and 1884.
- Carl Peters ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carl_Peters)): German figure whose 1885 charter for the German East Africa Company helped start the European scramble in East Africa.
- Emperor Ezana: Aksumite ruler whose conversion around 321 helped establish Christianity in Ethiopia.
- Ahmad ibn Ibrahim al-Ghazi ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ahmad_ibn_Ibrahim_al-Ghazi)): Muslim leader known as Ahmad Gran who nearly destroyed the Solomonid state before being killed in 1543.
- Galawdewos ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Galawdewos)): Ethiopian emperor who used Portuguese firearms and mobile tactics to defeat Ahmad Gran.
- Yohannes IV ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yohannes_IV)): Ethiopian emperor who restored unity, resisted Egypt and the Mahdists, and limited Italy to the coast.
- Menilek II ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Menilek_II)): Ethiopian ruler who defeated Italy at Adwa and expanded Ethiopia into southern and western regions.
- Sayyid Maxamed Cabdulle Xasan ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mu%E1%B8%A5ammad_ibn_%27Abdall%C4%81h_Hassan)): Somali nationalist leader who joined Islam, anti-imperialism, and Somali unity against Britain, Italy, and Ethiopia.
- Haile Selassie I ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haile_Selassie)): Ethiopian emperor who modernized the state, resisted Italy, returned after World War II, and was deposed in 1974.
- Maxamed Siyaad Barre ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siad_Barre)): Somalian president who invaded the Ogaden and later fled as Somalia disintegrated.
- Mengistu Haile Mariam ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mengistu_Haile_Mariam)): Ethiopian Dergue leader whose regime fought Somalia, Eritrean nationalists, and ethnic rebellions before falling in 1991.

## Interesting Facts

- Kilimanjaro reaches 19,340 feet, or 5,895 metres, making it the highest mountain in Africa.
- Eastern Africa ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/East_Africa)) had an estimated population of 293,654,000 in 2009 across Djibouti, Eritrea, Ethiopia, Kenya, Somalia, Tanzania, and Uganda.
- Rhapta exported ivory, rhinoceros horn, tortoiseshell, and coconut oil, but no slave exports are mentioned there.
- Husuni Kubwa at Kilwa had more than 100 rooms and was described as the largest single building in sub-Saharan Africa.
- Manda had coral-block seawalls, some blocks weighing up to a ton, and imported Islamic pottery and Chinese porcelain.
- Fort Jesus at Mombasa was designed with an Italian architect and built with help from Indian masons beginning in 1593.
- Zanzibar's cheap cotton cloth from the United States became known locally as Americani after American traders captured that trade.
- Bigo had a ditch system more than 6.5 miles long and may have served as both a royal capital and a defended cattle enclosure.
- Oromo age-set government changed every eight years, and each new warrior class sought resources through raiding and cattle rustling.
- Adwa was a rare 19th-century victory by an African state over a European colonial army and forced Italy to recognize Ethiopian independence.
- Somalia irredenta was symbolized on Somalia's flag by star points representing Djibouti, northern Kenya, and the Ogaden.
- Eritrea became independent in 1993 after the EPLF helped defeat Mengistu's forces and became its de facto government.
