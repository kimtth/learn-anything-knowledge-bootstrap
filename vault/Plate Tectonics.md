# Plate Tectonics

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> Plate tectonics explains Earth's moving lithospheric plates and the convergent, divergent, and transform boundaries where earthquakes, volcanoes, trenches, ridges, and mountain systems form. It united continental drift, seafloor spreading, magnetic stripes, heat flow, fossils, and global plate geometry into a single geological framework.

## Introduction

![[plate_tectonics_001.gif|420]]

*World map of tectonic plates and active boundaries*

Plate tectonics ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plate_tectonics)) is the theory of the dynamics of Earth's lithosphere, the rigid outer shell that overlies the plastic asthenosphere. Formulated in the 1960s, it revolutionized Earth science by explaining mountain building, volcanoes, earthquakes, seafloor spreading, continental drift, ocean-basin formation, and the reconstruction of past continental and oceanic arrangements.

Earth's lithosphere is about 100 km thick and broken into about a dozen large plates and several smaller ones. These plates move relative to one another at about 5 to 10 cm per year. At boundaries they converge, diverge, or slide past one another, producing most seismic and volcanic activity. Continents are embedded within plates and move passively with them over geologic time.

The theory is a scientific synthesis, combining continental drift, seafloor spreading, seismic belts, magnetic stripes, heat flow, ocean trenches, and global plate geometry.

Its strength is that many independent observations fit one framework. Earthquake depths trace subducting slabs, magnetic stripes record seafloor spreading, young oceanic crust lies near ridges, trenches mark convergence, and matching fossils or rock belts link formerly joined continents. Plate tectonics unified facts that had once seemed separate.

## Crust, Mantle, and Lithosphere

Continental crust is granitic, relatively buoyant, and often about 40 km thick, while oceanic crust is denser, basaltic, and about 6 km thick. Both rest on ultramafic mantle. The Mohorovicic discontinuity, or Moho, marks the crust-mantle boundary. The lithosphere includes crust and uppermost rigid mantle; below it, the hotter asthenosphere deforms more readily.

This density contrast creates two broad surface levels: continents stand higher, while oceanic crust sits lower and forms deep ocean basins.

## Plate Boundaries

At divergent margins, plates pull apart, pressure drops, mantle partially melts, basaltic magma rises, and new crust forms. At convergent margins, oceanic plates subduct beneath younger oceanic crust or buoyant continents, producing trenches, volcanic arcs, earthquakes, and recycling into the mantle. Transform faults link plate boundaries through shear motion as plates slide past one another.

## Continental Rifting and Ocean Formation

Continental rifting begins when lithosphere uplifts, stretches, fractures, and is invaded by magma. Rift valleys such as the East African Rift may widen until continental crust splits and a new ocean forms. New basaltic oceanic crust develops along ridges, cools, thickens, subsides, and eventually becomes dense enough to subduct.

Whether magmatism begins the rift or stretching first causes melting remains a debated detail, but both processes cooperate as continents separate.

## Subduction and Collision

Older oceanic lithosphere becomes denser than the surrounding mantle and ultimately subducts. Where oceanic and continental plates meet, the oceanic plate usually sinks because continental crust is too buoyant. When all oceanic lithosphere between continents is consumed, continental collision begins, subduction slows or ceases, and mountain ranges such as the Himalayas rise.

## Plate Motions and Reconstructions

Plate motions can be treated geometrically because plates approximate rigid shells on a sphere. Transform faults define small circles around poles of rotation, and rates vary with distance from the pole. To reconstruct past positions, geoscientists use relative plate motions and reference frames such as hot spots, if they are assumed to be deep-mantle plumes with fixed positions.

## Precursors to Continental Drift

Similar Atlantic coastlines were noticed by [[Francis bacon|Francis Bacon]], Buffon, and Alexander von Humboldt. Antonio Snider-Pellegrini linked matching fossils to former continental connection, while Eduard Suess proposed Gondwanaland and sunken continental blocks. Frank B. Taylor anticipated collision ideas by linking arcuate mountains to equatorward continental creep.

## Wegener and Isostasy

In 1912, Alfred Wegener argued for continental drift, using coastline fit, continuity of fold belts, matching rocks and fossils, and paleoclimate evidence. He rejected sunken land bridges because isostasy made large submerged continental blocks implausible. His weakness was mechanism: proposed polar flight and tidal forces were too weak, as Harold Jeffreys showed. Arthur Holmes proposed mantle convection in 1929.

## Seafloor Spreading and Transform Faults

Harry Hess proposed that new oceanic crust forms at ridges, moves outward, cools, subsides, and is destroyed in trenches. Robert S. Dietz named this seafloor spreading. J. Tuzo Wilson explained transform faults as boundaries where active seismic slip occurs only between ridge segments, a prediction confirmed by Lynn R. Sykes.

## Magnetic Anomalies and Acceptance

Seafloor magnetic surveys by Arthur D. Raff and Ronald G. Mason revealed regular alternating magnetic bands. The Vine-Matthews hypothesis explained these as records of geomagnetic reversals preserved symmetrically on spreading seafloor. This evidence, combined with earthquakes, ridges, trenches, heat flow, and ocean-crust ages, transformed continental drift into the accepted theory of plate tectonics.

## Dissenting Models

Some scientists resisted plate tectonics. Harold Jeffreys rejected drift because of mantle-property estimates. Vladimir Belousov and others favoured vertical-motion models. S. Warren Carey proposed an expanding Earth model, accepting Pangea breakup but attributing new ocean basins to planetary expansion. It failed to explain subduction, compression, mechanism, and older drift evidence.

## Oceans and Sea Level

Plate tectonics changes sea level by altering ocean-basin shape, ridge volume, crustal age, continental subsidence, volcanism, greenhouse gases, and glaciation. Pangea's breakup flooded continental margins, while older, cooler oceanic crust allowed basins to hold more water. Subduction preferentially removes old crust, lowering average ocean-crust age and raising seafloor elevation.

## Ocean Chemistry and Sediments

Plate tectonics influences ocean water composition by building mountains, altering erosion, and changing river sediment and chemical delivery. The Ganges, draining the Himalayas, carries vastly more sediment than the Mississippi. Continental drainage, mountain belts, hydrothermal activity, and seafloor processes help regulate ocean chemistry over geologic time.

## Life, Evolution, and Extinction

Plate tectonics affects [[Life|life]] through ridge ecosystems, chemosynthesis, ocean circulation, climate, continental positions, migration routes, isolation, and competition. Fragmented continents allow separate faunas to evolve; land bridges such as the Isthmus of Panama reconnect them. The Permian extinction may have involved Pangea formation, reduced shallow seas, altered ocean currents, oxygen changes, and Siberian Traps volcanism.

## Climate

Paleoclimate changes with continental assembly and breakup. Pangea, surrounded by Panthalassa, produced different currents and temperature gradients. Tethys allowed circumglobal equatorial flow. Later, Australia and South America drifted north, opening a circum-Antarctic seaway that isolated Antarctica and promoted polar cooling. The closing of equatorial seaways, including the Isthmus of Panama, further reshaped circulation.

## Core Equations

Plate motion is described by rates, distances, and geologic time:

$$v=\frac{d}{t}$$

This ratio turns the preceding idea into a measurable comparison, with the numerator as the quantity of interest and the denominator as the scale used for comparison.

$$\text{spreading rate}=\frac{\text{new crust width}}{\text{time}}$$

## Key Events & Developments

- 1620: [[Francis bacon|Francis Bacon]] noted Atlantic coastline similarities.
- 1912: Alfred Wegener proposed continental drift.
- 1929: Arthur Holmes proposed mantle convection as a mechanism.
- 1960s: Plate tectonics was formulated from drift, spreading, magnetism, and seismicity.
- 1962: Thomas Kuhn used scientific revolutions to frame theory change.
- 1970: James W. Valentine and Eldridge M. Moores linked tectonics and diversity.
