# Encyclopaedias and Dictionaries (Encyclopaedia)

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> An encyclopaedia is a reference work that surveys knowledge broadly or treats a field comprehensively. Its history reflects changing ideals of education, scholarly synthesis, classification, authorship, and access to information.

## Introduction

An encyclopaedia ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Encyclopedia)) is a reference work that covers all branches of knowledge, or that treats a particular branch in comprehensive detail. For more than 2,000 years such works have served as summaries of extant scholarship, shaped to be comprehensible to the readers of their era. The word itself comes from the Greek phrase enkyklios paideia, meaning "general education," and originally described a circle or complete system of learning — an all-around intellectual formation. When the French writer François Rabelais used the term in French for the first time in his *Pantagruel* (chapter 20), he was still speaking of education in this classical sense rather than a book.

## Organization

![[attachments/encyclopaedias_and_dictionaries_encyclopaedia_001.webp|420]]

*Encyclopedia on a wooden bookshelf*

From the earliest times encyclopaedia makers wrestled with the question of how to arrange knowledge. The two principal models that emerged were the alphabetical arrangement and the classified or systematic arrangement, each with its own philosophy of what an encyclopaedia should accomplish. The classified encyclopaedia ordered knowledge according to a hierarchy of subjects, reflecting a philosophical view of the unity and interrelation of all learning. The alphabetical encyclopaedia, by contrast, placed convenience and ease of lookup above any pretension to intellectual order.

## Interrelations

![[attachments/encyclopaedias_and_dictionaries_encyclopaedia_002.webp|420]]

*2011-07 Colllier s encyclopedia in librabry*

An encyclopaedia does not arise in isolation. Every new work builds on the experience and contents of its predecessors, sometimes with explicit acknowledgment, sometimes without. The German publisher Friedrich Arnold Brockhaus bought the bankrupt encyclopaedia of Gotthelf Renatus Lobel in 1808 and transformed it into his famous Konversationslexikon. The Jesuits, on the other hand, adapted Furetière's *Dictionnaire universel* without acknowledgment in their *Dictionnaire de Trévoux* of 1704. Classical writers frequently incorporated whole passages from earlier encyclopaedias.

## Readership

Encyclopaedia makers have generally kept a specific audience in mind. Cassiodorus wrote for "simple and unpolished brothers"; the Roman statesman Cato wrote for the guidance of his son; the Franciscan encyclopaedist Bartholomaeus Anglicus wrote for ordinary people; the abbess Herrad of Landsberg wrote for her nuns; the German professor Johann Christoph Wagenseil wrote for children. *Encyclopaedia Britannica* was designed for the curious and intelligent layman. The editor of *The Columbia Encyclopedia* in 1935 aimed to provide a compact work written simply enough to serve as a guide to a young Abraham Lincoln. The Jesuit Michael Pexenfelder made his audience plain by casting his *Apparatus Eruditionis* (1670) as a series of conversations between teacher and pupil.

## Contributors

Many distinguished scholars have contributed to encyclopaedias over the centuries. Because signed articles were not standard practice until the mid-to-late 20th century, not all contributions are identifiable. It is known that the English encyclopaedist John Harris enlisted the help of the naturalist John Ray and Sir Isaac Newton for his *Lexicon Technicum* of 1704. The *New Cyclopaedia* (1802–20) of Abraham Rees included articles on music by the organist and historian Charles Burney and on botany by Sir J. E. Smith.

## Language

The language of Western encyclopaedias was almost exclusively Latin ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin)) up to the era of the first printed works. Latin offered the advantage of international availability, making texts accessible to scholars across borders. The drawback was that Latin excluded the great majority of readers. A movement therefore arose, from early days, to translate major encyclopaedias into vernacular languages.

## The Contemporary World

Encyclopaedias have long reflected the civilization in which they appeared — indeed, many early compilers emphasized this by including words such as *speculum* ("mirror") and *imago* ("image") in their titles. As early as the 2nd century, the Greek scholar Julius Pollux was defining current technical terms in his *Onomastikon*. In the 13th century Vincent of Beauvais quoted pagan and Christian philosophers freely and without differentiation, reflecting the relative intellectual tolerance of his era.

## Volumes and Size

![[attachments/encyclopaedias_and_dictionaries_encyclopaedia_003.webp|420]]

*Small Soviet Encyclopedia 06*

Throughout the entire history of encyclopaedias there has been enormous variation in the number of volumes. Many Chinese encyclopaedias far exceeded any Western work in size. Pliny's *Historia naturalis* comprised about 2,500 chapters. Johann Heinrich Zedler's *Universal-Lexikon* was planned for 12 volumes but eventually filled 64. The publishers of Diderot's *Encyclopédie* faced a lawsuit (1768–78) for producing a 26-volume work instead of the promised 10 volumes. The German Allgemeine Encyclopädie of Ersch and Gruber had reached 167 volumes before it was discontinued. The major Soviet encyclopaedia comprised more than 50 volumes.

## Authorship

The first encyclopaedia makers had no doubts about their ability to work single-handedly. Cassiodorus, Honorius Inclusus, and Vincent of Beauvais all justified this confidence, though their task was largely that of the anthologist. Once encyclopaedias began to include original writing, however, the editorial task grew enormously more complex.

## Indexes

The major adjunct of the modern encyclopaedia is undoubtedly its index. As early as 1614 the bishop Antonio Zara included a type of index in his *Anatomia ingeniorum et scientiarum*. The Greek professor Johann Jacob Hoffman added an index to his *Lexicon universale* of 1677. The *Encyclopédie* was completed by a two-volume *Table analytique et raisonnée* covering all 33 volumes of text, supplements, and plates. *Encyclopaedia Britannica* included individual indexes to lengthier articles in its 2nd edition (1777–84) and provided its first separate index volume for the 7th edition (1830–42).

## Illustrative Material

The use of illustrations in encyclopaedias goes back almost certainly to the time of St. Isidore. One of the most beautiful examples of an illustrated encyclopaedia was the abbess Herrad of Landsberg's 12th-century *Hortus deliciarum*. In many earlier encyclopaedias illustrations were more decorative than informative, but from the end of the 17th century better encyclopaedias began to include more functional engravings and diagrams. The magnificent engraved plates of the *Encyclopédie* became one of its most celebrated features.

## Yearbooks and Supplements

The practice of issuing yearbooks to update encyclopaedias developed gradually. The *New International Encyclopaedia* issued a yearbook from 1908 (with retrospective coverage to 1903), and *Encyclopaedia Britannica* issued one yearbook in 1913 before resuming with the *Britannica Book of the Year* in 1938. By the second half of the 20th century, yearbooks had become a common feature of most general encyclopaedias.

## Electronic Encyclopaedias

The rapid pace of technological advancement led encyclopaedia publishers to exploit new information technologies for storage, retrieval, and distribution. During the 1960s and 1970s these technologies revolutionized editorial production: the computer terminal linked to a mainframe storing the encyclopaedia's contents became the key to editorial workflow. By the 1980s and 1990s the growth of telecommunications networks and personal computers opened a new possibility — delivery of encyclopaedic databases through media other than the printed page.

## History of Encyclopaedias in the West

### Early Development

The first fragments of an encyclopaedia to have survived are the work of Speusippus (died 339/338 BCE), a nephew of Plato's. Speusippus conveyed his uncle's ideas in writings on natural history, mathematics, and philosophy. [[Aristotle and Aristotelianism|Aristotle]]'s wide-ranging lectures at the Lyceum were equally influential, and he and [[Plato and Platonism (Plato)|Plato]] appear to have been the originators of the encyclopaedia as a means of providing a comprehensive cultural background. The Greek approach was to record the spoken word; the Romans aimed instead to epitomize existing knowledge in readable form.

### The Medieval Period

The transition from ancient to medieval encyclopaedism was shaped by the need of the Christian Church to preserve and transmit classical learning. Cassiodorus, a Roman statesman who became a monk, wrote his *Institutiones* in the early 6th century as a guide for his monastery's library and educational program. He made the organized study of theology and the liberal arts central to monastic culture, helping to preserve much of what survived of ancient learning.

### The Renaissance and 16th–17th Centuries

The advent of printing in the 15th century transformed the production and distribution of encyclopaedic knowledge. One of the earliest printed works of encyclopaedic character was Gregor Reisch's *Margarita philosophica* ("The Philosophical Pearl," 1496), a comprehensive illustrated textbook of the seven liberal arts addressed to youth. The period saw the gradual shift from Latin to vernacular encyclopaedias and the consolidation of alphabetical arrangement as the dominant form.

### The Encyclopédie of Diderot and d'Alembert (1751–1772)

The Encyclopédie, ou Dictionnaire raisonné des sciences, des arts et des métiers — the "Reasoned Dictionary of Sciences, Arts, and Trades" — was the supreme intellectual achievement of the French Enlightenment. Edited by Denis Diderot and the mathematician Jean le Rond d'Alembert, it began publication in 1751 and was completed in 1772 in 28 volumes of text and plates, later supplemented to a total of 33 volumes. It set out not merely to compile knowledge but to change the way Europeans thought about the world.

### Encyclopaedia Britannica (1768)

The first edition of Encyclopaedia Britannica appeared in 1768–71, compiled by three Scotsmen: the printer Andrew Bell, the bookseller Colin Macfarquhar, and the scholar William Smellie. Aware of the shortcomings of the *Encyclopédie*, they devised a new plan: about 45 principal subjects with full-length treatments (printed across the whole page) supported by 30 longer articles, all within a single alphabetical sequence interspersed with brief entries and cross-references. Some principal articles on medical subjects extended to more than 100 pages each.

### The Brockhaus Konversationslexikon

In Germany, Friedrich Arnold Brockhaus reworked the bankrupt encyclopaedia of Renatus Gotthelf Lobel into his Konversations-Lexikon, first issued 1796–1811. Brief, well-designed articles packed with facts, comprehensive coverage, and a reputation for accuracy and currency — these were the ingredients of one of the most successful encyclopaedias the world has seen. Brockhaus personally supervised much of the revision of individual articles and took great pride in providing the latest information.

### The 19th Century

The 19th century saw encyclopaedia production reach extraordinary heights of scholarship and ambition, alongside some notable failures. Abraham Rees, having served a long apprenticeship revising Chambers's *Cyclopaedia*, at last produced a completely original and finely illustrated *New Cyclopaedia* (1802–20), the only serious rival to the Britannica of its generation. What might have been the greatest encyclopaedia of the century, the *Encyclopaedia Metropolitana* (1817–45), failed because of the early withdrawal of its designer, Samuel Taylor Coleridge, and subsequent financial troubles. From it, however, came one of the most important contributions to the philosophy of encyclopaedia making since Bacon: Coleridge's *On Method* (1818).

### The 20th Century and Beyond

The 20th century opened with a Russian edition of Brockhaus (1890–1906) and S. N. Yushakov's *Bolshaya entsiklopedya* ("Great Encyclopaedia," 1900–09), modeled on the German Meyer encyclopaedia. The most important Soviet work was the 65-volume Bolshaya sovetskaya entsiklopedya ("Great Soviet Encyclopaedia," 1926–47), whose first edition was eventually discredited for ideological bias. The second edition (1949–58), though adopting a Marxist-Leninist framework, was less biased on non-political subjects, drew on 8,000 scholars, and included truly international bibliographies. One complete volume was devoted to the Soviet Union alone. A 30-volume third edition followed from 1970 to 1978, available in English translation from 1973 to 1983.

## Specialized Encyclopaedias

### Biography

The first real effort toward a specialized encyclopaedia focused on biography. The *Allgemeines Gelehrten-Lexikon* (1750–51, "General Scholarly Lexicon") was compiled by the German biographer Christian Gottlieb Jocher and continued by Johann Cristoph Adelung and others; it remains valuable today. Two further efforts of note followed: J. C. F. Hoefer compiled the *Nouvelle Biographie générale* (1852–66), and J. F. Michaud produced the *Biographie universelle* (1811–62). These two great works were to some extent competitive, which improved both their coverage and content. After their publication, the task of recording international biographical information largely reverted to general encyclopaedias.

### Chemistry

The development of specialized encyclopaedias in chemistry coincided with the flourishing of chemical science. William Nicholson was first in the field with his *Dictionary of Chemistry* (1795), on which Andrew Ure's standard *Dictionary of Chemistry* was later based. The German chemist Martin Heinrich Klaproth issued his *Chemisches Wörterbuch* in 1807, but the landmark work was the *Handbuch der theoretischen Chemie* (1817–19) by Leopold Gmelin, so excellent that it continued appearing in new editions from the Gmelin-Institut long after its first publication.

### Music

The encyclopaedic tradition in [[The Art of Music (Music)|music]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Music)) is paralleled only by chemistry in its richness and continuity. The *Musikalisches Lexikon* (1732) of the German composer and lexicographer Johann Gottfried Walther began the trend. The very successful *Historisch-biographisches Lexicon der Tonkünstler* (1790–92) of Ernst Ludwig Gerber followed, succeeded by the *Biographie universelle des musiciens* (1835–44) of the Belgian composer François-Joseph Fétis, director of the Brussels Conservatoire.

### Philosophy

The publication of G. W. F. Hegel's *Encyklopädie der philosophischen Wissenschaften* (1817) — a compendium of his entire philosophical system in three parts: Logic, Nature, and Mind — influenced general encyclopaedia editors throughout the 19th century. The standard work in the specialized philosophy field was for many years the *Dictionary of Philosophy and Psychology* (edited by James Mark Baldwin, new edition 1960). The *Encyclopedia of Philosophy* (1967, reprinted 1996) provided a more substantial modern work, and the *Routledge Encyclopedia of Philosophy* (1998) — edited by Edward Craig and also made available online — was the first multivolume philosophy encyclopaedia published in more than 30 years.

### Architecture, Art, and Classical Studies

The Architectural Publication Society began issuing its *Dictionary of Architecture* as early as 1852, requiring 40 years to complete. A more modern counterpart is *Wasmuths Lexikon der Baukunst* (1929–37). Further coverage of art was provided by the *Encyclopedia of World Art* (1959–68), the *Enciclopedia dell'arte antica, classica e orientale* (1958–66), and *Grove's Dictionary of Art* (1996, also online).

### Religion and Literature

Religion received its first great specialized encyclopaedia from the Swiss theologian Johann Jakob Herzog, whose *Real-Encyklopädie für protestantische Theologie und Kirche* appeared in 1854–68. The abridged English edition was prepared by Philip Schaff, a Swiss-born American church historian, and gave rise to *The New Schaff-Herzog Encyclopedia of Religious Knowledge*. The Scottish clergyman James Hastings was responsible for four encyclopaedic works: *A Dictionary of the Bible* (1898–1904), *A Dictionary of Christ and the Gospels* (1906–08), the *Encyclopaedia of Religion and Ethics* (1908–26), and *A Dictionary of the Apostolic Church*.

## Key Events & Developments

- 339/338 BCE: Speusippus produces the earliest surviving encyclopaedic fragments, covering natural history, mathematics, and philosophy.
- c. 183 BCE: Cato the Censor writes the *Praecepta ad filium*, the first known Roman encyclopaedic effort.
- 1st century CE: Pliny the Elder completes the *Historia naturalis*, the most influential ancient encyclopaedia.
- Early 7th century: St. Isidore of Seville compiles the *Etymologiae*, shaping medieval learning for centuries.
- 13th century: Vincent of Beauvais compiles the *Speculum majus*, the most ambitious medieval encyclopaedia.
- 1559: Paul Scalich first uses the word "encyclopaedia" in a book title, at Basel.
- 1674: Louis Moreri confirms popular preference for vernacular alphabetical encyclopaedias with the *Grand Dictionnaire historique*.
- 1697: Pierre Bayle's *Dictionnaire historique et critique* introduces critical footnotes and Enlightenment skepticism.
- 1704: John Harris's *Lexicon Technicum* introduces specialist contributors and becomes the first modern English encyclopaedia.
- 1728: Ephraim Chambers's *Cyclopaedia* establishes the systematic use of cross-references and becomes the most influential 18th-century model.
- 1751–72: Diderot and d'Alembert publish the Encyclopédie, the crowning achievement of the French Enlightenment.
- 1768–71: First edition of Encyclopaedia Britannica appears, blending comprehensive articles with alphabetical arrangement.
- 1796–1811: Friedrich Arnold Brockhaus launches his Konversations-Lexikon, setting the pattern for half of all subsequent encyclopaedias.
- 1818: Coleridge's *On Method* becomes a landmark in the philosophy of encyclopaedia organization.
- 1880–83: Beilstein publishes the first edition of his *Handbuch der organischen Chemie*, the most extensive specialized scientific encyclopaedia ever compiled.
- 1929: *Encyclopaedia Britannica* introduces the policy of continuous revision.
- 1935: *Encyclopédie française* launches as a major new classified encyclopaedia.
- 1983: *Academic American Encyclopedia* becomes the first encyclopaedia offered online to a mass market.
- 1989: First multimedia CD-ROM encyclopaedia (Compton's) released.
- 1994: *Britannica Online* launched over the Internet.
- 2001: Wikipedia launched in English, growing to two million articles by 2007.

## Interesting Facts

- The word encyclopaedia ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Encyclopedia)) was first applied to a book by Paul Scalich in 1559; before that, similar works were called dictionaries or given fanciful titles like "Garden of Delights."
- Pliny the Elder's *Historia naturalis* contained about 2,500 chapters — his nephew estimated it covered 20,000 matters of importance — and was so widely used that one Latin writer drew nearly 90 percent of his own work from it.
- The publishers of Diderot's *Encyclopédie* were sued for producing 26 volumes instead of the 10 volumes they had promised subscribers.
- The German *Allgemeine Encyclopädie* of Ersch and Gruber reached 167 volumes before it was discontinued — it was never completed.
- Zedler's *Universal-Lexikon*, planned for 12 volumes, eventually filled 64.
- [[Lenin]] contributed articles to the Russian Granat encyclopaedia, including one on Karl Marx and one on the Russian agrarian problem.
- Beilstein's *Handbuch der organischen Chemie* grew from a two-volume work in 1880–83 to more than 300 volumes by the end of the 20th century, plus two computer databases.
- *Encyclopaedia Britannica*'s 11th edition (1910–11) contained almost twice as many articles as the previous major edition but only about 15–16 percent more words — because subjects were broken into smaller fragments.
- The *CRC Handbook of Chemistry and Physics* had reached its 84th edition by 2003.
- In Denmark, three major new multivolume encyclopaedias all appeared in the same decade of the 1890s — a situation with no parallel in encyclopaedia history.
- Wikipedia, launched in 2001, reached two million articles in just six years, radically outpacing any previous encyclopaedic project in speed of growth.
- The first encyclopaedia offered online to a mass market was the *Academic American Encyclopedia* in 1983 — nearly 20 years before Wikipedia.
