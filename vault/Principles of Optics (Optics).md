# Principles of Optics (Optics)

## Introduction

Optics ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Optics)) is the science of the origin, propagation, transformation, and effects of light and related phenomena. It has two main branches: physical optics, which studies the nature and properties of light itself, and geometrical optics, which studies how lenses, mirrors, and other devices form images.

The meaning of optics expanded over time. It first referred to the eye and vision, then to optical instruments such as lenses, and later to many applications of light even when the receiver is not the eye but a detector such as a photographic plate or television camera.

In the 20th century, optical methods were extended beyond visible light to X-rays, ultraviolet, infrared, and microwave radio waves. This made optics part of the broader study of electromagnetic radiation, though the focus here is mainly on image formation by lenses, mirrors, prisms, gratings, and coherent optical systems.

## Geometrical Optics and Images

An optical image ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Image)) can be treated as a pattern formed by rays passing through or reflecting from surfaces. Geometrical optics uses rays as directions of propagation and studies how they bend, reflect, and combine to form real or virtual images.

Reflection follows the rule that the angle of reflection equals the angle of incidence, both measured from the normal to the surface. Plane mirrors form virtual images and can redirect beams. Concave mirrors can form real images of distant objects, while convex mirrors form virtual images.

Large telescopes use mirrors because there is no practical size limit comparable to that of lenses. Isaac Newton preferred mirrors for telescope objectives because the lenses of his day produced poor images, and modern large astronomical telescopes are reflecting instruments.

## Refraction and Dispersion

*Snell's law diagram showing refracted ray geometry*

Refraction occurs when light crosses the boundary between two transparent media and bends because its speed changes. The ratio of the velocity of light in air to its velocity in a medium is the refractive index for a particular colour or wavelength.

The law of refraction, or Snell's law, connects incident and refracted directions through the refractive indices of the two media. Because blue light usually has a higher refractive index than red light in glass, different colours bend by different amounts.

Dispersion is the difference between refractive indices for blue and red light. Optical glass is described by mean refractive index and dispersive power. The development of glass with controlled refractive and dispersive properties allowed better photographic lenses, including anastigmats.

Glassmaking for lenses improved from selected window or table glass to carefully stirred, cooled, remelted, molded, and annealed optical glass. Lead oxide, barium oxide, rare-earth elements, and lanthanum glass changed refractive index and dispersion, making high-quality optical design possible.

## Ray Tracing and Paraxial Theory

Ray tracing calculates the path of light through lenses and mirrors. Simple graphical constructions can trace refraction, but high-quality lens design requires trigonometrical computation of many meridional and skew rays to several decimal places.

A paraxial ray lies very close to the optical axis and has very small angles. By reducing exact ray-tracing formulas to the small-angle limit, designers can find image positions and focal properties without tracing rays whose angles are too small for ordinary tables.

The optical invariant, also called the Lagrange or Smith-Helmholtz theorem, relates image and object sizes to refractive index and ray angle. It helps determine magnification once a paraxial ray has been traced through the system.

[[Gauss]] showed in 1841 that, for paraxial rays, any complex lens can be replaced by principal points and focal points. This Gauss theory makes it possible to describe object-image distances, focal lengths, and magnification without tracking every surface in detail.

## Lenses, Pupils, and Relay Systems

All optical systems have an aperture stop that limits beam diameter. By analogy with the eye, this stop is called the iris, and its images in object and image space are the entrance pupil and exit pupil. Adjustable irises in photographic lenses control illumination and depth of field.

Vignetting occurs when lens mounts cut into oblique beams, so pupils are not fully filled with light. This reduces illumination in the outer field. Telescope and microscope systems often have the objective mount as the limiting aperture, with an exit pupil where the observer's eye must be placed.

A relay lens can invert an image or extend an optical system, as in a rifle sight or periscope. Field lenses bend oblique beams so they reach the relay lens properly. Such systems show how pupils, objectives, reticles, relay lenses, and eyepieces must be coordinated.

## Mirrors and Detectors

Curved mirrors can replace lenses as image-forming elements. Reflecting telescopes use concave primary mirrors and often secondary mirrors to magnify or reposition the image. Parabolic and hyperbolic shapes correct aberrations.

Modern telescope mirrors are usually glass coated with silver or aluminum. Aluminum is highly reflective and resists tarnish better than silver. Large mirrors are difficult because tiny distortions from weight or temperature can blur the image, so support and thermal control are major engineering problems.

An image may be received by the eye, photographic film, photoelectric cells, infrared detectors, image intensifiers, television camera tubes, or electro-optical systems. Since about 1925, many electrical detectors have extended optical sensing beyond visible light. Space cameras combine optics, film, scanning, photocells, and radio transmission.

## Projection Screens, Prisms, and Dispersing Devices

Projection screens and prisms are practical components of image display and beam control. Prisms can deflect, invert, or reverse images, and beam splitters divide light into separate paths. Colour television cameras used beam splitters to form red, green, and blue images on separate tubes.

A prism and a diffraction grating both spread light into a spectrum. A prism disperses because refractive index varies with wavelength, spreading blue more than red. A grating uses diffraction from many fine grooves, bending red most and blue least, the opposite of a prism.

Spectroscopes and spectrographs use these dispersing devices to separate colours for observation or photography. Gratings can provide high spectral resolution because each wavelength is reinforced in a particular direction by interference among waves from many grooves.

## Nonclassical Imaging Systems

Aspheric surfaces are nonspherical optical surfaces used when plane and spherical surfaces cannot correct aberrations well enough. Parabolic telescope mirrors and molded elliptic surfaces on highway reflectors are examples. Cylindrical surfaces have power in only one meridian.

Cylindrical lenses change magnification differently in perpendicular directions. They are used in anamorphic motion-picture systems to compress and later stretch wide-screen images. Toric spectacle lenses correct astigmatism in the eye by having different curvatures in different meridians.

Bifocal and trifocal spectacle lenses provide multiple powers in one lens. Fresnel-type lenses, proposed by Georges-Louis Buffon and Augustin-Jean Fresnel, use concentric zones to reduce thickness while preserving focusing effect.

## Aberrations and Image Brightness

The five main Seidel aberrations are spherical aberration, coma, astigmatism, Petzval curvature, and distortion. Spherical aberration means different zones of a lens focus at different axial distances. Coma gives off-axis point images a cometlike shape.

Astigmatism produces radial or tangential blurring in the outer field. Petzval curvature curves the image surface, and distortion moves image points inward or outward, producing barrel or pincushion shapes. Even corrected lenses retain small residual aberrations that matter in high-quality photography.

Image brightness depends on object luminance, optical transmittance, and aperture. Photometric units such as candle power, luminance, lumens, and foot-candles describe light output and illumination. Designers must ensure detectors receive enough light for satisfactory response.

## Optics and Information Theory

A new era in optics began in the early 1950s when communication and information theory were applied to imaging. An optical system can be treated as a communication channel that relays information from object to image.

With incoherent illumination, there is a linear relationship between object and image intensity distributions. The optical analogue of an electronic impulse response is the image of a point source. Once the impulse response is known, the image of any object can be described as a superposition of weighted point responses.

The transfer function describes how an optical system transmits spatial frequencies. Object information can be decomposed into periodic spatial components, each multiplied by the transfer function. Frequencies for which the transfer function is zero cannot appear in the image.

## Coherence and Optical Processing

Coherence matters because incoherent and coherent light combine differently. Incoherent imaging adds intensities, while diffraction and interference with coherent illumination add complex amplitudes. Real light can also be partially coherent, so intermediate states must be described.

Coherent optical processing often uses a two-lens system. A coherent beam illuminates an object; the first lens forms the Fraunhofer diffraction pattern, or spatial frequency distribution; a filter modifies that pattern; and the second lens forms a processed image.

Filters may block selected spatial frequencies, adjust amplitude, adjust phase, or combine both. Blocking filters can remove raster lines or halftone dots. Phase and amplitude filters can enhance contrast, differentiate images, or help correct aberrated photographs when the original transfer function is known.

The phase-contrast microscope is an example of optical processing because it makes phase differences visible as intensity differences. This is valuable for biological samples that differ mainly in refractive index rather than absorption.

## Holography

Holography is a two-step coherent image-forming process. First, a record is made of the interference pattern between light diffracted by an object and a coherent reference wave. Second, the hologram is illuminated coherently to reconstruct images of the object.

Dennis Gabor described wave-front reconstruction in 1948 to improve electron-beam image resolution. Holography later became most successful with visible light. A hologram usually forms both a real image and a virtual image.

Gabor's original in-line Fresnel hologram used a collinear background wave, which restricted object types and produced twin-image problems. Off-axis holography introduced a separate angled reference beam, separating reconstructed image beams and making the method much more versatile.

A holographic image can show three-dimensionality and parallax, unlike an ordinary two-dimensional photograph. Full-colour holograms can be made by recording separate red, green, and blue holograms at the same time.

## Applications of Holography

Holographic image-forming applications include microscopy, particle-size analysis, high-speed photography, gas-flow recording, data storage, image formation through random media, and non-optical holography such as acoustic holography.

Non-image-forming holographic applications include nondestructive testing of manufactured materials. Tire testing for hidden debonds between plies is one example. Holography extends interferometry to larger and more complex objects.

A hologram can also act as an optical element. It can be used to make specialized gratings, holographic filters, and coherent optical processing devices. In particle-size analysis, pulsed ruby lasers freeze moving droplets or dust particles into stationary reconstructed images for later study.

## Key Events & Developments

- 1841: [[Gauss]] published a foundational theory of paraxial lenses.
- 1856: Justus von Liebig invented silvering on polished glass for mirrors.
- 1884: Barium oxide glass improved photographic lens design.
- 1938: Rare-earth glass improved optical materials.
- 1948: Dennis Gabor described holography.
- 1960s: Lasers sustained the growth of coherent optics and holography.

- Isaac Newton ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isaac_Newton)): Favoured reflecting telescope objectives over poor early lenses.
- Joseph-Louis Lagrange ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph-Louis_Lagrange)): Associated with the optical invariant.

## Interesting Facts

- Blue light usually bends more than red light in glass prisms.
- The largest telescopes use mirrors, not lenses.
- A diffraction grating spreads red and blue in the opposite order from a prism.
- Holograms can preserve depth and parallax.
- A coherent optical processor can remove printed raster lines by filtering spatial frequencies.
