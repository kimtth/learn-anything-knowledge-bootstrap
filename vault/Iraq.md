# Iraq

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> Iraq occupies the Tigris-Euphrates heartland where ancient Mesopotamian civilizations, Islamic institutions, Ottoman rule, British mandate government, and modern oil politics converged. Baath rule, wars, sanctions, and the Iraq War profoundly reshaped its state and society.

## Introduction

Iraq ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iraq)) is a country of southwestern Asia whose lands were historically known as Mesopotamia, the alluvial region between the Tigris and Euphrates that produced some of the world's earliest civilizations, including Sumer, Akkad, Babylon, and Assyria. The area later became part of Persian, Greek, Roman, and Islamic empires, and Baghdad became the capital of the Abbasid Caliphate in the 8th century.

The modern Iraqi state was created after [[World Wars (World War I)|World War I]] from the Ottoman provinces of Mosul, Baghdad, and Al-Basrah. It gained formal independence in 1932 but remained under strong British influence. A monarchy fell in 1958, the Baath Party seized enduring power in 1968, and Saddam Hussein led Iraq into the Iran-Iraq War, the Persian Gulf War, years of sanctions, and the Iraq War.

## Land and Environment

![[attachments/iraq_002.webp|420]]

*Map of the Tigris and Euphrates river system*

Iraq's borders touch Turkey, Iran, Syria, Jordan, Saudi Arabia, Kuwait, and the Persian Gulf. Its coastline is only about 12 miles, giving it very limited maritime access. The country's location makes it one of the easternmost Arab states and a bridge between the Levant, Anatolia, Iran, Arabia, and the Persian Gulf.

Topography divides into four main regions: the alluvial plains of central and southeastern Iraq, Al-Jazirah between the Tigris and Euphrates, the western and southern deserts, and the northeastern highlands of Iraqi Kurdistan. The alluvial plains are low, poorly drained, flood-prone, marshy in places, and historically central to agriculture and settlement.

The lower plains extend roughly 375 miles from Balad and Al-Ramadi toward the Persian Gulf and cover nearly one-third of the country. Lake Al-Hammar, seasonal marshes, and abandoned river channels show why drainage, flooding, and river-course change have always shaped settlement.

Al-Jazirah is an arid upland north of the alluvial plains, with the Sinjar Mountains and salt flats such as Milhat Ashqar. The western and southern deserts cover nearly two-fifths of Iraq and include rocky Al-Hajarah and sandy Al-Dibdibah. The northeast rises through hills, plateaus, basins, and mountain ridges associated with the Taurus and Zagros systems.

In the northeast, only part of the upland is truly mountainous, but the terrain rises toward ridges along the Turkish and Iranian borders. Ghundah Zhur on the Iran-Iraq frontier is the highest point, and river basins between ridges provide the main corridors for settlement.

The Tigris-Euphrates system drains Iraq. Both rivers rise in Turkey, cross Iraq, and meet near Al-Qurnah to form the Shatt al-Arab, which flows into the Persian Gulf. The Tigris receives tributaries such as the Great Zab, Little Zab, and Diyala and can flood severely; the Euphrates has no large Iraqi tributaries and is heavily affected by upstream use in Turkey and Syria.

Water control is essential because Iraq must manage flood, drought, irrigation, salinity, and international river disputes. Dams and canals link the Tigris, Lake Al-Tharthar, and the Euphrates. Syria and Iraq agreed in 1990 on sharing Euphrates water entering Syria, while Turkey promised minimum flow but no comprehensive three-country settlement existed.

Soils and climate vary sharply. Lowland alluvial soils can be fertile but suffer from salinity, poor drainage, high evaporation, and overirrigation. Desert soils are coarse and poor in humus. Iraq has hot, arid lowlands and a cooler, wetter northeast, with summer heat extreme in Baghdad and winter precipitation concentrated between November and April.

Salinity affects a very large share of cultivated land because high water tables, evaporation, and overirrigation bring alkaline salts to the surface. In the northeast, higher rainfall can sustain pasture and some rain-fed farming, while most lowland agriculture remains dependent on irrigation.

## People, Languages, and Religion

Iraq's society is religiously and ethnically diverse because the modern state joined several Ottoman provinces and regions. Arabs form roughly two-thirds of the population, Kurds about one-fourth, and smaller minorities include Turkmen, Assyrians, Chaldeans, Armenians, Yazidis, Mandaeans, Jews, Bahais, and others.

Arab Iraqis include both Shiite and Sunni Muslims and are tied strongly to family, tribe, and extended kinship. Tribal affiliation has continued to influence politics, rural life, family businesses, local authority, and relations with the central state, even where urbanization weakened older structures.

Kurds are a distinct Indo-European-speaking people concentrated in Iraqi Kurdistan and related regions of Turkey, Iran, and Syria. Most Iraqi Kurds are Sunni Muslims, speak Kurdish dialects, and maintain tribal, musical, costume, and cultural traditions. Kurdish movements repeatedly sought autonomy or independence and rebelled against Iraqi governments, especially from 1961 onward and again during and after the Iran-Iraq War.

Languages include Arabic, Kurdish, Turkish, Turkmen, Azerbaijanian, Syriac, Persian, and English in commerce. Arabic is the official language and has several dialects. Kurdish is official in the Kurdish Autonomous Region. Bilingualism is common among minorities.

Religion ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Religion)) is predominantly Muslim, with Shiites slightly more than half and Sunnis roughly two-fifths of the population. Iraq is unusual because both major Islamic sects are large and politically significant. From the creation of the state until the fall of Saddam's government in 2003, ruling elites were mainly Sunni Arabs, despite the Shiite majority.

Iraqi Shiism has deep spiritual importance. Al-Najaf, Karbala, Al-Kufah, and Samarra are major Shiite holy centres linked to Ali, the imams, and Muhammad al-Mahdi al-Hujjah. Iraqi Shiites were politically and economically marginalized under Sunni-dominated governments but gained significant political power after the transition to elective government following 2003.

Religious minorities include Christians, Yazidis, Mandaeans, Jews, and Bahais. Iraq's Jewish community traced itself to the Babylonian Exile but largely emigrated after rising anti-Jewish violence and the creation of Israel. Christian communities include Assyrians, Chaldeans, Syriac Orthodox, and Eastern Orthodox groups; many left after the Iraq War because of poverty and extremist violence.

## Settlement and Society

Settlement ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Human_settlement)) follows ancient patterns around the Tigris and Euphrates, fertile lowlands, cities, foothills, and the Kurdish northeast. About one-third of Iraqis remained rural in the period described, with villages clustered for defense and agriculture. Mountain villages are generally smaller and more isolated.

[[Baghdad]] is Iraq's political, economic, and communications centre and contains a large share of the national population. Rural migrants, especially Shiite Arabs from the south, moved there for jobs, services, and ration access during sanctions. Sectarian violence after 2003 made formerly mixed neighbourhoods more homogeneous, and Sadr City became a major Shiite urban district.

Housing and services suffered from migration, displacement, sanctions, war damage, shortages of water, electricity, sanitation, and underinvestment. Southern marsh dwellers traditionally built distinctive reed houses with barrel-vaulted roofs, while mud brick is common in the south and stone more common in the north.

[[Education]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Education)) expanded rapidly after the 1958 revolution and was state-funded. Primary education is compulsory, secondary education widely available, and Iraq historically produced many scientists, administrators, technicians, and skilled workers. Sanctions, war, politicization, shortages, and loss of access to modern materials reduced educational quality after the early 1990s.

## Economy

Iraq's economy was largely agricultural until the 1950s, then became a state-dominated, oil-financed, centrally planned economy. By 1980, Iraq had one of the largest economies in the Arab world, but the Iran-Iraq War, invasion of Kuwait, Persian Gulf War, sanctions, and Iraq War devastated output, finance, infrastructure, and industry.

Oil dominates exports and public revenue. Iraq's oil reserves were among the world's largest, allowing the Baath regime to finance development, heavy industry, welfare programs, arms purchases, and military expansion in the 1970s. Under sanctions, oil exports were restricted and later supervised through the UN oil-for-food program.

Before the embargo, Iraq imported military equipment, vehicles, industrial goods, textiles, construction materials, and food, while oil supplied nearly all export value. Non-oil exports such as dates, cotton, wool, animal products, and fertilizers remained secondary. After 1991, legal trade nearly stopped until supervised oil sales resumed.

Manufacturing ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manufacturing)) grew after the mid-1970s through heavy industrialization and import substitution. Petrochemicals, refining, steel, aluminum, tractors, electrical goods, cables, tires, fertilizers, detergents, and liquefied natural gas expanded with foreign assistance, especially from the Soviet bloc. War and embargo cut manufacturing deeply, though some recovery occurred after 1997 when parts and credit became available.

Finance ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Finance)) was state-controlled after banks and insurance companies were nationalized in 1964. The Central Bank issues the dinar, while Rafidain and Rashid served as major commercial banks. After the Persian Gulf War, inflation, debt, frozen assets, sanctions, and oil-for-food escrow arrangements badly damaged finance; creditors later cancelled large portions of debt beginning in 2004.

Trade ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trade)) before sanctions included imports of military equipment, vehicles, industrial goods, textiles, construction materials, and food, while exports were almost entirely oil but also included dates, cotton, wool, animal products, and fertilizers. The embargo after Kuwait brought legal trade nearly to a halt until oil-for-food exports began; the embargo was lifted after the 2003 invasion.

Services, labour, and tourism were also damaged. The dinar's collapse reduced purchasing power, unemployment rose, and most salaries went to food and shelter. Tourism was limited by instability despite Iraq's archaeological and religious heritage. Trade unions existed but were controlled by the Baath Party and later by state structures rather than independent bargaining.

## Government, Security, and Culture

The judiciary after the 2005 constitution is administered through the Supreme Judicial Council, with a Supreme Court empowered to interpret the constitution and settle disputes. Under Baath rule, exceptional courts often bypassed ordinary law and served the regime's security needs.

The Baath Party was Arab nationalist and socialist, and after 1968 Iraq became effectively a one-party state. Opposition groups included the Iraqi Communist Party, Kurdish Democratic Party, Patriotic Union of Kurdistan, Dawah Islamic Party, Supreme Council of the Islamic Revolution in Iraq, and later other parties. Many were outlawed, purged, exiled, or repressed under Baath rule.

The military repeatedly intervened in politics from 1936 to 1968. Saddam's regime built one of the Arab world's largest armies but also distrusted officers, purged them, and surrounded the armed forces with security, intelligence, paramilitary, and presidential-guard structures loyal to the ruler. The army was damaged in 1991, reduced afterward, and no match for U.S.-led forces in 2003.

Cultural life ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Culture)) is Arab and Islamic but highly diverse. Iraq includes Bedouin-related tribes, central farmers, southern marsh dwellers, northeastern dryland cultivators, and Kurdish mountain herders. The Baath regime promoted ancient Mesopotamian heritage, built museums and festivals, reconstructed a version of Babylon, invoked tribal and Islamic themes, and saturated public culture with Saddam's personality cult.

Cultural institutions include the Iraq Museum, founded in 1923, the National Library, the Mosul Museum, and archaeological sites tied to Mesopotamian, Abbasid, and Ottoman history. The Iraq Museum was heavily looted after the 2003 invasion, though thousands of stolen objects were later recovered.

## Islamic and Medieval History

Early Islamic Iraq was a former Sasanian province with rich irrigation agriculture and mixed Persian, Aramaic-speaking, Arab, Christian, Jewish, and other communities. Arab conquest in the 7th century brought Iraq into the Islamic world, while Al-Kufah and Al-Basrah became major garrison cities and political centres.

The Abbasid Revolution began in Khorasan and reached Iraq in 749, when Abu al-Abbas al-Saffah was proclaimed caliph at Al-Kufah. Al-Mansur founded Baghdad in 762 as Madinat al-Salam, the City of Peace. Baghdad's site joined access to the Tigris, Euphrates, Zagros routes, agricultural hinterland, and imperial administration, making it the greatest city of the early Abbasid world.

Al-Mansur's Round City was a government quarter with four gates, palace, and main mosque at the centre. Markets were soon moved to Karkh, while Harbiyyah housed Khorasani soldiers and the east bank developed as a palace quarter for al-Mahdi.

Seljuq rule began when Toghril Beg entered Baghdad in 1055 and displaced Buyid control. The Abbasid caliphs remained symbolically important but politically dependent on military strongmen. Madrasahs such as the Nizamiyyah spread Sunni doctrine and bureaucratic learning, while Seljuq authority later fragmented among regional dynasties.

Mongol conquest ended the Abbasid caliphate in Baghdad. Hulegu besieged the city in 1258, executed al-Mustasim, and made Iraq part of the Il-Khanid world. Baghdad was devastated and reduced to provincial status, while Tabriz became a major political and commercial centre.

Turkmen and Safavid periods followed. The Kara Koyunlu and Ak Koyunlu ruled parts of Iraq in the 15th century, while messianic Shiite movements such as the Mushasha arose in the marshes. Shah Ismail I took Baghdad in 1508, but Suleyman I captured it for the Ottomans in 1534, reorienting Iraq westward toward Anatolia and Syria.

## Ottoman Iraq and the British Mandate

Ottoman Iraq lasted from 1534 to 1918 and was divided among Mosul, Baghdad, Al-Basrah, Shahrizur, and Al-Hasa at different times. It served as a buffer against Iran, a route for trade and pilgrimage, and a region where tribal society, urban notables, Shiite communities, Kurdish districts, Janissaries, governors, and provincial households shared power uneasily.

Local autonomy grew in the 17th and 18th centuries, including Mamluk rule in Baghdad. Buyuk Suleyman Pasa reached the height of Mamluk power from 1780 to 1802, strengthening his household, curbing Janissaries, fostering trade and agriculture, and struggling against Bedouin and Wahhabi pressures.

British influence expanded after a permanent British agent was permitted in Baghdad in 1798. The last Mamluk governor, Daud Pasa, tried to modernize with European weapons and advisers but fell after Ottoman centralization, local opposition, floods, and plague in 1831.

Midhat Pasa governed Baghdad from 1869 to 1872 and introduced major reforms: city-wall demolition, street improvements, tramway, park, water system, hospital, textile mills, savings bank, schools, printing press, newspaper, steamer service, Persian Gulf shipping, dredging, land registration, municipalities, councils, and conscription. His reforms aimed to pacify tribes, improve taxation, and expand cultivation but often disrupted communal landholding.

The British mandate followed World War I, when Britain created a state from Mosul, Baghdad, and Al-Basrah. British officials debated direct and indirect rule, Arab nationalism rose, and Faysal I, son of Husayn ibn Ali, became king. Iraq entered the League of Nations as an independent state on October 3, 1932.

## Monarchy, Republic, and Baath Rule

The monarchy struggled after independence. The Assyrian uprising of 1933, King Faysal's death, the youth of King Ghazi, weak parties, competing politicians, military interventions, British influence, and foreign-policy disputes undermined legitimacy. Iraq experienced repeated coups and instability before the monarchy's collapse.

The 1958 revolution was led by Free Officers under Abd al-Karim Qasim and Abd al-Salam Arif. On July 14, 1958, troops seized Baghdad, killed members of the royal house, proclaimed a republic, and ended the Hashemite monarchy. Republican politics were then marked by military factions, Arab nationalism, communism, land reform, Kurdish issues, and repeated coups.

The Baath Party returned to power in 1968 after an earlier failed experience in 1963. It promoted Arab nationalism, socialism, anti-Israeli rhetoric, state-led development, and party control. Relations with Syria, Jordan, Egypt, Iran, Gulf monarchies, Palestinians, and Kurds were often tense, despite Iraq's ambition to lead the Arab world.

Saddam Hussein became the regime's central power through control of internal security, kinship networks, the Presidential Guard, and ruthless elimination of opponents. Ahmad Hasan al-Bakr became a figurehead by the late 1970s, and Saddam assumed the presidency in 1979, purging rivals and personalizing the state.

## Wars, Sanctions, and the Iraq War

The Iran-Iraq War began after Iran's 1979 revolution increased ideological and border tensions. Saddam presented Iraq as defending the Arab world against revolutionary Shiite Islam, while Iran under Ruhollah Khomeini denounced the secular Baath regime. The war lasted from 1980 to 1988, devastated both countries, militarized Iraqi society, deepened repression, and left Iraq heavily indebted.

The invasion of Kuwait grew from debt, oil prices, Iraqi claims against Kuwait, suspicion of Gulf states, and Saddam's regional ambitions. Iraq invaded Kuwait in 1990, prompting the U.S.-led coalition of the Persian Gulf War. Defeat in 1991 led to uprisings by Kurds and Shiites, harsh repression, safe havens in Kurdistan, and the draining of southern marshes.

The Kurdish safe haven created space for autonomous rule but also intensified rivalry between the Kurdish Democratic Party and the Patriotic Union of Kurdistan. Baghdad continued trying to influence the north and pursued ethnic cleansing around areas such as oil-rich Kirkuk.

The UN embargo was tied to Resolution 687, border demarcation, surrender of weapons of mass destruction, missile limits, and inspections by UNSCOM. Iraq resisted inspections, while the United States and United Kingdom carried out air strikes. The oil-for-food program began after Iraq accepted UN-supervised oil sales in 1996, bringing food and medicine but leaving sovereignty and humanitarian disputes.

The Iraq War followed the September 11, 2001 attacks and U.S. arguments that Iraq's hostility, weapons ambitions, and terrorism links made disarmament urgent. Resolution 1441 in 2002 demanded renewed inspections. The United States and United Kingdom judged Iraqi cooperation insufficient, while France, Germany, and Russia wanted more time. U.S.-led forces invaded on March 20, 2003.

Post-2003 Iraq faced the fall of Baath institutions, foreign occupation, reconstruction under the Coalition Provisional Authority, insurgency, sectarian violence, Kurdish autonomy, debt cancellation, oil-sector problems, inflation, displacement, and constitutional rebuilding. The 2005 constitution prohibited exceptional courts and restructured national institutions, but violence and fragmentation remained severe.

The new order made federalism, sectarian representation, and oil revenue inseparable. Shiite parties, Sunni Arab communities, Kurdish authorities, secular nationalists, militias, tribes, and external powers all competed over security, ministries, budgets, disputed territories, and control of petroleum resources. Iraq's postwar problem was therefore not only rebuilding roads or elections; it was rebuilding trust in a state whose borders, resources, and institutions had been repeatedly used as instruments of domination.

The Coalition Provisional Authority took over the public sector after the invasion, while international donors pledged reconstruction funds in Madrid in 2003. The Paris Club later cancelled most of Iraq's debt to its members, but violence, shortages of replacement parts, inflation, unemployment, and weak oil production slowed recovery.
