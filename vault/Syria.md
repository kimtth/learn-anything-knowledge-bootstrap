## Introduction

![[attachments/syria_001.webp|420]]

*Relief map of Syria*

Syria is a country on the eastern Mediterranean coast in southwestern Asia. Its modern territory includes part of the Golan Heights occupied by Israel since 1967. The capital, Damascus, lies in an oasis on the Barada River at the foot of Mount Qasiyun.

Modern Syria does not match ancient Syria ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syria)), which referred more broadly to fertile lands between the eastern Mediterranean and the northern Arabian desert. Since independence in 1946, Syria has faced instability caused by social, religious, ethnic, class, military, and ideological conflict.

In 1970, Hafiz al-Assad took power and built an authoritarian state focused on domestic stability, security, military strength, and recovery of territory lost to Israel. After his death in 2000, his son Bashar al-Assad became president. Early reform gestures faded, and his government continued to rely on the military and security services to suppress dissent.

## Land

Syria borders Turkey, Iraq, Jordan, Lebanon, Israel, and the Mediterranean Sea. Its short coastline stretches about 110 miles, with sandy bays, rocky headlands, low cliffs, and coastal plains.

The Al-Ansariyyah Mountains run north to south beside the coastal plain. East of them lies the Ghab Depression and the Orontes valley. The Anti-Lebanon Mountains mark the border with Lebanon, and Mount Hermon is Syria's highest point.

Much of the country consists of undulating plains known as the Syrian Desert, mostly rock and gravel steppe rather than sand. Smaller ranges such as Mount Al-Duruz, Abu Rujmayn, and Bishri cross parts of the south and centre.

## Drainage, Soils, and Climate

The Euphrates River is Syria's most important water source and its only navigable river. The Euphrates Dam at Tabaqah, completed in the 1970s, created Lake Al-Asad. Other rivers include the Orontes, which flows north through the Ghab Depression, and the Yarmuk, which forms part of the Jordan border.

Most drainage flows underground. Porous sandstone, chalk, basalt, and limestone allow water to form springs, subterranean rivers, and shallow water sheets, though these can be exhausted quickly or turn saline in dry areas.

Syrian [[Soils|soils]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soil)) are often poor in phosphorus and organic matter except for black soils in Al-Jazirah. Clays, loams, calcareous soils, gypsum soils, and alluvial soils occur in valleys such as the Euphrates and Ghab.

The coast and western mountains have a Mediterranean climate, with wet winters and a long dry season. Inland areas are more arid, with colder winters and hotter summers. Precipitation falls rapidly eastward, from 30 to 40 inches on the coast and mountains to less than 5 inches in Al-Hamad.

## Plant and Animal Life

Syrian vegetation ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vegetation)) includes yew, lime, fir, grains, olives, grapevines, apricots, oaks, poplars, citrus, date palms, garigue, maquis, and drought-resistant shrubs. Forests are limited and mainly mountain-based, especially in the Al-Ansariyyah region.

Useful plants include sumac, wild pistachio, laurel, mulberry, tobacco-smoking pine branches, khubbayzah, akkub, truffles, and licorice. Overuse of forests has turned many into scrub, though reforestation and protection projects exist.

Animal life includes steppe and mountain species, domestic livestock, and migratory birds. The steppe is especially tied to sheep herding and seasonal movement by nomads and seminomads.

## People

The Syrian population developed from many origins, but the dominant cultural identity is Arab. Semitic peoples such as Aramaeans, Assyrians, Chaldeans, and Canaanites had deep influence, while Greek, Roman, and Turkish influences were more political and economic.

Minorities include Kurds, Armenians, Turkmen, Assyrians, Circassians, Bedouins, Alawites, Druze, Christians, and formerly larger Jewish communities. Many Kurds live in the northeast; Armenians include old Arabized communities and later arrivals after World War I.

Arabic ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arabic)) is spoken by the great majority. Kurdish, Armenian, Turkish, Adyghian, English, and French are also used, especially in minority communities, border regions, cities, and educated groups.

Most Syrians are Muslims. Sunnis form the majority, while Alawites are the next largest Muslim group and are concentrated in Latakia, Homs, and Hamah governorates. Druze live mainly in Al-Suwayda. Christians, about one-tenth of the population in this period, include Greek Orthodox, Greek Catholic, Syriac, Armenian, Maronite, Protestant, Nestorian, Latin, and Chaldean communities.

This pluralism made Syrian identity both rich and politically delicate. Arabic language and Arab nationalism offered one framework for unity, but region, sect, tribe, city, class, and minority status all shaped loyalties. The modern state had to govern Damascus, Aleppo, coastal Alawite districts, Druze mountain areas, Kurdish northeastern communities, Bedouin zones, Christian urban communities, Palestinian refugees, and borderlands tied to Turkey, Iraq, Jordan, Lebanon, and Israel. Syrian politics therefore cannot be understood apart from the management, suppression, or mobilization of social diversity.

## Settlement and Demography

![[attachments/syria_002.webp|420]]

*Umayyad Mosque in Damascus*

Syria has four traditional regions: coastal strip, mountains, cultivated steppe, and desert steppe. The coast has fertile plains and the ports of Latakia and Tartus. The mountains are associated with Alawite settlement. The cultivated steppe includes Damascus, Aleppo, Homs, Hamah, and Al-Qamishli.

The desert steppe is linked to nomads, seminomads, oases, caravan towns, and sheep grazing. Palmyra, or Tadmur, is the most famous oasis town, located near the northern edge of the Syrian Desert.

Syria's population grew quickly and remained young, with high birth rates and life expectancy above the world average. About half the population lived in urban centres, while population density was highest in the northwest, southwest, northeast, and along major rivers.

War and politics shaped migration. Many Golan Heights residents were displaced after Israel occupied the area in 1967. Palestinian refugees arrived after 1948, Iraqi refugees after 2003, and many Syrians emigrated because of political turmoil, economic pressure, or workforce opportunities abroad.

## Economy

Socialism ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Socialism)) became official economic policy in 1963. The state controls vital sectors, including oil refineries, large electricity plants, railways, and manufacturing plants, while private shops, cooperatives, and small businesses continue in parts of retail and production.

The [[The Forms Of Government Their Historical Development (Government)|government]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Government)) has tried to mobilize economic potential, reduce underemployment, limit luxury imports, encourage savings, and discourage emigration. Since 1990, privatization has been modest because leaders feared that rapid liberalization could endanger political stability.

Agriculture ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agriculture)) employs a large share of the population and produces wheat, sugar beets, barley, corn, millet, cotton, lentils, tomatoes, potatoes, melons, onions, olives, grapes, apples, citrus, tobacco, and livestock. Wheat depends heavily on rainfall, while cotton is the most reliable export crop.

Manufacturing includes [[Textile Industries (Textile)|textiles]], engineering, cement, glass, pharmaceuticals, plywood, batteries, food processing, fertilizers, petroleum refining, and dairy products. Traditional crafts such as damask steel, metalwork, wood engraving, jewelry, inlay, and silk brocade declined with industrial processing.

## Finance, Trade, Services, and Transport

The Central Bank of Syria issues the Syrian pound and controls other banks. Public banks finance trade, real estate, industrial development, agriculture, cooperatives, small manufacturers, and artisans. Since 2000, small private banks and the Damascus Securities Exchange have appeared as cautious reforms.

Syria has often had an unfavourable trade balance, offset by tourism, transit trade, foreign aid, and Syrians' overseas earnings. Imports include machinery, vehicles, drugs, food, and fabrics. Exports include petroleum, phosphates, cotton, barley, lentils, fabrics, dried fruit, vegetables, skins, and raw wool.

The service sector ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tertiary_sector)) contributes heavily to national income and employed about half the workforce in the early 21st century. Tourism draws on ancient ruins, Classical sites, Muslim and Christian religious sites, Crusader buildings, medieval Islamic architecture, and UNESCO World Heritage sites.

Roads are Syria's main transport network. Important routes link Damascus with Aleppo and Baghdad. Railways connect Aleppo, Damascus, Al-Qamishli, Latakia, Tartus, Homs, Tadmur, Lebanon, Jordan, Turkey, and Iraq-linked routes. Latakia and Tartus are the chief ports, and Damascus and Aleppo have international airports.

## Government and Society

The 1973 constitution declared Syria part of the Arab homeland and promised legislative power, expression, and equality before law, but real authority remained concentrated in the presidency and ruling party system.

Syrian law draws on Islamic jurisprudence and the French civil code. Courts include summary courts, First Instance Courts, appeals courts, a high court of appeal, a constitutional court, a military tribunal, a mufti's court, and sectarian personal-status courts for non-Muslim communities.

The ruling Arab Socialist Bath Party dominates political life through party organs, the National Progressive Front, and the presidency. Elections are held under universal adult suffrage but are generally not considered free and fair by international observers. Alawites have dominated politics since the 1960s.

Military service is compulsory for adult males. The army is the largest armed-force branch and has roles in defence, public works, road construction, and public health. Syria also has an air force, a small navy, reserves, and ties to Palestinian guerrilla organizations.

## Welfare, Housing, Education, and Culture

Health facilities include state hospitals, private hospitals, sanatoriums, armed-forces hospitals, outpatient clinics, maternal and child-care centres, antituberculosis centres, malaria-eradication centres, and rural health centres. Medical services are unevenly distributed and concentrated in large cities.

Rapid urban migration and industrialization created a housing shortage. Traditional Damascus houses used unbaked brick, wood, and stone, while modern buildings use concrete and official buildings often use hewn stone. The state builds low-income flats in cities.

Syrian [[Education|education]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Education)) begins at age six and includes six years of compulsory primary school, three years of lower secondary, and three years of upper secondary education. Universities include Damascus, Aleppo, Tishrin, and Al-Bath.

Syrian culture blends Arab, Mediterranean, and European elements. Family life is central, visits and meals are important social customs, arranged marriages are less common but parental approval remains influential, and urban neighbourly ties often cross religious lines.

The arts include [[The Art of Literature (Literature)|literature]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Literature)), music, dance, theatre, film, television, painting, sculpture, and traditional crafts. The Ministry of Culture promotes Arab national identity while Syrians also follow regional and international media.

## Ancient and Classical History

![[attachments/syria_003.webp|420]]

*Temple of Bel ruins at Palmyra*

Human remains and tools in Syria reach back to the Middle Paleolithic. Natufian culture later supported fishing, hunting, and gathering wild grains. By the 9th millennium BCE, Mureybet showed pottery and cultivation of einkorn wheat.

Writing developed in southern Babylonia around 3000 BCE, while earlier clay tokens may have been an archaic record system. Ebla, south of Aleppo, has yielded more than 17,000 tablets from about 2600-2500 BCE, revealing a powerful Northwest Semitic kingdom.

After [[Alexander the Great]] died in 323 BCE, Syria was divided between Seleucids and Ptolemies. The Seleucids founded cities such as Antioch, Seleucia Pieria, Apamea, and Laodicea. Greek culture spread through urbanization, municipal autonomy, and settlement.

Rome eventually took control. Syrian prosperity under Roman rule came from agriculture, industries such as purple dyeing, glassmaking, weaving, and metalwork, and caravan trade through Palmyra, Damascus, Bostra, and Petra. Antioch became one of the empire's great cities.

Under Byzantine rule, Syria remained economically and culturally active but faced Persian and Arab pressure. Khosrow I captured Antioch in 540, and Khosrow II's invasion beginning in 606 was only reversed by Heraclius before new turmoil arrived.

## Islamic and Medieval History

Arab Muslim armies entered Syria in 633-634, led especially by Khalid ibn al-Walid. Damascus surrendered in 635, Heraclius was defeated at the Yarmuk River in 636, and the conquest was nearly complete by 640.

Under Muawiyah, Syria became the base of the Umayyad Caliphate, with Damascus as capital. The early Umayyad period brought expansion, wealth, administration, and monuments such as the Dome of the Rock and Great Mosque of Damascus.

The Abbasid takeover in 750 shifted power from Syria to Iraq. Syria became a dependent province, Arabic spread further, Christians faced harsher treatment under some caliphs, and political control later passed through Tulunid, Hamdanid, Ikhshidid, Fatimid, Seljuq, and other powers.

The Ayyubids, after Saladin, ruled principalities in Aleppo, Hamah, Homs, Damascus, Baalbek, and Transjordan. They promoted Sunni Islam, built madrasahs, improved security, revived commerce, and supported architecture and learning.

The Mamluks defeated the Mongols at Ayn Jalut in 1260 and reunited Syria with Egypt under Baybars I. They later expelled the Crusaders from Acre and other strongholds and ruled Syria for more than two centuries.

## Ottoman, Mandate, and Independence History

Ottoman rule weakened by the early 19th century. Muhammad Ali of Egypt sent Ibrahim Pasha into Syria in 1831. Ibrahim ruled for nearly a decade from Damascus, encouraged agriculture and European trade, but became unpopular because of taxes, disarmament, and conscription.

Later Ottoman reforms reorganized Syria into provinces such as Damascus, Aleppo, and Beirut. Railways, telegraphs, new schools, Western-style education, and administrative reforms expanded state control and changed urban society.

During [[World Wars (World War I)|World War I]], Syria became an Ottoman military base. British and imperial forces under Allenby advanced from Egypt, while Arab forces under Faysal, helped by T.E. Lawrence, joined the campaign and helped capture Damascus.

Postwar promises of Arab independence conflicted with the Sykes-Picot Agreement and French ambitions. France took control of Syria and Lebanon under mandate arrangements, while nationalist resistance continued.

Syria gained independence in 1946. The defeat in Palestine in 1948 discredited Arab governments, especially Syria's. The new republic was divided by religion, class, region, urban-rural differences, landownership, Pan-Arab goals, and military intervention.

## Bathist Rule, Foreign Policy, and Civil War

The 1961 coup ended union with Egypt and briefly restored older notables. In March 1963, Bathist supporters in the army seized power. Rivalries with Nasserists and Iraqi Bathists, plus Alawite military influence, produced a police state and a party claiming Pan-Arab legitimacy.

Hafiz al-Assad came to power in 1970 and built a durable authoritarian system. Syria condemned Iraq's invasion of Kuwait in 1990 and sent troops to the UN coalition. It also joined Arab-Israeli peace talks from the Madrid conference in 1991, especially over the occupied Golan Heights.

Relations with Turkey worsened over water, Israel, and Syrian support for the PKK, but an agreement in 1998 forced Abdullah Ocalan from Syria and closed PKK camps. Syria also maintained a strong presence in Lebanon until pressure after the 2005 assassination of Rafiq al-Hariri made that position increasingly difficult.

In 2011, protests began in Dara after children were arrested for antigovernment graffiti. Security forces killed protesters, and demonstrations spread to Baniyas, Latakia, Homs, Hamah, and other cities. The regime used live fire, arrests, military operations, communication cuts, tanks, artillery, and snipers.

The uprising became a civil war. The government blamed foreign-sponsored armed groups, offered limited concessions, and lifted emergency measures, but repression deepened opposition. Amateur videos and eyewitness accounts spread information because journalists faced heavy restrictions.
