# Land Reform and Tenure (Land Reform)

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> Land reform changes the ownership, distribution, use, or control of land to address inequality, agricultural productivity, rural conflict, and political power. Its programs range from redistribution and tenancy reform to collective and market-oriented approaches, with consequences for farming and social organization.

## Introduction

Land reform ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Land_reform)) is a purposive change in agricultural landholding, land ownership, cultivation methods, or the relation of agriculture to the wider economy. It may be carried out by governments, social groups, or revolution, and its meaning has changed as land has served as production factor, wealth store, status symbol, and source of political power.

Land tenure ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Land_tenure)) shapes wealth, income, privilege, and responsibility. Tenure laws define titles, ownership forms, disposal rights, and claims to land income. Private ownership tends to produce class differentiation, while public ownership removes some distinctions but may restrict individual control.

Modern land reform has broadened into agrarian reform. It no longer means only redistributing land titles; it may include tenancy change, credit, cooperatives, rural infrastructure, productivity, women's rights, technology, and the integration of agriculture into national development.

## Mexico

The Mexican reform followed the revolution and began with a 1915 decree. Its main target was land of Indian villages that had been illegally absorbed by haciendas. Peons were legally free but often trapped in debt and virtual serfdom under landlords.

The reform goal was to restore land to legal owners, rebuild collective Indian villages called ejidos, reduce poverty and inequality, and stabilize politics. The 1917 constitution confirmed the reform but also protected private property, creating loopholes that slowed change.

Lazaro Cardenas and the Agrarian Code of 1934 made reform more effective. Villages gained land and peons gained more liberty, but poverty and land concentration continued. Small-scale farming was encouraged, helping social and political goals more than early agricultural output.

## Reforms Since World War II

Postwar reform became wider and more diverse. Socialist reforms stressed land to the tiller, cooperation, collective ownership, large-scale operation, mechanization, and development. Capitalist-oriented reforms kept private ownership, family farming, and dual tenure while seeking democracy, equality, stability, and growth.

Changing reform ideas moved from community development in the 1950s, to productivity and technology in the 1960s, to integrated rural development in the 1970s, and then to target groups and basic needs. Later thinking also tried to include women as equal reform beneficiaries.

## Japan

Japan's reform came after World War II under Allied Occupation pressure. Although Meiji reforms had abolished formal feudalism, rich owners and moneylenders had produced absentee landlords and perpetual tenants. In 1943, tenants operated 66 percent of the land and paid high rents in kind.

The 1946 reform law set ceilings on holdings, expropriated excess land, resold it to tenants, compensated landlords, protected tenancy contracts, reduced rents, and supported marketing and credit cooperatives. The Local Autonomy Law of 1947 further shifted village power downward.

The results were rapid. Tenancy fell by more than 80 percent within two years, village incomes became more equal, peasant status improved, and yields per unit of land rose. The reform was designed for Japan's literacy, industrial level, and need for democratization.

## Egypt

Egypt's reform followed the 1952 revolution that overthrew the monarchy. It affected only about 12 percent of arable land but was applied thoroughly. Before reform, land concentration, tiny holdings, widespread tenancy, high rents, middlemen, and expensive credit shaped rural poverty.

The Agrarian Reform Law set a ceiling of 200 faddans, later 100, with special allowances for sons. Excess land was expropriated and distributed in small parcels, landlords received bonds, beneficiaries repaid over time, and new owners had to join cooperatives for production, marketing, and credit.

Tenancy reform replaced traditional arrangements with contracts, capped rents at 50 percent of output, and limited tenant holdings to reduce subletting. The reform strengthened peasant morale and political support, though its economic effects were limited by the small share of land redistributed.

## Southeast Asia

Southeast Asian reform often followed the Japanese model, especially in Taiwan, South Korea, and former South Vietnam. These reforms were influenced by American advisers and anticommunist governments and aimed to stabilize politics, improve living standards, and encourage development.

Taiwan's reform from 1949 to 1953 reduced rents to 37.5 percent, sold public tenant land to tenants, and bought private tenant land for resale to tenants. Landlords partly received industrial shares, linking agrarian reform to industrial development.

Vietnam's reform began in 1955, reduced rents, capped holdings, and redistributed excess land in small parcels. It ended when South Vietnam collapsed and was replaced by the socialist model of North Vietnam. Taiwan's reform, supported by credit, education, technology, and services, became the region's major success case.

## Latin America

Latin American reform after World War II responded to social instability, population growth, land concentration, plantation economies, rural poverty, nationalism, foreign ownership, and international pressure. Reforms varied sharply with the ideology of the government in power.

[[Brazil]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brazil)) used indirect and mild measures such as taxing idle land, protecting tenants, and settling the Amazon, with modest results because settlers faced severe physical and biological hardship. [[Peru]] nationalized feudal estates and ran them through Agricultural Societies of Social Interest to preserve efficient enterprises.

Cuba's reform followed the 1958 revolution. It kept some private ownership but reduced it in favour of the public sector, attacked latifundia, expropriated foreign-company land, and aimed to improve rural living standards while transforming political and economic power.

## Conclusions

Land reform ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Land_reform)) and agrarian reform have become nearly synonymous because modern programs reach beyond tenure into production, rural society, and development. Reform has often been a crisis-management tool, broad enough to handle immediate danger but rarely deep enough to remove underlying causes.

Reform choices are difficult. Policymakers must balance private ownership with inequality, public or collective ownership with restrictions, labour-intensive employment with low productivity, and revolutionary effectiveness with disruption. Capitalist reforms usually favour private ownership, while socialist reforms favour collective or public control.

Land redistribution has often improved peasant status and political stability but produced limited economic transformation. It may remove landlord contributions without replacing them, create inefficient minifarms, restrict labour mobility, or threaten large-scale production. Only comprehensive socialist reforms have significantly narrowed the gap between agriculture and other sectors.

## Key Events & Developments

- 1915: Mexican reform began restoring illegally alienated village lands.
- 1934: The Agrarian Code and Cardenas strengthened Mexican reform.
- 1946: Japan's reform law redistributed landlord land to tenants.
- 1952: Egyptian reform followed revolution against the monarchy.
- 1949-1953: Taiwan's reform moved from rent reduction to tenant ownership.
- 1958: Cuba's revolution produced a radical land reform.
