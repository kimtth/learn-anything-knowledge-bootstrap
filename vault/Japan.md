# Japan

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> Japan is an island country shaped by mountainous terrain, seismic and volcanic activity, dense settlement, distinctive language and religious traditions, and a highly developed economy. Its history moves from early and medieval institutions through Tokugawa rule, Meiji imperial expansion, wartime defeat, and postwar transformation.

## Introduction

Japan ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Japan)) is an island country off the east coast of Asia, stretching about 1,500 miles through the western North Pacific in a northeast-southwest arc. Its four main islands are Hokkaido, Honshu, Shikoku, and Kyushu, with Honshu by far the largest. The Ryukyu, Izu, Bonin, and Volcano island groups extend the country southward and eastward.

Tokyo, in east-central Honshu, is the capital and one of the world's most populous cities. Japan's landscape is rugged and mountainous, with more than four-fifths of the land surface made up of mountains. Mount Fuji, at 12,388 feet, is the highest peak and the best-known national symbol.

Japanese life is marked by complexity and contrast. Japan has an ancient cultural tradition, a strong sensitivity to natural beauty, highly formalized aesthetic values, dense cities, advanced technology, and one of the world's most literate populations. Since 1950, it has also been one of the world's most economically and technologically advanced societies.

Japanese history ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_Japan)) begins in archaeology with Paleolithic, Jomon, and Yayoi cultures, enters written record through Chinese sources in the 1st century BCE, develops political unity under the Yamato court, absorbs Chinese and Korean cultural influences, builds classical capitals at Nara and Heian-kyo, passes through centuries of warrior rule, reopens to the West in the 19th century, becomes an imperial power, suffers defeat in World War II, and emerges as a major postwar economy.

## Land, Relief, and Geological Instability

Japan's boundaries are maritime. The Sea of Japan separates it from Korea and Russia to the west; the Sea of Okhotsk and Kuril Islands lie to the north and northeast; the Pacific lies east and south; and the East China Sea separates southwestern Japan from China. Tsushima, between Kyushu and Korea, helps define the Korea and Tsushima straits.

The mountains reflect intense Quaternary mountain-building, faulting, warping, earthquakes, volcanism, and coast movement. Japan has no broad structural plains like more stable continental regions. Instead, small mountain blocks are broken by lowlands and deep saddles, and steep slopes are cut by dense river valleys.

Alluvial fans, deltaic plains, uplifted former sea bottoms, volcanic ash uplands, bays, headlands, and sand-ridge coasts give Japan a young and unstable physical character. The Kanto, Nobi, and Osaka plains are among the important lowlands, but flat land is limited and heavily used.

[[Earthquakes]] and volcanic eruptions are recurring hazards. Japan experiences about 1,000 tremors annually, most minor but some destructive, including the Tokyo-Yokohama earthquake of 1923 and the Kobe earthquake of 1995. At least 60 volcanoes have been active in historical time, and major eruptions have occurred on Hokkaido, in the Izu Islands, and in Kyushu.

## Drainage, Soils, Climate, Flora, and Fauna

Freshwater is both abundant and difficult to manage. Japan's rivers are generally short, swift, and fed by small drainage basins, which complicates irrigation, dam construction, industrial supply, and domestic use. Major rivers include the Teshio, Ishikari, Kitakami, Tone, Shinano, Kiso, Tenryu, and Chikugo.

Lake Biwa ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lake_Biwa)), in central Honshu, is Japan's largest lake, covering 259 square miles. Other lakes occupy tectonic depressions, volcanic basins, drowned valleys, and coastal lagoons. Japan's soils range from podzolic and brown forest soils to red and yellow soils, volcanic ash soils, peat, muck, gley paddy soils, and reclaimed polder soils.

The climate ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Climate)) is monsoonal and varied by latitude, seas, ocean currents, and mountains. Winter winds from Siberia bring snow and rain to the Sea of Japan side and dry weather to the Pacific side. Summer winds bring warmth and rain, while typhoons are frequent and destructive in late summer and early fall.

Ocean currents shape climate and marine life. The warm Kuroshio flows north along the Pacific coast, the Tsushima Current brings moisture to the Sea of Japan side, and the cold Oya Current meets warmer water off northern Japan, producing fogs and rich fisheries.

Vegetation ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vegetation)) ranges from semitropical rainforest in the Ryukyu and Bonin islands to evergreen laurel forest, deciduous broad-leaved forest, coniferous forest, bamboo undergrowth, alpine plants, and planted or altered landscapes. Cherry blossoms, maples, cedars, camellias, pines, and bamboo have cultural as well as ecological significance.

Animal life remains diverse in remote mountains and surrounding waters. Bears, wild boars, tanuki, foxes, deer, hares, weasels, Japanese macaques, sea turtles, snakes, frogs, giant salamanders, insects, migratory birds, whales, dolphins, tuna, salmon, sardines, crabs, clams, oysters, and koi all appear in Japan's natural and cultural world.

## People, Language, Religion, and Settlement

The Japanese people form the overwhelming majority of the population and are ethnically close to other East Asian peoples. The old Edo-period class divisions among warriors, farmers, craftsmen, merchants, nobles, and outcast groups have mostly disappeared, but burakumin descendants still face discrimination.

Ainu, Okinawans, resident Koreans, and Chinese-descended communities complicate the image of complete homogeneity. Many Koreans migrated to Japan before and during World War II, often under coercive colonial conditions, and those who remained after the war and their descendants have faced legal and social discrimination.

Japanese is the national language, while Ainu is nearly extinct. Chinese writing entered around the 4th century CE, and Japanese adapted Chinese characters, or kanji, before developing the kana syllabaries by the 9th century. Modern written Japanese combines kanji, hiragana, katakana, and many Western loanwords, especially from English.

Standard Japanese, based on the Tokyo dialect, spread through the national school system and broadcasting. Local dialects remain important and are sometimes mutually difficult to understand. Linguists often connect Japanese to the Altaic language group and note affinities with Korean, though vocabulary differs strongly.

Religion ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Religion)) in Japan is plural and overlapping. Shinto, Buddhism, Christianity, shamanistic practices, and new religions coexist without one tradition dominating daily life. Many families combine Shinto rites, Buddhist funerary practices, household Buddhist altars, local shrine worship, and secular routines.

[[Shinto]] is polytheistic and honours kami associated with natural objects, places, ancestors, and major historical figures. Buddhism entered officially from Korea in the mid-6th century CE, became a national religion in the Nara period, and diversified into sects such as Tendai, Shingon, Pure Land, Zen, and Nichiren. State-supported Shinto after the Meiji Restoration was abolished after World War II.

Settlement ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Human_settlement)) reflects mountains, rice fields, fishing coasts, castle towns, industrial belts, and commuter suburbs. Traditional rural units, once called mura and now often aza, bound households through local ties, festivals, irrigation, and common obligations. Industrialization, commuting, seasonal labour, and migration to cities have reduced the separateness of rural communities.

## Economy, Agriculture, Resources, and Energy

Japan's economy achieved extraordinary growth in the 20th century, especially after World War II. Industrial production, domestic demand, export strategy, technology, capital investment, and manufacturing efficiency turned a devastated country into one of the world's leading producers of motor vehicles, steel, consumer electronics, machinery, precision equipment, and high-technology goods.

The service sector ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tertiary_sector)) dominates employment and GDP, but manufacturing remains central to Japan's global position. The economy has long depended on trade because Japan lacks many fossil fuels, minerals, and food resources. Yet the domestic market is large enough that trade's share of GDP has often been lower than in some other export-oriented economies.

Postwar recovery began under Allied occupation with land reform, labour reform, reconstruction, and policies associated with American banker Joseph Dodge. The Korean War in 1950 created demand for Japanese goods and triggered investment that helped launch decades of growth. By the early 1970s, oil shocks, wage increases, yen appreciation, trade friction, and structural distortions slowed rapid expansion.

Agriculture ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agriculture)) is constrained by mountains, limited arable land, immature soils, small farms, and an aging farm population. Wet-rice cultivation remains central, supported by abundant rainfall and irrigation networks. Rice, wheat, barley, potatoes, fruits, vegetables, tea, livestock, dairy, and beef production are important, but Japan imports nearly half its food requirements.

Forestry and fishing are significant but limited by geography and economic change. Forest resources are extensive but often inaccessible. Coastal and deep-sea fishing, aquaculture, cultured pearls, salmon, tuna, sardines, mackerel, shellfish, and fish markets have long mattered, though international restrictions and resource pressure changed the industry.

Mineral resources are generally small or poor in quality. Japan has limestone, some coal, iron ore, zinc, lead, copper, sulfur, gold, silver, tungsten, chromite, manganese, natural gas, and minimal oil, but mining declined sharply. By the early 21st century, Japan imported virtually all iron and copper needs and depended heavily on imported fuels.

Energy policy has tried to reduce dependence on foreign oil. Thermal power plants produce most electricity, using imported oil, coal, and liquefied natural gas. Hydroelectric potential is uneven and seasonal. Since the 1970s, the government promoted nuclear power as a domestically produced nonpolluting source, eventually making it a major part of installed generating capacity.

## Manufacturing, Finance, Trade, and Transport

Manufacturing ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manufacturing)) shifted from light industries toward heavy, highly processed, and advanced production. Shipbuilding, automobiles, steel, synthetic rubber, aluminum, sulfuric acid, plastics, cement, paper, chemicals, machinery, robots, cameras, computers, microelectronics, telecommunications equipment, and consumer goods made Japan a formidable industrial power.

Industrial organization depended on high capital investment, productivity growth, skilled systems, technological innovation, and keiretsu corporate groups. Keiretsu cross-shareholding helped firms coordinate long-range strategies and survive structural change, though deregulation, finance shifts, mergers, and global competition weakened old boundaries.

Finance ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Finance)) became globally important in the 1980s, when Japanese banks and the Tokyo Stock Exchange rose to world prominence. Much of the boom rested on speculative real-estate and securities values. The bubble burst in the early 1990s, leaving banks with bad loans, forcing government intervention, and producing long recovery.

The Bank of Japan, established in 1882, issues the yen and influences financial policy. Late-1990s reforms made it autonomous from the Ministry of Finance and created supervisory agencies. Japan Post Bank privatization was completed in 2007, and large bank mergers continued into the early 21st century.

Exports drove postwar growth even when exports' share of national income stayed limited. Textiles and food declined, while machinery, electronics, transport equipment, chemicals, metals, and high-value goods rose. The United States long remained the largest export market, while China grew to rival it in the early 21st century.

Imports reflect scarce resources: fuels, raw materials, foodstuffs, machinery, chemicals, and manufactured goods. High trade surpluses brought pressure from trading partners, especially the United States, to open Japanese markets. Domestic distribution long relied on complex wholesaling, small retailers, cash transactions, and later department stores, supermarkets, credit cards, mail order, and online commerce.

Transport is highly developed despite mountains, crowding, and urban congestion. Roads lagged behind rail and port development, and big cities suffer traffic, exhaust, and noise problems. Trucks carry most domestic freight, while railways remain central for passenger commuting.

The Shinkansen began in 1964 on the New Tokaido Line between Tokyo and Osaka and became the emblem of modern Japanese rail. Surface links now connect the four main islands through tunnels, bridges, and ferries, including the undersea links between Kyushu and Honshu, the Hokkaido-Honshu railway tunnel opened in 1988, and bridges between Honshu and Shikoku.

[[Telecommunications Systems (Telecommunication)|Telecommunications]] are among the world's most advanced. Remote mountain villages and islands are linked by postal, telephone, satellite, fibre-optic, mobile, and Internet networks. Nippon Telegraph and Telephone privatization began in the mid-1980s, and Japan became a leader in mobile phones, Internet connections, and high-definition television.

## Government, Security, Health, Welfare, Housing, and Education

The constitution was promulgated in 1946 and came into force in 1947, replacing the Meiji Constitution of 1889. Sovereignty shifted from the emperor to the people, fundamental rights were guaranteed, the emperor became symbol of the state and unity of the people, and Article 9 renounced war as a sovereign right.

The judiciary is independent and includes the Supreme Court, high courts, district courts, family courts, and summary courts. The Supreme Court has final review and judicial-review power. Universal adult suffrage applies to citizens age 20 or older in the source period, and the Diet includes the House of Representatives and House of Councillors.

[[Political Parties and Interest Groups (Political Party)|Political parties]] emerged in the Meiji period, were suppressed in the war years, and resumed after 1945. Postwar politics were long shaped by conservative dominance, opposition parties, electoral reforms, rural-urban vote imbalance, factional competition, and public debates over corruption, economic management, and security.

Security policy is constrained by Article 9. Japan had no armed forces beyond police between 1945 and 1950, but the Korean War led to the National Police Reserve and later the Self-Defense Forces. The United States-Japan security treaty of 1960, reaffirmed in 1970, kept U.S. bases in Japan, especially Okinawa.

[[Police]] are organized under national and prefectural public-safety bodies, with local police stations and neighbourhood koban. Violent crime rates are low by international comparison, firearms restrictions are strict, and organized crime groups known as yakuza or boryokudan have long been targets of enforcement.

Health is supported by high living standards and comprehensive national insurance. Japan's aging population, low birth rate, and high life expectancy place increasing pressure on medical costs. Cancers became the leading cause of death around 1980, while cerebrovascular disease declined; heart disease, pneumonia, accidents, and suicide also matter.

Welfare expanded during high growth after the mid-1950s. Social insurance covers health, pensions, unemployment, workers' accidents, elderly care, disability services, and child welfare. Land reforms from 1946 to 1950 and progressive taxation reduced prewar inequality, but income, property, and regional differences remained.

Housing ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Housing)) improved after severe postwar shortages through state loan agencies, public housing, corporate housing, and private ownership. Most people came to live in owner-occupied homes, often detached houses, and living space increased. Yet land prices and metropolitan crowding continued to make housing expensive in Tokyo, Osaka, and other large urban regions.

[[Education]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Education)) is central to Japan's social mobility and industrial success. Compulsory elementary school lasts six years and compulsory middle school three years; kindergarten and high school are not compulsory but became nearly universal. Entrance examinations, school ranking, juku cram schools, and competition for Tokyo and Kyoto universities shape family life and social status.

## Culture, Arts, Daily Life, Cuisine, and Media

Japanese culture developed through selective borrowing and adaptation. China, Korea, India through Buddhism, and the West all shaped Japanese forms, but outside influences were repeatedly absorbed into indigenous patterns. Buddhist deities entered Shinto worlds, Chinese writing was adapted to Japanese language, and Western institutions were remade during modernization.

Aesthetic ideals include shibui, refined understatement; wabi, cultivated simplicity and poverty; and sabi, appreciation of age, fading, and transience. These values are linked to Buddhist ideas of impermanence and appear in gardens, tea, calligraphy, architecture, drama, poetry, ceramics, lacquer, painting, and daily etiquette.

Traditional arts include tea ceremony, ikebana, calligraphy, gardening, architecture, painting, sculpture, bugaku, Noh, kyogen, Bunraku, Kabuki, folk performance, pottery, lacquer, dyeing, weaving, and wood-block printing. Kyoto, Nara, Edo, regional towns, temples, shrines, merchants, warriors, and court elites all supported different artistic currents.

UNESCO-recognized sites reflect cultural depth, including historic monuments at Kyoto and Nara, the Atomic Bomb Dome at Hiroshima, and the Shimane silver-mining area. Japanese gardens, temples, shrines, castles, and old urban districts preserve forms shaped by aristocratic, Buddhist, warrior, merchant, and modern histories.

Popular culture is urban and media-driven. Tokyo-based mass media transmit styles across the country, and young urban consumers are associated with fast-changing trends. Jazz, rock, blues, karaoke, manga, television, film, and hybrid Japanese-Western popular music belong to the postwar cultural landscape.

Family life long revolved around workplace and school. Traditional male breadwinner and female household-finance roles have changed as more women work outside the home, often part-time. In rural areas, women increasingly managed agriculture when men worked in manufacturing or service jobs.

Japanese cuisine is known for subtle flavours, raw or lightly cooked foods, seasonal presentation, rice, seafood, soy products, noodles, and tea. Sushi, sashimi, tempura, tofu, miso soup, sukiyaki, shabu-shabu, soba, udon, green tea, sake, beer, and distilled beverages all reflect regional and modern tastes.

[[Broadcasting]] was long shaped by NHK, which became a public corporation financed by license fees. Commercial radio began in 1951, NHK television in 1953, and commercial television in 1955. Private networks, satellite and cable television, digital broadcasting, and high-definition television became important later.

## Ancient and Classical Japan

![[japan_002.webp|420]]

*Satellite image of Japan in May 2003*

Prehistoric Japan includes Paleolithic settlement, the Pre-Ceramic era, Jomon culture, Yayoi culture, and the rise of Yamato. Humans probably reached the islands through former land connections with Korea and northeastern Asia. Paleolithic tools show hunting, gathering, fire use, pit dwellings, and cave habitation, though much remains uncertain.

Jomon culture ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/J%C5%8Dmon_period)), from about 10,500 to about 300 BCE, is named for cord-marked pottery described by Edward S. Morse. Jomon society made pottery, used polished tools, hunted, gathered, fished, and gradually experienced climatic and subsistence changes that encouraged new technologies such as the bow and arrow.

Yayoi culture, from about the 3rd century BCE to the 2nd or 3rd century CE, brought wet-rice agriculture, iron tools, bronze ritual objects, paddy fields, and stronger continental influence from China and Korea. Rice cultivation probably entered from the Yangtze River delta area through northern Kyushu.

The Yamato court emerged in central Honshu and probably achieved substantial unification by the mid-4th century. Large kofun tombs, military activity in Korea, Chinese records, and domestic chronicles point to rulers combining sacred authority, manpower control, agriculture, and expanding political power.

[[Buddha and Buddhism (Buddhism)|Buddhism]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buddhism)) entered from Paekche, traditionally in 538 or 552, and was first valued for its magical, artistic, and state-protecting power. Prince Shotoku, regent for Empress Suiko, linked Buddhist ideals with Chinese Confucian political models and helped move Yamato toward centralized rule.

The Taika reforms, ritsuryo codes, Nara capital, provincial temples, and state Buddhism created a classical order. Emperor Shomu and Empress Komyo promoted Buddhism, kokubunji provincial monasteries, and the Great Buddha at Todai Temple. Ganjin, after repeated attempts, reached Japan in 753 and founded the Ritsu sect.

Heian-kyo, founded in 794 by Emperor Kammu, moved power away from Nara temples. Kammu reorganized government, campaigned against the Ezo, and supported new Buddhist sects. Saicho founded Tendai on Mount Hiei, while Kukai founded Shingon on Mount Koya.

Heian society preserved formal government while land, taxation, and power shifted. Shoen private estates grew, public revenue weakened, Fujiwara marriage politics dominated the court, and provincial warrior families gained strength. The elegant court culture of Kyoto produced refined literature, etiquette, and artistic forms, but the ritsuryo state hollowed out.

## Medieval Japan

Minamoto Yoritomo founded a warrior government at Kamakura after defeating the Taira in the Gempei War. In 1185 he gained authority to appoint shugo military governors and jito estate stewards; in 1192 he became shogun. Civil authority in Kyoto and military authority in Kamakura formed a practical dyarchy.

The Hojo family became the real power after Yoritomo's death in 1199. Hojo regents controlled shogunal appointments, defeated Emperor Go-Toba in the Jokyu Disturbance of 1221, occupied Kyoto, confiscated estates, and extended bakufu power across the country.

The Mongol invasions of 1274 and 1281 were major turning points. Kublai Khan's forces landed in Kyushu, but Japanese defence, coastal walls, and typhoons destroyed or drove back the fleets. The invasions strained the Kamakura regime because defensive costs brought little reward to warriors.

The Muromachi bakufu arose under Ashikaga Takauji, with later consolidation under Ashikaga Yoshimitsu. Yoshimitsu moved the bakufu to Kyoto's Muromachi district in 1378, unified northern and southern courts, controlled great shugo, built the Golden Pavilion, and reopened trade with Ming China.

The Onin War of 1467-1477 devastated Kyoto and spread warfare into the provinces. Shogunal succession disputes, famine, debt cancellation, shugo rivalry, and local uprisings broke older authority. Villages, local warriors, and emerging daimyo increasingly defended themselves and challenged established governors.

The Sengoku period saw gekokujo, the overthrow of superiors by inferiors, as new daimyo replaced old shugo houses. Daimyo issued domain laws, surveyed land, controlled villages, built irrigation, reorganized castles and roads, developed castle towns, and encouraged commerce, markets, money circulation, and long-distance trade.

## Early Modern Japan

![[japan_003.webp|420]]

*Japan-Tokyo-tower-1024*

Oda Nobunaga began national unification from Owari in the 1550s and 1560s. He occupied Kyoto, used firearms effectively, defeated rival daimyo and powerful Buddhist institutions, separated warriors from peasants, surveyed lands, rewarded retainers, and reversed a century of feudal fragmentation.

Toyotomi Hideyoshi, a peasant-born commander under Nobunaga, succeeded him after Akechi Mitsuhide assassinated Nobunaga. By 1590, Hideyoshi controlled Japan from Kyushu to Tohoku. His land surveys from 1583 to 1598, sword hunt, class separation, and national administration clarified land rights and hardened social order.

Tokugawa Ieyasu won the Battle of Sekigahara in 1600 and founded the Edo bakufu in 1603. The Tokugawa system, often called bakuhan, combined shogunal authority with domains ruled by daimyo. Edo, later Tokyo, became the political centre, while Kyoto retained imperial prestige.

Tokugawa rule promoted order through class hierarchy, alternate attendance, castle-town networks, Confucian ideology, limits on Christianity, control of foreign contacts, and relative seclusion. Orthodox Chu Hsi thought, advanced by thinkers such as Hayashi Razan, stressed loyalty, hierarchy, ruler-subject order, and legitimacy.

Edo culture flourished through literacy, urban merchants, publishing, Kabuki, Bunraku, haikai poetry, ukiyo-e wood-block prints, ceramics, lacquer, textiles, food, etiquette, and regional production. Ogata Korin, Miyazaki Yuzen, Hishikawa Moronobu, Ando Hiroshige, and Katsushika Hokusai shaped distinctive arts.

Tokugawa crisis deepened in the 18th and 19th centuries. Fiscal strain, famine, peasant uprisings, city riots, corruption, and external pressure shook the bakufu. The Tempo-era famine and disorders of the 1830s, including the Oshio Heihachiro revolt in Osaka, exposed social tension.

Matthew C. Perry entered Uraga Bay in 1853 with U.S. warships, forcing the opening of Japan after earlier British, French, Dutch, and American pressures. The Treaty of Kanagawa in 1854 and Harris Treaty in 1858 weakened bakufu legitimacy, energized imperial loyalism, and intensified the slogan Sonno joi, or Revere the emperor! Expel the barbarians!

## Meiji, Empire, and World War II

The Meiji Restoration of 1868 returned political centrality to the emperor under Mutsuhito, later called Meiji, and began a social and political revolution. The new state abolished the domains, centralized administration, built a modern army, expanded education, reformed taxation, encouraged industry, adopted Western institutions, and promulgated the Meiji Constitution in 1889.

Imperial expansion grew from security concerns, nationalism, economic ambition, and treaty inequality. Japan incorporated the Ryukyu Islands in 1879, competed with China over Korea, and fought the Sino-Japanese War in 1894-1895. The Treaty of Shimonoseki recognized Korean independence, ceded Taiwan and the Pescadores to Japan, and gave economic concessions.

The Russo-Japanese War of 1904-1905 followed competition over Korea and Manchuria. Japanese forces won on land and sea, including Admiral Togo Heihachiro's victory at Tsushima. The Treaty of Portsmouth gave Japan primacy in Korea, interests in southern Manchuria, the Liaotung leasehold, and southern Sakhalin, but no indemnity, angering many Japanese.

[[Korea]] was annexed in 1910, and Japan entered World War I on the Allied side, seizing German holdings in China and the Pacific. The Twenty-one Demands of 1915, the Versailles settlement of 1919, and intervention in Siberia increased Chinese and Western distrust of Japan.

Interwar politics mixed constitutional government, party cabinets, labour unrest, rural hardship, urban culture, naval limitation treaties, and growing military resentment. The Washington treaties of 1921-1922 restrained naval power and replaced the Anglo-Japanese Alliance, while universal male suffrage and party politics expanded under pressure.

Militarism grew from economic crisis, ultranationalism, army autonomy, China policy, and distrust of parties. The Kwantung Army seized Manchuria in 1931, created Manchukuo with P'u-i as figurehead, and led Japan to withdraw from the League of Nations in 1933. The military revolt of 1936 shifted power further toward military conservatives.

The Pacific War began after Japan expanded in China and Indochina, faced U.S. oil embargoes and asset freezes, and failed negotiations with Secretary of State Cordell Hull. The attack on Pearl Harbor on December 7, 1941, or December 8 in Japan, brought the United States fully into war.

Japan's early victories included Manila, Singapore, the Dutch East Indies, and Rangoon in early 1942, but Midway in June 1942 cost four carriers and many pilots, and Guadalcanal ended with withdrawal in 1943. Saipan's fall in 1944 put U.S. bombers within range of Tokyo.

Defeat came after bombing, blockade, Okinawa, internal political paralysis, and Allied insistence on unconditional surrender. Firebombing devastated major cities, and atomic bombs destroyed Hiroshima and Nagasaki in 1945. Japan surrendered in August 1945, ending empire and war.

## Occupation, Postwar Growth, and Later Change

The Allied occupation lasted from 1945 to 1952 under SCAP, led by Douglas MacArthur until 1951. Though nominally multinational, occupation policy was largely American and relied on Japanese government organs to carry out reforms.

Occupation reforms demilitarized and democratized Japan. The empire was dismantled, armed forces demobilized, State Shinto disestablished, nationalist organizations abolished, war industries dismantled, police powers decentralized, education reformed, wartime leaders purged, war crimes tried, and a new constitution created.

Economic reconstruction and social change built the foundation for postwar democracy. Land reform weakened landlordism, labour rights expanded, women's legal and political status changed, zaibatsu conglomerates were broken up, and American models were adapted into Japanese institutions.

High growth in the 1950s and 1960s transformed Japan into a manufacturing and financial power. By the early 1970s, technology gains, disappearance of surplus rural labour, heavy-industry competition, urban congestion, pollution, land prices, dollar devaluation, the 1973-1974 oil embargo, and dependence on imported fuel and food ended the easy-growth era.

Late postwar Japan sought global integration, free trade, energy diversification, nuclear power, and new markets. Economic nationalism revived at times, but Japan also became a major donor, investor, lender, manufacturer, and participant in U.S.-Japan alliance management.

The bubble economy of the late 1980s pushed real estate and securities prices to unsustainable levels. Its collapse in the early 1990s damaged banks, securities markets, business confidence, and public finance, producing a prolonged period of slow growth and reform.

The early 21st century saw Japan facing aging, low fertility, fiscal strain, globalization, regional competition from China and South Korea, security debates, environmental pressures, and questions about political reform. Yet it remained a major manufacturing, trading, financial, cultural, technological, and diplomatic power.

Fukushima gave Japan's energy problem a new political and social force. Because the country has few domestic mineral and fuel resources, postwar policy had encouraged conservation, imported energy, and nuclear power. The 2011 earthquake, tsunami, and nuclear crisis exposed the risk behind that strategy, deepened public debate over reactors and regulation, and linked energy security to disaster preparedness, environmental trust, local communities, and national economic planning.

- Fujiwara Kamatari ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fujiwara_Kamatari)) and Fujiwara Fuhito ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fujiwara_Fuhito)): Foundational figures in Fujiwara court power and ritsuryo codes.
- Saicho ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saicho)): Founder of Tendai Buddhism on Mount Hiei.
- Kukai ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kukai)): Founder of Shingon Buddhism on Mount Koya.
- Minamoto Yoritomo ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minamoto_Yoritomo)): Founder of the Kamakura warrior government.
- Hojo Masako ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hojo_Masako)) and Hojo Tokimasa ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hojo_Tokimasa)): Central figures in Hojo regency power.
- Ashikaga Takauji ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ashikaga_Takauji)): Founder of the Muromachi warrior regime.
- Ashikaga Yoshimitsu ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ashikaga_Yoshimitsu)): Muromachi shogun who consolidated power and built the Golden Pavilion.
- Oda Nobunaga ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oda_Nobunaga)): First major Sengoku unifier and military innovator.
- Toyotomi Hideyoshi ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Toyotomi_Hideyoshi)): Unifier who surveyed land, disarmed peasants, and completed national consolidation.
- Tokugawa Ieyasu ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tokugawa_Ieyasu)): Founder of the Edo bakufu after Sekigahara.
- Hayashi Razan ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hayashi_Razan)): Confucian thinker who helped justify Tokugawa order.
- Matthew C. Perry ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matthew_C._Perry)): U.S. commodore whose fleet forced Japan's opening crisis.
- Emperor Meiji ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emperor_Meiji)): Symbolic centre of Japan's modernizing restoration.
- Ito Hirobumi ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/It%C5%8D_Hirobumi)): Meiji statesman and resident general in Korea.
- Togo Heihachiro ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Togo_Heihachiro)): Admiral who destroyed the Russian Baltic fleet at Tsushima.
- Tojo Hideki ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tojo_Hideki)): Wartime leader associated with Japan's Pacific War government.
- Douglas MacArthur ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Douglas_MacArthur)): SCAP commander who directed occupation reforms.
- Joseph Dodge ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph_Dodge)): American banker linked to postwar stabilization policy.

## Interesting Facts

- Mount Fuji is Japan's highest mountain at 12,388 feet.
- Japan ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Japan)) has about 1,000 tremors annually, though most are minor.
- Lake Biwa ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lake_Biwa)) covers 259 square miles and is the largest Japanese lake.
- Japanese macaques at northern Honshu live near the world's northern limit of monkey habitation.
- Kanji came from Chinese writing, while kana syllabaries were developed from it by the 9th century.
- Article 9 renounces war, making Japan's Self-Defense Forces a recurring constitutional debate.
- The Shinkansen began operation in 1964, the same year Tokyo hosted major international attention.
- Karaoke, literally empty orchestra, was invented in Japan in the early 1970s.
- The Kuroshio is a warm current often compared with the Atlantic Gulf Stream.
- The bubble economy linked real estate and securities speculation before collapsing in the early 1990s.
