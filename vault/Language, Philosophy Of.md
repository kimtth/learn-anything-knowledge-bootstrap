# Language, Philosophy Of

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> Philosophy of language examines meaning, reference, truth, interpretation, and how words and sentences connect thought with the world. Its modern debates draw on logic, linguistics, pragmatics, and major thinkers from Frege and Wittgenstein to Quine and Chomsky.

## Introduction

Philosophy of language ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philosophy_of_language)) investigates the nature of language, the relations among language, users, and the world, and the concepts used to describe language in ordinary speech and linguistics. It differs from linguistics because its questions are conceptual rather than empirical, though it must respect linguistic facts.

Language and thought ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linguistic_relativity)) are central because language lets humans describe the past, imagine the future, consider counterfactuals, communicate beliefs, share emotions, and build social worlds. Specialized languages in mathematics and science also let humans theorize about matters they could not otherwise grasp.

Intentionality links language with the directedness of thought toward things beyond itself. Language allows people to escape the here and now, unlike animal signalling systems that remain tied to immediate environments.

## Skepticism

[[Plato and Platonism (Plato)|Plato]]'s Cratylus raised a basic problem: if names are arbitrary conventions, how can language give knowledge of things? The Sophists treated language as a tool of persuasion, while Plato sometimes suggested that words must be naturally connected with what they name.

Conventionalism about names threatens to become conventionalism about truth. If tiger names a class only by convention, then saying an animal is a tiger may seem to reveal nothing about reality. This worry explains traditions that give names sacred power and ideals of plain, self-interpreting language.

Linguistic relativity later reworked the problem. In the Sapir-Whorf view, people live partly within the conceptual world made by their language. A weak version says language influences thought and makes translation difficult; a strong version says language determines thought so deeply that common conceptual schemes disappear.

Donald Davidson opposed strong relativism. Interpretation requires treating speakers as mostly right about many things and sharing a conceptual scheme with them. If no common scheme were possible, one could not treat others as thinking at all.

## Frege And Russell

Gottlob Frege helped create modern philosophy of language by placing sentence meaning before word meaning and distinguishing sense from reference. His work on mathematical language showed that philosophical progress could come through analysis of how thoughts are expressed.

Bertrand Russell developed the theory of descriptions. A phrase such as the present king of France need not name anything to be meaningful. Russell analyzed it as a quantified claim: there is exactly one present king of France, and that individual is bald. The sentence is false, not meaningless.

The linguistic turn grew from Frege and Russell. Instead of asking directly about substance, time, mind, or number, philosophers increasingly studied the language in which such claims are made. Analysis of language became a general philosophical method.

## Wittgenstein's Tractatus

Ludwig Wittgenstein's Tractatus treated sentences as pictures of states of affairs. Elementary propositions shared logical form with what they represented, while complex propositions were built by logical operations such as conjunction and negation.

Logical form was, for early Wittgenstein, shown rather than said. Logic was tautological, making explicit what was already contained in propositions. Ethical, theological, and metaphysical sentences could not picture facts and were therefore called senseless, a verdict that also seemed to threaten the Tractatus itself.

## Later Wittgenstein

Language-games replaced the earlier picture theory in Wittgenstein's later work. Meaning is not fixed by hidden mental ideas or strict definitions but by use within shared human practices. Words such as game work through family resemblance rather than one essence common to every case.

Rule following depends on communal practice, not private mental objects. Wittgenstein rejected the idea that understanding red requires comparing objects with a private image. People learn to use words by participating in public forms of life.

The private-language argument challenged the idea that purely private sensations could be objects of language. If only one person could check a term's use, there would be no difference between using it correctly and merely seeming to do so. Meaning therefore requires public criteria.

## Quine

W.V.O. Quine rejected two dogmas of empiricism. First, he denied a sharp division between logic and empirical science. Second, he denied that each empirical sentence has a fixed set of experiences that would confirm or disconfirm it.

Quine's web of belief treats science as an interconnected structure. A recalcitrant experience does not refute one sentence in isolation; any belief may be preserved if enough adjustments are made elsewhere. Similar attacks on immediacy appeared in Wilfrid Sellars's rejection of the myth of the given and Jacques Derrida's critique of presence.

## Chomsky

Noam Chomsky changed the field through Syntactic Structures in 1957. He argued that language users master an infinite range of grammatical sentences despite hearing only limited, imperfect examples. This poverty of the stimulus suggested that much linguistic knowledge is innate.

Generative grammar encouraged philosophers to treat natural languages as rule-governed systems. If speakers can recognize both grammaticality and meaningfulness across indefinitely many sentences, semantics also needs systematic rules. This helped launch new formal studies, including possible-world semantics.

## Implicatures And Figurative Language

H.P. Grice explained how speakers communicate more than literal sentence meaning. Conversational implicatures arise because hearers assume rational cooperation. A sentence such as she got married and raised a family often implies temporal order, though the literal meaning does not require it.

Flouting maxims can create meaning. A recommendation letter that mentions only punctuality and penmanship may imply that the applicant is not qualified because the writer gives too little relevant information.

Metaphor and figurative language raise further problems because their force is not simply literal meaning. Friedrich Nietzsche treated literal truths as worn-out metaphors, while later philosophers connected metaphor with seeing one thing in terms of another. Poetry, moral language, and expressive speech often matter more for practical effect than representation.

## Key Events & Developments

- 428/427-348/347 BCE: [[Plato and Platonism (Plato)|Plato]] framed early problems of naming in Cratylus.
- 1905: Russell's theory of descriptions clarified nonreferring phrases.
- 1921: Wittgenstein's Tractatus presented the picture theory.
- 1951: Quine's Two Dogmas of Empiricism attacked analytic empiricism.
- 1953: Wittgenstein's Philosophical Investigations developed meaning as use.
- 1957: Chomsky's Syntactic Structures launched the cognitive revolution in linguistics.
