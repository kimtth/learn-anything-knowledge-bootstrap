# Domestic Cats

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> Domestic cats are small carnivorous mammals domesticated from wildcat ancestors. Their anatomy, hunting behavior, reproduction, and relationship with humans make them a familiar companion species.

## Introduction

Domestic cats are the smallest members of the cat family, but they still carry the clear form of a hunter: a flexible body, a balanced tail, a shaped head, and sharp teeth and claws that suit an active predatory life. Like other felids, they are built for speed, coordination, and close control of movement, and their bodies show how deeply the carnivore pattern shapes every part of them.

The domestic cat seems to have begun its close relationship with humans very early, probably around the beginnings of agriculture in the Middle East. One important sign is a cat skeleton found with a human burial in southern Cyprus, which suggests that cats were already being kept or tolerated there thousands of years ago. A later find in China shows cats feeding near grain-eating animals in farming settlements, which fits the idea that people welcomed cats because they hunted mice and other pests that threatened stored food.

## General features and special adaptations

The ordinary house cat is usually a small animal, though weight can vary a great deal, especially among nonpedigreed cats. Its digestive system is short, which fits a flesh-eating diet, and its skin and hair follicles help it react quickly to injury, infection, or danger. When a cat feels threatened, the body can make it bristle, arch its back, and hiss, so a small animal can look much larger and more dangerous than it really is.

## Teeth

Cat teeth are designed for killing and cutting rather than chewing. The canines stab and hold prey, while the molars cut flesh into pieces, and the cat has no flat grinding teeth that would let it chew food in the way humans do. Because of this, the mouth and jaw are built around tearing rather than crushing, and the animal swallows food in pieces instead of working it into a paste.

## Claws

The cat’s claws are protected by a special retracting mechanism. When the claws are not being used, the end bone of the toe pivots so that the claw is drawn back out of the way. When the claws are extended, the foot spreads wider and becomes much more dangerous, turning the paw into a strong weapon for catching prey, climbing, and defending itself efficiently.

## Senses

Cats are mostly nocturnal, and their eyes are adapted for low light. A special layer in the retina makes the eyes more sensitive, which also explains why they can seem to shine at night when light falls on them. Their pupils can become narrow slits or open wide, depending on brightness, and their vision does not distinguish colours clearly. The third eyelid, or haw, is another useful feature and is often taken as a sign of the cat’s general health.

## Behaviour

Cats use many signals to show mood and intention. Facial expressions, body posture, tail position, and vocal sounds all help them manage distance from other animals and from people. Rubbing the head, lips, chin, or tail against furniture or a person is a familiar social act because those body parts contain scent glands that help create a stable and recognizable smell in the cat’s surroundings.

## Behavioral problems

Domestication can create stress in cats when the home environment changes too quickly or when their needs are not met. Some behaviour that owners dislike is not truly abnormal, but it can still cause serious trouble in the household. The most common such problem is urinating or defecating outside the litter box, which can come from both physical illness and emotional insecurity. Causes may include bladder disease, constipation, blocked glands, or the arrival of a new family member in the home.

## Nutrition

Domestic cats should eat in a way that matches their natural role as meat eaters. Their teeth, stomach, and short intestine all point toward a diet based on flesh, and they do best when they receive nutrients from prey-like food rather than from heavy starches. Cats may sometimes eat grass or small amounts of plant material, but this is only a minor part of their diet and may help with digestion or hair balls.

## Reproduction

Cats reach sexual maturity relatively early, usually between seven and twelve months. Females can come into heat several times a year, and during these periods they call loudly and repeatedly. Pregnancy lasts a little over two months, and birth normally happens fairly quickly. Kittens are born in a litter, usually with several young at once, and each kitten begins life in its own amniotic sac before being cleaned by the mother.

## Genetics

Cats have been bred less aggressively than dogs, and that has limited the amount of scientific knowledge about inheritance in them. Their matings are also hard to control unless the animals are fully confined. Because of this, the genetic differences between breeds are smaller than those seen in dogs, and cat breeds mostly differ in coat colour and coat texture rather than in dramatic body shape.

## Colour

The most common coat colours include blotched tabby, black, and orange. Orange is tied to a gene that produces creams, yellows, gingers, and reds, while solid white is dominant over the other colours. Tortoiseshell patterns appear when orange and nonorange genes combine in a sex-linked way, which is why male tortoiseshell cats are rare and usually sterile. Tortoiseshell-and-white cats are also almost always female, and this makes colour a useful window into cat genetics.

## Breeds

The number of recognized breeds grew sharply after the 1950s as cats became more popular as companion animals. Even so, most show cats fall into two broad families of type: the long-haired Persian and the domestic shorthair. Many subcategories are based on coat colour, and several well-known breeds are then described within that wider pattern, including Siamese, Himalayan, Abyssinian, Burmese, Manx, rex, and Russian blue.
