# Electromagnetic Radiation

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> Electromagnetic radiation is energy propagated by coupled electric and magnetic fields. Radio waves, visible light, X rays, and gamma rays differ in wavelength and frequency but are manifestations of the same physical phenomenon.

## Introduction

Electromagnetic radiation ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electromagnetic_radiation)) is described in two connected ways. In classical theory, it is energy moving through free space or through matter at the universal speed of light. This energy travels as linked electric and magnetic fields. The two fields change with time, stand at right angles to each other, and are also at right angles to the direction in which the wave moves. Radio waves, visible light, X rays, and gamma rays are all examples of the same basic phenomenon, even though their frequencies, wavelengths, energies, sources, effects, and uses are very different.

## General considerations

### Occurrence and importance

About 0.01 percent of the mass-energy of the universe occurs as electromagnetic radiation. Human life is surrounded by it, and modern communication, medicine, astronomy, lighting, heating, navigation, and sensing depend on one or another part of its spectrum. Life on Earth depends especially on radiation from the Sun. Solar radiation is transformed by photosynthesis into plant matter and by biosynthesis into zooplankton, which forms a basic step in the ocean food chain.

### The electromagnetic spectrum

The electromagnetic spectrum ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electromagnetic_spectrum)) is the full range of electromagnetic frequencies, from very low radio frequencies to the very high frequencies of gamma rays. The range is enormous. Moving from radio waves to visible light increases frequency by a factor comparable to a million billion, and moving from visible light to gamma rays adds another similarly huge increase. The same spectrum can be described in classical terms by frequency and wavelength, or in quantum terms by photon energy.

### Generation of electromagnetic radiation

Electromagnetic radiation ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electromagnetic_radiation)) is produced whenever a charged particle, such as an electron, is accelerated or decelerated. The emitted energy comes from the charged particle, so the particle loses energy when it radiates. A radio antenna is a simple example. Charge in the transmitting antenna oscillates at a chosen frequency, producing an electromagnetic wave. A receiving antenna tuned to the same frequency can absorb the wave and turn it back into oscillating charge motion.

### Two categories of radiation generation

Radiation generation divides into two broad kinds. Some systems produce a broad continuous range of frequencies. Warm substances, including the Sun and heated metals, are examples. Other systems emit and absorb only discrete frequencies that are characteristic of particular atoms, molecules, or nuclei. A radio transmitter tuned to one frequency is a man-made example of a discrete-frequency source.

### Continuous spectra of electromagnetic radiation

Continuous spectra are emitted by warm substances because heat is the irregular motion of electrons, atoms, and molecules. At higher temperature, this motion becomes faster. Since electrons are much lighter than atoms, their irregular motion easily creates irregular oscillating charge motion. Each small oscillation can be imagined as a tiny antenna that emits and absorbs radiation at its own frequency.

### Discrete-frequency sources and absorbers of electromagnetic

Discrete-frequency radiation appears in familiar colours from gas-filled lamps, dyes, pigments, sodium lamps, mercury lamps, and lasers. Its usual sources are atoms and molecules. Each atom or molecule has only certain internal energy states, often called quantum states. It can change internal energy only by specific amounts. When it drops from a higher state to a lower state, it emits a photon whose energy equals the difference between the two states. It absorbs a photon only when the photon energy matches an allowed difference.

### Properties and behaviour

The properties and behaviour of electromagnetic radiation follow from its interaction with charged particles and from the way waves add together. Radiation can scatter, reflect, refract, interfere, propagate in coherent or incoherent trains, change apparent frequency through the Doppler effect, and be absorbed by atmospheres and materials in selective frequency ranges. These behaviours connect the general theory to practical effects such as blue sky, red sunsets, lenses, radio reception, greenhouse warming, and gravitational lensing.

### Scattering, reflection, and refraction

When a charged particle meets an electromagnetic wave, the electric field pushes the charge back and forth at the wave frequency. The moving charge then becomes a source of secondary radiation at the same frequency. This process is scattering. The energy used to move the charge and emit the secondary wave comes from the original wave. The energy radiated by the oscillator rises very rapidly with frequency, so higher-frequency light scatters much more strongly than lower-frequency light.

### Superposition and interference

Superposition means that two electromagnetic waves of the same frequency can occupy the same place and that their electric and magnetic fields add. If the fields point in the same direction at the same time, the resulting field is stronger. Because intensity depends on the square of field strength, two equal in-phase waves can give four times the intensity of either wave alone. This is constructive interference.

### Propagation and coherence

Once generated, an electromagnetic wave is self-propagating because a changing electric field creates a changing magnetic field and a changing magnetic field creates a changing electric field. If an antenna transmits for eight minutes, the beginning of the wave train reaches the Sun just as the antenna is switched off, because light takes a little more than eight minutes to travel from Earth to the Sun. The long wave train then continues beyond the Sun at the speed of light.

### Speed of electromagnetic radiation and the Doppler effect

In free space, electromagnetic radiation always travels at the universal speed of light, no matter how the observer or the evidence is moving. This fact is unlike ordinary motion. A person chasing an airplane or moving toward it measures different relative speeds, but a moving observer still measures light in free space at the same speed. This was experimentally verified by Michelson and Morley in 1887 and became a basic axiom of Einstein's relativity.

### The greenhouse effect of the atmosphere

The greenhouse effect ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greenhouse_effect)) depends on selective absorption and emission by atmospheric substances. The atmosphere lets much visible sunlight reach and warm Earth's surface, but it blocks a large part of the infrared radiation emitted by the warmed surface. The atmosphere becomes thinner with altitude, so absorption is weaker high above Earth. Different frequency ranges are blocked by different processes: the ionosphere affects low frequencies, molecular vibrations and rotations absorb infrared radiation, and electronic excitations absorb ultraviolet and X-ray radiation.

## Radio waves

Radio waves ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radio_wave)) are used for wireless sound, information transmission, communication, maritime navigation, and aircraft navigation. Information is carried by changing the electromagnetic carrier wave through amplitude modulation, frequency modulation, or digital pulse modulation. A real transmission uses a band of frequencies rather than one single frequency. The width of this band depends on how much information is being sent, from about 10,000 Hz for telephone sound to about five megahertz for high-definition television.

## Microwaves

Microwaves extend from 1,000 to 300,000 MHz, corresponding to wavelengths from about 30 centimetres to one millimetre. Hertz produced and studied them in 1886, but practical use required later devices such as the klystron and magnetron. Microwaves carry high-speed telegraphic data between Earth stations and between ground stations, satellites, and space probes. Synchronous satellites about 36,000 kilometres above Earth support international broadband communication, including television, telephone, and FAX.

## Infrared radiation

Infrared radiation lies beyond the red end of visible light and above radar and microwave frequencies. the evidence gives its range as about 10^12 to 5 x 10^14 Hz, or wavelengths from 0.1 to 7.5 x 10^-5 centimetre. William Herschel discovered it while studying sunlight with a prism and a thermometer, finding heating beyond the red part of the visible spectrum. Infrared radiation is strongly associated with heat because warm objects emit it and many molecular vibrations absorb it.

## Visible radiation

Visible radiation is the narrow part of the spectrum that human eyes detect. Violet light has only about twice the frequency of red light, and visible wavelengths run roughly from 7 x 10^-5 centimetre for red to 4 x 10^-5 centimetre for violet. A photon near the centre of the visible spectrum, yellow light, has an energy of about 2.2 eV. This is about a million times the energy of a television-wave photon and about a billion times the energy of a typical radio-wave photon.

## Ultraviolet radiation

Ultraviolet radiation lies between visible light and X rays. Johann Wilhelm Ritter discovered it in 1801 after learning of Herschel's infrared discovery. Ritter looked beyond the violet end of sunlight's visible spectrum and found invisible rays that darkened silver chloride more effectively than visible light. Sources include the Sun, hot objects, synchrotron radiation sources, mercury or xenon arc lamps, and gas discharge tubes containing atoms with energy levels matched to ultraviolet photons.

## X rays

X rays were discovered accidentally in 1895 by Wilhelm Conrad Rontgen while he was studying cathode rays in a low-pressure gas discharge tube. He noticed fluorescence from a barium compound even when the evidence was covered, showing that a penetrating radiation was present. Later, J.J. Thomson identified cathode rays as electrons. X rays were soon connected with electromagnetic radiation, and their wavelengths were placed around 10^-8 to 10^-11 centimetre, corresponding to photon energies from about 200 to 100,000 eV.

## Gamma rays

Gamma rays ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gamma_ray)) are the most penetrating radiation discussed in . Six years after Henri Becquerel discovered radioactivity in 1896, Ernest Rutherford identified alpha, beta, and gamma rays in radioactive decay. Alpha particles were later identified as helium nuclei, beta rays as electrons, and in 1912 gamma rays were shown to have the properties of very energetic electromagnetic radiation. Gamma-ray photons from radioactive nuclei can be 10,000 to 10,000,000 times more energetic than visible photons.

## Historical survey

The historical survey follows the development from early ideas about light and action at a distance to classical electromagnetic theory and then to quantum theory. It presents classical radiation theory as one of the great achievements of physics because it unified electricity, magnetism, and light. It then shows why that classical theory, though powerful, could not explain blackbody radiation, the photoelectric effect, Compton scattering, and the dual wave-particle behaviour of radiation.

Max Planck, speaking in 1931 on the 100th anniversary of James Clerk Maxwell's birth, called classical electromagnetic radiation theory a major triumph of human thought. The theory was important because it joined electricity, magnetism, and light in one framework and became a foundation for later mechanics and quantum theory. It developed from older problems about whether forces could act across empty space and whether light needed a material ether to travel.

### Relation between electricity and magnetism

As early as 1760, Leonhard Euler suggested that the same ether carrying light might also be responsible for electrical phenomena. Electricity developed more slowly than mechanics and optics, while magnetism had advanced through use of the magnetic compass. By the end of the 18th century, electrostatics was well known and Poisson had prepared a mathematical description of charges at rest, but electricity and magnetism still seemed separate except for their inverse-square attraction and repulsion laws.

### The electromagnetic wave and field concept

Michael Faraday introduced the field concept and field lines of force outside material bodies. Around a magnet or electric charge, the field describes the force that another small magnet or charge would feel at each point. Iron filings over a magnet can make magnetic field lines visible. This field idea was essential because it replaced vague action across empty space with a physical state assigned to every point in space.

### Speed of light

The speed of light was studied long before modern electromagnetism. In 1676, Ole Romer noticed that eclipses of Jupiter's moon Io occurred later than expected when Earth and Jupiter were farther apart. He explained the delay by the finite travel time of light and estimated that light took about 11 minutes to travel from the Sun to Earth. In 1728, James Bradley used stellar aberration to obtain another astronomical measure of light's speed.

## Development of quantum theory

![[attachments/electromagnetic_radiation_002.webp|420]]

*Spark Museum Electromagnetic Radiation*

The development of quantum theory began when classical wave theory failed to explain several key radiation experiments. Classical theory handled propagation, interference, reflection, refraction, and radio waves very well, but it could not explain the exact spectrum of thermal radiation, why light ejects electrons from metals in frequency-dependent ways, why X rays lose energy in scattering as if photons were particles, or how individual photons can build up interference patterns.

### Blackbody radiation

The quantum story begins with blackbody radiation. Josef Stefan found experimentally in 1879 that the energy radiated per second from a heated surface rises as the fourth power of its absolute temperature. Ludwig Boltzmann derived this law in 1889 for an ideal body that absorbs and emits all frequencies. The resulting Stefan-Boltzmann law explains why a surface at the Sun's temperature of about 6,000 K emits vastly more energy per area than a surface near Earth's temperature of 300 K.

### Photoelectric effect

Heinrich Hertz discovered the photoelectric effect by accident in 1887 while generating and detecting electromagnetic waves with induction coils and spark gaps. When he placed the receiver spark gap in a dark case, the spark became smaller. He found that ultraviolet light from the transmitter spark enhanced the receiver spark. This showed that light could affect electrical arcing. Soon afterward, ultraviolet radiation was found to cause negatively charged particles to leave solid surfaces.

### Compton effect

The Compton effect gave strong evidence for the particle nature of radiation. In 1922, Arthur Holly Compton studied X-ray scattering and found that scattered X rays lost energy and emerged with slightly lower frequency. The energy loss increased with the scattering angle. This behaviour could be explained as an elastic collision between an X-ray photon and an electron at rest, like a collision between two particles.

### Resonance absorption and recoil

Gustav Robert Kirchhoff had observed in the mid-1800s that atoms and molecules emit and absorb radiation at characteristic frequencies, with the same frequencies for emission and absorption in a given substance. In the photon picture, however, recoil creates a problem. When an atom emits a photon, the atom recoils and takes away a small kinetic energy. The emitted photon therefore has slightly less energy than the internal energy difference. When another atom absorbs a photon, it also must recoil, so the photon would need extra energy.

### Waveparticle duality

Waveparticle duality is the paradox that radiation behaves like particles in the photoelectric and Compton effects but like waves in interference and diffraction. Niels Bohr initially rejected light quanta and tried to explain particle-like effects statistically. In 1923 he argued that light quanta seemed to exclude a rational definition of frequency and wavelength, which were needed for interference. The next year, Louis-Victor de Broglie extended the duality to matter itself.

### Double-slit interference

Double-slit interference shows the difficulty of combining waves and photons. A wave front passing through two openings, A and B, produces bright and dark bands on a screen. Dark bands appear where the distances from the two slits differ by odd half-wavelengths, causing destructive interference. Bright bands appear where the distances differ by zero or whole wavelengths, causing constructive interference. This is a wave-front division version of the same interference rule used in Michelson's interferometer.

### Quantum electrodynamics

Quantum electrodynamics, or QED, is the theory that fuses the wave and particle descriptions of electromagnetic radiation. It explains how individual photons can be registered as equal pulses in detectors and still build up a double-slit interference pattern one photon at a time. In QED, photons are particles, but not ordinary billiard-ball particles. Their behaviour and their interactions with charged particles follow quantum rules.

## Core Equations

Electromagnetic radiation links wavelength, frequency, and photon energy:

$$c=\lambda\nu$$

This wave relation connects speed, wavelength, and frequency; for light in vacuum, the speed is fixed, so shorter wavelengths have higher frequencies.

$$E=h\nu$$

This relation says that photon energy is proportional to frequency; higher-frequency radiation carries more energy per photon.

## Interesting Facts

- Visible light is only a tiny part of the electromagnetic spectrum, yet it is the range to which human eyes, water transparency, and photosynthesis are closely adapted.
- Photon energy rises with frequency, so a yellow visible photon is far more energetic than a television-wave photon and vastly more energetic than a typical radio photon.
- Water is unusually transparent in the visible range but absorbs strongly in infrared and ultraviolet ranges, helping explain why eyes work in visible light.
- Radio waves ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radio_wave)) can be reflected by the ionosphere, which helped make long-range radio possible despite Earth's curvature.
- Microwave radar can measure both distance and speed by using travel time and the Doppler shift of reflected pulses.
- Gamma rays ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gamma_ray)) can create electron-positron pairs when their energy is high enough and they pass near a nucleus.
- Blackbody radiation forced a break with classical physics because classical theory predicted too much high-frequency radiation.
- Double-slit interference still appears when photons pass through the apparatus one at a time, showing why ordinary particle pictures are not enough.
- Gauge invariance connects an abstract phase rule in quantum theory with the concrete conservation of electric charge.
- Renormalization removed infinities from QED calculations by using the observed effective mass and charge of particles rather than unreachable bare values.
