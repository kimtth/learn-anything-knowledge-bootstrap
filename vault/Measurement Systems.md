# Measurement Systems

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> Measurement systems assign stable units and standards to physical quantities such as length, mass, area, volume, temperature, light, pressure, and current. Their history moves from customary units shaped by trade and conquest to planned metric and International System standards used by science and commerce.

## Introduction

![[attachments/measurement_systems_001.webp|420]]

*Egyptian cubit rods*

Measurement systems ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/System_of_units_of_measurement)) connect numbers with physical quantities and events. Older systems focused on mass, length, area, and volume, while later systems added temperature, light, pressure, and electric current. A working system needs uniformity, named units, and stable standards.

Two historical patterns shaped weights and measures ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unit_of_measurement)). Evolutionary systems, such as the British Imperial system, grew from custom, while planned systems, especially the International System of Units ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_System_of_Units)), were designed for science and wider public use. Trade and conquest carried many Middle Eastern units westward into Greek, Roman, Gaulish, and British practice.

## The Egyptians

[[Egypt]] produced one of the most important ancient standards, the royal cubit ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cubit)), developed about 3000 BC. It measured about 524 mm, or 20.62 inches, and was based on the arm from elbow to fingertips. A black granite master cubit checked the cubit sticks used in daily measurement, making Egyptian linear measurement unusually controlled for its time.

The cubit was divided into 28 digits, with palms, hands, spans, and smaller fractions built from those digits. This precision is shown by the Great Pyramid of Giza, whose sides vary only about 0.05 percent from their mean length, suggesting a planned square of 440 by 440 royal cubits.

Egyptian measurement also served land and taxation needs. The annual Nile River flood required benchmarks and surveying so boundaries could be restored after the water fell. The weight system used the kite, deben, and sep, while liquid measures included ro, hin, hekat, khar, and cubic cubit.

## The Babylonians

Babylonia contributed early weights that spread widely through commerce. The mina existed in surviving forms of about 640 grams and 978 grams, and shaped weights include a five-mina duck and a thirty-mina swan. The shekel, later familiar in Hebrew use as both coin and weight, also began in Babylonian practice.

Babylonian length and volume measures were closely developed. The kus, or Babylonian cubit, measured about 530 mm, while the qa held a handbreadth cube of liquid and was linked to one great mina of water. The mina, talent, shekel, qa, and gur influenced Hittites, Assyrians, Phoenicians, and Hebrews.

Greek and Roman systems reshaped older Mediterranean measures. Greeks used the finger, Olympic cubit, metretes, and talent, borrowing from Egyptian, Babylonian, and Phoenician trade. Romans organized length around the foot, digit, inch, palm, cubit, pace, stade, mile, and league, while Roman weights shifted after 268 BC when the silver denarius helped define a new pound.

[[China]] developed separately but shared broad patterns with western metrology. Units used body measures, varied by region and craft, and lacked regular links between length and volume. Shi Huang Di, first emperor in 221 BC, fixed the shi, zhi, and zhang, while decimal notation and pitch-tested grain vessels improved accuracy.

Medieval [[Europe]] inherited Roman forms but multiplied them into local variants shaped by Celtic, Anglo-Saxon, Germanic, Scandinavian, Arabic, and commercial needs. [[Charlemagne]] and later kings tried to impose uniformity, while trade fairs in Champagne helped standardize the ell for wool cloth, yet regional differences continued.

In England, medieval measures survived longer than most European systems. Magna Carta in 1215 demanded common measures for grain and wine, and the Assize of Weights and Measures defined the yard, perch, inch, furlong, and acre. English weight stayed complicated through troy, avoirdupois, apothecaries' weights, stones, wool sacks, and provincial standards.

## British Reform

The Weights and Measures Act of 1824 simplified British practice by defining one imperial gallon as the volume occupied by ten imperial pounds of distilled water under specified conditions. The 1878 act repeated that gallon definition and redefined the yard as the distance between two gold plugs in a bronze bar.

British reform continued into the 20th century. Other units were standardized around the same era, and in 1963 Parliament redefined all English weights and measures in terms of the metric system. A national changeover began two years later, showing how a customary system could be formally tied to metric standards even after centuries of local development.

## The United States Customary System

![[attachments/measurement_systems_002.png|420]]

*Modern metric system chart*

[[George Washington]] asked Congress in 1790 for uniformity in currency, weights, and measures. Decimal currency was adopted, but customary English units remained deeply embedded in American commerce, tools, containers, and public habits. Thomas Jefferson supported a more rational approach, while John Quincy Adams later judged the metric system excellent but politically difficult to adopt.

The [[United States of America (United States)|United States]] kept older English measures while trying to align standards with Britain. The yard standard came from [[London]], and the avoirdupois and troy pounds matched British values, but the American gallon kept Queen Anne's 231 cubic inches and the U.S. bushel retained a Winchester form dating to the 15th century.

Federal standard work expanded in the 19th century and became the National Bureau of Standards in 1901, later the National Institute of Standards and Technology. The bureau built standards, coordinated states, tested materials, and published scientific data, while the Metric Act of 1866 allowed metric use.

The metric system grew from the search for a rational, uniform system during the French Revolution. Gabriel Mouton proposed decimalized, Earth-based measures in 1670. In 1790, Charles-Maurice de Talleyrand pushed the French Academy of Sciences to study reform, leading to a metre based on one ten-millionth of a meridian quadrant.

Jean Delambre and Pierre Mechain measured the meridian from [[Barcelona]] to Dunkirk, a task slowed by war. A provisional metre appeared in 1793, French adoption came in 1795, and the final standard metre was presented in 1799. The system defined the gram, litre, are, hectare, and stere with Greek and Latin prefixes.

Metric adoption advanced through Napoleonic Europe, Latin America, the Soviet Union, China, and the Meiji Restoration in Japan. Britain and the United States moved more slowly, but many scientific, medical, industrial, optical, and photographic uses became metric during the 20th century.

## The U.S. Metric Study

The U.S. Congress authorized a study in 1968 on growing metric use worldwide. The resulting report reached Congress in July 1971 and argued that the United States should move toward the International Metric System.

## A Metric America

D.V. Simone's Metric America, A Decision whose Time has Come was issued as National Bureau of Standards Special Publication 345. It favored national change to the International Metric System, while Parliament in Britain went further with a long-range changeover program.

## The International System of Units

The International System of Units ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_System_of_Units)) arose because older metric standards and specialized scientific subsystems no longer met modern needs. The original 18th-century standards lacked enough accuracy, so the 11th General Conference on Weights and Measures met in [[Paris]] in October 1960 and created SI.

## SI Base Units

SI base units include the metre, kilogram, second, ampere, kelvin, mole, and candela. Since 1983, the metre has been defined by the distance light travels in a vacuum in 1/299,792,458 second. The second is defined by 9,192,631,770 cycles of radiation from a cesium-133 atomic transition.

The kilogram was long tied to a platinum-iridium cylinder kept near [[Paris]], with a duplicate serving the United States. Because the prototype was found in 1989 to differ by 50 micrograms from copies, the CGPM agreed in 2011 to begin redefining it through Planck's constant. Similar 2011 proposals tied the ampere to elementary charge, the kelvin to Boltzmann's constant, and the mole to the Avogadro constant.

Other SI definitions link the ampere to force between parallel wires, the kelvin to the triple point of water, the mole to entities in 12 grams of carbon-12, and the candela to luminous intensity at a specified radiation frequency.

## Key Events & Developments

- 3000 BC: [[Egypt]] developed the royal cubit and checked working cubit sticks against a granite master standard.
- 221 BC: Shi Huang Di unified important Chinese measurement regulations, fixing basic weight and length units.
- 1215: Magna Carta demanded common measures for grain and wine in England.
- 1790-1799: French metric reform moved from academy study to a final standard metre and a new decimal system.
- 1960: The 11th CGPM created the International System of Units ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_System_of_Units)) in Paris.
