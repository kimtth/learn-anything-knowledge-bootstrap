# Manchester

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> Manchester is a northwestern English city whose explosive growth in cotton manufacturing made it central to the Industrial Revolution. Its mills, coal, canals, railways, surrounding towns, and later textile decline reveal the changing geography of industrial urbanization.

## Introduction

![[attachments/manchester_001.webp|420]]

*Manchester skyline in the evening*

Manchester is a city and metropolitan borough in Greater Manchester, northwestern England, mostly in historic Lancashire but extending south of the River Mersey into historic Cheshire. It is the core of northern England's largest metropolitan area and remains a major regional city, though it no longer holds the industrial power that made it central to the Industrial Revolution ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Industrial_Revolution)). Its growth was dramatic: from a market town of 10,000 people in 1717 to a cotton-manufacturing and commercial city of more than 300,000 by 1851.

By the early 20th century, Manchester had linked with surrounding cotton towns such as Bolton, Rochdale, and Oldham, forming a conurbation of 2,350,000 people by 1911. Later growth slowed as textiles declined through foreign competition and technological obsolescence. The city lies on the Manchester embayment, a plain of river gravels and glacial drift, with coal measures below and Pennine and Rossendale uplands nearby. Industrial zones, suburbs, old mill towns, river valleys, rail lines, and former canals shaped the metropolitan pattern.

## Climate

Manchester's climate is mild, moist, and misty, with mild winters, cool summers, frequent rain, and heavy cloud. Rainfall of about 32 inches falls on at least half the days in an average year. Industrial pollution once reduced sunshine, produced serious fogs, and worsened bronchitis and tuberculosis, but air-pollution control raised annual sunshine to about 1,300 hours and reduced serious fogs to about 20 days.

The city center is also an architectural record of 19th-century wealth. Banks, warehouses, public buildings, the Ship Canal Company offices, and Alfred Waterhouse's Town Hall show styles from Greek classical to Victorian Gothic. Adaptive reuse turned the Royal Exchange into a theatre, Central Station into an exhibition center, and Castlefield, including the world's oldest railway station, into a science and industry museum. Later office towers and the Arndale Centre show modern redevelopment and retail change.

Greater Manchester is compact and crowded, and the old core lost many young families to suburbs, overspill estates, and more prosperous regions. From 1961 to 1981, Manchester city lost nearly one-third of its population through migration. High-rise housing experiments often failed, and planning shifted toward conserving and improving old housing. Commonwealth immigration, especially from the [[West Indies]] and the Indian subcontinent, created multiracial communities, notably in Moss Side and textile towns.

## Industry

Manchester's economy long contrasted the diversified core with textile-dependent towns on the northern and eastern margins. By the late 20th century, textiles had shrunk to a remnant, clothing had declined, and engineering contracted sharply. Chemical growth concentrated in fine chemicals and pharmaceuticals, while paper and printing remained stable because Manchester was England's second newspaper-production center after Greater London.

The wider economy shifted toward services, especially professions, finance, banking, administration, and personal services. This increased demand for educated workers while reducing manual jobs, creating persistent unemployment in disadvantaged inner districts. Some factories moved to the urban fringe or into empty cotton mills, making former textile towns new spaces for small and struggling firms.

Manchester remains a regional trade and transport center, with a Bank of England branch, the Northern Stock Exchange, the Co-operative Wholesale Society headquarters, and a major crown court. Ringway Airport, 10 miles south, became the leading British international terminal outside London and the second airfreight terminal. From 1894 to 1986, Manchester was a seaport through the Manchester Ship Canal ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manchester_Ship_Canal)), though larger ships and changing trade later closed the upper canal to traffic.

## Government

Greater Manchester is a cohesive metropolitan unit, but government has often been fragmented among boroughs. The 1972 Local Government Act created the metropolitan county of Greater Manchester from 1974, with the county handling strategic services and boroughs managing education, housing, and many local services. In 1986, the metropolitan county lost administrative powers, leaving Manchester and other boroughs as effective unitary authorities.

Education and welfare are major civic strengths. Victoria University of Manchester began in 1851, gained a charter in 1872, became part of a federal university in 1880, and separated again in 1903. Together with the Institute of Science and Technology, the University of Salford, and a large polytechnic, it made Manchester a higher-education center. Its medical school and teaching hospitals, including Christie Hospital, support specialist treatment and cancer research.

Manchester's cultural life includes the Lowry, opened in 2000, the Halle orchestra, municipal and university libraries, Chetham's Library, the Whitworth Art Gallery, Manchester City Art Gallery, Manchester Museum, the Museum of Science and Industry, football clubs, Old Trafford cricket, and a strong pop scene linked to Factory Records, Joy Division, the Smiths, and Oasis.

## History

The Roman fort Mamucium ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mamucium)) was established around AD 78-86 near the Rivers Medlock and Irwell, but the site later lay empty for centuries. In 919, Edward the Elder repaired the Roman site against Norsemen, while the later town grew near the Irk and Irwell. Manchester became a borough before 1301, gained importance after a 1421 college of priests, and developed schools and institutions such as Chetham's Hospital and Manchester Grammar School.

By the 16th century, Manchester was an important wool-market borough, and by 1620 fustian weaving began the cotton industry that transformed southern Lancashire after 1770. Canals brought coal and linked Manchester to Liverpool by 1776, the first cotton mill appeared in the early 1780s, and the Liverpool and Manchester Railway opened in 1830. Local government lagged until incorporation in 1838.

In the later 19th century, Manchester became the commercial and financial center of the cotton trade, expanded engineering, opened the Manchester Ship Canal ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manchester_Ship_Canal)) in 1894, and developed Trafford Park, Britain's first and largest industrial estate. It also led in politics, journalism, music, education, reform, and economic thought. The Peterloo Massacre of 1819, Friedrich Engels's observations in the 1840s, John Dalton's atomic theory, and the Manchester school of economics all show its wider influence. By the late 20th century, redevelopment and a broader economy were replacing the old textile base.

## Interesting Facts
