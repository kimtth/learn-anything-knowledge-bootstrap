## Hungarian Magyarorszag

Hungary, or Magyarorszag, is a landlocked country of central [[Europe]] with [[Budapest]] as its capital. Its modern identity is deeply shaped by the 1920 Treaty of Trianon ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Treaty_of_Trianon)), which removed about 71 percent of historic Hungarian territory after [[World Wars (World War I)|World War I]]. The resulting Trianon Syndrome ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trianon_syndrome)) remained a strong public memory before 1945, was suppressed during Soviet domination, and reappeared after independence in 1990.

Hungarians are linguistically unusual in Europe because Hungarian ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hungarian_language)) is not related to any major European language. This isolation helped strengthen attachment to Latin ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin)), which served culture, scholarship, administration, and noble public life until 1844. Hungary also claims long political continuity: an independent state from about 896 to 1526, partial identity under Ottoman and Habsburg rule, and partnership in Austria-Hungary from 1867 to 1918.

[[Budapest]], on both banks of the Danube, has been a central anchor of Hungarian culture. Despite repeated national disasters, Hungarians have remained proud of achievements in science, scholarship, music, literature, and the arts. In the 20th century, many Hungarian scientists and artists emigrated, especially to the [[United States of America (United States)|United States]], where they contributed to atomic research, computing, economics, music, and other fields.

## Relief

Hungary lies roughly between 45 and 49 degrees north and 16 and 23 degrees east, bordered by [[Czech and Slovak Republics (Slovakia)|Slovakia]], [[Ukraine]], Romania, Serbia, Croatia, Slovenia, and Austria. Its relief is dominated by lowlands: the Little Alfold in the northwest and the much larger Great Alfold across central and southeastern Hungary.

A low mountain backbone extends about 250 miles, or 400 km, from southwest to northeast, separating plains and basins. Transdanubia includes the Bakony Mountains and uplands around Lake Balaton, while the Northern Mountains include volcanic peaks such as Mount Kekes, Hungary's highest point at 3,327 feet, or 1,014 metres.

Water strongly shapes the land. The Danube and Tisza are the main rivers, and Lake Balaton, at about 231 square miles, is central Europe's largest freshwater lake. Floodplains, alluvial fans, sand dunes, loess, and buried alluvial plains form much of the lowland surface. Long levee systems protect large areas from floods, while lakes, wetlands, and national parks support tourism and conservation.

## Climate

Hungary has a moderately dry continental climate ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continental_climate)) because it lies inside the Carpathian Basin. The mean annual temperature is about 50 F, or 10 C. January averages range from 25 to 32 F, and July averages from 64 to 73 F, with extremes from 109 F in summer to -29 F in winter.

Rainfall is moderate and uneven. Lowlands usually receive 20 to 24 inches of precipitation, while higher areas receive 24 to 31 inches. The central and eastern Great Alfold are the driest regions, and southwestern uplands are the wettest. Human activity has greatly reduced natural vegetation; about half the land is regularly cultivated, with beech in the highest woodland zones and oak-scrub communities lower down.

Hungary's animal life includes deer and wild pigs in upland forests, and rodents, hares, partridge, and pheasant in the lowlands. Marsh birds once common in wetlands survive mainly in reserves. Freshwater fish include pike, bream, and pike perch. Industrial regions have suffered from significant water and air pollution.

## People

Since [[World Wars (World War I)|World War I]], Hungary has become ethnically more homogeneous, and more than nine-tenths of the population is ethnically Hungarian. The Hungarian language belongs to the Ugric branch of the Uralic family; it is most closely related to Khanty and Mansi, and more distantly to Finnish, Estonian, Sami, and Samoyedic languages.

Ethnic Hungarians are descended from Finno-Ugric Magyars and assimilated Turkic, Slavic, and Germanic groups. The largest minority is the Roma. Other minorities include Germans, Slovaks, Croats, Romanians, Serbs, Poles, Slovenians, Rusyns, Greeks, and Armenians. Significant Hungarian communities also live in neighbouring countries because the 1920 frontiers left many Magyar speakers outside the state.

## Religion

Hungary has no official religion and guarantees religious freedom. More than half the people are Roman Catholic, especially in western and northern areas; about one-fifth are Calvinist, concentrated in eastern Hungary; Lutherans form another significant minority. Smaller Christian groups include Greek Catholic, Eastern Orthodox, and Unitarian communities.

The Jewish community, about 5 percent of the population before [[World Wars (World War II)|World War II]], was devastated by the [[Holocaust]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Holocaust)). Under communism from 1949, the state was officially atheistic and restricted churches, schools, and property. After 1990, more than 200 religious groups were officially registered, though nominal membership does not always mean active belief.

## Settlement Patterns

Hungary's settlement regions reflect both geography and history. The Great Alfold includes Kiskunsag between the Danube and Tisza and Transtisza east of the Tisza. Kecskemet is a market centre; Debrecen is the largest city of the plain; and Hortobagy preserves steppe traditions, native cattle, horses, and sheep as national heritage.

The Little Alfold in the northwest is more favourably endowed and has stronger agriculture and industry, with Gyor as its main city. Transdanubia west of the Danube includes Lake Balaton, Pecs, Veszprem, health resorts, wine areas, and the Bakony uplands. The Northern Mountains include industrial basins, Tokaj and Eger vineyards, recreation centres, and Miskolc.

Nearly two-thirds of the population is urban, and about one-third of urban dwellers live in the [[Budapest]] metropolitan area. Provincial centres such as Debrecen, Miskolc, Szeged, Pecs, and Gyor have broad regional functions. Rural settlement differs sharply: the Great Alfold has fewer but larger villages and isolated farmsteads called tanyak, while Transdanubia and the Northern Mountains have many small nucleated and linear villages.

Population growth peaked in the late 1970s and early 1980s, followed by negative natural increase. Modernization lowered birth rates as urban habits spread into the countryside. Emigration also mattered: before [[World Wars (World War I)|World War I]] about 1.3 million people left overseas, and after communism and the breakup of Yugoslavia, about 100,000 refugees came from Romania and former Yugoslav lands.

## Economy

Before [[World Wars (World War II)|World War II]], Hungary was mostly agrarian. After 1948, Soviet-style forced industrialization created a planned economy, heavy industry, low wages, expensive consumer goods, and many new jobs in industry and services. Agriculture's share of employment fell from more than half to about one-eighth by the 1990s, while industry reached nearly one-third of the active population by the late 1980s.

The New Economic Mechanism of 1968 introduced market-style reforms and allowed private businesses. By the late 1980s, private activity produced about one-third of GDP, nearly three-fifths of services, and more than three-fourths of construction. After 1989, the transition to a market economy caused GDP, industrial output, and employment to fall sharply, but later foreign investment, privatization, banking reform, and trade integration helped reshape the economy.

Agriculture ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agriculture)) remains important even though it accounted for only about 4 percent of GDP by 2005. Hungary is nearly self-sufficient in food. Major crops include wheat, corn, sugar beets, potatoes, sunflower seeds, apples, grapes, and plums, and viticulture is important in the Northern Mountains. After collectivization, private plots were highly productive; after 1990, land was reprivatized and private farms became the norm.

Resources include fertile soil, abundant water, modest fossil fuels, bauxite, manganese, and other minerals. Communist industrialization favoured mining, metallurgy, chemicals, engineering, and electric power, but much of this industry lacked modern technology after state socialism collapsed. Food, tobacco, wood, paper, chemicals, telecommunications equipment, and automobiles adapted more successfully during restructuring, often with foreign investment.

Finance changed from a Soviet single-tier system to a two-tier banking system beginning in 1987. A stock exchange opened in 1990, private and partly foreign banks expanded, and the forint became convertible for business. Trade shifted away from Comecon, where exchanges had used fixed prices and barter, toward market economies. [[Germany]] became the leading partner; Hungary joined the OECD in 1996 and the European Union in 2004.

By the early 2000s, services supplied almost two-thirds of GDP and employment. Tourism grew, drawing visitors from neighbouring countries, western Europe, North America, and Australia. Railways had long been central, but market transition cut freight traffic and subsidies. Roads, Danube shipping, Budapest air links, telecommunications, mobile phones, computers, and Internet access became increasingly important.

## Government and Society

Between 1867 and 1948, Hungary had a functioning parliament, parties, and a relatively independent judiciary, though autocratic elements persisted. After the communist takeover in 1948, rival parties were abolished or absorbed, and the Hungarian Workers' Party controlled the state. In 1989, constitutional revision introduced multiparty representative democracy, separated powers, free elections, a Constitutional Court, and ombudsman protections.

The postcommunist system has a unicameral National Assembly, a president with limited authority, a prime minister responsible for government, and local government through 19 counties, Budapest, cities, towns, and villages. A controversial new constitution, advanced by Viktor Orban's centre-right government, came into force in 2012 and changed the constitutional language and institutional balance.

Justice is administered through the Supreme Court, county courts, local courts, prosecutors, and the Constitutional Court that began work in 1990. Elections are held every four years for citizens age 18 and older, using a mixed direct and proportional system. After 1990, long-term parties included the Hungarian Democratic Forum, Smallholders, Free Democrats, Christian Democrats, Fidesz, and the Hungarian Socialist Party.

Security changed after the Cold War. The armed forces fell from about 155,000 members in 1989 to just under 60,000 in 1999, while modernization prepared Hungary for NATO, which it joined in 1999. Compulsory male military service ended in 2004.

Health care expanded under state socialism, and free health care was guaranteed by the 1970s. Welfare was broad but costly: social insurance spending rose from 4 percent of GDP in 1950 to about one-fifth by 1990. Housing shortages persisted despite one million urban state housing units built between 1956 and 1985, and the postcommunist housing market became sharply polarized.

Education began to follow a German model after the 1868 law on universal education. After 1948, nationalized Soviet-style mass education used eight years of general school and four years of secondary education. After 1990, private, religious, and alternative gymnasium structures returned. Higher education was reorganized around integrated universities, private and religious institutions, American-style doctorates, and revived habilitation.

## Cultural Life

Hungarian culture combines peasant traditions, German and Jewish urban culture, coffeehouse intellectual life, and Roma music. Since the early 19th century, culture has been political because it helped build national consciousness. Theatre, opera, and literature made writers and poets into national figures, while governments used subsidy, regulation, and, under state socialism, ideological control.

Traditional rural dress, food, songs, dances, weddings, Easter, and Christmas rituals survived into the 20th century but were badly weakened by modernization. They lived on as folk art and tourist display. Cuisine features goulash, bean soup, beef stew, paprika, fish soup, lecsó, pálinka, wine, and beer. Important holidays include March 15, August 20, and October 23, commemorating revolution, St. Stephen, and the 1956 uprising.

Literature and language reform were central to national life. Ferenc Kazinczy helped create modern literary Hungarian, and Ferenc Kolcsey's Hymnusz of 1823, later set by Ferenc Erkel, became the national anthem. Major literary figures include Sandor Petofi, Janos Arany, Endre Ady, Attila Jozsef, Miklos Radnoti, Gyorgy Konrad, Peter Nadas, Peter Esterhazy, and Imre Kertesz, who won the Nobel Prize in 2002.

Music, visual arts, cinema, scholarship, and sports also became internationally important. Bela Bartok, Zoltan Kodaly, Franz Liszt, Erno Dohnanyi, and Georg Solti are central musical names. Tivadar Csontvary-Kosztka and Laszlo Moholy-Nagy gave painting and design broader modern importance. Hungarian scientists and scholars such as Theodore von Karman, Leo Szilard, Edward Teller, John von Neumann, Eugene Wigner, Albert Szent-Gyorgyi, and Paul Erdos contributed far beyond Hungary.

Press, publishing, and media changed sharply after communism. The first Hungarian-language newspaper, Magyar Hirmondo, appeared in 1780. Under state socialism, censorship, state publishing, and public libraries expanded in controlled forms. In the late 1980s censorship relaxed and then ended, private publishers multiplied, print runs fell, smaller libraries closed, and electronic media, cable and satellite television, personal computers, and the Internet became central to leisure.

Hungary's earliest history begins with Roman Pannonia and Dacia, later Germanic, Slavic, Hun, Bulgar, and Avar movements, and the collapse of Avar power around 800. The standard view dates Magyar occupation of the middle Danube basin to the late 9th century, though Gyula Laszlo's double-conquest theory places an earlier Magyar-related arrival in 670.

The Magyars, pushed west by the Pechenegs and led by Arpad, crossed the Carpathians probably in 895. They destroyed Moravia in 906, occupied Pannonia in 907, and raided widely until defeat at Lechfeld in 955. Geza turned toward Western Christianity, and his son Stephen I, married to Gisella, was crowned king around 1000, founding a Christian kingdom of counties and bishoprics.

The Arpad monarchy built institutions but suffered dynastic conflict. Ladislas I and Coloman consolidated authority and brought Croatia into union with Hungary. Bela III strengthened royal income and French courtly influences. Andrew II's extravagance led to the 1222 Golden Bull, which confirmed noble rights and legalized resistance to unlawful royal commands.

The Mongol invasion of 1241-42 devastated Hungary and destroyed much of its population. Bela IV rebuilt with castles, settlers, and stronger defenses. The Arpad line ended in 1301. Under Angevin kings, especially Charles Robert and Louis I, order, gold mining, commerce, and foreign influence grew, but later foreign dynasties and Ottoman pressure complicated politics.

Sigismund strengthened Diet and palatine institutions but was resented for absentee rule and arbitrariness. Ottoman danger grew after 1389 and the 1396 defeat at Nicopolis. Janos Hunyadi became the great defender against the Turks and saved Belgrade in 1456. His son Matthias Corvinus, king from 1458 to 1490, created a Renaissance court, improved administration and law, and maintained the mercenary Black Army, but at high tax cost.

After Matthias, the weak Jagiellon kings let defenses decay. Gyorgy Dozsa's peasant rebellion in 1514 was crushed, and Istvan Werboczi's Tripartitum reinforced noble privilege. Suleyman I captured Belgrade in 1521, and the Battle of Mohacs in 1526 destroyed the medieval kingdom. By 1541, Hungary was split into Royal Hungary under the Habsburgs, Ottoman central Hungary, and semi-independent Transylvania.

Partition produced religious, political, and cultural division. Transylvania became a Protestant-leaning centre with relative religious tolerance, while Royal Hungary stayed tied to the Habsburgs and Catholic renewal under figures such as Peter Pazmany. Princes including Istvan Bocskay, Gabor Bethlen, and the Rakoczi family tried to defend Hungarian and Transylvanian autonomy between Ottoman and Habsburg powers.

Ottoman rule ended gradually after the failed Turkish siege of [[Vienna]] in 1683. Buda was retaken in 1686, and the 1699 Treaty of Carlowitz restored most of Hungary to Habsburg control. Ferenc Rakoczi II's rebellion from 1703 to 1711 failed, but the settlement of Szatmar preserved noble privileges and the kingdom's separate status.

In the 18th century, Charles III secured Hungarian acceptance of the Pragmatic Sanction in 1723. Maria Theresa gave peace and population recovery but kept Hungary subordinate to Austrian economic interests, while the 1767 Urbarial Patent regulated peasant obligations. Joseph II later tried centralization, Germanization, and equal taxation without coronation, provoking resistance; Leopold II restored constitutional forms.

The reform era grew after the Diet resumed in 1825. Istvan Szechenyi argued that noble privilege, forced labour, and peasant subjection weakened the nation. Ferenc Deak, Jozsef Eotvos, and Lajos Kossuth pressed reform, Magyar language rights, and political modernization. The nationalities question sharpened because Magyarization clashed with Slovak, Romanian, Serb, Croat, German, and other identities.

The revolution of 1848 produced the March Laws, ending villein status, expanding representation, creating a responsible Hungarian ministry under Lajos Batthyany, and restoring Hungarian autonomy. Conflict with [[Vienna]], Croatian ban Josip Jelacic, and non-Magyar nationalities grew into war. A rump Diet declared Habsburg deposition on April 14, 1849, but Russian intervention helped defeat Hungary; Artur Gorgey surrendered at Vilagos in August 1849.

After repression and absolutism, Deak helped negotiate the 1867 Compromise, forming Austria-Hungary. Hungary gained internal independence while common ministries handled foreign affairs, defense, and related finance. The 1868 Croatian settlement and Nationalities Law tried to manage minority questions, but Magyar political dominance remained. Economic growth, railway building, Budapest's formation in 1872-73, Jewish emancipation, education reform, and agricultural modernization transformed society.

The Dual Monarchy also faced land hunger, rural poverty, limited suffrage, and nationality tensions. Kalman Tisza and later Istvan Tisza defended Liberal and Magyar control. The assassination of Archduke Francis Ferdinand on June 28, 1914, led to [[World Wars (World War I)|World War I]]. Hungary mobilized about 3.8 million men, lost about 661,000, and by 1918 faced economic collapse.

Mihaly Karolyi became prime minister in 1918, but territorial losses, social unrest, and Allied pressure led to crisis. Bela Kun's Soviet Republic ruled briefly in 1919 before Romanian occupation and counterrevolution. Miklos Horthy became regent in 1920. The Treaty of Trianon ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Treaty_of_Trianon)) left Hungary with 35,893 square miles from the historic 125,641, and 7.6 million people from more than 20.8 million, while over 3.2 million Hungarian mother-tongue speakers went to successor states.

Interwar Hungary struggled with refugees, inflation, unemployment, antisemitism, and revisionism. Istvan Bethlen stabilized politics with a 1924 League loan, but the Depression aided right radicalism under Gyula Gombos and successors. Territorial revision came through German and Italian support: the First Vienna Award in 1938, Carpatho-Ruthenia in 1939, and the Second Vienna Award in 1940.

During [[World Wars (World War II)|World War II]], Pal Teleki killed himself after the Yugoslav crisis, and Hungary entered deeper Axis alignment. Laszlo Bardossy sent forces against the Soviet Union, while Miklos Kallay later tried to balance German pressure with secret Western contacts. The Hungarian army suffered disaster at Voronezh in 1943. Germany occupied Hungary on March 19, 1944; Ferenc Szalasi's Arrow Cross seized power later, and about 550,000 of 800,000 Jews perished despite rescue efforts by people such as Raoul Wallenberg.

Soviet-backed politics after 1945 first promised democracy, but the communists controlled the Interior Ministry after the relatively free 1945 election and destroyed opposition through pressure, manipulated elections, forced party mergers, and police power. Matyas Rakosi imposed Stalinism, nationalization, terror, show trials such as Laszlo Rajk's, and imprisonment of Jozsef Cardinal Mindszenty. The 1949 constitution declared a people's republic.

After [[Stalin]]'s death, Imre Nagy briefly offered a New Course in 1953, but hard-line politics returned. The 1956 revolution began on October 23, and Nagy declared neutrality and withdrawal from the Warsaw Pact. Soviet forces invaded on November 4; Pal Maleter and Nagy were later executed. About 200,000 refugees left, and Janos Kadar built a regime that combined repression, later amnesties, consumer concessions, and the 1968 New Economic Mechanism.

By the late 1980s, debt, reform pressure, opposition organizing, and Soviet change weakened the communist order. Kadar was replaced in 1988; Miklos Nemeth, Imre Pozsgay, and reform communists negotiated with opposition groups; and the republic was proclaimed on October 23, 1989. Free elections in 1990 brought Jozsef Antall to power.

Postcommunist governments alternated between conservative, liberal, and Socialist-led coalitions while pursuing privatization, Western integration, and market reform. Gyula Horn continued reforms after 1994; Viktor Orban first governed from 1998 to 2002; Hungary joined NATO in 1999 and the European Union in 2004. Fiscal crisis, protests, and a 2008 rescue package preceded a major Fidesz victory in 2010, the 2010 Ajka red sludge disaster, and the new constitution of 2012.
