# Electronic Games

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> Electronic games are interactive games run by computer circuitry across arcade, console, personal-computer, handheld, mobile, and networked platforms. Their development joins computing technology with rules, audiovisual design, and player interaction.

## Introduction

An electronic game ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Video_game)), also called a computer game or video game, is any interactive game operated by computer circuitry. It is played on many platforms, including shared and personal computers, arcade machines, video consoles connected to home televisions, handheld systems, mobile phones, and server-based networks.

## From chess to Spacewar! to Pong

The idea of playing games on computers is almost as old as the computer itself. In 1950 the mathematician Claude Shannon proposed that computers could be programmed to play chess and asked whether that would mean a machine could think, stimulating decades of [[Artificial Intelligence|artificial intelligence]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artificial_intelligence)) research. Several landmark games began as laboratory demonstrations: in 1958 William A. Higinbotham built Tennis for Two ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tennis_for_Two)) on an oscilloscope at Brookhaven, and in 1962 Steve Russell and others created Spacewar! ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spacewar%21)) on the PDP-1 at MIT.

## Personal computer games

By the late 1970s, games could be written for home computers with their own microprocessors and operating systems, such as the Apple II (1977), the IBM Personal Computer (1981), the Atari 800, and the Commodore 64. Designers used this flexibility to explore new genres inspired by Dungeons & Dragons and the text adventure, and interactive fiction flourished, above all in the Infocom company's Zork series, which relied on language parsing rather than graphics.

## The return of video consoles

After the home-console collapse of the early 1980s, Japanese firms revived the market with the Nintendo Entertainment System (1985) and the Sega Genesis (1989), whose battery-backed cartridges let players save games in progress. Deep titles such as Super Mario Bros., The Legend of Zelda, and the Final Fantasy series followed, and a later console generation—the PlayStation 2, GameCube, and Xbox around 2000-2001—competed on 3D graphics and networking.

## Handheld and mobile games

![[attachments/electronic_games_003.webp|420]]

*Nintendo Game Boy with a Tetris cartridge*

Nintendo opened large-scale portable play with the Game Boy ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Game_Boy)) in 1989, whose pack-in puzzle game Tetris became an international best-seller; the Game Boy line sold more than 100 million units, and the DS family followed from 2004. Mobile phones became game platforms too, from Snake on Nokia handsets in 1997 to the Apple iPhone of 2007 and its App Store of 2008.

## Networked games and virtual worlds

![[attachments/electronic_games_004.webp|420]]

*Player using a networked computer game setup*

Faster processors, 3D graphics, and the Internet produced new genres in the 1990s and 2000s: fast first-person shooters such as Doom and Quake, motion-captured sports games such as Madden NFL, and massively multiplayer online games such as Ultima Online, EverQuest, and World of Warcraft, where thousands of players create avatars in persistent worlds. Around these games grew communities of clans, fan sites, modifications, and player-made machinima.

## New forms

As electronic games entered the mainstream of commerce and culture, developers turned to player-created content in titles such as The Sims and LittleBigPlanet, explored social networks as game platforms, and applied game mechanics to many other fields of activity.

## Core Equations

Engineering and technology topics often combine efficiency, signal flow, and computational cost:

$$\eta=\frac{\text{useful output}}{\text{input}}$$

Efficiency compares useful output with total input, so losses appear as the gap between the two.

$$SNR=10\log_{10}\left(\frac{P_s}{P_n}\right)$$

Signal-to-noise ratio compares signal power with noise power on a logarithmic decibel scale.

$$T(n)=O(n\log n)$$
