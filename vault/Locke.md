# Locke

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> John Locke was an English philosopher whose empiricism and political liberalism shaped Enlightenment thought, constitutional government, religious toleration, natural rights, and government by consent. His work connected new science with accounts of knowledge, property, personal identity, and resistance to arbitrary power.

## Introduction

![[attachments/locke_001.webp|420]]

*Portrait of John Locke*

John Locke was an English philosopher whose work helped found modern empiricism and political liberalism. His thought influenced the European Enlightenment, the United States Constitution, and later ideas of toleration, government by consent, and limits on political power.

Locke's philosophy was close to the new science associated with Robert Boyle, Isaac Newton, and the Royal Society. His political ideas grew from social contract theory, natural law, religious toleration, and resistance to divine-right monarchy.

Locke's early life was shaped by Puritan sympathy within the Church of England and by the English Civil Wars. His father served with parliamentary forces, and Locke grew up rejecting the idea that kings ruled by divine right. He entered Westminster School in 1647, near the events surrounding the execution of Charles I in 1649.

## Oxford

Locke entered Christ Church, Oxford, in 1652. He found the official curriculum, still centred on Aristotle and medieval learning, dull, but he read newer thinkers such as [[Francis bacon|Francis Bacon]] and Rene Descartes outside the syllabus.

At Oxford, Locke met advocates of the new science, including John Wilkins, Christopher Wren, Thomas Willis, Richard Lower, Robert Hooke, and especially Robert Boyle. Medical research, careful observation, and distrust of empty speculation became central to his habits of thought.

The Restoration of 1660 brought monarchy back but also helped move scientific collaborators to London, where the Royal Society developed. Locke's early essays on government and natural law already rejected absolute monarchy and innate moral knowledge, preparing his mature political and epistemological work.

## Two Treatises of Government

In 1666, Locke met Anthony Ashley Cooper, later first earl of Shaftesbury, and joined his household as aide and physician. Shaftesbury's support for constitutional monarchy, Protestant succession, Parliament, civil liberty, toleration, and economic expansion matched Locke's developing commitments.

Political danger increased during the exclusion crisis over whether James, a Roman Catholic, should be barred from succession. Locke lived in [[France]] from 1675 to 1679, returned to England during renewed conflict, and fled to Holland in 1683 after Shaftesbury's fall.

The Two Treatises of Government ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Two_Treatises_of_Government)), published in 1690 but largely composed earlier, answered this crisis and later justified the Glorious Revolution ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glorious_Revolution)) of 1688. Locke's religious framework held that God created humans with reason so they could know natural law, duties, and moral order.

The first treatise attacked Sir Robert Filmer's Patriarcha, which defended royal power through descent from Adam. Locke argued that such divine-right theory lacked common sense, evidence, and any binding claim over later generations.

The second treatise described people as naturally free and equal under the law of nature. In the state of nature, each person may enforce that law, but this creates risk of injustice and violence; people therefore consent to civil government to protect life, liberty, and property.

## Property

Locke's account of property begins with self-ownership: each person owns his own body. A person can then acquire property by mixing labour with unowned things, so cultivated land, crops, or a house may become the worker's property.

This view anticipated later labour theory of value, though Locke also held that no one should hoard surplus produce in the state of nature while others lack enough. Money changed society because people conventionally accepted a durable object without intrinsic value, allowing larger accumulation.

Locke returned from property to [[The Forms Of Government Their Historical Development (Government)|government]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Government)) by arguing that political society must enforce legitimate contracts and protect rights. The majority should govern, laws must apply equally, taxation requires consent, powers should be divided, and a ruler who destroys rights may be resisted, even by revolution.

Locke's political vision strongly influenced the [[United States of America (United States)|United States]] and [[France]]. His ideas shaped the U.S. Declaration of Independence of 1776, the broad outline of constitutional government, and the French Declaration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen in 1789.

## An Essay Concerning Human Understanding

Locke remained in Holland from 1683 to 1689, wrote the first Letter on Toleration, and completed An Essay Concerning Human Understanding. The Essay asked what the human mind can understand and aimed to clear away confused philosophical language.

Locke attacked innate ideas, including supposed innate knowledge of God, morality, logic, and mathematics. Infants do not possess such conscious ideas, and different cultures show wide disagreement; knowledge instead begins from experience.

Book II explains that ideas come from sensation and reflection. Sensation gives ideas from the external world, such as colour, sound, and taste; reflection gives awareness of mental acts such as thinking, doubting, willing, pleasure, and pain.

Locke distinguished primary qualities, such as size, shape, weight, and solidity, from secondary qualities, such as colour, taste, and smell. Ideas of primary qualities resemble object features, while secondary qualities are powers in objects to produce experiences in perceivers.

## Personal Identity

Locke made personal identity a major philosophical problem. He distinguished sameness of body, sameness of human being, and sameness of person, arguing that personal identity depends on continuity of consciousness through memory.

His discussion of association of ideas explained how ideas become linked through repeated experience. This mattered for education, habit, and later associationist psychology, influencing thinkers such as David Hartley.

## Language

Book III offered one of the first sustained modern philosophical studies of linguistic meaning. Locke held that words stand for ideas in the speaker's mind, allowing private thoughts to be communicated.

Because only particular things exist, general words require explanation. Locke answered with abstraction: the mind forms a general idea, such as triangle, by leaving aside differences among particular triangles and retaining what they share.

## Knowledge

Book IV defined knowledge narrowly as the perception of agreement or disagreement among ideas. This allowed certainty in cases such as black not being white, mathematical proofs, one's own present existence, and demonstrative proof of God, but it made most scientific expectation only probability.

Locke still treated probability ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Probability)) as essential for practical life. People act on expectations that stones will fall and water will quench thirst, even though such beliefs do not meet his strict standard of knowledge.

He also warned against Enthusiasm, the acceptance of claims on inadequate grounds because one is already disposed to believe them. A later problem came from his claim that ideas are the immediate objects of experience: if so, knowing an external world beyond ideas became difficult, and this issue dominated 18th-century epistemology.

Locke's other works included Some Thoughts Concerning Education, which stressed virtue, wisdom, manners, exercise, study, recreation, and practical learning, and The Reasonableness of Christianity, which treated Christianity as rational and morally grounded.

After the Glorious Revolution, Locke returned to England in 1689 with the party accompanying the future Queen Mary II. He helped political projects such as the English Bill of Rights, declined a diplomatic post, and spent his last years at Oates in High Laver with Damaris Masham, revising works and answering critics until his death in 1704.

## Key Events & Developments

- 1632: John Locke was born into a Puritan-sympathetic Church of England family.
- 1647: Locke entered Westminster School.
- 1652: Locke entered Christ Church, Oxford.
- 1666: Locke met Anthony Ashley Cooper, later Shaftesbury.
- 1683: Locke fled to Holland during political danger.
- 1688-1689: The Glorious Revolution ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glorious_Revolution)) brought Locke back to England.
- 1690: Two Treatises of Government and An Essay Concerning Human Understanding appeared.
- 1704: Locke died at High Laver, Essex.

## Key People

- John Locke ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Locke)): Philosopher of empiricism, toleration, natural rights, and limited government.
- Anthony Ashley Cooper: Locke's patron and political ally, later first earl of Shaftesbury.
- Robert Boyle ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Boyle)): Scientific collaborator whose observational method influenced Locke.
- Sir Robert Filmer ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sir_Robert_Filmer)): Defender of divine-right monarchy whom Locke attacked.
- William III and Mary II ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mary_II)): Protestant monarchs whose accession Locke defended through revolution theory.
- Damaris Masham ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Damaris_Masham)): Friend who housed Locke during his last years.

## Interesting Facts

- Lockean empiricism denies innate ideas and makes experience the origin of mental materials.
- Locke's property theory begins with ownership of one's own body.
- Personal identity, for Locke, depends on memory and continuity of consciousness.
- [[George Washington]] reportedly described Locke as the greatest man who had ever lived.
- Locke's strict definition of knowledge made much of science probable rather than certain.
