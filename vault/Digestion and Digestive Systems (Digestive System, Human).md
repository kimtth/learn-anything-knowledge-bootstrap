# Digestion and Digestive Systems (Digestive System, Human)

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> The human digestive system consists of the alimentary canal and associated organs that ingest food, digest it, absorb nutrients, and remove undigested material.

## Introduction

The human digestive system is the set of organs that breaks food into forms the body can use and then removes what cannot be used. It includes the digestive tract, which carries food from the lips to the anus, and also the glands and supporting organs that add juices for digestion. The process is both physical and chemical: food is broken into smaller pieces, mixed with fluids, and turned into substances that can pass into the blood and lymph.

The system starts in the mouth and ends in the large intestine and rectum, but it works as one connected pathway. Food only becomes useful after it is chewed, mixed, moved, digested, and absorbed, so each organ has a special role. The system can be followed step by step, beginning with the upper tract and then moving through the stomach, intestines, liver, pancreas, hormones, immunity, and development.

## Mouth and oral structures

In the mouth, digestion begins in a limited way, but the food is prepared for later stages. Mastication, or chewing, breaks food into smaller pieces, while the tongue and cheeks keep it in place and mix it with saliva. This makes the food easier to swallow and easier for enzymes to act on later, even though most chemical digestion still happens farther down the tract.

The lips, cheeks, roof of the mouth, gums, teeth, and tongue all support this work. The teeth cut, tear, and grind, the tongue shapes the bolus, and the mucous lining helps protect the mouth during speech and eating. The lips and cheeks also help seal the mouth and keep food moving between the teeth until swallowing is ready to begin.

## Salivary glands

The major salivary glands are the parotid, submandibular, and sublingual glands. They are racemose glands with acini and ducts, and their cells include serous cells that make watery fluid and mucous cells that make mucus. The parotid glands are mostly serous, the submandibular glands are mixed, and the sublingual glands are mostly mucous.

The glands are controlled by the sympathetic and parasympathetic nervous systems. Nerve signals increase or adjust saliva flow when food, touch, smell, or even learned food cues are present. The result is a constant background secretion that rises sharply during eating and helps prepare the mouth for chewing and swallowing.

## Saliva

Saliva is more than moisture. It dissolves food, lubricates it, and starts starch digestion with amylase, so the first chemical step of digestion can begin in the mouth. It also helps taste, because taste buds need substances in solution before they can respond well.

Saliva has water, ions, proteins, enzymes, and buffer systems that keep its pH fairly stable. It also protects the mouth by washing away food residue, limiting bacterial growth, and carrying away some unwanted substances. Without it, the mouth becomes dry, taste weakens, and speaking and eating become more difficult.

## Pharynx

The pharynx is a shared passage for food and air. It connects the mouth to the esophagus and also to the nose, larynx, and middle ear. Its shape and muscles make swallowing possible while still allowing breathing when food is not passing through.

During swallowing, the tongue, soft palate, and pharyngeal constrictors move the bolus downward in a coordinated wave. The soft palate blocks the nasal passage, the larynx rises, and the upper esophageal sphincter opens at the right moment. This protects the airway and sends food safely into the esophagus.

## Esophagus

The esophagus is a muscular tube that carries food from the pharynx to the stomach. Its wall has four layers: mucosa, submucosa, muscularis, and outer connective tissue. Its upper part uses striated muscle, the middle part mixes striated and smooth muscle, and the lower part uses smooth muscle.

The esophagus is normally closed except during swallowing. Peristaltic waves move the bolus downward, and the upper and lower sphincters help control entry and prevent backflow. The lower sphincter is especially important because it guards against reflux of stomach contents into the esophagus.

## Stomach Anatomy

The stomach stores food, mixes it, and prepares it for controlled release into the small intestine. It begins digestion of carbohydrates and proteins and turns the meal into chyme, a semi-fluid mixture. Its location under the diaphragm and its curved shape help it act as a reservoir and mixing chamber.

Its regions, including the fundus, body, antrum, and pyloric canal, each support a different part of that work. The lining folds into rugae when empty and stretches when full, while the muscular wall churns the contents with gastric juice. The pyloric region regulates the slow exit of chyme into the duodenum.

## Small Intestine Anatomy

The small intestine is the main organ for digestion and nutrient absorption. It is long, narrow, and highly specialized, with the duodenum, jejunum, and ileum arranged in sequence. The duodenum receives chyme from the stomach and also the secretions from the pancreas and liver, which makes it the key starting point for intestinal digestion.

Its structure helps it do several jobs at once. The mucosa forms folds, villi, and tiny surface projections that increase area for absorption, while the duodenum is shaped to receive bile and pancreatic juice through the papilla. The jejunum is especially important for absorption, and the ileum finishes absorption before the contents pass into the colon.

## Absorption

The small intestine absorbs through active transport, passive diffusion, facilitated diffusion, and endocytosis. Each method fits a different kind of nutrient, and each depends on the chemical nature of the substance and the state of the intestinal membrane. The large surface area of the intestine makes these processes efficient.

Most absorption happens in the jejunum, but the duodenum is crucial because it normalizes the intestinal contents and receives bile and pancreatic juice. Surface folds and villi multiply the area for transfer, and the enterocytes on the surface carry the transport systems that move sugars, amino acids, fats, vitamins, and minerals into the body.

## Secretions

Many secretions enter the small intestine from the pancreas, liver, and intestinal glands. These secretions are controlled by nerves and hormones, and they respond strongly to chyme, acid, fat, and other local stimuli. Together they make the intestinal environment suitable for digestion and absorption.

The pancreatic juice contains enzymes for proteins, fats, and carbohydrates, while bile helps handle fats. The intestinal juice is usually thin and slightly alkaline, and it includes mucus, salts, and enzymes that support digestion at the surface of the mucosa. In the duodenum, Brunner glands add extra mucus for protection.

## Large Intestine Anatomy

The large intestine absorbs water and salts and forms waste for elimination. It includes the cecum, colon, sigmoid colon, rectum, and anus, and it begins where the ileum opens into the cecum. Its first part receives material through the ileocecal valve, which helps control the flow from the small intestine.

The colon has several bends and long segments that move contents slowly. As water is removed, the material becomes more solid, and the sigmoid colon and rectum hold feces until defecation. This part of the tract is less about digestion and more about finishing the body’s handling of waste.

## Liver

![[attachments/digestion_and_digestive_systems_digestive_system_human_002.webp|420]]

*Digestive organs page 83*

The liver is the largest gland in the body and one of the most important metabolic organs. It manages carbohydrates, proteins, fats, bile acids, bilirubin, and many drugs. It also helps regulate storage and transport of nutrients, so it sits at the center of digestion and metabolism.

Its gross anatomy is large and complex, with right and left lobes, blood vessels, and the porta hepatis. The liver receives blood from both the hepatic artery and the portal vein, which means it processes both oxygenated blood and blood carrying absorbed nutrients from the intestine. This makes it a major filter and processing station for the whole body.

## Microscopic Structure

Under the microscope, the liver shows lobules made of cords of hepatocytes around central veins. Sinusoids run between the cords, allowing blood to pass close to the liver cells, and Kupffer cells help remove foreign material. Canaliculi carry bile away from the hepatocytes and into the bile ducts.

This microscopic design explains the liver’s function. The cells can both process bloodborne substances and make bile, while the narrow spaces and ducts allow fast exchange and drainage. The structure is simple in outline but highly efficient in action.

## Biliary Tract Anatomy

The biliary tract begins inside the liver and carries bile to the intestine. The right and left hepatic ducts join to form the hepatic duct, which then connects with the cystic duct from the gallbladder and becomes the common bile duct. The duct passes through the pancreas and usually meets the pancreatic duct before opening into the duodenum.

The gallbladder stores and concentrates bile. The sphincter at the duodenal opening controls bile flow, and the entire tract links the liver, gallbladder, pancreas, and duodenum into one functional unit. This unit is essential for fat digestion and for the timed release of bile during meals.

## Bile

Bile helps disperse and digest fat in the small intestine. It is made in the liver and concentrated in the gallbladder, where water and some ions are reabsorbed. Its main chemical parts are bile acids, phospholipids, cholesterol, bilirubin, and inorganic ions.

Fat in the duodenum stimulates cholecystokinin, which makes the gallbladder contract and release bile. Bile salts also help form micelles, which keep fat fragments in a form that can mix with water and reach the absorptive surface. In this way bile is central to fat absorption, not just fat breakdown.

## Pancreas

The pancreas is both an exocrine gland and an endocrine gland. Its exocrine cells make digestive enzymes that enter the duodenum, while its endocrine islets make hormones that control blood sugar and intestinal activity. This dual role makes the pancreas important for both digestion and metabolism.

The acinar tissue makes proteases, lipases, amylases, and protective proteins, and the ducts deliver them to the intestine. The islets of Langerhans make insulin and glucagon, and they also produce other hormones that influence gut movement and secretion. The pancreas therefore supports food breakdown and internal balance at the same time.

## Absorption and Metabolism Overview

Attention now turns from organ anatomy to the chemistry of digestion and absorption. The body absorbs nutrients and minerals, hormones regulate gut activity, and the digestive system protects itself. These processes link digestion to whole-body metabolism.

Digestion is not only mechanical. Absorption depends on transport systems, enzymes, bile, hormones, and tissue structure, so the intestine, liver, pancreas, and blood all work together. The major nutrients can be considered one by one.

## Carbohydrates

Carbohydrates are absorbed mainly as simple sugars. Starch must be broken into monosaccharides by salivary amylase, pancreatic amylase, and brush-border enzymes such as lactase, maltase, sucrase, and trehalase. Humans cannot fully digest cellulose, so not all plant carbohydrate is usable.

After absorption, glucose can be used for energy or stored as glycogen in the liver. The liver also helps convert other sugars into forms the body can use, so carbohydrate handling is not just a matter of intestinal digestion. It is part of a larger system that stores, releases, and balances sugar in the blood.

## Proteins

Protein digestion starts in the stomach and continues mainly in the duodenum and jejunum. Pepsin begins the process, but the pancreatic enzymes are stronger and do most of the work. They break large protein chains into small peptides and then into amino acids.

The intestine activates pancreatic proteases with enterokinase and trypsin. This activation chain is important because it keeps the enzymes inactive until they reach the gut, which protects the pancreas from self-digestion. By the end of the process, the body can absorb amino acids for growth, repair, and energy.

## Fats

Most dietary fat is stored as triglycerides, and these must be made water-friendly before they can be absorbed. Bile acids and pancreatic lipase work together to break fat into smaller units and to form micelles that can move through the watery intestinal environment. Medium-chain fats are handled more easily than long-chain fats.

The products of fat digestion pass through the intestinal cells and then into the lymph or portal blood, depending on the kind of fatty acid. Colipase helps lipase work in the presence of bile salts, so digestion depends on cooperation between the pancreas and the biliary tract. Without this cooperation, fat absorption becomes poor.

## Calcium

Calcium is needed for bone, cell membranes, muscle action, and nerve function. Its absorption is strongest in the duodenum and depends on stomach acid, vitamin D, and active transport across the enterocyte surface. Too much fat, magnesium, or oxalate can interfere with this process.

The body keeps calcium under tight control because it is essential for many systems. When intake or absorption is poor, calcium may be taken from bone, which can weaken the skeleton. This makes calcium balance a major concern in both nutrition and disease.

## Magnesium

Magnesium supports neuromuscular function, bone chemistry, and nucleic acid synthesis. About two-thirds of dietary magnesium is absorbed, and the kidneys regulate how much stays in the body. Its balance is closely linked with calcium.

Low magnesium can appear in diuretic use, kidney failure, alcoholism, diabetes, and malabsorption. When magnesium is low, calcium may move out of bone, so deficiency can affect more than one mineral system at once. Magnesium supplements can help when hypocalcemia is tied to malabsorption.

## Hematinics

Hematinics are substances needed to make healthy blood cells. Folic acid, vitamin B12, and iron are the main examples, and vitamin D also supports healthy blood formation indirectly. Each one has a different path of absorption and a different role in red blood cell production.
Deficiency in any of these substances can lead to anemia or poor blood cell maturity. Blood formation depends on digestion, absorption, and liver storage, so a nutritional problem in the gut can become a blood problem in the body. This links nutrition directly to hematology.

## Vitamin B12

Vitamin B12 is essential for blood cell formation and for folate movement into cells. It must bind to intrinsic factor, a protein from the stomach, before it can be absorbed in the distal ileum. The liver stores much of the body’s B12 reserve.

The absorption process is slow and very specific. Without intrinsic factor, B12 transport fails, which is why stomach function is so important for preventing certain kinds of anemia. This is one of the clearest examples of how the stomach supports later nutrient uptake.

## Iron

Iron is needed for hemoglobin and other cell reactions. It is absorbed mainly in the duodenum, helped by stomach acid and vitamin C, while phytates, phosphates, and low acid reduce uptake. The body does not easily excrete extra iron, so control of absorption is important.

When blood loss rises, the body increases iron absorption. Iron moves from the enterocyte into transferrin in the blood, which carries it through the portal system and to the liver and other tissues. This makes iron balance a delicate mix of diet, digestion, and body demand.

## Vitamin D

Vitamin D acts like a hormone and comes from sunlight and diet. It is formed from a sterol precursor in the skin and then processed in the liver and other tissues. It is important for bone health and for calcium transport in the intestine.

When sunlight or dietary supply is low, deficiency can cause rickets or osteomalacia. Vitamin D also supports calcium absorption in the gut, so its role reaches beyond the skeleton into the whole mineral balance of the body. Vitamin D also has broader blood and inflammatory effects.

## Insulin

Insulin is made by pancreatic beta cells when blood glucose rises. It helps cells take up and store glucose in muscle and fat tissue, so it lowers blood sugar after a meal. It is one of the main hormones that connects digestion to energy storage.
Insulin is a treatment for diabetes mellitus. Its action opposes glucagon, which means the two hormones work as a balancing pair. Together they keep the body from swinging too far in either direction.

## Glucagon

Glucagon is made by pancreatic alpha cells when blood sugar falls. It raises blood glucose by stimulating glycogen breakdown and new glucose production in the liver. It also reduces some gastric and pancreatic secretions.

Like insulin, glucagon is part of glucose control after eating and between meals. The hormone protects against low blood sugar and helps the liver release stored fuel when the body needs it. This gives the body a backup system for energy supply.

## Somatostatin

Somatostatin is an inhibitory hormone from pancreatic delta cells. It reduces acid, pepsin, gastrin, insulin, and other gut hormones, and it also slows gallbladder and intestinal movement. Its role is mainly to restrain overactivity.

This makes somatostatin a brake inside the digestive system. It limits secretion and motility when food enters the gut, so it helps keep digestive responses balanced rather than excessive.

## Serotonin

Serotonin is made in enterochromaffin cells in the gut and in related cells elsewhere. It acts as a local messenger rather than as a classic circulating hormone in this context. It is also linked with other local mediators such as histamine and kinins.

Its main importance here is regulatory, not nutritional. It helps coordinate gastrointestinal signaling and is widely distributed through the digestive tract, showing how many chemical signals work inside the bowel wall itself.

## Cholecystokinin

Cholecystokinin is released when food, especially fat, enters the duodenum. It contracts the gallbladder, relaxes the bile-duct sphincter, and stimulates pancreatic enzyme secretion. It also increases intestinal movement.

The hormone is one of the key links between food in the intestine and the release of digestive juices. It makes sure bile and enzymes arrive when they are needed, which improves fat digestion and overall intestinal processing.

## Motilin

Motilin is released between meals and helps the stomach and small intestine move. It stimulates the fundus and antrum, speeds gastric emptying, and can tighten the lower esophageal sphincter. Its action fits the fasting state rather than the feeding state.

This hormone helps clear the digestive tract between meals. By promoting movement when the gut is empty, it prepares the stomach and intestine for the next meal and keeps motility patterns organized.

## Neurotensin

Neurotensin is released from the ileum when fat enters the intestine. It changes motility and relaxes the lower esophageal sphincter, while also limiting vagal stimulation of acid and pepsin secretion. This makes it part of the intestine’s feedback control.

The hormone shows how the lower small intestine can influence upper gut activity. Its actions slow and redirect digestive work, especially after fat has entered the bowel.

## Pancreatic polypeptide

Pancreatic polypeptide is made by special endocrine cells in the pancreas after protein meals. It is tied to vagal and cholinergic activity, and its level is often raised in diabetes. It is another signal that helps tune pancreatic function.

Its exact role is less central than insulin or glucagon, but it still matters in digestive regulation. It belongs to the network of hormones that respond to eating and helps show how many signals the pancreas can release.

## Secretin

Secretin is released from the duodenum when acid enters the intestine. It stimulates bicarbonate production by the pancreas, which helps neutralize the acid from the stomach. This protects the intestinal lining and creates a better environment for enzymes.

Secretin is one of the major chemical messengers in digestion. It coordinates acid control with pancreatic response, so the duodenum does not receive chyme that is too harsh or too acidic.

## Vasoactive intestinal peptide

Vasoactive intestinal peptide is found mainly in nerves in the gastrointestinal tract. It lowers acid release, supports bicarbonate secretion, and increases water and electrolyte secretion. It also relaxes sphincters and slows intestinal transit.

The peptide works as a broad local regulator. Its main effect is to calm and loosen gut activity, which can be useful when the bowel needs more fluid movement and less acid exposure.

## Substance P

Substance P is present in vagus nerves and the myenteric plexus. It stimulates saliva, smooth muscle contraction, and inflammatory responses. It is a strong messenger, although its full role in digestion is still uncertain.

Its presence shows how nervous tissue and digestive tissue overlap. It can influence both movement and secretion, so it is part of the larger communication system of the gut.

## Endorphins and enkephalins

Endorphins and enkephalins are opioid-like peptides in the digestive nerves. They bind to the same receptors as morphine-like substances, and they may help modulate smooth muscle and endocrine release in the gut. They are not classic blood hormones.

Their possible function is local and regulatory. They may help fine-tune gut movement and peptide release, showing that the digestive system uses its own internal chemical signals in a very detailed way.

## Bombesin

Bombesin is another gut peptide found in intrinsic nerves. It can stimulate gastrin release, pancreatic enzymes, and gallbladder contraction, although these effects may partly happen through cholecystokinin. Its main physiological role is uncertain.

Even with that uncertainty, bombesin belongs to the network of local gut messengers. It may act as a signal that helps coordinate digestion, especially when food is moving through the stomach and intestine.

## Prostaglandins

Prostaglandins help control smooth muscle activity in the gastrointestinal tract. They also protect the mucosa by increasing mucus and bicarbonate secretion and by helping repair the lining after injury. This protective role matters against acid, alcohol, aspirin, bile acids, and other irritants.
Gastrointestinal immunity also plays a role. The gut contains lymphoid tissue, lymphocytes, plasma cells, Peyer patches, and other defense structures that help the body respond to bacteria, viruses, parasites, toxins, and foreign proteins. This makes the digestive tract not only a digestive surface but also an important immune barrier.

## Origins of the Vertebrate Gut

![[attachments/digestion_and_digestive_systems_digestive_system_human_001.webp|420]]

*Primitive digestive system (development of digestive system)*

Embryology and evolution show how the digestive tract develops in the embryo and how the vertebrate plan emerged. In amphioxus, gastrulation creates an endoderm-lined cavity that becomes the digestive tract, and the blastopore becomes the anus while a new mouth appears later. This gives a clear picture of how the earliest body plan can form.

In vertebrates, the foregut, hindgut, stomodeum, and proctodeum develop into a complete tract with two openings. This explains the deep evolutionary pattern of the digestive system, where the basic tube and its openings are shaped early in development and then specialized into the organs seen in humans.

## Embryology and Evolution of the Vertebrate Digestive Tract

The vertebrate digestive tract begins from endoderm and develops in parallel with the rest of the body. The foregut and hindgut extend into the embryo, and the stomodeum and proctodeum later connect to them. This creates the digestive tube that later becomes mouth, gut, and anus.
Evolution and development fit together. Even though mammalian embryos develop in more complex ways than amphioxus, the same basic pattern remains: an internal tube, an anterior opening, and a posterior opening. That shared plan is the evolutionary base of the human digestive system.

## Interesting Facts

- Human digestion involves the mouth, esophagus, stomach, small intestine, large intestine, liver, gallbladder, and pancreas.
- The system shows a close link between structure and function.
- Absorption happens mainly in the small intestine, while the large intestine helps manage water and waste.
