# Slavery

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> Slavery is a legal and social regime in which people are treated as property and denied normal rights, kinship standing, mobility, and control over labour and family life. The source compares household and productive slavery across ancient, African, Asian, Islamic, European, and American societies, distinguishing it from related unfree conditions and tracing its institutional foundations and abolition.

## Introduction

![[attachments/slavery_001.webp|420]]

*Diagram of the Brookes slave ship*

Slavery ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slavery)) is the condition in which one human being is owned by another. A slave was usually treated in law as property, or chattel, and lacked many rights held by free persons. Scholars disagree on one exact definition, but several features commonly appear: ownership by another person, few legal rights, social marginality, control of labour, control of movement, and limits on family or sexual choice.

A slave was often an object of law, not a full subject of law. In many societies the slave was not personally liable for contracts or wrongs in the same way as a free person. A slave might have some protections, but always fewer rights than the owner. The slave was also removed from normal lines of kinship, becoming an outsider or "socially dead" person whose relatives could not defend rights or seek vengeance.

Slave regimes differed in severity. A mild regime might leave some rights, family life, or paths to freedom. A severe regime restricted almost every part of life. Slaves were created through war, raiding, birth, punishment, debt, sale by parents, self-sale under starvation, and trade. For slavery to persist, societies also needed demand for labour, outsiders who could be enslaved, and authorities willing to enforce slave property.

## Types and Related Conditions

The most common type was household slavery, also called domestic or patriarchal slavery. Such slaves served owners in homes, courts, armies, temples, or households. They were often signs of status and consumption. Some merged partly into owners' families as adopted sons, concubines, wives, servants, or dependents.

The second major type was productive slavery, where slaves produced market goods in mines, plantations, workshops, or agriculture. This was less common but historically powerful in Classical Athens, Roman Italy, parts of the Islamic world, the circum-Caribbean New World, and some African societies. Productive slavery could reshape whole economies.

A slave society was different from a slave-owning society. In a slave society, slaves were numerous, central to production or politics, and deeply affected law, family, social thought, and the economy. In a slave-owning society, slaves existed but were not the main foundation of institutions.

Slavery also differed from serfdom, indentured servitude, debt peonage, and forced labour, though boundaries could blur. Serfs usually had some legal rights and were bound to land, while slaves were bound to owners and could often be sold away. Indentured servants worked for a fixed term, often to repay passage. Debt peons and criminal peons had fewer protections and could resemble slaves in practice.

## Historical Spread

The origins of slavery are unknown, but it appeared very early in recorded history. It existed in Shang China and was studied in Han China, where perhaps 5 percent of the population was enslaved. China had slaves into the 20th century, but abundant labour, flexible family boundaries, and other conditions kept it from becoming a slave society.

Slavery existed in [[India]], Korea, Thailand, Burma, Central Asia, and many other Asian regions. In India, British observers in 1841 estimated eight or nine million slaves. In some areas of Thailand and Burma, a quarter to a third of the population may have been enslaved.

In the Middle East, slavery was present from the start of recorded history. The Babylonian Code of Hammurabi discussed it about 750 BCE. Ancient Egypt had slaves, though modern scholarship rejects the older belief that slaves built the great pyramids. Slavery also appears in biblical societies and across Islamic societies from Arabia and North Africa to Pakistan and Indonesia.

[[Europe]] also knew slavery. Slaves appeared in England, Scandinavia, France, Germany, Poland, Lithuania, and Russia. Viking raiding fed international slave markets, and Russia developed partly from slave raiding routes between Scandinavia and Byzantium. Russian household slaves were converted into house serfs in the early 1720s, and house serfs were freed by Tsar Alexander II in 1861.

Slavery existed throughout [[Africa]] before Islam and before the Atlantic trade. African societies included both slave societies and slave-owning societies. About 18 million Africans entered Islamic trans-Saharan and Indian Ocean slave trades between 650 and 1905. Between the late 15th century and 1867, roughly 7 million to 10 million Africans were shipped as slaves to the New World.

## Slave Societies

[[Athens]] was the first known major slave society. After Solon abolished citizen slavery about 594 BCE, wealthy Athenians relied on enslaved outsiders. By Classical Athens, slaves may have made up about one-third of the population. They worked in homes, crafts, and especially the Laurium silver mines, helping support Athenian wealth and elite leisure.

Roman Italy became another major slave society from about the 2nd century BCE to the 4th century CE. Conquest, markets, agricultural estates, law, and labour demand produced a society in which roughly 30 percent of the population was enslaved. Roman slavery declined as slaves became coloni, or serfs, and as sources of outside slaves became harder to reach.

Several Islamic slave societies were also important. The Baghdad caliphate imported military captives and Zanj slaves, some used to clear saline lands and grow sugar in Lower Iraq. The Crimean Khanate, from about 1475 to 1783, relied heavily on raiding Slavic and Caucasian peoples and selling captives. The Ottoman Empire used slaves in cities, armies, administration, households, harems, crafts, and shipping.

In Africa, large slave societies appeared in the Sokoto caliphate, Ghana, Mali, Segou, Songhai, Ouidah, Kanem, Bornu, and other regions. In some Fulani jihad states between 1750 and 1900, one- to two-thirds of the population consisted of slaves. After the Atlantic trade was limited, some African societies redirected slave labour to mining and market crops such as peanuts, palm oil, sesame, and millet.

The best-known slave societies were in the circum-Caribbean and the Americas. African slave imports began in the early 16th century. After the sugar revolution, many Caribbean islands became plantation societies dominated by imported African labour. About two-thirds of all people shipped across the Atlantic ended up in sugar-producing regions.

## Abolition

![[attachments/slavery_002.webp|420]]

*Page of the Emancipation Proclamation*

The abolition movement of the 19th century transformed the world history of slavery. In the United States, the Civil War and Abraham Lincoln's Emancipation Proclamation of January 1, 1863, put slavery on the road to extinction. The Thirteenth Amendment then ended slavery constitutionally.

Other Western Hemisphere abolitions followed or preceded in stages. Puerto Rico abolished slavery in 1873 with apprenticeship provisions, and Cuba in 1880. [[Brazil]], strengthened by coffee slavery after the 1820s, was the last Western Hemisphere country to abolish slavery; all Brazilian slaves were manumitted on May 13, 1888.

Britain pushed abolition in many regions after ending slavery in much of Latin America and South Asia. In India, Act V of 1843 stopped British courts from enforcing slave claims, and the Penal Code of 1861 made slaveholding a crime. British pressure later targeted African slave-trading states, including Lagos, Nigeria, Malawi's Indian Ocean trade route, and Zanzibar.

European colonization in the second half of the 19th century ended slavery in many parts of Africa, East Asia, and Southeast Asia, though forced labour and slavery-like systems persisted. China formally abolished slavery in the early 20th century, and some scholars compare the Soviet Gulag camps, which lasted until 1956, to state slavery.

## Agriculture

![[attachments/slavery_003.webp|420]]

*Slave plantation in Yucatan*

Large numbers of slaves worked in agriculture ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agriculture)), but not all crops encouraged slavery equally. Grain crops such as rye, oats, wheat, millet, and barley were less often slave crops, though slaves sometimes sowed or harvested them. Commercial crops needing intense labour, such as olives, grapes, sugar, cotton, tobacco, coffee, and some rice, were more attractive to slave owners.

Crop profitability helped decide whether a slave-owning society became a slave society. Roman estates used slaves in vineyards and olive groves. Ninth-century Iraq became a slave society through sugar cultivation. African slave labour was important for rice, coconut, coffee, cloves, kola nuts, peanuts, and sesame in some regions.

In [[Brazil]], the gang labour system of the late 16th century made sugar production highly profitable. A plantation using gang labour could produce about 39 percent more output from similar inputs than free farms or non-gang slave farms. The reason was brutal: enslaved workers could be driven under overseers and drivers in ways free labourers could not.

Sugar and rice were especially deadly. Tobacco and coffee also used gang labour, but they were less physically demanding and had lower mortality. Domestic service remained probably the most widespread slave occupation across history, including water carrying, wood cutting, cooking, cleaning, shopping, child care, and personal attendance.

## Key Events & Developments

- 18th-12th century BCE: Slavery ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slavery)) existed in Shang China.
- About 750 BCE: The Babylonian Code of Hammurabi treated slavery as an established institution.
- About 594 BCE: Solon abolished citizen slavery in Athens, increasing reliance on enslaved outsiders.
- 16th century: African slave imports to the Americas expanded with mining and plantation economies.
- January 1, 1863: Abraham Lincoln's Emancipation Proclamation began slavery's legal destruction in the United States.
- 1888: [[Brazil]] manumitted all remaining slaves.

- Solon ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solon)): Athenian lawgiver whose abolition of citizen slavery helped shift Athens toward enslaved outsiders.
- Philip II of Macedonia ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philip_II_of_Macedonia)): Defeated Athens at Chaeronea in 338 BCE, after which some slaves were freed.
- Catherine the Great ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catherine_the_Great)): Russian empress whose state liquidated the Crimean Khanate in 1783.
- Tsar Alexander II ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tsar_Alexander_II)): Freed Russian house serfs from their lords in 1861.
- Abraham Lincoln ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abraham_Lincoln)): Issued the Emancipation Proclamation during the U.S. Civil War.

## Interesting Facts

- Social death describes how slaves were cut off from recognized kinship and political belonging.
- Domestic slavery was more common across world history than plantation slavery.
- Slave societies could exist without productive plantation slavery if slaves were still central to social and political life.
- Gang labour in Brazilian sugar production was profitable because enslaved workers were violently driven.
- Atlantic and Islamic trades both moved millions of Africans, but they often demanded different age and sex groups.
