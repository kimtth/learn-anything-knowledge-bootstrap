## Introduction

![[attachments/teaching_001.webp|420]]

*Teacher and students in a classroom*

Teaching ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Teaching)) is the profession of giving instruction, especially in elementary schools, secondary schools, and universities. By membership it is the world's largest profession; in the late 20th century there were about 30 million teachers worldwide.

Teachers' roles vary by country, but differences within a country are often greater than differences between countries. The most useful division is by the age and level of students: primary-school teachers, secondary-school teachers, and university teachers.

Elementary teachers are the largest group, making up nearly half of teachers in some developed countries and three-fourths or more in some developing countries. University teachers are the smallest group but often have the strongest claim to older professional status.

Teaching shares the main criteria of a profession: formal training, specialized knowledge, certification or validation of membership, and standards of intellectual, practical, and ethical performance. Yet teaching young children was not widely considered a profession before the 20th century.

Earlier elementary teaching was often treated as an art or craft. Many teachers were young, undertrained men and women who had been good pupils and learned by observing their own teachers. Only university professors and some elite secondary teachers were likely to be regarded like doctors, lawyers, or clergy.

## Salaries and Status

Teacher salaries for elementary and secondary teachers have often been low, especially before 1955. In early 20th-century industrialized countries, many teachers earned little more than semiskilled labourers.

Primary teachers who were women and had limited academic preparation often received especially low pay. In poorer countries, village teachers could earn far less than teachers in selected city schools. Low salaries often forced teachers to take second jobs or combine teaching with family and household duties.

Moonlighting has been more common where school days are shorter or teaching loads are lighter. Male teachers were often more likely than women to add outside work, especially summer employment or second jobs during the school year.

Within primary and secondary education, status mobility is limited. A classroom teacher usually remains a classroom teacher unless moving into administration or specialties such as curriculum, counselling, or special education. University teaching has more formal rank progression, such as instructor, assistant professor, associate professor, and professor.

## Geographic Mobility

University teachers are highly mobile within and between countries. They often belong to international professional organizations and may think of themselves as part of a worldwide scholarly profession.

Primary and secondary teachers have less geographic mobility. Licensing rules, language barriers, and national school systems limit movement across borders. Countries with teacher shortages, including some African nations and India, have sometimes recruited North American and European teachers and granted licenses.

Within national systems, mobility depends on administrative structure. In centralized systems such as [[France]] or England, teachers may be licensed for the whole system and move more easily, while in local or state-based systems mobility may be more restricted.

## Professional Identity

The public-school teacher's professional identity developed slowly. In 1901, Boston educator James P. Monroe argued that most schoolteachers outside colleges did not yet have the training, corporate self-respect, broad education, or professional responsibility of doctors, lawyers, or engineers.

Later observers such as Martinus J. Langeveld described ambivalent teacher stereotypes: limited independence or social courage on one side, but industriousness, intellectual interest, achievement motivation, and love of teaching children on the other.

Events of the 1960s helped teachers become more socially and politically active as a group. The idea that teaching is simply a woman's profession also weakened, though many industrialized countries still had more women than men at the primary level.

The sex ratio among teachers varies widely. Some European traditions included many male rural teachers, while other systems feminized primary teaching strongly. Globally, teaching includes large numbers of both men and women.

## Building the Profession

![[attachments/teaching_002.webp|420]]

*Teacher training classroom*

After [[World Wars (World War II)|World War II]], many countries had to create or rebuild teaching professions. Some were old civilizations becoming modern, such as India and China; some were tribal societies becoming nations, as in central Africa; and Israel was a religious society becoming a modern state.

New countries often copied school systems from older nations but had to staff them with local human resources. This produced many training patterns, emergency solutions, and uneven professional standards.

Professionalization required improved teacher education, certification, career structures, salaries, supervision, curricula, and public respect. The struggle differed between mass primary schooling and older university systems.

## Teacher Roles

Teachers fill many school roles: instructor, disciplinarian, evaluator, guide, counselor, substitute parent, organizer, curriculum worker, bureaucrat, scholar, researcher, and member of a teachers' organization.

Teachers also fill community roles: public servant, representative of middle-class morality, expert in knowledge or skills, community leader, and agent of social change. In societies where teaching is not fully professionalized, teachers may perform fewer of these roles.

Role conflicts are common. A disciplinarian role may conflict with being a student confidant, a creative scholar may conflict with being a bureaucrat, and a moral representative may conflict with being an agent of social change. Teachers must shift flexibly among roles.

## Curriculum and Autonomy

The curriculum is shaped by families, governments, churches, religious authorities, business interests, and economic authorities, all of which care about how children and youth are formed.

In developed societies, these groups employ teachers to do educational work and negotiate expectations with them. The more professional teachers become, the more autonomy they demand and receive within agreed goals and methods.

Elementary teachers are expected to teach basic mental skills such as reading, writing, and arithmetic. They also teach facts and attitudes favourable to the nation, church, or other institution supporting the school.

Secondary and university teachers usually teach more specialized subjects, but they also serve social, moral, civic, and institutional purposes. This makes teaching both a knowledge profession and a public role.

## Tenure and Recognition

![[attachments/teaching_003.webp|420]]

*Historical classroom scene*

Classroom teachers gradually won [[Land Reform and Tenure (Land Reform)|tenure]]. Early teachers were often employed on annual contracts and had no guarantee of reappointment. Later, contracts renewed automatically unless notice was given before the next school year.

As school systems became more organized, many adopted life tenure or tenure until retirement after two or three years of satisfactory service. Under tenure, teachers could be removed only for specific reasons such as incompetence or moral misconduct, and they had the right to a formal hearing.

Teacher activists such as Margaret Haley fought for professional recognition, better conditions, and public-school reform. Public-school teachers' professional history combined upgraded training with struggles over salary, tenure, public respect, and collective power.

University teachers followed a different path. Because higher education served a smaller population and required fewer instructors, university faculties often formed inner circles selected by university authorities. Their professional recognition was less tied to mass union struggle.

## Associations and Unions

University teachers often join scholarly associations by discipline, such as groups in chemistry, psychology, sociology, gerontology, and human development. Selective prestige associations, including academies and national societies, honour leading scholars and promote professional causes.

International associations give university teaching a cosmopolitan character. In the late 20th century, meetings and exchanges worked to bridge gaps between former communist countries and European-North American scholarly networks.

Most countries have major teachers' organizations, sometimes voluntary and sometimes obligatory. Examples include the National Union of Teachers in England, the Japanese Teachers Union, the Federation Generale d'Enseignement in France, and the Australian Teachers Union.

In the former Soviet Union, teachers' unions were divided by educational level and provided pensions, vacation pay, sick leave, and other welfare benefits. In other countries, teacher organizations changed according to gender, school level, headmaster status, technical education, and political structure.

Teachers' unions and associations support salary bargaining, tenure rights, pensions, professional standards, legal protections, working conditions, and public education policy. They can also become political actors in debates about school reform and social change.

## Key Events & Developments

- 1901: James P. Monroe criticized weak professional standards among schoolteachers.
- 1955: Teacher salaries rose sharply in some countries after long periods of low pay.
- 1960s: Teachers became more socially and politically active in many places.
- Post-World War II: New and newly independent countries built or rebuilt teaching professions.
- Tenure systems: Replaced annual insecurity with protected professional status in many school systems.

## Key People

- James P. Monroe: Boston educator who argued that teachers needed broader education and stronger professional responsibility.
- Martinus J. Langeveld: Dutch professor of education who described ambivalent occupational stereotypes of teachers.
- Margaret Haley ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Margaret_Haley)): Teacher activist associated with struggles for recognition and reform.
- Carl Sandburg ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carl_Sandburg)): Praised Haley's victories against political and financial interests.
