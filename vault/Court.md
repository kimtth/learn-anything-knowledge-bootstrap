# Court

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> Courts are institutions that apply law, settle disputes, determine legal responsibility, and review governmental action. Their procedures and authority are central to justice, constitutional order, and the rule of law.

## Introduction

Courts ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Court)) are the institutions where disputes are heard and decided under judicial authority. They fit within the judicial branch, relate to the legislative and executive branches, and organize legal power differently in common-law and civil-law systems.

The word court can name the judging body, the building, the hearing room, or the institution that resolves civil, criminal, ecclesiastical, and military disputes.

The central issue is legitimacy. Courts usually lack money and coercive force, yet their decisions are obeyed because people accept their right to decide disputes. Marbury v. Madison ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marbury_v._Madison)) is the classic example of judicial review, which extends to lower courts and other legal traditions.

This weakness gives courts a special dependence on procedure, public reason, and acceptance by officials who must carry judgments into effect.

Procedure gives judicial authority a visible form. Notice, hearings, evidence rules, impartial judges, reasoned opinions, appeal, and enforcement all help distinguish adjudication from private force or political command. Courts therefore depend on habits of legality as much as on written constitutional design.

## Judicial legitimacy

Legitimacy is the foundation of judicial power. A court must be seen as a proper source of decisions if its judgments are to be accepted, and that makes public trust as important as formal authority.

This idea also explains why courts can influence politics without force. Their decisions matter because they are treated as final, lawful, and binding within the system that created them.

## Judges

Judges are the people who direct proceedings, interpret law, and give the court its practical authority. The role of a judge can range from a highly specialized professional office to a more limited local function, depending on the legal system.

Judges do not work alone. They depend on lawyers, clerks, bailiffs, probation officers, and, where used, jurors, but the judge remains the person who turns procedure into a legal decision.

The contrast between a rural justice of the peace and a national supreme-court justice shows how wide the judicial role can be within one legal order.

## Lay judges

In some legal systems, especially at lower levels, lay judges or magistrates play an important role. These officials may not be full-time legal professionals, but they can still handle large numbers of ordinary cases effectively.

Their presence shows that courts are not always reserved for elite specialists. In many places, community service and everyday justice depend on people who combine local knowledge with limited judicial powers.

## Professional judges

Civil-law systems usually rely more heavily on professionally trained judges than common-law systems do. These judges are often career officials whose legal training and institutional role make them central to the administration of justice.

Professional judging gives the court more continuity and expertise, especially in complex or routine matters. At the same time, it can make the system feel more bureaucratic and less jury-centered than the common-law model.

## Court personnel

The court depends on many supporting roles, including lawyers, clerks, bailiffs, probation officers, and jurors where juries exist. The court is an organized institution rather than a single room or a single official.

These supporting people help move a case from filing to judgment. Their work shows that justice is a collective process that depends on order, record keeping, and procedure.

## Common-law and civil-law systems

Common-law and civil-law courts organize legal authority differently. Common-law systems tend to rely more on precedent, adversarial procedure, and juries, while civil-law systems tend to rely more on trained judges and codified law.

The comparison makes clear that courts are shaped by history as much as by legal theory. Each system has its own way of balancing expertise, authority, and fairness.

## Judicial review and precedent

Judicial review ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Judicial_review)) explains how courts can invalidate laws or executive actions that conflict with a constitution. In that role, the court is not simply applying law; it is also testing the law against higher legal standards.

This power makes courts a major check inside constitutional government. It also shows why courts matter even when they have little coercive force: their decisions can change what the rest of government is allowed to do.

In the United States this power is not limited to the highest court. Lower courts also decide constitutional questions in ordinary civil and criminal proceedings, including disputes over speech limits, segregation rules, or the legality of searches.

In England, by contrast, part-time lay magistrates dispose of most ordinary criminal cases, while professional judges focus more on serious crimes and civil matters. This institutional contrast illustrates different ways legal systems distribute judicial authority.

## Key Events & Developments

- medieval period: The court develops from a ruler's household into an institution that also handles law, ceremony, and administration.
- early modern period: Courts become more formal, with separate procedures for civil and criminal justice.
- modern period: Courts grow into central organs of the state, balancing legal authority with public procedure and appeal.

## Key People

- Henry II: Often linked with the growth of common-law courts in England.
- William the Conqueror ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_the_Conqueror)): Represents the royal control from which many court traditions developed.
- Sir Edward Coke ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sir_Edward_Coke)): Associated with the legal independence of the courts and the common law.
- Judges: The officials who turn the court into a place of legal judgment rather than only royal ceremony.
