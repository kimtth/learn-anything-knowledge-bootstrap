## Introduction

A virus is a small infectious agent with simple composition that can multiply only inside living cells of animals, plants, or bacteria. The word comes from Latin terms meaning slimy liquid or poison. Viruses are not visible with an ordinary light microscope, do not grow on artificial media, and require special methods for study.

The biological nature of viruses became clear through late 19th-century work on tobacco mosaic disease. In 1892, Dmitry I. Ivanovsky showed that the disease could pass through filters that trapped bacteria. In 1898, Martinus W. Beijerinck argued that the agent was a new kind of infectious entity, which he called contagium vivum fluidum ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Virus)). In 1915 and 1917, Frederick W. Twort and Felix H. d'Herelle identified bacteriophages, viruses that infect bacteria.

The study of human viruses required new hosts and laboratory systems. In 1933, Wilson Smith, Christopher H. Andrewes, and Patrick P. Laidlaw transmitted influenza to ferrets. In 1941, George K. Hirst detected influenza virus in chicken-embryo tissues by its ability to agglutinate red blood cells. Later cell-culture techniques made modern virology possible.

## Definition

![[attachments/viruses_002.webp|420]]

Viruses occupy a special taxonomic position. They are not plants, animals, or prokaryotic bacteria, and in a strict sense they are not free-living organisms. They cannot reproduce, make proteins, generate energy, or carry on metabolism without a host cell.

All true viruses contain nucleic acid, either DNA ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/DNA)) or RNA, and protein. The infectious extracellular form is the virion. At least one viral protein forms a protective shell, or capsid, around the nucleic acid. Some viruses contain internal enzymes, and some have a surrounding lipoprotein envelope.

Viruslike agents show how minimal infectious systems can be. Viroids are disease-causing particles that contain nucleic acid without structural proteins. Prions are mainly protein and are associated with degenerative brain diseases in mammals, including humans. True viruses remain quintessential parasites because they must use host ribosomes, enzymes, energy systems, membranes, and raw materials.

## Structure and Replication

Viral shape depends on nucleic acid, capsid proteins, and sometimes an envelope. Helical viruses, such as tobacco mosaic virus, assemble protein subunits around a strand of nucleic acid, forming rigid or flexible rods. Polygonal viruses are often icosahedral, with a twenty-sided capsid made from repeating protein units called capsomeres. Complex viruses may contain internal proteins not visible on the surface.

Many viruses also have a lipoprotein envelope around the nucleocapsid. Envelopes often contain glycoprotein spikes that help the virus attach to host cells. Enveloped viruses are usually more sensitive to drying, heat, detergents, and solvents than nonenveloped viruses, because damage to the envelope can destroy infectivity.

Viruses multiply by entering a cell, releasing or exposing their genetic material, producing viral nucleic acids and proteins through host machinery, assembling new virions, and leaving the cell. Some leave by killing the host cell; others bud through membranes and acquire envelopes. Because viral replication uses host systems, antiviral treatment must target viral processes without severely damaging host cells.

## Lysogeny and Latency

Some bacterial and animal viruses can remain dormant in infected cells. In lysogeny, viral DNA becomes integrated into the host chromosome and is copied as the host cell divides. The dormant viral form is a prophage. The classic example is lambda virus in Escherichia coli. Stress such as ultraviolet light can activate the prophage, leading to viral replication and destruction of the host cell.

Lysogeny can transfer genetic material between bacteria. If a prophage exits the host chromosome incorrectly, it may carry host genes into new bacterial cells. This process helps explain how viruses can affect evolution and bacterial traits.

In animals and humans, latency describes viruses that remain in tissues after initial infection and later reactivate. Herpesviruses are especially important latent viruses. Herpes simplex virus type 1 causes recurrent cold sores; type 2 causes recurrent genital infections; cytomegalovirus can become serious during other illnesses or immune weakness; and varicella-zoster virus causes chickenpox before later reappearing as shingles.

## Disease, Prevention, and Treatment

Viruses were first known through disease, but most viruses infecting bacteria, plants, animals, and humans do not necessarily cause illness. Some bacteriophages transfer useful genetic information between bacteria, and plant or animal viruses may move genes between related hosts. Modern biotechnology uses viral vectors to carry functional genes into cells, raising hopes for repairing genetic defects.

Disease depends on both the virus and the host. Some viruses cause short acute illnesses, some cause persistent or chronic infections, and some can transform cells into cancers. Acute viruses often rapidly damage cells or shut down host protein and nucleic-acid synthesis. Others persist silently in tissues and reactivate much later.

Prevention includes sanitation, clean water, waste disposal, personal cleanliness, and vaccination. Edward Jenner's 1796 use of cowpox to prevent smallpox became one of medicine's great successes; in 1980, the World Health Organization declared smallpox eliminated. Rabies remains hard to eradicate because it persists in wild animal reservoirs. Influenza is difficult because its antigens change through frequent minor mutations and larger periodic shifts.

Antibiotics do not work against viruses. Antiviral drugs must inhibit viral replication without harming host-cell functions. Neuraminidase inhibitors can act against influenza, especially early in infection. Other antiviral agents include ribavirin, acyclovir, and zidovudine, or AZT. Interferons, proteins produced by vertebrate cells, can inhibit viral multiplication and have antiviral and possible anticancer importance.

## Evolution and Classification

![[attachments/viruses_003.webp|420]]

*Electron micrograph of influenza A virus particles*

Viruses were once thought to be primordial life-forms, but this is unlikely because they lack the machinery needed for independent life. They probably evolved from cellular genes or mobile genetic elements that acquired the ability to move between cells. Plasmids, cellular RNA transcripts, reverse transcription, and proto-oncogene similarities all support the idea that viruses came from pieces of cellular nucleic acid.

Viruses can also evolve rapidly and cross species barriers. Animal viruses may jump into new hosts and cause severe disease. Coronaviruses, influenza viruses, and other RNA viruses illustrate how mutation and host switching can create new outbreaks.

Classification is based mainly on nucleic acid type, genome structure, capsid shape, and envelope. The first division separates DNA viruses from RNA viruses. DNA and RNA viruses are further divided by single- or double-stranded genomes, segmented or nonsegmented genomes, positive- or negative-sense RNA, rodlike or icosahedral nucleocapsids, and presence or absence of an envelope.

The basic taxonomic group is the family, usually ending in -viridae. Important DNA virus families include Poxviridae, Adenoviridae, Herpesviridae, Hepadnaviridae, Papillomaviridae, Parvoviridae, and Polyomaviridae. Important RNA virus families include Picornaviridae, Togaviridae, Flaviviridae, Coronaviridae, Orthomyxoviridae, Paramyxoviridae, Rhabdoviridae, Filoviridae, Arenaviridae, Bunyaviridae, Retroviridae, and Reoviridae.

Examples show the medical range of these families. Picornaviridae include polioviruses and rhinoviruses. Orthomyxoviridae include influenza viruses. Paramyxoviridae include measles, mumps, and respiratory syncytial viruses. Rhabdoviridae include rabies virus. Filoviridae include Marburg and Ebola viruses, which can cause severe hemorrhagic fevers. Retroviridae include lentiviruses that cause AIDS in humans and related diseases in animals.
