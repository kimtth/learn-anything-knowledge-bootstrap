## Weather and Climate

Weather is the short-term state of the atmosphere at a particular place: temperature, humidity, cloud, wind, air pressure, visibility, and precipitation. [[Climate and Weather (Climate)|Climate]] describes the typical range, pattern, and variability of weather over many years, commonly measured across periods of about 30 years. A cold week does not disprove a warming climate, and a hot day does not by itself prove climate change; weather is an event, while climate is the statistical background in which events occur.

Most weather occurs in the troposphere, the lowest layer of the atmosphere. The Sun heats Earth unevenly because of latitude, seasons, cloud cover, surface type, and day length. Warm surfaces heat air from below, causing it to expand and rise; cooling causes air to become denser and sink. These differences in temperature and density create pressure differences, and air moves in response to them, producing wind.

## Water, Clouds, and Precipitation

Water vapour is essential to weather because it carries energy. Evaporation absorbs heat from land or water, while condensation releases heat when water vapour forms cloud droplets or ice crystals. Rising air cools, and if it reaches saturation, condensation can create clouds. For precipitation to form, droplets or crystals must grow large enough to fall through the air as rain, snow, sleet, or hail.

Air can be lifted in several ways. Convection occurs when warm air rises from a heated surface, often producing showers and thunderstorms. Frontal lifting occurs where warm and cold air masses meet. Orographic lifting occurs when wind is forced up a mountain slope, producing wetter windward sides and drier rain-shadow regions. These processes explain why a location’s terrain and proximity to water strongly affect its typical weather.

## Pressure Systems and Storms

A low-pressure system usually involves rising air, cloud formation, and unsettled weather, while a high-pressure system often involves sinking air and clearer conditions. Earth’s rotation deflects moving air through the Coriolis effect, causing large-scale winds to curve. The direction differs between hemispheres, and friction near the surface modifies the pattern further.

Midlatitude weather is organized by fronts and jet streams. A cold front advances when denser cold air pushes beneath warm air, often producing a narrow band of showers or storms. A warm front usually rises more gently over cooler air and can bring widespread cloud and steady precipitation. Jet streams are narrow corridors of strong wind high in the atmosphere; their bends and waves help steer storm systems and influence heat, cold, drought, and heavy rainfall.

## Forecasting and Climate Connections

Weather forecasting combines observations from surface stations, balloons, aircraft, radar, satellites, ocean buoys, and computer models. Models use physical laws to estimate how the atmosphere will evolve, but forecasts become less certain as small errors grow over time. Forecasters therefore communicate probabilities and likely impacts, not only a single temperature or rain prediction.

Oceans influence weather by storing and moving heat. El Niño–Southern Oscillation changes Pacific sea-surface temperatures and atmospheric circulation, shifting rainfall and storm patterns far from the tropical Pacific. Climate change adds energy and moisture to the atmosphere and ocean, affecting heat waves, heavy rainfall, drought risk, wildfire conditions, sea level, and the intensity of some storms. It does not create every event, but it changes the conditions under which weather develops.

## Key Events & Developments

- 19th century: telegraphs allowed weather observations to be shared quickly enough for early forecasting.
- 20th century: numerical models, radar, and satellites transformed forecasting and storm monitoring.
- 21st century: climate research increasingly connects warming trends with changing weather risks.

## Key People

- Robert FitzRoy: helped establish early organized weather forecasting in Britain.
- Vilhelm Bjerknes: advanced the physical and mathematical study of weather systems.
- Meteorologists: combine observations, models, and local knowledge to communicate weather risk.

## Interesting Facts

- Thunderstorms can form when warm moist air rises rapidly and becomes unstable.
- [[Radar]] estimates precipitation and storm motion by measuring reflected radio waves.
- Forecast uncertainty grows with time because small atmospheric differences can lead to different outcomes.
