# Chemical Compounds

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> Chemical compounds are distinct substances formed when two or more elements combine in fixed compositions and structures. Their classification, naming, bonding, functional groups, synthesis, spectroscopy, and reaction patterns provide the practical language for understanding substances from simple salts to pharmaceuticals and polymers.

## Introduction

Chemical compounds ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemical_compound)) are substances made of identical molecules that contain atoms of two or more elements. The formula of a compound tells which elements are present and in what relative numbers, as in methane and water. Compounds may be molecular, as in methane, or ionic, as in sodium chloride, which does not contain molecules but an extended array of ions.

This basic definition shows how chemistry links atoms into a huge range of substances with different properties. The key idea is that a compound is not just a mixture of elements; it is a new substance with its own structure, composition, and reactivity.

## Types and Classification

Compounds vary widely in physical state, colour, toxicity, and usefulness, so chemists classify them to make the diversity manageable. One broad distinction is between molecular and ionic compounds, but there are also many other ways to organize the subject, including the difference between organic and inorganic compounds.

Within inorganic chemistry, families such as oxides, hydrides, sulfides, nitrides, and phosphides appear. In organic chemistry, common subclasses include alcohols, carboxylic acids, amines, aromatic compounds, and many more. This classification is a practical map for understanding properties and reactivity, not a rigid taxonomy.

## The Periodic Table and Bonding

![[attachments/chemical_compounds_002.webp|420]]

*Periodic table of the chemical elements*

Compound formation can be understood through the periodic table. Metals in the lower left of the table tend to lose electrons and form cations, while nonmetals in the upper right tend to gain electrons or share electrons in covalent bonds. Electronegativity increases across a period and decreases down a group, which helps explain why some pairs of elements form ionic compounds and others form polar covalent compounds.

Examples such as cesium fluoride and hydrogen halides show that large electronegativity differences favour ion transfer, while more moderate differences favour polar sharing. Atomic size is another important factor, because size affects how strongly nuclei attract outer electrons and therefore how likely an atom is to react.

## Naming Ionic and Molecular Compounds

![[attachments/chemical_compounds_001.webp|420]]

*Sodium chloride crystal structure showing sodium and chloride ions*

There are clear rules for naming ionic compounds. The cation is named first, then the anion, and simple anions end in -ide. If a metal can form more than one cation, Roman numerals are used to show the charge, as in iron(II) chloride and iron(III) chloride. Older names such as ferric and ferrous are also mentioned as historical alternatives.

For binary molecular compounds, a prefix system is used. The first element keeps its full name, the second is named like an anion, and prefixes indicate the number of atoms. Compounds of nitrogen and oxygen illustrate this, with names such as dinitrogen monoxide, nitrogen monoxide, and dinitrogen pentoxide distinguishing one compound from another.

## Polyatomic Ions and Acids

Other compounds contain polyatomic ions, especially ammonium and the oxyanions. Series such as chlorite, chlorate, perchlorate, and hypochlorite are named according to the number of oxygen atoms they contain. Naming compounds that include these ions follows the same basic logic as naming simpler ionic compounds.

Acids are compounds that can release hydrogen ions. If the anion contains no oxygen, the acid name begins with hydro- and ends with -ic, as in hydrochloric acid. If oxygen is present, the naming depends on whether the anion ends in -ate or -ite, giving names such as sulfuric acid, sulfurous acid, phosphoric acid, and nitrous acid. This system shows how chemical names can carry structural information.

## Organic Compounds

Organic compounds are, in general, carbon compounds and are central to life and industry. Foods, proteins, hormones, fibres, fuels, paints, drugs, rubbers, dyes, plastics, and pesticides all show the enormous practical range of organic chemistry. The carbon atom is special because it forms strong bonds to itself and to many other elements, allowing chains, rings, and branched frameworks.

Organic chemistry also has a short but important history. For a long time, organic and inorganic compounds were thought to belong to separate worlds, partly because organic substances were linked to living organisms. That idea gave way as chemists learned to synthesize organic compounds in the laboratory and to treat them by the same rational methods used for inorganic chemistry.

## Functional Groups and Reactivity

The central organizing idea in organic chemistry is the functional group. Molecules with the same functional group tend to react in similar ways because the group imposes a characteristic pattern of electron distribution and bond strength. Strong, nonpolar C-C and C-H bonds make simple hydrocarbons relatively unreactive, while polar bonds or weak bonds create sites where reactions can begin.

Major functional groups include alkanes, alkenes, alkynes, aromatic hydrocarbons, thiols, amines, halides, aldehydes, and ketones. Each group is linked to a familiar pattern of structure and reactivity, such as addition to double bonds, the characteristic smell of thiols, the basicity of amines, or the reactivity of carbonyl compounds.

## Chemical Synthesis

Chemical synthesis is the construction of complex compounds from simpler ones. It has three main purposes: industrial production, the confirmation of natural-product structures, and the creation of new compounds for testing theories or exploring properties. Synthetic chemistry is both practical and intellectual, because it supports manufacturing and also deepens structural understanding.

Synthetic routes are guided by functional-group reactivity. This means that chemists do not simply join atoms at random; they choose reaction pathways that exploit predictable bond changes, allowing them to build target molecules step by step.

## Spectroscopy and Structure

Before modern instrumentation, chemists had to infer structure from reactions and simple physical properties. Spectroscopy changed this by letting chemists learn about structure through the absorption, scattering, and emission of electromagnetic radiation. X-ray crystallography can determine precise structures for crystalline substances, but spectroscopy is more generally useful because it works for many kinds of molecules.

Spectroscopy is a major tool for identifying molecules and distinguishing compounds that may otherwise appear similar. This marks a major shift in chemistry from indirect structural inference to instrument-based structural analysis.

## Reactions of Organic Compounds

Organic compounds undergo several major reaction types. In hydrolysis, water breaks a molecule apart and becomes part of the products, as in ester hydrolysis. In condensation, two molecules combine and a small molecule such as water is eliminated, and many polymerization reactions belong to this class.

In acid-base reactions, a proton transfers from an acid to a base, often producing a salt. In oxidation-reduction reactions, carbon atoms change electron density by gaining or losing bonding partners such as oxygen or hydrogen. These reaction classes show how organic chemistry can be organized by a few recurring patterns of bond change.

## Conclusion

Chemical compounds are the practical heart of chemistry: once elements are connected into compounds, the resulting substances can be named, classified, synthesized, analyzed, and transformed. The major themes are bonding, structure, function, and reactivity, with naming systems and analytical tools providing the language needed to work across the entire field.

## Core Equations

Chemical formulas encode element ratios and conservation of atoms in reactions:

$$\mathrm{2H_2 + O_2 \rightarrow 2H_2O}$$

This balanced reaction shows hydrogen and oxygen combining to form water while conserving atoms on both sides.

$$\mathrm{CH_4 + 2O_2 \rightarrow CO_2 + 2H_2O}$$

Read the left side as the reactants or starting materials and the right side as the products; the coefficients show the proportions needed to conserve atoms or charge.

## Key Events & Developments

- 18th century: Chemistry begins to classify compounds by composition and reaction behaviour.
- 19th century: Periodic relationships and valence theory reshape understanding of compound formation.
- mid-20th century: Spectroscopy becomes a major tool for determining molecular structure.
- 20th century: Organic synthesis expands into industrial polymers, pharmaceuticals, and new natural-product work.

## Key People

- Linus Pauling ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linus_Pauling)): His electronegativity scale underlies the modern understanding of chemical bonding.
- Friedrich Wohler ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Friedrich_Wohler)): His laboratory synthesis of organic compounds helped break down the old divide between organic and inorganic chemistry.

## Interesting Facts

- Sodium chloride is ionic and does not contain discrete NaCl molecules.
- Benzene helped define the special chemistry of aromatic compounds.
- More than nine million organic compounds are known.
- Functional groups are the main reason a molecule’s reactivity can be predicted from its structure.
