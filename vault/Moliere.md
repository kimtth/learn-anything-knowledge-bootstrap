# Moliere

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> Moliere was the French playwright and actor whose comedies made social hypocrisy, pretension, and self-deception into subjects of theatrical criticism. His career, major plays, contested public position, comic theory, and influence explain his decisive role in French classical drama.

## Introduction

![[attachments/moliere_001.webp|420]]

*Portrait of Moliere*

Moliere was the stage name of Jean-Baptiste Poquelin, the greatest writer of French comedy. He was both actor and playwright, and his comic art joined traditional forms with a new double vision of truth and falseness, intelligence and pedantry, ordinary sense and absurdity.

## Early Life and Training

Moliere was baptized in [[Paris]] on January 15, 1622, and died there on February 17, 1673. Sacred and secular authorities often opposed him, but his plays eventually won lasting acclaim.

He was educated at the College de Clermont, later the Lycee Louis-le-Grand. His father expected him to inherit a royal household appointment, but in 1643 Moliere renounced that path and joined others in founding the Illustre-Theatre.

The young company failed financially, and Moliere was twice imprisoned for debt in 1645. For about 13 years the troupe toured the provinces, appearing in places such as Nantes, Toulouse, Lyon, Montpellier, and Beziers. These years trained him as actor, manager, playwright, and negotiator with audiences and authorities.

## Return to Paris

Moliere's early plays included L'Etourdi and Le Depit amoureux. His return to Paris in 1658 brought a performance before Louis XIV at the Louvre, where a short comic entertainment helped win favour from Philippe, duc d'Orleans.

After 1658, reliable facts about Moliere centre on theatre work rather than private life. He was not an author in the usual literary sense; he wrote plays to be acted, often published only to prevent piracy, and left several unpublished.

## Career and Major Plays

![[attachments/moliere_002.webp|420]]

Moliere's career was a struggle to keep actors, audiences, and support. He held his company together through technical skill and force of personality while competing with other theatres.

Les Precieuses ridicules first showed his Parisian comic method. It mocked affected cultural pretension through provincial girls deceived by valets posing as refined masters. The play's contrast between false elegance and plain speech pointed toward his later comedy.

The company moved to the Palais-Royal in 1661. A heroic comedy, Dom Garcie de Navarre, failed, but that failure helped push Moliere toward the sharper social and psychological comedy of Le Misanthrope.

L'Ecole des femmes, first performed in 1662, caused scandal and confirmed his comic genius. Its pedant, Arnolphe, tries to control a young woman by keeping her ignorant, but his plan collapses when natural feeling and awakened temperament defeat his artificial system.

Moliere married Armande Bejart in 1662. Their marriage was unhappy and later became the subject of hostile gossip, but reliable evidence is limited.

Moliere answered attacks on L'Ecole des femmes with stage discussion plays, especially La Critique de L'Ecole des femmes and L'Impromptu de Versailles. These works defended his new comedy and realistically presented rehearsal-room life.

The greatest conflict came with Tartuffe ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tartuffe)), first staged in partial form in 1664. Religious opposition blocked public performance for years because the play portrayed a pious hypocrite who enters a bourgeois household, manipulates its master, and attempts seduction under cover of devotion.

Dom Juan also scandalized audiences. Its aristocratic hero rejects obligations to parents, tradesmen, doctors, and God, while expecting others to serve him. His servant Sganarelle forms an earthy and superstitious opposite, like a French counterpart to Don Quixote and Sancho.

Moliere faced unstable receipts, unpaid subsidies, rival authors, unreliable actors, illness, and censorship. In 1666 to 1667, when Tartuffe ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tartuffe)) and Dom Juan were blocked and the theatre closed after the Queen Mother's death, he wrote five plays in one season.

Le Misanthrope was quickly treated as a masterpiece by discerning viewers. Its central figure, Alceste, has high principles but is blind to himself, while Celimene embodies the flexible intelligence of society.

In 1668, L'Avare transformed the old comic miser into a disturbing figure whose worship of money suggests cruelty, loneliness, and near insanity. George Dandin, often dismissed as farce, explores a fool who may be right but cannot prove it.

In 1669, Tartuffe ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tartuffe)) finally received permission and became the greatest success of Moliere's career. Its final version expands the comic paradox of a man who acts like an ascetic while being driven by ordinary lust and appetite.

Moliere's last years produced Monsieur de Pourceaugnac, Le Bourgeois Gentilhomme, Psyche, Les Fourberies de Scapin, Les Femmes savantes, and Le Malade imaginaire ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Imaginary_Invalid)). These works ranged from social climbing and learned pretension to medical jargon, superstition, greed, and charlatanry.

## Death and Dramatic Art

Le Malade imaginaire ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Imaginary_Invalid)) was Moliere's final play. During its fourth performance on February 17, 1673, he collapsed on stage and died soon after. Because he had not formally renounced acting or received the sacraments, he was buried quietly after sunset.

As an actor, Moliere wanted tragic success but was naturally comic. His flexible face, energy, mimicry, and instinct for turning incidents into scenes shaped his writing. He wrote roles he could act: choleric masters, servants, foolish bourgeois figures, henpecked husbands, and superstitious old men.

His plays should not be reduced to arguments or doctrines. Moliere's theatre animates absurdity rather than preaching reason. His real strength lies in dramatic suggestion, verbal energy, stage violence, and the rhythm of discussion more than plot.

## His Theory of Comedy

Moliere's defenses of Tartuffe ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tartuffe)) helped define his comic theory. The comic is the visible form attached to what is unreasonable, so people can recognize and avoid it. Incongruity, disguise, deception, contradiction, and the gap between appearance and reality are central to comedy.

## A New Kind of Comedy

Moliere's new comedy is only incidentally funny; it depends on a double vision of wisdom and folly, right and wrong, nature and pretense. He mixes registers so characters play roles, overplay them, forget them, or expose themselves by speaking out of turn.

## Style and Legacy

French critics often value Moliere's formality, poetry, and intellectual penetration. His works are not simple realism or psychological case studies; they are patterned fantasies of speech, movement, music, dance, and social contradiction.

Moliere repeatedly opposes social polish to natural humanity. Dialogues often begin politely and end in insults, revealing the person beneath the veneer. His comedy lives where wit, nature, reason, and absurd fact collide.

## Key Events & Developments

- 1622: Jean-Baptiste Poquelin, later Moliere, was baptized in [[Paris]].
- 1643: He broke from his expected career and joined the Illustre-Theatre.
- 1645 to 1658: Provincial touring trained him as actor-manager and dramatist.
- 1662: L'Ecole des femmes caused scandal and confirmed his comic originality.
- 1664 to 1669: Tartuffe ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tartuffe)) faced censorship before becoming his greatest success.
- 1673: Moliere collapsed during Le Malade imaginaire ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Imaginary_Invalid)) and died in [[Paris]].

## Key People

- Moliere ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moliere)): French actor-playwright who reshaped comedy through dramatic incongruity and stage vitality.
- Madeleine Bejart ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Madeleine_Bejart)): Actress who helped lead Moliere into theatrical enterprise.
- Armande Bejart ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Armande_Bejart)): Moliere's wife and an actress associated with roles in his mature plays.
- Louis XIV ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Louis_XIV)): King whose favour and court demands shaped Moliere's Paris career.
- Philippe, duc d'Orleans: Royal patron whose support helped Moliere's company after its return to Paris.
- Jean Racine ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jean_Racine)): Young playwright briefly staged by Moliere's company before moving to a rival theatre.

## Interesting Facts

- Moliere wrote plays for performance and did not treat them primarily as printed literature.
- Tartuffe ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tartuffe)) was blocked for years but became his greatest theatrical success.
- He died after playing a sick man in Le Malade imaginaire ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Imaginary_Invalid)) while actually ill.
- French critics often see his plays as formal poetic patterns, not merely social satires.
- Later comic artists, including Charlie Chaplin, have often been compared to Moliere.
