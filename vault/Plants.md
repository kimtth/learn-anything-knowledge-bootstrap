## Introduction

Plants ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plant)) are multicellular eukaryotic members of kingdom Plantae, generally characterized by photosynthetic nutrition, localized unlimited growth, cellulose-containing cell walls, stationary existence, absence of nervous systems, and life histories alternating haploid and diploid generations. They range from tiny duckweeds to giant sequoias and include roughly 275,000 to 300,000 described species.

Plants evolved from aquatic ancestors, colonized nearly all terrestrial environments, and in some cases returned to fresh or salt water. They sustain life by photosynthesis, converting solar energy into organic chemical energy and releasing oxygen. Human life depends on plants for food, flavourings, wood, fibres, medicines, insecticides, fuels, resins, oils, rubber, soil protection, water regulation, and atmospheric quality.

More than half of humanity depends heavily on the grass crops rice, corn, and wheat, showing how one plant family became central to global food supply.

## Bryophytes

[[Bryophytes]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bryophyte)) are nonvascular plants with dominant gametophyte generations. Mosses, class Musci, include true mosses and peat mosses such as Sphagnum. Their leafy gametophytes have phyllids, caulids, rhizoids, and sporophytes with foot, seta, capsule, calyptra, operculum, and peristome. Liverworts, class Hepaticae, may be leafy or thalloid and have unicellular rhizoids. Hornworts, class Anthocerotae, have ribbonlike gametophytes and hornlike sporophytes.

Sphagnum peat mosses are commercially important because they hold large amounts of water and can protect plant roots during shipment or replanting. Liverworts preserve an older medicinal name from the doctrine of signatures, while hornworts release spores gradually as their slender sporophytes split and twist with humidity changes.

## Vascular Plants

Vascular plants ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vascular_plant)), or tracheophytes, possess xylem for water and support and phloem for food conduction. Unlike bryophytes, their dominant generation is the sporophyte, and they have true stems, leaves, and roots. Cuticles reduce water loss, stomata regulate gas exchange, roots absorb minerals and water, stems conduct materials, and leaves capture sunlight. Vascular plants include ferns, gymnosperms, and flowering plants.

The evolution of xylem and phloem allowed plants to grow taller and live away from constant surface water, making large terrestrial vegetation possible.

Vascular tissue also changed competition for light. Taller sporophytes could lift leaves above neighbours, disperse spores or seeds more widely, and create shaded habitats beneath them. This helped produce forests, soils rich in organic matter, and complex terrestrial ecosystems in which plants became the main architects of habitat.

## Ferns and Lower Vascular Plants

[[Ferns and Other Lower Vascular Plants (Fern)|Ferns]], division Filicophyta, are diverse vascular plants common in tropical and subtropical regions. They often have rhizomes, coiled fiddleheads, fronds, sori, sporangia, haploid spores, and free-living gametophytes. Whisk ferns, lycopods, spike mosses, quillworts, horsetails, and scouring rushes represent other lower vascular lineages, some descended from formerly dominant Carboniferous swamp-forest groups.

## Seed Plants

Seed plants include gymnosperms and angiosperms. They share dominant sporophytes, vascular tissue, roots, stems, leaves, and photosynthetic pigments, but reproduce by seeds. Seeds contain an embryo, food-storage tissue, and a protective seed coat, making them a major evolutionary innovation. Secondary growth from vascular cambium produces wood and allows many seed plants to become large.

The seed protects the young sporophyte and can delay growth until conditions favour establishment, giving seed plants an advantage over spore-only reproduction.

## Gymnosperms

[[Gymnosperms]] have naked seeds exposed on cone scales and include conifers, cycads, ginkgo, and gnetophytes. Conifers are among the largest and oldest living organisms and are economically important for lumber, pulpwood, turpentine, and resins. Cycads are ancient seed plants; Ginkgo biloba is the sole surviving ginkgophyte; gnetophytes include Ephedra, Gnetum, and the desert plant Welwitschia.

## Angiosperms

[[Angiosperms]], or flowering plants, evolved about 130 million years ago from gymnosperm ancestors. Their defining feature is the flower, with ovules enclosed in carpels. Enclosed seeds, animal pollination, efficient vascular tissues, and nutritive endosperm helped flowering plants dominate modern floras. Monocots and dicots differ in cotyledons, vascular-bundle arrangement, venation, pollen, secondary growth, and flower-part numbers.

## Stems, Leaves, Roots, and Flowers

Angiosperm stems may form rhizomes, tubers, corms, bulbs, runners, and stolons for storage, dormancy, or asexual reproduction. Woody dicots develop bark, wood, vascular cambium, rays, and often annual rings. Leaves are primary photosynthetic organs, while roots anchor plants, absorb water and minerals, and store food. Flowers are modified shoots with sepals, petals, stamens, carpels, ovaries, styles, stigmas, ovules, and varied sexual arrangements.

Leaf and stem anatomy help distinguish major angiosperm groups. Dicot stems usually arrange vascular bundles in a ring and may form secondary wood, while monocot stems scatter vascular bundles through the ground tissue and usually lack a functional vascular cambium. Dicot leaves commonly have netted veins, while monocot leaves more often show parallel venation.

## Life Histories and Reproduction

*Angiosperm life cycle diagram showing sporophyte and gametophyte stages*

Land plants have haplodiplontic life histories with alternating multicellular gametophyte and sporophyte phases. Development includes mitosis, meiosis, fertilization, cytoplasmic division, and nuclear fusion. Land plants are oogamous, with nonmotile eggs and usually motile sperm in many groups. In vascular plants, the sporophyte became dominant, and in seed plants the gametophyte became highly reduced and protected.

## Nutrition and Minerals

Plants are mostly oxygenic photoautotrophs, using light energy, carbon dioxide, and water to synthesize sugars while releasing oxygen. They also synthesize amino acids and vitamins from fixed carbon and inorganic elements. Essential macroelements include carbon, hydrogen, oxygen, nitrogen, potassium, calcium, magnesium, phosphorus, and sulfur. Microelements include iron, chlorine, manganese, boron, copper, molybdenum, zinc, and nickel.
Plant roots take up minerals according to solubility, soil chemistry, root surface area, and the mobility of ions through soil water.

## Photosynthesis

[[Photosynthesis]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Photosynthesis)) occurs in chloroplasts containing pigments, cytochromes, quinones, enzymes, thylakoids, grana, and stroma. Light reactions use photosystems I and II to make ATP and NADPH, split water, and release oxygen. Dark reactions, especially the Calvin-Benson cycle, use ATP and NADPH to fix carbon dioxide into sugars. These processes link plants to food chains, oxygenation, and fossil-fuel formation.

## Ecology and Biomes

Plants occur in patterns shaped by [[Climate and Weather (Climate)|climate]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Climate)), history, soils, disturbance, and dispersal. Forests are major reservoirs of carbon, biodiversity, nutrient cycling, and energy-water exchange. Boreal coniferous forests, tropical moist forests, tundra, taiga, grasslands, prairies, steppes, savannas, deserts, and alpine vegetation form broad terrestrial communities. Primary and secondary succession replace disturbed or newly available habitats, though severe tropical deforestation can cause long-term impoverishment.

The boundaries between biomes can shift when temperature, water supply, snow cover, or fire patterns change. Forest gives way to tundra at cold limits, to grassland at drier margins, and to desert under stronger aridity. Tropical forests may not recover normally after large clearings because the surrounding landscape no longer provides the conditions needed for ordinary succession.

## Dispersal, Islands, and Domestication

Plant distribution depends on dispersal by wind, water, animals, spores, seeds, and historical continental change. Pangea's breakup and isolation of continents produced endemism; volcanic islands develop floras from chance colonists. Charles Darwin's Galapagos observations helped inspire evolutionary theory. Domestication, beginning 10,000 years ago or more, transformed plants such as manioc, corn, beans, squash, chili, tomato, avocado, papaya, cotton, sisal, and vanilla into human-dependent cultigens.

Some plants are timed to disturbance: fire-adapted cones may remain closed until heat opens them, releasing seeds into a cleared habitat with less competition.

## Conservation and Climate Change

Plants are threatened by climate change ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Climate_change)), fossil-fuel combustion, deforestation, land-use change, methane, nitrous oxide, and CFCs. Rapid warming may shift climatic zones faster than forests and communities can regenerate, disrupting agriculture and ecosystems. Conservation works best through ecosystem management, preserving large areas of indigenous vegetation and successional landscapes rather than focusing only on single endangered species.

The source emphasizes the scale of warming risk: a 1 C temperature change can correspond to roughly 60 to 100 miles of latitudinal shift in middle latitudes. If plant communities cannot migrate or regenerate quickly enough, soil decay may release still more carbon dioxide and methane, creating feedback that accelerates atmospheric change.

## Evolution and Fossils

Plant evolution is recorded in fossils of external forms, cellular structures, pollen, spores, and sometimes chemical or ultrastructural features. Land-plant evidence dates at least to the Ordovician, and vascular plants appear by the Late Silurian or Early Devonian. Early plants such as Cooksonia and Rhynia had forking axes, terminal sporangia, stomata, and simple conducting tissues. Later evolution produced leaves, roots, wood, forests, heterospory, seeds, and flowering plants.

## Classification

Modern plant classification is phylogenetic, grouping plants by common ancestry rather than only superficial similarity. Major living groups include Bryophyta, Lycophyta, Sphenophyta, Filicophyta, gymnosperm divisions, and angiosperms. Monocots include grasses, orchids, palms, and lilies; dicots include many woody and herbaceous flowering plants. Molecular evidence continues to revise plant relationships.

Older systems divided plants into cryptogams and phanerogams, but current treatments usually elevate major plant lineages to divisional rank.
