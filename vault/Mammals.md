# Mammals

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> Mammals are vertebrates distinguished by milk-producing glands, hair, specialized jaw and ear structures, a diaphragm, and characteristic reproductive strategies. Their diversity, behavior, ecology, anatomy, and evolutionary classification explain their success across terrestrial and aquatic habitats.

## Introduction

![[attachments/mammals_001.png|420]]

*Mammal phylogenetic tree*

Mammals ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mammal)) are vertebrates whose young are nourished by milk from mammary glands. They are also marked by hair, a lower jaw hinged directly to the skull, three middle-ear bones, a diaphragm, a single left aortic arch, and mature red blood cells without nuclei. Except for egg-laying monotremes, mammals bear live young; marsupials complete much development attached to the mother, while placental mammals develop longer in the womb.

## Diversity

![[attachments/mammals_002.png|420]]

*Mammal middle ear anatomy*

Mammalian diversity ranges from tiny bats and shrews to the blue whale ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blue_whale)), which can exceed 30 metres and 180 metric tons. About 5,000 living species occupy land, air, trees, burrows, fresh water, and seas. Rodents are the most numerous order, while the aardvark alone represents Tubulidentata. Tropical continents hold the greatest diversity, and isolation shaped regional faunas such as South American mammals, Australian monotremes and marsupials, and Holarctic mammals across North America and Eurasia.

Mammals are deeply tied to human history through food, clothing, transport, labour, laboratory research, veterinary medicine, sport hunting, furs, ivory, rhinoceros horn, ambergris, crop damage, livestock predation, and disease transmission. Conservation has become urgent because the IUCN reported by the early 21st century that nearly one-quarter of mammals faced extinction risk, mainly from habitat destruction and hunting. The Steller's sea cow, discovered in 1741 and gone by 1768, shows how quickly human use could eliminate a species.

Natural history emphasizes endothermy, parental care, learning, flexible behaviour, and adaptive specialization. Since the Cenozoic began about 65.5 million years ago, mammals radiated from small active terrestrial or semiarboreal predators into tree, ground, aerial, underground, freshwater, and marine forms.

## Reproduction

Female reproductive cycles are controlled by pituitary and ovarian hormones. The estrous cycle includes follicle growth, estrogen-driven uterine preparation, ovulation, corpus luteum formation, progesterone secretion, and either implantation support or return to a nonreproductive state. Primates have a related menstrual cycle, while monotremes lay yolky shelled eggs and marsupials have short intrauterine development before tiny young attach to teats.

Placental mammals form a true placenta, where embryonic and maternal tissues exchange nutrients and gases without actual blood mixing. Gestation ranges from about two weeks in golden hamsters to 21-22 months in African elephants. Young may be precocial, as in hares and grazing mammals, or altricial, as in rabbits, carnivores, and many rodents. Milk from mammary glands supplies fat, protein, lactose, vitamins, and salts, with composition varying sharply across species.

## Behaviour

Mammalian young depend on maternal nourishment, allowing extended training and nongenetic transfer of information. Learning, memory, brain complexity, and flexible response to short-term environmental change helped mammalian success. Some mammals are mostly solitary, while others form reproductive or defensive groups with dominance hierarchies, ritualized behaviour that reduces fighting, sexual dimorphism, mating priority for dominant males, and play that trains social behaviour.

## Territoriality

A home range is the area used by an individual, while a territory is the defended part of that range. Mammals often mark territories with urine or gland secretions, as lemurs do. Even species without formal territories may fight to maintain individual distance, producing more economical spacing within available habitat.

## Ecology

Mammals respond to environmental stress through acclimatization, behaviour, and physiology. Torpor, hibernation, and estivation reduce energy use; true hibernation lowers body temperature, breathing, circulation, and peripheral blood supply. Bears show winter dormancy rather than true hibernation. Summer estivation answers heat, drought, or food shortage, while burrows and other microhabitats protect many desert mammals.

Migration is another response. Temperate bats migrate or hibernate, caribou move from tundra to forest edge, whales and seals travel between polar and temperate waters, gray whales migrate to calving grounds near South Korea and Baja California, and elk or bighorn sheep make elevational movements. Lemmings and snowshoe hares may disperse irregularly when population pressure strains food supplies.

## Populations

Mammal populations include prereproductive, reproductive, and postreproductive ecological ages. Growth depends on recruitment, emigration, death, and survival to breeding age. Some rodents mature before 4 weeks, gestate for three weeks or less, and may overlap pregnancy with lactation. Larger mammals generally live longer, while some bats reach nearly 20 years, captive echidnas more than 50, horses more than 60, elephants more than 80, and bowhead whales perhaps 200 years or more.

## Locomotion

Mammalian locomotion includes plantigrade walking, cursorial running, saltatory leaping, graviportal support in elephants and rhinoceroses, climbing, gliding, brachiation, swimming, and true flight. Bats are the only truly flying mammals. Cursorial mammals may be digitigrade, as dogs are, or unguligrade, as horses are. Aquatic sirenians, cetaceans, and pinnipeds evolved swimming adaptations independently, with cetaceans completely dependent on water for support and insulation.

Food habits also radiated widely. Carnivores often serve as top predators, herbivores as primary consumers, shrews eat heavily to support high metabolism, and blue whales eat krill. Ant and termite specialists such as echidnas, numbats, anteaters, aardvarks, and pangolins show convergent evolution. Australian marsupials filled many placental-like niches, and bats diversified into insect, fruit, nectar, fish, vertebrate, and blood-feeding forms.

## Dentition

Dentition reflects feeding specialization. Primitive mammal teeth had sharp cusps for flesh or insect exoskeletons. Herbivores evolved complex cheek teeth, expanded crowns, gaps in tooth rows, grinding premolars, and sometimes continuously growing teeth. Omnivores such as bears, pigs, and humans have low rounded bunodont molars. Myrmecophagous mammals often evolved strong claws, long skulls, extensible tongues, reduced jaws, and simplified or lost teeth.

## Skeleton

Mammalian bones grow through epiphyses at long-bone ends, producing strong joints before maturity and determinate length growth. The axial skeleton protects the brain, spinal cord, heart, and lungs, while the appendicular skeleton includes limbs and girdles. Most mammals have 7 neck vertebrae; atlas and axis specialize head movement, ribs form a thoracic basket, sacral vertebrae fuse for pelvic attachment, and tail vertebrae vary widely.

The mammalian skull expanded around a larger brain, formed a secondary palate for breathing while chewing or suckling, developed paired occipital condyles, turbinal bones, and auditory bullae, and moved reptilian jaw bones into the middle ear as malleus and incus. The lower jaw is a single dentary bone articulating with the squamosal. Limbs began as five-toed structures but changed through reduction, fusion, or specialization for running, climbing, swimming, and other movements.

## Muscles

Mammalian muscles generally resemble reptilian patterns, but skin and jaw muscles changed strongly. The panniculus carnosus moves skin independently, helping with insect irritation, shivering, and in some groups facial expression. Jaw muscles divided into temporalis and masseter functions, with the masseter especially important for forward-backward grinding in rodents and other mammals.

## Viscera

The digestive tract varies with diet, especially in herbivores. Ruminants such as cattle and deer have up to four stomach chambers, ceca house bacteria that break down cellulose, and some rabbits, hares, mountain beavers, and insectivores practice coprophagy to recover nutrients from specialized fecal pellets. Mammalian kidneys use nephrons, loops of Henle, and active resorption; desert rodents can conserve water through concentrated urine, and mammals excrete protein waste as urea.

Male reproduction includes testes cooled in the scrotum, ducts, accessory glands, semen, and an erectile penis, sometimes with a baculum. Female tracts vary from duplex to simplex uteri in placentals, paired structures in marsupials, and cloacal reproductive openings in monotremes. Mammals have complete separation of heart ventricles, myogenic cardiac rhythm, enucleate red blood cells, diaphragm-driven negative-pressure breathing, laryngeal sound production, complex endocrine regulation, and an expanded neopallium linked to learning and intelligent response.

## Mammal

Mammal phylogeny traces mammals to therapsid synapsids of the Triassic, about 251 million to 200 million years ago. Mammal traits evolved at different rates in a mosaic pattern, including heterodont teeth, improved limb mechanics, a secondary palate, endothermy, hair, diaphragm, four-chambered heart, anucleate red cells, and the shift from reptilian jaw bones to middle-ear ossicles. For fossils, the dentary-squamosal jaw joint is often used to mark Mammalia.

## Classification

Classification ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taxonomy)) uses anatomy, physiology, serology, genetics, and fossils. Teeth and jaws are especially important because enamel preserves well and dental form records feeding radiation. The fossil record is comparatively good for mammals and can trace some lineages, such as horses, in detail.

## Don E. Wilson

The classification modified from McKenna and Bell recognizes class Mammalia with almost 5,000 species in 29 orders. Subclass Prototheria includes egg-laying monotremes: echidnas and the platypus. Subclass Theria includes live-bearing Metatheria, or marsupials, with nearly 300 species, and Eutheria, or placental mammals, with about 4,700 species.

## Superorder Australidelphia

Australidelphia includes nearly 200 marsupial species, such as kangaroos, koalas, wombats, possums, carnivorous marsupials, bandicoots, bilbies, marsupial moles, and the monito.

## Superorder Ameridelphia

Ameridelphia includes about 80 marsupial species, mainly opossums and shrew or rat opossums. Eutherian mammals include large orders such as rodents, bats, shrews and moles, primates, even-toed ungulates, cetaceans, odd-toed ungulates, and many others.

## Uranotherians

Uranotherians group hyraxes, manatees and dugongs, and elephants as closely related ungulates. Other placental orders include aardvarks, carnivores, pikas and rabbits, armadillos, anteaters and sloths, tree shrews, elephant shrews, and pangolins.
