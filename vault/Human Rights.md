# Human Rights

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> Human rights are moral and increasingly legal claims that protect the values, capabilities, agency, and well-being people need because they are human. Their modern international development after World War II addresses exploitation, persecution, oppression, deprivation, and the conditions of a just society.

## Introduction

Human rights ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Human_rights)) are rights that belong to individuals or groups simply because they are human, because humans are vulnerable, or because such rights are necessary for a just society. They refer to values and capabilities that protect human interests, support human agency, and are claimed as universal for all human beings, present and future.

The idea of human rights begins from two observations: people everywhere need certain values and capabilities for individual and collective well-being, and those needs are often frustrated by exploitation, oppression, persecution, and deprivation. Human rights are therefore both moral claims and, increasingly, legal claims developed through national and international processes.

## Historical development

The phrase human rights became common only after [[World Wars (World War II)|World War II]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_War_II)), the founding of the [[United Nations]] in 1945, and the adoption of the Universal Declaration of Human Rights ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Universal_Declaration_of_Human_Rights)) in 1948. It replaced older language such as natural rights and the rights of Man, partly because natural law became controversial under legal positivism and because the rights of Man did not clearly include women.

Ancient [[Greece]] and [[Rome]] helped form early ideas through Stoicism, natural law, and the belief that some principles of justice applied beyond local citizenship. Later religious and philosophical traditions, including medieval Christian thought, connected law with moral order. These older sources did not create modern human rights law, but they supplied ideas of universal obligation and human dignity.

The modern language of rights gained power in struggles against absolutism. The American Declaration of Independence of 1776 announced rights to life, liberty, and the pursuit of happiness. The French Declaration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen of 1789, shaped in part by Marquis de Lafayette, declared that men are born and remain free and equal in rights and that political association exists to preserve natural and imprescriptible rights.

The doctrine of natural rights also had critics. Edmund Burke feared that abstract rights claims could encourage social upheaval, while David Hume and Jeremy Bentham attacked natural rights from other directions. Bentham famously dismissed them as nonsense upon stilts, arguing that declarations could substitute for effective legislation.

## Debates over legitimacy and priority

Modern human rights debates often ask which rights are legitimate and which should have priority. Civil and political rights, such as liberty, expression, voting, and due process, have sometimes been contrasted with economic, social, and cultural rights, such as work, health, education, and social welfare. Other debates oppose universalism to cultural relativism, or individual rights to collective values.

These rights debates can be useful when they expose how liberty and individualism have excused capitalism or Western expansion, or how equality, collectivism, and culture have excused authoritarian government. But one-sided positions are dangerous. A credible human rights movement must support broad sharing of values and capabilities, because real societies need both freedom and welfare, both individual protection and collective conditions for dignity.

The legitimacy-priority debate also fails to match reality when it treats rights as separable blocks. In practice, civil, political, economic, social, and cultural rights depend on one another. Speech matters less without education; political participation is weak without basic security; welfare programs can become oppressive without liberty and accountability.

## Developments before World War II

*Am I Not a Man and a Brother? abolitionist medallion*

Before [[World Wars (World War II)|World War II]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_War_II)), international concern for rights grew through several separate streams. One was the International Law of State Responsibility for Injuries to Aliens, which developed rules for the treatment of foreigners. Thinkers such as Francisco de Vitoria, Grotius, and Emmerich de Vattel argued that aliens as well as nationals had certain natural rights and deserved fair treatment.

Another stream was humanitarian intervention and minority protection. In the 19th and early 20th centuries, military actions, diplomatic pressure, treaties, and declarations sought to protect oppressed minorities in the Ottoman Empire, Syria, Crete, the Balkans, Romania, Russia, central and eastern Europe, and the Middle East. Motives were not always pure, but these actions helped internationalize concern for persecuted groups.

The fight against [[Slavery|slavery]] became another foundation of international human rights. Treaties beginning with the 1814 Treaty of Paris and culminating in the 1926 International Slavery Convention brought major commercial powers into efforts to suppress slavery and the slave trade. At the same time, the International Committee of the Red Cross inspired agreements to protect victims of war and limit the conduct of hostilities, forming modern international humanitarian law.

Labour rights also entered international law. Multilateral labour conventions began in 1906, and the International Labour Organisation, established in 1919, promoted standards on industrial health and safety, hours of work, paid holidays, and later broader social concerns. These efforts showed that rights could involve workplace conditions and social welfare, not only political liberty.

## The United Nations and human rights

The UN Charter made human rights a central purpose of the [[United Nations]]. It called for friendly relations based on equal rights and self-determination and for international cooperation in promoting respect for human rights and fundamental freedoms without distinction of race, sex, language, or religion. Member states pledged joint and separate action toward these purposes.

The Charter also contained limits. At the [[San Francisco]] conference, proposals for direct enforcement of human rights were rejected, and the Charter preserved domestic jurisdiction except when the Security Council found a threat to peace, breach of peace, or act of aggression. This produced continuing legal and political debate over whether the UN had only promotional authority or stronger standing to press states on human rights.

UN responsibility for human rights spread across several bodies. The General Assembly, its Third Committee, the Economic and Social Council, the Human Rights Council, the UN High Commissioner for Human Rights, the Secretariat, specialized agencies, and other commissions all became involved. The Trusteeship Council ended active operations in 1994 after Palau became independent.

## The UN Commission on Human Rights and the Human Rights Council

The UN Commission on Human Rights served from 1946 to 2006 as the UN's main human rights policy organ. For its first two decades it believed it lacked authority to handle complaints, so it focused on standard-setting. Its most important products included the Universal Declaration of Human Rights ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Universal_Declaration_of_Human_Rights)), the International Covenant on Economic, Social and Cultural Rights, and the International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights, together known with protocols as the International Bill of Human Rights.

From 1967, the Commission was explicitly authorized to deal with violations. It developed working groups, special rapporteurs, special representatives, special committees, and other procedures to investigate gross violations, evaluate country situations, and study thematic issues. In the 1990s, it gave more attention to economic, social, and cultural rights, development, adequate living standards, and the rights of women, children, minorities, indigenous peoples, and vulnerable groups.

By the early 21st century, the Commission was widely viewed as ineffective, partly because states with poor rights records could become members. It was replaced in 2006 by the UN Human Rights Council, a subsidiary body of the General Assembly. The Council was charged with strengthening protection and promotion worldwide and with discussing human rights situations throughout the year.

The Human Rights Council adopted an institution-building package in 2007. Its mechanisms include the universal periodic review, which assesses every UN member state; an Advisory Committee for thematic advice; a complaint procedure for individuals and civil-society organizations; and special rapporteurs, independent experts, working groups, and representatives who report on country situations and themes.

## The Universal Declaration of Human Rights

The Universal Declaration of Human Rights ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Universal_Declaration_of_Human_Rights)) was adopted without dissent by the UN General Assembly on December 10, 1948. It includes civil and political rights such as equality before law, protection against arbitrary arrest, fair trial, freedom of thought, conscience, religion, opinion, expression, peaceful assembly, association, and property.

The UDHR also includes economic, social, and cultural rights: the right to work, form and join trade unions, rest and leisure, an adequate standard of living, health, well-being, and education. It is not a treaty and was intended as a common standard of achievement rather than a direct legal obligation. Even so, many of its provisions gained greater legal importance through use by states, courts, and UN bodies.

## The International Covenant on Economic, Social and Cultural Rights

The International Covenant on Economic, Social and Cultural Rights opened for signature on December 16, 1966, and entered into force on January 3, 1976. It elaborates rights from the UDHR, including work, trade unions, health, and education. Unlike many civil and political rights, these rights were generally to be achieved progressively, according to maximum available resources.

The ICESCR still imposed an immediate duty of nondiscrimination based on race, colour, sex, language, religion, political or other opinion, national or social origin, property, birth, or other status. States report to UN bodies on steps and progress. In 2008, an Optional Protocol created an individual-complaints mechanism through the Committee on Economic, Social and Cultural Rights.

## Optional Protocols

The International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights opened for signature on December 19, 1966, and entered into force on March 23, 1976. It elaborates civil and political rights from the UDHR, including nondiscrimination, but excludes the UDHR rights to property and asylum. It also includes the self-determination of peoples and minority rights to culture, religion, and language.

The ICCPR created the Human Rights Committee, whose independent experts review state reports and may handle interstate allegations when states accept that procedure. The First Optional Protocol allows individual complaints by people under the jurisdiction of states that accept it. The Second Optional Protocol, adopted in 1989, concerns abolition of the death penalty.

Other UN instruments expanded human rights protections. Important conventions addressed racial discrimination in 1965, discrimination against women in 1979, torture in 1984, and the rights of the child in 1989. These treaties elaborated the International Bill of Human Rights and created specialized monitoring systems.

## Human rights beyond the UN

Human rights also developed outside the main UN treaty system. The Helsinki process began with the Conference on Security and Co-operation in Europe in Helsinki in 1973 and concluded with the Helsinki Accords in 1975. The conference linked East-West security, borders, cooperation, human rights, fundamental freedoms, and self-determination during the Cold War.

Regional systems further shaped human rights law. European, Inter-American, African, and other regional arrangements developed treaties, commissions, courts, and complaint procedures suited to their regions. These systems did not replace universal standards but added forums, enforcement methods, and regional interpretations.

## Key Events & Developments

- 1215: Magna Carta became an early symbol of legal limits on authority.
- 1776: The American Declaration of Independence proclaimed unalienable rights.
- 1789: The French Declaration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen declared freedom and equality in rights.
- 1814: The Treaty of Paris began major treaty efforts against the slave trade.
- 1919: The International Labour Organisation was established.
- 1945: The [[United Nations]] was founded with human rights among its purposes.
- 1948: The Universal Declaration of Human Rights ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Universal_Declaration_of_Human_Rights)) was adopted.
- 1966: The ICESCR and ICCPR opened for signature.
- 1975: The Helsinki Accords linked security and human rights.
- 2006: The UN Human Rights Council replaced the Commission on Human Rights.
