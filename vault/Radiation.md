# Radiation

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> Radiation is energy carried by electromagnetic waves or moving particles whose interactions with matter produce physical, chemical, biological, and technological effects. The source connects quantum absorption and emission, ionization, particle tracks, dose, and applications from vision and photosynthesis to medicine and communications.

## Introduction

Radiation ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radiation)) is the flow of atomic and subatomic particles and of waves such as heat rays, light rays, and X rays. Matter is constantly exposed to radiation from cosmic and terrestrial sources. Radiation transfers energy to its surroundings, and that transfer can produce physical, chemical, and biological effects.

Radiation has two broad forms. [[Electromagnetic Radiation|Electromagnetic radiation]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electromagnetic_radiation)) travels at the speed of light in free space and includes radio waves, microwaves, infrared rays, visible light, ultraviolet rays, X rays, gamma rays, and neutrinos. Particulate radiation ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Particle_radiation)) includes electrons, protons, neutrons, alpha particles, and other particles that have rest mass and become radiation when moving at high speed.

Living matter depends on some forms of radiation, as in plant photosynthesis and animal vision, but unusual kinds or high doses can injure organisms. Radiation is therefore both a normal part of life and a source of hazard. Its applications in medicine, research, industry, photography, surface treatment, and communications depend on controlled energy transfer.

## Energy States and Interaction with Matter

Atoms and molecules have ground states and higher energy states fixed by quantum mechanics. When radiation is absorbed, matter is excited to a higher state; when energy is emitted, it falls to a lower state. Allowed transitions between states determine which wavelengths or particles can be absorbed or emitted.

If absorbed energy is high enough, molecules can dissociate into smaller products or free radicals. Atoms and molecules can also ionize, producing ions and electrons. Atomic nuclei may absorb and emit gamma rays or become unstable enough to emit positrons, electrons, alpha particles, neutrons, neutrinos, or split into lighter nuclei.

The interaction of radiation with matter is one of the most important processes in nature. Early in cosmic history, energy transfer helped create conditions in which atoms and simple molecules such as water, ammonia, and methane could form. Later, more complex molecules and life-related chemistry depended on radiation-driven and radiation-influenced processes.

## Electromagnetic Radiation and Light

![[attachments/radiation_001.webp|420]]

*Electromagnetic spectrum diagram showing wavelengths and radiation types*

[[Electromagnetic Radiation|Electromagnetic radiation]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electromagnetic_radiation)) can be described as fields, waves, or particles depending on wavelength, photon energy, and experimental scale. The distinctions are classical and practical rather than fundamental, because quantum theory treats all electromagnetic radiation through photons and wave-particle duality.

Ordinary light behaviour such as straight-line travel, reflection, refraction, and image formation belongs to geometrical optics. More unusual effects such as interference, diffraction, polarization, the photoelectric effect, Compton scattering, and pair production reveal the wave and particle aspects of light. This dual character helped lead to quantum theory.

When light crosses a boundary between media, it may be reflected, refracted, or absorbed. Dispersion means that refractive index varies with frequency, so a prism separates white light into colours. In normal dispersion, violet bends more than red; near natural electron frequencies, anomalous dispersion can appear and phase velocity may exceed light speed without carrying energy faster than light.

## Quantum Absorption and Emission

Quantum mechanics ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_mechanics)) explains why atoms and molecules absorb and emit only certain energies. Electronic states include vibrational and rotational levels, so a visible or ultraviolet transition often contains many possible sub-transitions. Selection rules describe which transitions are likely or forbidden.

As light passes through matter, its intensity decreases through absorption. Weakly absorbing media allow direct measurement of transmitted or refracted light, while strongly absorbing media may require study of reflected light. Far-ultraviolet absorption has revealed information about electronic energy levels and collective energy losses.

Spontaneous emission occurs randomly in direction and phase, while induced emission has the same direction, polarization, and propagation as incident light. If more particles occupy an upper energy level than a lower one, stimulated emission can amplify light. This is the basis of lasers, including ruby and helium-neon lasers.

## Particle Aspects of Light

The photoelectric effect, Compton effect, and pair production show light behaving as particles. If photon energy exceeds an electron's binding energy, energy can remove an electron from an atom or solid. In Compton scattering, a photon transfers part of its energy to an electron and emerges with a longer wavelength.

In pair production, a photon passing near a nucleus creates an electron-positron pair. The minimum energy is 1,020,000 electron volts, equal to the rest energy of the two particles. X rays and gamma rays often act first by producing energetic electrons, which then cause excitation, ionization, and chemical change along their paths.

## Passage of Matter Rays

Charged particles such as ions and alpha particles deposit energy along tracks as they pass through matter. If the medium is thick enough, the particle slows almost to zero and its energy is absorbed, producing physical, chemical, and biological changes. In thin matter, the particle may emerge with reduced energy.

Linear energy transfer describes how much energy is lost per unit path length. Heavy charged particles often create dense tracks of ionization and excitation, while electrons create more separated clusters called spurs. Track structure strongly affects later chemistry and biological damage.

The stopping power of a medium depends on particle charge, velocity, and the atomic properties of the absorber. Work by Hans Bethe and others used quantum mechanics to describe stopping numbers and mean excitation potentials. These formulas are important for radiation measurement, particle identification, and dose calculation.

## Range

The range of a charged particle is its total path length before stopping, while penetration is the net distance along its original direction. Heavy high-speed particles usually travel nearly straight, so range and penetration are similar. Scattering makes the difference larger for lighter particles.

Particle range can be computed from stopping-power formulas or measured experimentally. Heavy-particle ranges are well known above a critical energy of about 1 MeV per atomic mass unit, and range often follows a power law with energy. Such relations help identify particles and estimate their energies.

## Electrons

Electrons lose energy differently from heavy particles because their small mass allows large deflections and large fractional energy loss in single encounters. Electron and positron stopping powers resemble those of other charged particles at the same velocity, but electron tracks show much more scattering and straggling.

Electron range is much shorter than that of a heavy particle starting at the same velocity. Relativistic effects become important above about 1,000,000 electron volts. At very low energies, stopping-power formulas become uncertain, though electrons continue to lose energy through electronic states, phonons, and low-frequency molecular motions.

## Neutrons

A neutron is uncharged, has spin like an electron, and has a mass slightly greater than a proton. In free space it decays into a proton, electron, and antineutrino with a half-life of about 12-13 minutes, but in matter it usually disappears through nuclear interactions first.

Neutron beams can come from nuclear reactors or from radium-beryllium mixtures. Neutrons interact by elastic and inelastic scattering, absorption, capture, and production of new nuclei. Because neutron mass is comparable to light atomic nuclei, elastic collisions can transfer large energy fractions, making hydrogen-rich materials effective moderators.

## Luminescence

Luminescence is light emission from excited matter. Historically, fast luminescence was called fluorescence and slow luminescence phosphorescence. Modern usage defines fluorescence as an allowed transition, often occurring in about 10^-9 second, and phosphorescence as a forbidden transition that may take 10^-6 second or longer.

Excited molecules may luminesce, internally convert to another state, or dissociate. Fluorescence usually comes from the lowest electronically excited state after higher excitations cascade downward. Triplet states often emit more slowly, remain metastable, or combine with other triplets to form highly excited states.

## Ionization and Excited Condensed States

Ionization is extreme excitation in which an electron is ejected, leaving a positive ion. The ionization potential is the minimum energy needed. The Franck-Condon principle states that during the short time of an electronic transition, the nuclear positions in a molecule do not change much, so ions often form in excited states.

In liquids, glasses, and solids, radiation can create collective states with no gas-phase equivalent. These include excitons, polarons, charge-transfer states, and plasmons. A solvated electron is an example of an electron associated with a group of molecules while carrying its polarization field.

## Photographic Process and Photochemistry

[[Photography (Photography, History of)|Photography]] is a major effect of radiation on matter and an important scientific tool in spectroscopy, photometry, X-ray work, and high-energy particle detection. In silver halide emulsions, absorption causes a halide ion to lose an electron, which is captured by a silver ion; development then grows a silver grain related to light exposure.

Photochemistry has two central laws. The Grotthuss-Draper law says light must be absorbed to cause an effect. The Stark-Einstein law says one primary physical or chemical act occurs per photon absorbed. Quantum yield measures product molecules per absorbed photon and may be near zero, near one, or very large in chain reactions.

## Radiation Chemistry

Radiation chemistry ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radiation_chemistry)) differs from photochemistry because a high-energy particle usually produces many ionizations and excitations rather than one primary act per photon. Positive ions produce dense tracks, while high-energy electrons produce separated spurs. The spatial distribution of energy deposition shapes later chemical reactions.

In water radiolysis, ionization and excitation produce solvated electrons, hydrogen atoms, hydroxyl radicals, molecular hydrogen, hydrogen peroxide, and other reactive species. Gas-phase systems show more fragmentation, while condensed phases often show ion-molecule reactions. Yields are reported empirically as molecules produced or destroyed per 100 eV of absorbed energy.

## Surface Effects

Radiation can modify surfaces, which control corrosion, wear, friction, and coating performance. Ultraviolet curing irradiates polymer coatings and causes cross-linking, making the coating behave like one giant molecule. It can increase hardness, reduce friction, improve wear resistance, and resist acids and corrosive agents.

Ion implantation bombards solids with energetic ions, often around 100 keV, from particle accelerators. Ions penetrate thousands of angstroms depending on energy, ion type, and target. Sputtering removes surface atoms through bombardment and is important in surface analysis and industrial processing.

## Biologic Effects of Ionizing Radiation

The biomedical effects of ionizing radiation are among the most thoroughly studied environmental hazards. Concern grew because even small exposures from nuclear fallout, medical radiography, nuclear power, and other sources may carry risk. Injury depends on radiation interaction with cells and the chain of chemical and biological reactions that follows.

After Wilhelm Conrad Rontgen announced X rays in January 1896, medical use spread quickly before hazards were understood. Elihu Thomson deliberately exposed a finger and described burns. Thomas Edison's assistant Clarence Dally suffered severe radiation injury, developed cancers, and died in 1904. More than 100 early investigators and physicians later died from exposure.

Radiation can act directly on biological targets or indirectly through free radicals. Target theory compared irradiation to machine-gun fire across a field, where injury requires hits on sensitive sites such as genes or chromosomes. High-LET radiations such as neutrons, protons, and alpha particles cluster ionizations more densely than X rays or gamma rays and often have higher relative biological effectiveness.

## Skin, Bone Marrow, and Organs

Skin injury depends on dose and exposure. A dose of about 6 Gy or more may cause temporary reddening within hours, followed weeks later by deeper reddening. Larger doses may cause blistering, ulceration, hair loss, and later abnormal pigmentation.

Bone marrow is highly radiosensitive because it produces blood cells. Whole-body irradiation can depress blood-cell replacement, causing infection, hemorrhage, or both. A dose below about 0.5-1 Sv usually causes mild temporary depletion, while a rapid dose above 8 Sv often causes fatal failure of blood-cell formation.

The gastrointestinal tract is vulnerable because its lining cells divide rapidly. A rapid dose above 10 Gy to much of the small intestine can cause a fatal dysentery-like reaction. Reproductive organs are also sensitive: immature sperm-forming cells can be temporarily affected by about 0.15 Sv, and higher doses can cause sterility.

## Non-Ionizing Radiation

Non-ionizing radiation such as radio waves, microwaves, and infrared rays usually acts mainly through heating. Hertzian waves excite molecular rotations and thermal agitation, while infrared rays excite vibrational modes and may also produce fluorescence. Unsaturated fats absorb these forms preferentially.

Diathermy, internal heating by high-frequency currents or microwaves, was recognized medically by 1909. It can relieve muscle soreness and sprain but can also cause deep burns because skin heat receptors may not warn of internal overheating. Microwave radiation at high intensity can damage eye lenses and produce cataracts.

## Medical Applications

Radiation is indispensable in [[Medicine|medicine]]. Diagnostic and therapeutic uses include radio waves, visible light, ultraviolet radiation, X rays, gamma rays, electrons, fast neutrons, protons, alpha particles, and pi-mesons. Imaging and treatment both rely on controlled interaction between radiation and tissue.

X-ray imaging records rays that pass through the body after differential absorption by bones and soft tissues. Conventional radiographs overlap structures along the beam path, while computerized tomography, introduced in the 1970s, produces cross-sectional images and has become central for diagnosis and monitoring.

Radionuclides help diagnose organ structure and function. Technetium-99m, with a six-hour half-life, is useful for imaging because it emits gamma rays and spreads through tissues. Iodine-131, with an eight-day half-life, concentrates in the thyroid and helps measure thyroid function.

High-LET radiation such as neutrons, protons, mesons, alpha particles, and heavy ions has been used to treat cancer and other lesions. These radiations may damage deep tumours more strongly and be applied more precisely than conventional X rays or gamma rays, reducing injury to surrounding tissue.

## Phototherapy, Lasers, and Diathermy

Ultraviolet radiation, or Wood's light, can diagnose fluorescent materials in some skin disorders and can treat selected diseases. Visible-light phototherapy treats newborn jaundice by using wavelengths of about 420-480 nanometres to help detoxify and eliminate bilirubin during exposures of 12-24 hours.

Lasers ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laser)) provide controlled and relatively bloodless surgery. Different wavelengths can target different cells or inclusions, allowing selective destruction of lesions such as port-wine stains. Laser light can pass through the cornea and lens to treat inner-eye lesions and can be delivered by optical fibres into vessels or body cavities.

Microwave diathermy warms internal tissues to treat deep inflammations and is being explored for hyperthermia in cancer therapy. Used with radiation therapy or chemotherapy, heat may help damage tumour tissue. The same heating effect requires careful control to avoid injury.

## Applications in Science and Industry

Industrial photochemistry uses light, often ultraviolet above about 185 nanometres, to start selected reactions through glass or quartz windows. Uses include photochlorination of aromatic compounds, sulfhydration of olefins, cyclohexanone oxime production, photopolymerization, sulfoxidation, vitamin-D synthesis, and possible isotope separation with tunable lasers.

Radiation processing uses high-energy electrons from accelerators or gamma rays from cobalt-60 and sometimes cesium-137. Electrons may have energies of 0.15-10 MeV, while cobalt-60 gamma rays have about 1.25 MeV. Such radiation modifies and synthesizes materials on an industrial scale.

## Lasers

Lasers ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laser)) are useful beyond medicine. In communications, laser light can carry voice and digital information at high rates, usually through optical fibres except in some satellite-to-satellite links. Fast beam control supports optical scanners, optical disc storage, and some computer printers.

High-power laser beams can vaporize surfaces, cut, scribe, weld, and support microanalysis with laser microprobes. Concentrated pulses on frozen deuterium-tritium pellets can initiate nuclear fusion. Lasers can also selectively destroy cell organelles that absorb a chosen wavelength, allowing mitochondria, chloroplasts, and other structures to be studied.

## Core Equations

Radiation energy and radioactive decay are commonly summarized as:

$$E=h\nu$$

This relation says that photon energy is proportional to frequency; higher-frequency radiation carries more energy per photon.

$$N(t)=N_0e^{-\lambda t}$$

Exponential decay describes a quantity that decreases by a constant fraction per unit time, as in radioactive decay.

## Key Events & Developments

- 1896: Wilhelm Conrad Rontgen's X-ray discovery rapidly entered medicine, while early injuries revealed serious hazards.
- 1904: Clarence Dally died after severe X-ray injury, becoming an early warning case in radiation safety.
- 1933: Felix Bloch estimated mean excitation potentials important for stopping-power theory.
- The 1970s: CT scanning became a key medical imaging method.
- 1909: Diathermy was recognized as a possible medical use of internal heating.

## Interesting Facts

- Radiation ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radiation)) includes both massless electromagnetic waves and massive particles moving at high speed.
- Stimulated emission makes lasers possible by causing light to amplify in the same direction and phase.
- High-LET radiation can be biologically more damaging because it concentrates molecular injury along dense tracks.
- Phototherapy can treat newborn jaundice with blue-visible light absorbed by bilirubin.
