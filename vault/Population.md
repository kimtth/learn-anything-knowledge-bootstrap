## Introduction

In human biology, population means the total number of inhabitants occupying an area, such as a country or the world. It changes through additions, mainly births and immigration, and losses, mainly deaths and emigration. Like other biological populations, human populations are limited by food supply, disease, and environmental conditions.

Human populations are also shaped by social customs and technology. Customs govern reproduction, marriage, family formation, and migration. Medicine and public health have reduced mortality and extended life spans. As a result, population change affects almost every part of society, including prosperity, health, education, family life, crime, language, and culture.

The study of population is demography. Its intellectual roots reach back to the 18th century, when scholars recognized that human mortality had statistical regularities. Demography draws on economics, sociology, statistics, medicine, biology, anthropology, and history. It uses limited evidence from earlier centuries and more reliable records from recent centuries to understand past, present, and possible future populations.

At the most basic level, a closed population changes by births and deaths. An open population also changes by immigration and emigration. This simple framework becomes complex because fertility, mortality, marriage, and migration are each affected by biology, culture, technology, policy, and economic conditions.

## Fertility

Fertility ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fertility)) is the actual level of achieved reproduction, while fecundity is the biological potential to reproduce. The gap between potential and actual fertility is large because women do not all begin reproducing at puberty, some never reproduce, some are widowed or unmarried, social behaviour restrains childbearing, and couples may use abstinence, contraception, abortion, or sterilization.

The difference can be seen by comparing high-fertility groups with late 20th-century Europe and North America. Hutterite women in North America who married between 1921 and 1930 averaged about 10 children per woman. Many women in Europe and North America during the 1970s and 1980s averaged about two children, far below biological potential.

Fertility ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fertility)) is shaped by several biological factors. These include the age of potential fertility, monthly probability of conception, spontaneous abortion and stillbirth, temporary infecundability after childbirth, and permanent sterility. British data suggest that age at menarche declined from about 16-18 years in the mid-19th century to under 13 years in the late 20th century, probably because of better nutrition and health.

Fecundability is the monthly probability of conceiving without contraception. Among newlyweds it commonly lies around 0.15 to 0.25, meaning a 15-25 percent chance each month. Conception is limited by the short fertile period in each cycle, cycles without ovulation, and early pregnancy losses before pregnancy is recognized.

## Contraception, Abortion, and Sterilization

Contraception lowers fertility by reducing the probability of conception. Methods differ between theoretical effectiveness and actual use-effectiveness. Modern methods such as oral pills and intrauterine devices have use-effectiveness above 95 percent. Older methods such as condoms and diaphragms can be highly effective if used correctly, but irregular use lowers average effectiveness.

The impact of contraception can be large. If monthly fecundability is 0.20, a 95-percent effective method reduces the chance of pregnancy to about 0.01 per month. This shows why fertility can fall sharply even when biological potential remains unchanged.

Induced abortion reduces fertility by ending pregnancy rather than by changing fecundability. It has long been practiced and is common in some settings. In some countries, officially registered abortions exceed one-third of pregnancies, and unregistered abortions may be important even where reported rates are low.

Sterilization removes fecundability completely. Tubal ligation and vasectomy became common in many countries and cultures. In the United States, voluntary sterilization became the most common single means of ending fertility for couples who had reached desired family size. In India, sterilization was sometimes encouraged by incentives and briefly by quasi-coercive measures in the 1970s.

## Mortality

Mortality is central to demography because individual deaths are unpredictable, but deaths in large populations show statistical regularity. This insight helped create life assurance and the life table, which summarizes longevity patterns and allows calculations of insurance premiums for groups with known characteristics.

The main comparative measure is life expectancy at birth. Premodern populations with poor sanitation and limited health care may have had life expectancies as low as 25-30 years. Infant and child mortality were especially severe: about 20 percent of newborns might die in the first year and another 30 percent before age five.

By the 1980s, life expectancy in developing countries averaged roughly 55-60 years, with higher levels in Latin America and lower levels in Africa. In developed countries of western Europe and North America it approached 75 years, and fewer than 1 percent of newborns died in the first year.

Females usually live longer than males, and this advantage grew as overall life expectancy increased. In late 20th-century industrial market economies, women averaged about 78 years and men about 71 years. In eastern European nonmarket economies, the gap was about 74 years versus 66 years.

## The Epidemiologic Transition

The epidemiologic transition is the shift from high infant and child mortality, famine, and epidemic disease toward degenerative and human-made diseases that mainly affect older people. Earlier transitions in industrial countries were tied to rising living standards, nutrition, and sanitation.

In developing countries, 20th-century mortality decline was often more rapid and more connected to organized health care and international disease-control programs. This means that mortality could fall even without the same internal economic development that earlier accompanied European and North American transitions.

Mortality change also affects old age. In the 1970s and 1980s, industrialized countries saw unexpectedly large declines in mortality among the elderly. In the United States, the population aged 85 and older rose from 590,000 in 1950 to 2,461,000 in 1980, creating major implications for health care organization and finance.

## Infanticide

Infanticide ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infanticide)), the deliberate killing of newborn infants, has long existed in human societies. It appears in ancient Greece, Rome, and China and continued in Europe until the 19th century. European forms included smothering infants in shared beds and abandoning unwanted infants to foundling hospitals, where mortality was extremely high.

In some societies, newborns were not considered fully human until an initiation rite days or years after birth, making killing before that rite socially acceptable. Motives included child spacing, fertility control, illegitimacy, deformity, orphanhood, twin births, and sex preference. With effective fertility regulation, infanticide became strongly disapproved in most societies, though it continued in some isolated traditional cultures.

## Marriage

Marriage strongly affects fertility, especially where conscious fertility control is uncommon. In many Asian and African societies, marriage occurs soon after female sexual maturation, around age 17. In Europe, delayed marriage has long been common, with average first marriage in some countries approaching 25.

Marital dissolution also matters. Widowhood was long common, but falling mortality reduced its effect on fertility. Divorce changed in the 20th century from an uncommon exception to a major cause of marital ending in some countries, sometimes terminating more than one-third of marriages.

Together, marriage patterns can remove 20 to 50 percent of potential reproductive years. Cohabitation also rose in many Western countries. In Sweden in the 1970s, about 12 percent of couples aged 16 to 70 living together were unmarried. In the United States in 1976, such arrangements approached 1,000,000, prompting the Census Bureau to create the category POSSLQ.

Extramarital fertility also rose in many Western countries. It accounted for about one in five births in the United States and Denmark and one in three in Sweden. These changes show that fertility depends not only on biology but also on household forms and social definitions of partnership.

## Migration

Migration changes any population that is not closed. It is usually defined as a permanent change of residence, conventionally for at least one year, to distinguish it from commuting or temporary movement. Migration has shaped human history, but it has often involved violence, bondage, mass mortality, genocide, and deep suffering rather than heroic exploration.

Early humans were hunters and gatherers who moved in search of food. With tools, clothing, language, and cooperation, they spread faster than any earlier dominant species and occupied every continent except Antarctica within about 50,000 years. Moving away from tropical diseases of African origins lowered mortality and allowed population growth.

Over thousands of years, growth made new hunting grounds insufficient. Human groups shifted toward migratory slash-and-burn agriculture and then more productive farming. Crops such as wheat and barley spread from the Middle East across Eurasia within about 5,000 years, showing how migration and agriculture transformed population distribution.

## Key Events & Developments

- the 18th century: Demography emerged from recognition that mortality has statistical regularities.
- 1921-1930: Hutterite marriage cohorts showed very high realized fertility, averaging about 10 children per woman.
- the 1970s: India briefly used quasi-coercive sterilization measures, and Sweden recorded high unmarried cohabitation.
- 1950-1980: The U.S. population aged 85 and older rose nearly fourfold.
- about 50,000 years ago: Humans occupied all continents except Antarctica.

## Interesting Facts

- Population change affects almost every part of society, from education to crime patterns.
- A 95-percent effective contraceptive can reduce a 20-percent monthly pregnancy chance to about 1 percent.
- Premodern life expectancy could be as low as 25-30 years because infant and child mortality were so high.
- Infanticide ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infanticide)) was sometimes socially accepted where infants were not considered fully human before initiation.
- Human migration often involved suffering and coercion, not only exploration and settlement.
