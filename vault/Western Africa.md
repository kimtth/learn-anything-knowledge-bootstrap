# Western Africa

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> Western Africa is a region of Atlantic coast, Guinea forests, savanna, western Sudan, and Sahel that includes diverse states and societies from Benin to Senegal. The source relates climate zones, river systems, precolonial development, colonialism, and regional political and economic patterns.

## Introduction

![[attachments/western_africa_001.webp|420]]

*Sahel waters in West Africa*

Western Africa is the western region of the African continent. It includes Benin, Burkina Faso, Ghana, Guinea, Guinea-Bissau, Liberia, Mali, Mauritania, Niger, Nigeria, Senegal, Sierra Leone, and Togo. The term West Africa ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/West_Africa)) is often used in a political and economic sense, while this regional definition excludes Cameroon, Chad, Equatorial Guinea, and the Saharan parts of Mali, Mauritania, and Niger. The region covers about 3,027,675 square miles and had an estimated population of 328,123,000 in 2009.

The northern part is the western Sudan ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sudanian_savanna)), a broad semiarid belt running about 2,500 miles from the [[Atlantic Ocean]] toward Lake Chad. It is a low plateau between the Sahara in the north and the Guinea Coast forests in the south. Rainfall changes sharply from less than 10 inches in the dry north to about 50 inches in the south. Its vegetation changes from the scrub of the Sahel to tall trees and high savanna grass.

South of the western Sudan ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sudanian_savanna)), the Guinea Coast equatorial forests run along the Atlantic and extend inland for about 100 to 150 miles. Drainage in the Sahara borderlands and savannas, where rainfall allows streams, flows either south through the Niger River system or inward toward the Lake Chad basin. Important coastal and upland features include the Mauritanian-Senegal Basin, the Senegal River, the Fouta Djallon, the Guinea Highlands, the Volta River, the Niger coastal plains, the Jos Plateau, and the Cameroon Highlands.

The peoples of Western Africa form a complex ethnic and linguistic mosaic. In the northern Saharan areas, Arabs and Imazighen of the Afro-Asiatic language family predominate. South of a line following the Senegal River, the Niger River, and southern Nigeria, most people speak Niger-Congo ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Niger%E2%80%93Congo_languages)) languages. Along the middle Niger and around Lake Chad, Nilo-Saharan languages related to peoples farther east are important.

## Historical sources and precolonial development

![[attachments/western_africa_002.webp|420]]

*Djingareyber Mosque in Timbuktu*

Written evidence for Western Africa becomes more substantial around 1000 CE, after Arabs had conquered North Africa and gained access to the northern ends of the trans-Saharan trade routes. Muslim geographers and historians from about the 9th to the 14th century described the bilad al-sudan, the lands of Black peoples south of the Sahara. Their accounts are valuable but limited, because they often judged non-Islamic societies harshly, exaggerated Islamization, and focused mainly on the trading towns of the northern savanna.

The earliest firsthand account was probably by Ibn Battutah, who visited the western Sudan in 1352-53. These Muslim sources show that northern western African societies were not merely tribal. They had towns, developed agriculture, market networks, long-distance trade, monarchies, taxation, tribute, military retainers, and officials. Islam increasingly shaped trade and government, but the economic and political foundations of these states already existed before regular Muslim contact.

Early Muslim attention focused on Kanem, north of Lake Chad, and ancient Ghana, near modern Mauritania and Mali. Kanem formed in the 9th and 10th centuries through contact between Saharan nomads and farming villages. Ancient Ghana was already highly organized by the early 9th century. In the 11th century, Abu Ubayd al-Bakri described Ghana's capital as two towns, one Muslim and stone-built and one Mande and mud-built, with a royal palace, farms, wells, satellite rulers, and taxes on gold and salt trade. Archaeology at Koumbi Saleh supports the picture of large stone towns supported by agriculture and commerce.

Several explanations are given for the rise of western African kingdoms. Older traditions and colonial-era historians often stressed invasions by Saharan Imazighen, producing the so-called Hamitic hypothesis. This view overstates outside influence. Pastoralists did move south and sometimes conquered, but they more likely took over and enlarged existing local monarchies. A second explanation stresses long local development from cattle herding and farming in the Sahara by at least 4000 BCE, followed by migration into savannas as the Sahara dried between about 8000 and 2000 BCE. A third factor was long-distance trade, especially the exchange of gold, ivory, agricultural goods, and slaves for salt, copper, horses, and luxury goods. Trade wealth helped rulers enlarge their armies, tax commerce, and control wider territories.

In the 10th century, the kings of Ghana extended control over the Sanhajah, Amazigh nomads around Audaghost who supplied salt and northern goods. This upset the balance between Ghana and the desert peoples. The Islamized Sanhajah united in the Almoravid movement in the 11th century and later dominated Ghana. Their pressure, together with internal quarrels and damage to agriculture, weakened Ghana. Around 1235, under Sundiata, the Keita rulers of Mali absorbed what remained of Ghana and built a larger empire from the upper Niger River goldfields.

The Mali empire grew by using the Niger River as a route to Timbuktu and Gao, cities linked to shorter trans-Saharan roads. Mande merchants, known as Dyula, spread trade east to the Hausa city-states and south from Jenne toward goldfields near the Black Volta and modern Ghana. As Muslim traders, the Dyula spread [[Muhammad and the Religion of Islam (Islam)|Islam]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islam)) among commercial classes and rulers. Mali's kings valued Islam for diplomacy and trade. Mansa Musa, who ruled from 1307 to 1332, became famous for his pilgrimage to [[Mecca and Medina (Mecca)|Mecca]] through [[Egypt]].

Mali's strength depended on control of the middle Niger River and the Songhai, who controlled fishing and canoe transport. In the 15th century, Mali lost Gao. Sonni Ali, ruling from 1464 to 1492, used traditional Songhai power and continuous campaigns to destroy Mali and create a new empire from Gao. After him, al-Hajj Muhammad Askia, a Mande Muslim general, ruled from 1493 to 1528. Songhai was strong eastward and influenced the Hausa states, while Kanem-Bornu revived near Bornu after earlier decline.

The Songhai empire reached north to the Taghaza salt mines, near Morocco. This threatened the balance of trans-Saharan trade, and in 1591 the Sadi dynasty of Morocco sent about 4,000 soldiers across the Sahara. Firearms and Songhai internal divisions helped the invaders capture Gao, Timbuktu, and Jenne. The Moroccans could not control the full trade network, and over time Tuareg, other Saharan groups, and the mixed military caste known as the Arma gained influence.

The Sudanic kingdoms affected peoples far beyond the savanna. The Fulani, a pastoral people speaking a Niger-Congo ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Niger%E2%80%93Congo_languages)) language, spread from the western Sudan toward the Senegal valley, Fouta Djallon, Macina, Hausaland, Bornu, and the Cameroon uplands. Some settled in towns or became Muslim clerics, while most kept pastoral life and exchanged animal products for crops and manufactured goods. Their scattered position later made them central to Islamic reform movements.

Mande influence also moved south. The Susu, after losing to the Keita in the struggle over Ghana's inheritance, moved toward modern Guinea and northern Sierra Leone. Other Mande traders and conquerors formed small kingdoms among West Atlantic peoples. In the 16th century, the Mane advanced from Liberia westward, conquered villages, created new ruling groups, and helped form or reshape peoples such as the Mende, Loko, and Temne.

Dyula trade in gold, slaves, kola nuts, salt, cloth, and northern goods helped transform modern Ghana and Cote d'Ivoire. By about 1500, Dyula merchants reached the coast of modern Ghana. Akan chiefs expanded political authority to control gold and kola routes. Forest areas became more settled, and villages became tributaries of ruling groups. States such as Bono, Banda, Bonduku, and Gonja show how trade encouraged new kingdoms, while rivalry with Akan and Dagomba powers limited Mande political control.

Eastern Guinea and modern Nigeria had older and partly independent centers of development. The Nok culture of central Nigeria used agriculture, iron, and other metals by about 500 BCE. Its terra-cotta art is linked to later court sculpture in Yorubaland and Benin. Ife was remembered as a center of monarchy and art, and archaeology shows urbanization in Yorubaland by at least the 11th century. Igbo Ukwu reveals a sophisticated 9th-century society with specialized bronze work, while the Niger delta city-states probably grew from earlier indigenous trade.

European sea trade began in the 15th century with the Portuguese. Their aim was to reach [[Asia]] by rounding Africa, contact Mali, and divert some trans-Saharan gold trade from Muslim North Africa to Christian Europe. The Cape Verde Islands became bases from the 1460s. In 1471, Portuguese captains reached the coast of modern Ghana, where Akan peoples traded gold for metals, cloth, and other goods. The region became known as Mina, or the Gold Coast.

The Portuguese crown tried to control the Gold Coast trade directly. In 1482, it built Sao Jorge da Mina, now Elmina Castle, on land leased from local Akan rulers, followed by forts at Axim, Shama, and Accra. These forts were meant to force local gold sales through Portuguese agents. Up to about 1550, the Portuguese may have obtained at least 12,400 ounces of gold a year from the Gold Coast.

Portugal was too poor and overextended to dominate all western African trade. It bought many goods demanded on the Gold Coast from elsewhere, including cloth from Morocco and goods from Benin and the Niger delta. Attempts to turn Benin into a Christian protectorate failed because the monarchy was strong enough to resist. From about 1520, Portuguese trade with the Niger delta was conducted mostly from Sao Tome and other Gulf of Guinea islands, where slaves worked plantations producing sugar and other tropical goods.

The first era of European involvement, from about 1450 to 1600, did not transform all of western Africa. Its strongest effect was on the Gold Coast, where coastal communities gained new wealth but also faced the problem of controlling European forts without losing sovereignty. Fort lands were leased, not ceded, and African rulers could withdraw supplies or allies if Europeans lost their confidence. More general effects included limited Christian influence, a Portuguese-based trade language, and new crops from tropical America, including maize and cassava.

The Atlantic slave trade expanded after the Dutch challenged Portuguese control. Dutch traders had distributed Iberian colonial goods in northern Europe, but conflict with [[Spain]] and [[Portugal]] pushed them into their own oceanic trade. From 1598, they established posts on the Gold Coast. By 1642, the Dutch had taken the Portuguese Gold Coast forts and dominated European trade from Cape Verde to the Niger delta. Plantation agriculture in the Americas created a growing demand for enslaved African labor.

By the 1660s, about 15,000 enslaved Africans were landed in the Americas each year. Britain and [[France]] then challenged Dutch power because they wanted direct labor supplies for their own American colonies. Wars between 1652 and 1713 led to many new forts and shifting control along the coast. After 1713, Britain and France became the main rivals. France was strongest around Goree and Saint-Louis in Senegal, while Britain became strongest on the Gold Coast and later through flexible private trade.

The Slave Coast, east of the Gold Coast, became central because its peoples had dense populations, trade routes to the interior, and demand for European metals, cloth, guns, ammunition, and spirits. Unlike the Gold Coast, trade there often operated through local rulers and mobile private traders rather than large fortified companies. In the 18th century, about 5.2 million Africans were imported into the New World. The peak came in the 1780s, when about 78,000 enslaved Africans were carried to the Americas each year.

The human cost of the slave trade was severe, though difficult to measure. Mortality during the Atlantic crossing may have averaged about 15 percent in the better-documented 18th and 19th centuries and perhaps more earlier. Western Africa supplied perhaps 60 percent of Atlantic slaves up to and including the 18th century, falling to about one-third in the 19th century. The trade probably slowed population growth more than it caused total depopulation, but it removed many people in prime working and childbearing ages and hit some regions harder than others.

African rulers and merchants usually sold enslaved people through existing political and commercial systems. Some were criminals, debtors, outcasts, or people already under servitude within their societies. Many others were captives from wars, raids, or kidnapping. Wars were not simply caused by European demand, because power in western Africa was often measured in control over people rather than land. Still, Atlantic demand made it profitable to exchange some human labor for imported goods, especially guns, cloth, metalware, beads, tobacco, and spirits.

The trade also reshaped coastal society. A new class of African merchants gained wealth outside older kinship limits. Some Europeans settled and married into local families, creating merchant dynasties such as the Brews of the Gold Coast. Returned former slaves from the Americas also influenced coastal trade. Currencies such as iron bars, gold dust, and cowrie shells became more standardized. In the Niger delta, merchant city-states developed the House system, where free people and enslaved dependents were organized into commercial corporations under leading traders.

The Atlantic trade helped create powerful Guinea kingdoms. In the Gold Coast hinterland, Denkyera, Akwamu, and Akyem fought to control routes to European forts. Out of rebellion against Denkyera came the Asante monarchy centered on Kumasi. By the early 18th century, Asante had crushed Denkyera, expanded north into Bono and Gonja, demanded tribute from Dagomba, and dominated most of modern Ghana except the Fanti coastlands.

Farther east, Oyo used cavalry from the late 17th century to control trade routes toward the Aja coast and other Yoruba peoples. Benin opened its ports to European merchants to share in slave-trade goods. Disorder among the Aja states allowed Dahomey to build a strongly centralized and militarized state between 1724 and 1734. Dahomey first tried to conserve labor, but pressure from Oyo and the need for guns and imports made the slave trade central at Whydah and nearby ports such as Badagri and Lagos.

Firearms became important symbols and tools of power, but they did not alone explain the rise of the Guinea kingdoms. Oyo often defeated musket-armed enemies with cavalry. Guns, like horses, were expensive to buy and maintain, and therefore became instruments of kings and their agents. Royal control over firearms and over exports such as slaves and gold strengthened centralized power and increased competition among states.

After the Moroccan conquest of the Niger Bend in 1591, Mande-style imperial dominance in the western Sudan declined. Tuareg and Arabized Amazigh groups gained influence over the Niger Bend, Timbuktu, and the salt trade. The Kunta became especially important by the 18th century, and Sidi Mukhtar, who died in 1811, used learning and holiness within the Qadiriyah brotherhood to mediate conflicts and support commerce.

Islam changed from a mainly merchant religion into a reforming force through the tariqa, or Muslim brotherhoods. The Qadiriyah and later the Tijaniyah trained devout Muslims who believed they should build a true Islamic society. This made the doctrine of jihad attractive, especially to Fulani clerics and pastoralists who felt pressured by settled rulers for rents, taxes, and services.

The first Fulani jihad took power in Bondu in the later 17th century. In the early 18th century, Fulani and Mande Muslims created a theocracy in Fouta Djallon, and later a similar movement arose in Fouta-Toro. The most famous jihad began in 1804, when Usman dan Fodio, a Fulani cleric in Gobir, declared rebellion after local rulers tried to suppress his independent Muslim community.

The Usman dan Fodio jihad rapidly transformed Hausaland. Discontented Fulani and oppressed Hausa peasants joined the movement against arbitrary rule and taxation. Within three years, most Hausa kings were replaced by Fulani emirs who recognized Usman's authority. Sokoto and Gwandu became capitals of a new empire. Fulani emirates expanded into Bauchi, Adamawa, Nupe, and Ilorin, accelerating the fall of Oyo. Bornu resisted under Muhammad al-Kanemi, who argued that Fulani clerics had no exclusive right to define Muslim government.

The Sokoto Caliphate ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sokoto_Caliphate)) was directed practically by Usman's brother Abdullahi and son Muhammad Bello. They installed emirs, collected tribute, and tried to align government and taxation with Muslim law. Over time the original scholarly impulse weakened, Fulani rulers intermarried with Hausa elites, and Hausa replaced Arabic in administration. Still, for more than half a century, about 200,000 square miles had a relatively unified and impartial system that benefited farming, industry, and trade.

Another jihad began in Macina around 1818 under Ahmadu ibn Hammadi, a local Qadiri Fulani. It created a theocratic state that included Jenne and Timbuktu. A third major jihad was led by al-Hajj Umar Tal, a Tukulor cleric from Fouta-Toro. After pilgrimage to [[Mecca and Medina (Mecca)|Mecca]], years at Sokoto, and links to the activist Tijaniyah, he built a military-religious community at Dinguiraye and traded with the Upper Guinea coast for firearms.

From 1852, Umar Tal conquered through the upper Senegal and Niger regions. Kaarta fell in 1854, Segu in 1861, and his forces reached toward Timbuktu in 1863. His empire was almost as large as Sokoto's but less stable, because many subjects accepted Islam only after conquest and because his captains ruled local fiefs. Umar was killed in 1864 while suppressing a rebellion in Macina. His conquests made the Tijaniyah the strongest brotherhood in western African Islam and helped secure Islam's dominance in the western Sudan.

## Colonization

![[attachments/western_africa_003.webp|420]]

*Sankore Mosque in Timbuktu*

The European partition of western Africa is often linked to industrial rivalries in Europe between about 1870 and 1914, the British occupation of [[Egypt]] in 1882, and the Berlin West Africa Conference of 1884-85. In western Africa, however, the roots of partition appeared earlier. Between 1854 and 1874, local pressures had already led [[France]] and Britain to create formal colonies in Senegal, Lagos, and the Gold Coast. European trade, missionary activity, anti-slavery policy, and African political instability weakened older coastal orders.

The abolition of the Atlantic slave trade began outside Africa. Evangelical Christianity, secular ideas linked to the French Revolution, and changing economic interests in Europe all encouraged opposition. [[Denmark]] banned slave trading in 1803, Britain in 1807, the [[United States of America (United States)|United States]] in 1808, [[Sweden]] in 1813, the [[Netherlands]] in 1814, and [[France]] in 1818. Britain mattered most because its ships had carried many slaves and its navy could police the seas.

British suppression was incomplete but influential. Between 1825 and 1865, the British anti-slave-trade squadron captured 1,287 slave ships and freed about 130,000 people, while about 1.8 million enslaved Africans still reached the Americas. The trade ended only in the late 1860s, after slavery was abolished in the [[United States of America (United States)|United States]] in 1865 and transport to Cuba and [[Brazil]] became too risky.

The British anti-slavery campaign created new bases of power on the coast. Sierra Leone became the main naval, political, and missionary center. British philanthropists had sent freed Black settlers to the future Freetown in 1787. After the settlement struggled, the British government took control in 1808. Freetown became the seat of anti-slave-trade courts and the naval squadron, and about 60,000 liberated Africans were landed there over the next 60 years.

British policy remained officially cautious, but trade drew it inland and along the coast. In the Niger delta, merchants quickly shifted from slaves to palm oil, which British industry needed for lubricants and soap. By the 1830s, British palm-oil purchases in western Africa were worth nearly $2 million a year, about nine-tenths from the Niger delta. The delta city-states were successful because they had waterways, dense populations, flexible merchant organization, and household production of palm oil.

The collapse of Oyo and the growth of Yoruba warfare increased slave exports from Dahomey, Lagos, and Badagri. Yoruba liberated Africans from Sierra Leone returned to Lagos, Badagri, and Abeokuta, bringing trade, Christianity, and Western education. British consuls and naval officers intervened first against Dahomey and then in Lagos politics. In 1851, the British navy restored a ruler who promised to stop the slave trade, and in 1861 Lagos was annexed.

On the Gold Coast, British influence grew through trade and conflict with Asante. George Maclean created an informal jurisdiction after 1828 that secured trade and peace. British officials returned in 1843, took over Danish forts in 1850, and struggled with revenue and taxation. After Dutch withdrawal in 1872, Britain invaded Asante, destroyed Kumasi, and declared the coast a colony in 1874.

Liberia was another product of the anti-slavery era. Private American organizations settled freed slaves on the coast where no place existed for them in the United States before 1863. Because Britain and France questioned the settlers' right to control and tax trade, and because formal U.S. policy opposed colonialism, the Republic of Liberia was proclaimed in 1847. Its settler government then struggled for many decades to control local African peoples without metropolitan resources.

France concentrated first on Senegal. After British withdrawal in 1817, French plans for plantations failed. Under Louis Faidherbe, governor from 1854 to 1865, France aimed to control exports from the western Sudan by advancing up the Senegal River and encouraging peanut production for European vegetable-oil demand. Senegal became both a model of French expansion and a base for wider conquest toward the Sudan.

By the 1870s, British trade was stronger than French trade. British merchants, focused on the Niger delta, Lagos, and the Gold Coast, had about $24 million in annual business, while French merchants had about $8 million, much of it on the Senegal River. This gave France stronger incentives to expand. German treaty-making in Togo and Cameroon in 1884 pushed Britain to declare protectorates, while France filled gaps and claimed coastlines that were not held by Liberia, [[Portugal]], or Britain.

French expansion inland moved mainly east from Senegal through the [[Sudan]]. Reaching the upper Niger between 1879 and 1883 gave French forces a route for rapid advance. By 1896, French troops from the Sudan linked with those that had conquered Dahomey in 1893-94 and threatened lower Niger territories important to Britain. Britain responded by securing the hinterlands of the Gold Coast, Lagos, and the Niger delta.

British rule in the Niger region grew from commercial organization. Sir George Goldie merged trading companies in 1879 to resist French competition, and the Royal Niger Company later gained government support. By 1900, Britain judged that a private company could not safely compete with French state power or enforce monopolies, so it removed the company's political role and took direct responsibility for conquering much of the Sokoto empire. In 1914, Northern Nigeria, Yoruba territories entered from Lagos, and the Niger delta protectorate were merged into the Colony and Protectorate of Nigeria.

By 1898, Europeans had claimed nearly all western Africa except about 40,000 square miles of Liberia. [[Portugal]] held only 14,000 square miles in Portuguese Guinea. [[Germany]] claimed Togo and the larger Cameroon territory. France claimed about 1.8 million square miles, much more than Britain's 450,000 square miles, but much French territory was Saharan, inland, thinly populated, and economically weak. In 1897, the four British colonies had trade worth about $24 million, compared with about $14 million for seven French territories.

European military conquest took decades after partition. Small African armies led by European officers and armed with rifles, machine guns, and artillery usually defeated jihad empires in pitched battles, but logistics and distance slowed control. Frederick Lugard did not defeat the Sokoto Fulani until 1900-03, and French control of what became Niger was not complete until the 1920s. Smaller, dense, and more homogeneous societies often resisted longer, including Asante in 1900-01, the Sierra Leone protectorate in 1898-99, and Igbo and Tiv areas until about 1918.

Samory Toure posed France's most difficult southern resistance. A Muslim Mande leader from a Dyula trading and military background, he built an empire from the Sierra Leone hinterland to western Gonja from the 1860s. His government recalled old Mali more than clerical jihad states. The French fought several campaigns against him before capturing and exiling him in 1898.

Colonial governments depended on African cooperation. European forces and administrations were tiny. Lugard conquered Sokoto with about 3,000 soldiers, only 150 of them European, and administered northern Nigeria with only about 200 Europeans. In the late 1930s, the British governor of the Gold Coast had only 842 Europeans to help control nearly four million people. Africans served as soldiers, police, clerks, messengers, craftsmen, laborers, and chiefs.

Both French and British colonial systems were hierarchical, with governors, secretariats, provincial officials, district officers, and African intermediaries. France treated chiefs as low-ranking parts of a French administrative machine. Britain developed indirect rule, especially under Lugard, by using and reshaping African rulers to govern locally under British control. Indirect rule fit northern Nigeria better than chiefless eastern Nigeria or complex Yoruba politics, and it conflicted with older British ideas of legislative councils as early forms of representative government.

French and British colonial ideals both narrowed after the scramble. France kept citizenship for people born in the four Senegalese communes of Saint-Louis, Goree, Rufisque, and Dakar, but most Africans became subjects without full rights. By 1937, only about 80,500 of 15 million people in French western Africa were citizens, and only 2,500 had acquired citizenship outside birth in the communes. In British colonies, legislative councils survived but stayed limited, with elected members appearing only in 1922 and representing tiny coastal electorates for a generation.

Colonial rule brought powerful economic change, but colonies were expected to pay for themselves. Revenue came mainly from customs duties and direct taxes such as poll or hut taxes. Coastal colonies with established export trade could raise money; inland territories such as northern Nigeria, French Sudan, and Niger could not. France created a federal government in 1895, and Britain unified Nigeria in 1912-14, partly so richer coastal revenues could subsidize poorer interiors.

Mining and infrastructure required colonial intervention. Private investors came early only where quick profits seemed possible, such as Gold Coast gold and Bauchi tin. Gold Coast gold mining used modern methods from 1878 but expanded only after Asante was defeated, railways began, and civil administration secured concessions. Colonial governments had to build railways, harbors, telegraph lines, roads, medical services, and schools because private enterprise would not build the basic systems needed for rule and growth.

French development faced the problem of vast inland territories. A railway from Kayes to Bamako began in 1882 but was not completed until 1906. Dakar became the better port, and a direct Dakar-Kayes line was built between 1907 and 1924. Inland French Sudan responded slowly, and cotton required further irrigation. French strategy shifted toward coastal colonies, where peanuts in Senegal, bananas in French Guinea, palm produce in Dahomey and Togo, and coffee, cocoa, bananas, and lumber in Ivory Coast increased exports. French western African trade grew from about $58 million in 1912-13 to about $600 million in 1956-57.

The British territories developed unevenly. The Gambia remained a narrow river colony with limited possibilities but some peanut prosperity. Sierra Leone had a dense population in a poor environment, exported palm produce, and later developed diamonds and iron ore, but mining created social and revenue problems. The Gold Coast became one of the strongest colonial economies, combining gold, manganese, bauxite, diamonds, and especially cocoa, which by 1913 outranked gold and later supplied between one-third and one-half of world cocoa. Governor Frederick Gordon Guggisberg used 1920s prosperity for the port at Takoradi, railways, roads, education, and medical services.

Nigeria was the largest and most difficult British colony. In 1912-13, its trade exceeded that of the Gold Coast and the combined French colonies, but it had a vast north with more than half the population and much less development. Railways from Lagos, the Niger, Kaduna, Kano, Port Harcourt, and Enugu gradually linked the regions. Southern cocoa and palm produce developed like the Gold Coast, while northern peanuts produced weaker results for a larger population. By the mid-1950s, Nigeria's total trade was about $800 million, but per-person trade and revenue lagged behind more prosperous territories.

Decolonization between 1957 and 1976 came from both European change and African reaction to colonial rule. [[World Wars (World War I)|World War I]] weakened European confidence and created the League of Nations mandates, which declared that former German territories should be prepared for self-government. The [[Great Depression]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Depression)) encouraged arguments for active colonial development. In 1929, Britain passed the first Colonial Development Act. During and after [[World Wars (World War II)|World War II]], Britain and France expanded development plans, partly because colonies supplied strategic raw materials and, for Charles de Gaulle, a base for continuing the fight against Germany.

Postwar plans increased African expectations. British legislative councils were reformed in 1945-48 so Africans outnumbered European officials, but many members were nominees or chiefs. France made colonies overseas territories in the 1946 constitution, but Africans received less power than they expected. Education produced new elites. By the mid-1950s, Nigeria had more than two million schoolchildren and the Gold Coast nearly 600,000, while French West Africa had about 340,000. Universities opened in the Gold Coast and Nigeria in 1948, and Dakar's French African university opened in 1950.

African political organization had older roots. The Aborigines' Rights Protection Society in the Gold Coast began in the 1890s to defend African lands and oppose exclusion from administration. In 1918-20, the National Congress of British West Africa demanded elective legislative councils. In French West Africa, politics first centered on Senegal's four communes, where voters sent a deputy to the French National Assembly and where a Senegalese Socialist party emerged by the 1930s.

After [[World Wars (World War II)|World War II]], educated Africans saw European systems becoming more entrenched while African skills and expectations had grown. In French West Africa, politicians formed the African Democratic Rally in 1946. Its alliance with the French Communist Party led to repression in 1948-50. In the Gold Coast, established politicians brought in Kwame Nkrumah in 1947 to organize mass support. Boycotts and riots in 1948 led to inquiry and constitutional change. A 1951 constitution created an elected African-dominated assembly, and Nkrumah's party negotiated further concessions until the Gold Coast became independent Ghana in 1957.

Other British colonies followed but with harder problems. The Gambia was too small and delayed independence until 1965. Sierra Leone, independent in 1961, had deep gaps between the Creole population around Freetown and the rest of the country. Nigeria had to balance a less developed, Muslim, aristocratic north with southern nationalist parties: the Action Group among the Yoruba west and the National Convention of Nigerian Citizens among the Igbo east. A three-region federation allowed east and west internal self-government in 1957. Northern leaders later joined a federal coalition with the NCNC, and Nigeria became independent in 1960.

French decolonization followed a different path. Felix Houphouet-Boigny led the African Democratic Rally away from the Communists and used African votes in the French National Assembly to gain wider colonial franchises and African ministerial responsibility by 1956. Leopold Sedar Senghor of Senegal and Ahmed Sekou Toure of Guinea warned that separate colonies would be too small and poor to resist French influence. In the 1958 referendum on Charles de Gaulle's French Community, all colonies accepted internal self-government except Guinea, which chose immediate independence. Senegal and French Sudan formed the Federation of Mali in 1959, then separated, and by 1960 the former French colonies were legally independent.

Portuguese and Spanish rule lasted longer. Guinean nationalists began armed struggle in 1962 and, after 10 years, controlled three-quarters of Portuguese Guinea. War in Guinea, Mozambique, and Angola helped cause the Portuguese dictatorship to fall in 1974. Guinea-Bissau was recognized as independent in 1974, followed by the Cape Verde Islands and Sao Tome and Principe in 1975. Spain granted independence to its equatorial African territory in 1968 without adequate preparation, producing chaos.

## Key Events & Developments

- 500 BCE: Ironworking and agriculture were present in central Nigeria's Nok culture, an early sign of complex local development.
- 1000 CE: Muslim geographical and historical sources began to give fuller evidence about the bilad al-sudan and trans-Saharan trade.
- 1235: Sundiata and the Keita rulers of Mali rose to power and absorbed the remains of ancient Ghana.
- 1471: Portuguese captains reached the Akan gold coast of modern Ghana, opening a major era of Atlantic trade.
- 1591: Morocco defeated Songhai and captured Gao, Timbuktu, and Jenne, ending long Mande-style imperial dominance in the western Sudan.
- 1804: Usman dan Fodio launched the jihad that created the Sokoto Caliphate ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sokoto_Caliphate)) and transformed Hausaland.
- 1807: Britain outlawed its slave trade, leading to naval suppression, stronger coastal intervention, and new forms of commerce.
- 1861: Lagos was annexed by Britain after anti-slave-trade intervention and disputes within the royal family.
- 1884-85: The Berlin West Africa Conference symbolized the European scramble, although western African partition had local roots before it.
- 1957: The Gold Coast became independent Ghana, setting the model for British West African decolonization.
- 1960: Nigeria and most former French western African colonies became independent.
- 1974-75: Guinea-Bissau, the Cape Verde Islands, and Sao Tome and Principe gained independence after Portugal's dictatorship fell.

## Key People

- Ibn Battutah ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ibn_Battutah)): Traveler whose visit to the western Sudan in 1352-53 gave one of the earliest firsthand accounts of the region.
- Abu Ubayd al-Bakri: 11th-century Cordoban geographer who described ancient Ghana's capital, court, and trade.
- Sundiata: Founder figure of Mali power around 1235, linked with the rise of the Keita empire.
- Mansa Musa ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mansa_Musa)): Ruler of Mali from 1307 to 1332, famous for his pilgrimage to [[Mecca and Medina (Mecca)|Mecca]] and for Mali's Islamic diplomacy.
- Sonni Ali ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sonni_Ali)): Songhai ruler from 1464 to 1492 who destroyed Mali's power and built a new empire from Gao.
- al-Hajj Muhammad Askia: Muslim Mande general who ruled Songhai from 1493 to 1528 and tied imperial power to Islam.
- Sidi Mukhtar ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sidi_Mukhtar)): Kunta religious leader who died in 1811 and helped make the Qadiriyah influential in western Sudanese Islam.
- Usman dan Fodio ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Usman_dan_Fodio)): Fulani scholar whose 1804 jihad created the Sokoto Caliphate ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sokoto_Caliphate)).
- Muhammad Bello ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muhammad_Bello)) and Abdullahi ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abdullahi)): Usman dan Fodio's son and brother, who organized and governed the eastern and western parts of the Sokoto empire.
- Muhammad al-Kanemi: Leader who defended Bornu against Fulani conquest and built a renewed Islamic state.
- al-Hajj Umar Tal: Tukulor Tijani leader whose 19th-century jihad built a large but unstable empire in the upper Senegal and Niger regions.
- Samory Toure ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samory_Toure)): Mande military and political leader who resisted French conquest until his capture in 1898.
- Louis Faidherbe ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Louis_Faidherbe)): French governor of Senegal from 1854 to 1865 who made Senegal a base for French inland expansion.
- Frederick Lugard ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frederick_Lugard)): British officer and governor who conquered northern Nigeria and systematized indirect rule.
- Kwame Nkrumah ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kwame_Nkrumah)): Gold Coast nationalist leader whose mass politics helped bring independence as Ghana in 1957.
- Felix Houphouet-Boigny ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Felix_Houphouet-Boigny)), Leopold Sedar Senghor ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leopold_Sedar_Senghor)), and Ahmed Sekou Toure ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ahmed_Sekou_Toure)): French West African leaders who shaped different paths toward independence in the 1950s.

## Interesting Facts

- Ancient Ghana was not the modern state of Ghana; it lay much farther north and west, near modern Mauritania and Mali.
- The Gold Coast name came from Akan access to gold, and the Portuguese word Mina meant "the mine."
- The Portuguese built Elmina Castle in 1482 on leased Akan land, showing that African rulers did not simply surrender sovereignty.
- The Atlantic slave trade peaked in the 1780s, when about 78,000 enslaved Africans were carried to the Americas each year.
- The Niger delta shifted rapidly from slave exports to palm oil because its waterways, dense population, and merchant city-states made oil collection and transport efficient.
- The Gold Coast became unusually prosperous under colonial rule because cocoa, gold, diamonds, manganese, bauxite, railways, and a compact territory reinforced each other.
- Ghana's independence in 1957 strongly influenced later British decolonization across western Africa.
