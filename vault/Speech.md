## Introduction

![[attachments/speech_001.png|420]]

*Diagram of the human larynx*

Speech ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Speech)) is human communication through spoken language. Many animals have voices and inflection, but humans can shape laryngeal tones into articulated oral language. Speech depends on a coordinated system of breath, voice production, resonance, and articulation.

Four main structures regulate spoken sound. A respiratory activator supplies air like a bellows. The larynx changes that air energy into voice. The pharynx shapes the individual voice pattern as a resonator. The oral cavity, including tongue, lips, palate, and jaw, forms speech sounds.

These parts usually work together, but they can also act separately. Whispering ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Whispering)) can produce audible speech without voice. Some yodeling uses phonation without normal oral articulation. Silent articulation without breath or voice can be used in lipreading.

## Respiratory Mechanisms

Quiet breathing differs from phonic respiration, the breathing used for speech. At rest, most adults breathe about 18 to 20 times per minute, with inhalation and exhalation nearly equal in length, depth, and air volume. Each quiet breath moves about half a litre of air.

In speech breathing, inhalation is deeper and faster than in quiet breathing, and exhalation is slower and controlled. A speaker may take in one or two litres of air and release it steadily for the length of an utterance. Trained speakers and singers can phonate on one breath for 30 to 45 seconds, and sometimes nearly a minute.

The maximum phonation time depends on body physiology, health, age, size, training, and the larynx's ability to close the glottis precisely. Laryngeal disease often shortens this time because the vocal folds cannot seal well enough to turn breath into sound efficiently.

Respiration is controlled by the [[Nerves and Nervous Systems (Nervous System)|nervous system]], especially centres in the brain stem. Emotion immediately changes speech breathing and voice quality. Fear may produce a timid voice, anger a barking voice, melancholy a weak monotone, and agitation a rough forceful sound. Nervous-system and breathing disorders may also be heard in the voice.

Voice training often begins with breathing because speech uses chest, abdominal, or mixed respiration. Many teachers prefer a balanced combination of pectoral and abdominal breathing. Extreme reliance on one breathing habit can be inefficient and may strain the voice.

## Brain Functions

![[attachments/speech_002.png|420]]

*Diagram showing Broca's and Wernicke's areas of the brain*

Speech production requires the brain to coordinate thought, movement, hearing, and feedback. The cortex guides voluntary speech and writing, while language centres usually lie in the dominant hemisphere, commonly the left hemisphere in right-handed people.

Motor areas send fibres to cranial and spinal nerves that control muscles of the mouth, throat, and larynx. Swallowing is an inborn reflex, but articulate speech requires learned control over many fine movements. This control depends on coordinated timing rather than one isolated muscle action.

Auditory feedback tells speakers about pitch, volume, inflection, articulation, word choice, and other audible features. Proprioceptive feedback from muscles, tendons, joints, and moving parts gives information about position and movement. Deafness, paralysis, and developmental disorders can reduce speech quality because these feedback systems are impaired.

## The Structure of the Larynx

The larynx is built from cartilages, muscles, nerves, blood vessels, and membranes. Its framework includes three single cartilages and three paired cartilages. The thyroid cartilage forms the main shield, the cricoid lies beneath it, and the epiglottis sits above as a laryngeal lid.

The paired arytenoid cartilages move the vocal cords sideways and help open or close the glottis. Other small paired cartilages sit near the arytenoids. Ligaments and membranes hold the cartilages together and connect the larynx below to the trachea and above to the throat structures.

Laryngeal muscles are external or internal. Extrinsic muscles move the whole larynx, while intrinsic muscles move the vocal folds and shape the glottis. The cricothyroid muscle stretches the vocal folds and is the main mechanism for raising pitch, helping distinguish registers such as chest voice and falsetto.

Nerve supply matters clinically. The superior laryngeal nerve supplies the cricothyroid, while most other laryngeal muscles receive the recurrent laryngeal nerve. Different nerve paralyses create different kinds of vocal-cord immobility and voice loss.

## Vocal Cords

The two true vocal cords ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vocal_cords)), or vocal folds, are the larynx's main valve for opening the airway during breathing and closing during swallowing. During phonation, they are brought close together so that air pressure from below can set them vibrating.

False vocal folds lie above the true cords. They close tightly during swallowing and other protective sphincter actions. If they are used for phonation, they can produce severe hoarseness called ventricular dysphonia.

The vocal cords ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vocal_cords)) are covered by stratified squamous epithelium because they must endure rapid vibration for many hours. This transitional tissue is vulnerable to chronic irritation, which helps explain why many laryngeal cancers begin on the vocal cords.

Vocal-cord length varies with age, sex, and body size. Before puberty, boys' and girls' larynges are similar. During puberty, the male larynx grows strongly under hormonal influence. Longer vocal cords generally produce lower voices, while shorter cords produce higher voices.

## Substitutes for the Larynx

Some patients lose the larynx through laryngectomy, often because of cancer. The trachea is brought to an opening in the neck, so normal vocal-cord voice is lost. The patient may be aphonic and effectively speechless unless a substitute voice is learned or supplied.

Esophageal voice is one rehabilitation method. Air is introduced into the esophagus and released through the upper esophageal sphincter, creating a low-pitched substitute voice. Training can raise the pitch enough for usable speech, and such methods have been studied since the 19th century.

An artificial larynx can help people unable to learn esophageal speech. A modern electric larynx uses batteries, a buzzing sound source, and a vibrating head held against the throat. The sound enters the pharynx through the skin, and ordinary articulation makes it intelligible.

## Voice Production

The myoelastic-aerodynamic theory explains voice as the interaction of muscle tension, vocal-fold elasticity, and air pressure. The vocal cords are brought together by muscles. Expiratory air pressure rises below them until the glottis opens, releasing a puff of air. Elasticity and airflow then bring the cords back together.

This opening and closing cycle repeats many times per second. Low pitches have fewer cycles per second, and high pitches have more. The vocal cords vibrate passively in the airflow; they do not flap actively like insect wings. Muscles position and tense them, while air pressure drives vibration.

High-speed motion pictures, stroboscopic methods, and observations of paralysis support this theory. A voice can sometimes remain nearly normal even with some vocal-cord paralysis, showing that aerodynamic and elastic factors are essential.

## Vocal Registers

![[attachments/speech_003.png|420]]

*Speech spectrogram*

Vocal registers are different patterns of voice production recognized for centuries in singing. Traditional terms include chest voice, midvoice, and head voice. They reflect both bodily sensations and real differences in vocal-fold adjustment, resonance, and acoustic output.

Scientific study of registers uses two approaches. One examines the visible laryngeal mechanism through laryngoscopy, laryngostroboscopy, high-speed filming, X-rays, respiration recordings, aerodynamic measurements, and electromyography. These methods show vocal-fold length, tension, closure, breathing support, and muscle coordination.

The second approach studies audible register differences acoustically. Electroacoustic analysis shows that chest voice is rich in higher harmonics, while falsetto is thinner. Listeners hear these differences as changes in quality, power, and pitch range.

## Intensity

Vocal intensity depends mainly on the amplitude of vocal-cord vibration and the pressure of the airstream below the glottis. Stronger expiratory effort usually creates greater loudness. Intensity also depends on how efficiently the larynx and resonating vocal tract radiate sound.

The vocal tract can act like a horn. A wide, flexible throat, open mouth, flat tongue, raised palate, and lowered larynx help project sound clearly. Laughter and yawning show this open resonator pattern. A tight or constricted throat produces muffled, choked, poorly carrying sound.

Emotion also changes intensity and resonance. Happy laughter sounds bright and ringing because the vocal tract opens widely, while despairing sobs sound tight because the throat constricts. These examples show how voice quality combines anatomy, physiology, and feeling.

## Singing and Speaking

Singing differs from speaking partly because of psychology and artistic intention. Many people have an inborn musical sense that develops through hearing and brain maturation, while formal singing in an artistic style requires training. Untrained singing often appears spontaneously in late childhood and after vocal maturation.

Singing uses inherited breathing, phonation, resonance, and articulation mechanisms but organizes them under musical pitch, rhythm, register, and style. Speaking uses the same physical instrument but normally serves language, communication, and meaning more directly than sustained musical tone.

## Key Events & Developments

- Late 19th century: Experimental phonetics described differences between quiet breathing and speech breathing.
- 1895 onward: X-ray methods made laryngeal position and vocal-cord tension visible.
- 19th century: Esophageal voice was described as a substitute after laryngeal loss.
- Modern period: Laryngostroboscopy, high-speed cinematography, aerodynamic measurement, and electromyography expanded knowledge of voice production.

## Key People

- Voice teachers ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voice_teachers)): Developed practical traditions of breath support, resonance, register control, and vocal training.
- Laryngologists: Studied vocal-fold structure, disease, paralysis, and rehabilitation.
- Experimental phoneticians: Measured breathing, phonation, articulation, and acoustic patterns of speech.

## Interesting Facts

- Whispering ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Whispering)) can produce speech without vocal-cord voice.
- Maximum phonation time can reach 30 to 45 seconds in trained speakers and singers.
- False-cord voice can create severe hoarseness when protective folds replace true vocal-fold vibration.
- Electric larynges let some laryngectomized patients speak by sending a buzz through the throat.
- Falsetto has a thinner acoustic spectrum than full chest voice.
