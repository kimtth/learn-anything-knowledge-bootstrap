## Introduction

![[attachments/number_theory_001.webp|420]]

*Ulam spiral*

Number theory ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Number_theory)) is the branch of mathematics concerned with properties of positive integers. Also called higher arithmetic, it is one of the oldest and most natural mathematical pursuits. Many of its questions are easy for nonspecialists to understand, but their proofs can require highly sophisticated methods. Once considered the purest mathematics, number theory gained major practical importance through digital computers, digital communication, factoring, primality testing, and cryptography.

Modern number theory includes elementary, algebraic, analytic, geometric, and probabilistic branches, classified by the methods used to study integers. Its roots go back to prehistoric counting, ancient Mesopotamian, Egyptian, Chinese, and Indian records, and Greek mathematics.

## Pythagoras

Pythagoras and his followers treated number as the key to understanding the cosmos, music, and harmony. They attached symbolic significance to perfect numbers, such as 6 and 28, which equal the sum of their proper divisors. They also knew the amicable pair 220 and 284, each of which equals the sum of the other's proper divisors. Later writers such as Nicomachus of Gerasa turned some of these ideas into numerical theology.

## Euclid

Euclid made number theory mathematical rather than mystical. In the Elements, he defined numbers, primes, composites, and perfect numbers and proved foundational results. The Euclidean algorithm finds the greatest common divisor of two whole numbers. The fundamental theorem of arithmetic says that every whole number factors uniquely into primes. Euclid also gave the classic proof that there are infinitely many primes.

## Euclid's Infinitely Many Primes Argument

Euclid's prime-number proof begins with any finite list of primes and forms a new number by multiplying them and adding 1. If the new number is prime, the list was incomplete; if composite, one of its prime factors is absent from the original list. Either way, no finite list contains all primes. Euclid also proved that numbers of a special form are perfect when the associated Mersenne factor is prime.

## Diophantus

![[attachments/number_theory_002.webp|420]]

*Diophantus Arithmetica manuscript*

Diophantus of Alexandria, active around AD 250, wrote Arithmetica, a collection of problems now associated with Diophantine equations. These equations require integer solutions, making them harder than similar equations over real numbers. Diophantus strongly influenced later mathematicians by turning number problems into structured algebraic challenges.

## Number Theory in the East

After the decline of Rome, Chinese and Indian mathematicians advanced number theory. Sun Zi studied simultaneous remainder problems, later generalized as the Chinese remainder theorem by Qin Jiushao. In India, Brahmagupta studied quadratic Diophantine equations, including what is now called Pell's equation, seeking squares that become squares after multiplication and addition.

## Fermat

Pierre de Fermat became central to modern number theory. In 1640, he stated Fermat's little theorem: if p is prime, then p divides a^p - a. He distinguished primes of the forms 4k + 1 and 4k - 1, asserting that primes of the first type are uniquely expressible as sums of two squares, while primes of the second type are not. He also used infinite descent to prove impossibility results.

Fermat's last theorem grew from his claim that no whole-number solutions exist for x^n + y^n = z^n when n is greater than 2. He offered many assertions without proof, leaving later mathematicians major challenges. His methods and conjectures shaped number theory for centuries.

## Disquisitiones Arithmeticae

Carl Friedrich Gauss's Disquisitiones Arithmeticae ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Disquisitiones_Arithmeticae)), published in 1801, reorganized number theory and moved it to a new level. Gauss gave the first modern proof of unique factorization, proved quadratic reciprocity, and introduced congruence notation. Congruence, such as 39 congruent to 4 modulo 7, became indispensable for studying divisibility, residues, primes, and modular arithmetic.

## From Classical to Analytic Number Theory

After Gauss, Sophie Germain, Adrien-Marie Legendre, Peter Gustav Lejeune Dirichlet, and Ernst Kummer developed deeper results. Germain contributed to Fermat's last theorem, Legendre and Dirichlet proved cases for fifth powers, and Kummer proved the theorem for many exponents. Dirichlet also proved that arithmetic progressions a, a+b, a+2b, and so on contain infinitely many primes when a and b are relatively prime.

## Open Problems and Modern Importance

Number theory retains many open problems. Unknowns include whether odd perfect numbers exist, whether infinitely many primes have form n^2 + 1, whether infinitely many twin primes exist, and whether Goldbach's conjecture is true. The Clay Mathematics Institute's Millennium Prize Problems show the continuing power of deep unsolved questions. Eric Temple Bell called number theory the last great uncivilized continent of mathematics, while Gauss famously called it the queen of mathematics.
