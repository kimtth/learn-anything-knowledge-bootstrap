## Introduction

Literature ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Literature)) is a body of written or orally transmitted works, especially imaginative poetry and prose valued for artistic intention and aesthetic excellence. It can be classified by language, nation, period, genre, subject, or cultural region.

The word comes from Latin littera, meaning a letter of the alphabet, but literature is not limited to written letters. Oral literature and the literature of preliterate peoples show that the art lies not only in marks on a page but in shaped language, memory, performance, and imagination.

Definitions often become circular because they already assume that readers recognize literary value. Literature may mean all human writing, the writing of one people or language, individual works, or works considered artistically excellent.

## Western and Eastern Criticism

Western literary criticism has long moved between constructive and expressive theories. [[Aristotle and Aristotelianism|Aristotle]] represents the constructive tendency: in the Poetics he analyzes tragedy and epic by structure, plot, action, and effect.

The treatise On the Sublime ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/On_the_Sublime)), traditionally attributed to Longinus, represents the expressive tendency. It asks what makes great writing great and stresses imaginative elevation, emotional force, and the writer's power to move readers.

This tension appears in later oppositions such as Scholasticism and Humanism, Classicism and Romanticism, Cubism and Expressionism. In reality, a literary work is both constructed and expressive: it is made by technique and charged by personality, vision, and emotion.

Asian criticism has been varied. Indian traditions produced technical manuals on tropes and style as well as philosophical reflection. Sanskrit literary culture often treated constructive and expressive elements as twin aspects of one reality.

Chinese criticism developed technical works on prosody and rhetoric but often emphasized subjective and expressive effects. Japanese criticism prized subtlety, poignancy, and fleeting depth, sometimes difficult for untrained readers to perceive.

## Poetry and Ambiguity

East Asian poetry raises the question of broad and narrow definitions of poetry ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poetry)). Chinese poetry is mainly lyric, meditative, and elegiac, rarely very long. Many Chinese poems are quatrains or only slightly longer than Western sonnets.

Some writers deliberately cultivate ambiguity, using words' multiple meanings and emotional overtones. Between the world wars, ambiguity became central in English and American poetry criticism, especially through T.S. Eliot, I.A. Richards, and William Empson.

[[Language]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Language)) is inherently complex. Even simple lyrics may later reveal meanings the author did not consciously plan. Great poets and prose stylists often work with at least partial awareness of language's deep sound and meaning possibilities.

## Translation

*Page from the Gutenberg Bible showing printed Latin text and decorated initial*

Translation ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Translation)) is difficult because literature depends on sound, rhythm, syntax, connotation, cultural memory, and word relationships. The translator must enter the author's world while creating an equivalent in another language.

No two languages are fully equivalent except in simple terms. Writers such as Catullus, Baudelaire, Pushkin, Sophocles, Plato, Shakespeare, and many Persian and Arabic poets resist translation because their effects are tied to specific verbal systems.

Some translations become classics in their own right. The King James Version of the Bible, published in 1611, is a major example of a translation with enduring literary excellence.

## Prosody and Structure

Prosody is the study of versification, especially metre, rhythm, syllable length, accent, pitch, rhyme, vowel repetition, consonant repetition, and cadence. Literature, like music, unfolds in time, and rhythm helps shape reading and listening.

English poetry has often relied on accent, syllable groups or feet, line length, rhyme, assonance, alliteration, and breath pauses. Greek and Latin poetry depended more consciously on long and short syllables.

Literary structure organizes time, attention, information, and expectation. Aristotle's dramatic pattern of presentation, development, complication, crisis, and resolution can be generalized, though great works often vary or resist such formulas.

Neoclassical critics invented the unities of time, place, and action from their reading of Aristotle, though Greek tragedy did not strictly follow them. Later critics also objected to mixed moods such as Shakespeare's comic relief in tragedy.

Novels may be tightly plotted or organically developed. Bildungsroman traces a hero's growth, as in Stendhal and Dickens. Flaubert's Madame Bovary is rigorously shaped, while Tolstoy, Dostoyevsky, and Balzac often overwhelm through broad realism.

Modernist writers such as James Joyce, Joseph Conrad, Ford Madox Ford, Virginia Woolf, Henry James, Ezra Pound, T.S. Eliot, and William Carlos Williams created new architectures through time shifts, multiple viewpoints, fragmented voices, and symbolic structures.

## Content, Symbol, Form, and Style

The content of literature is as wide as the human wish to communicate. [[Language]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Language)) is not just a dictionary of words but a living system of sound, meaning, social use, memory, and symbolic relation.

Words are symbols. They refer to things, other words, abstract ideas, emotions, values, and shared experiences. Literature uses this symbolic power to create worlds, arguments, images, myths, and forms of human presence.

The division between form and content is artificial except for analysis. Form shapes content, and content shapes form. Critics who insist too strongly on message against style often miss that style is how meaning exists in literature.

## Literature and Society

Literature ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Literature)) depends on social institutions as well as individual imagination. Courts, temples, theatres, schools, publishers, patrons, libraries, newspapers, universities, censorship offices, prizes, salons, and reading publics all influence what is written, preserved, translated, or forgotten. A poem or novel may seem private, but it reaches readers through language communities, material media, education, and systems of prestige. Literature can therefore reinforce inherited values, give form to dissent, or preserve experiences that official history leaves out.

Style is the way an author's mind expresses itself in words. Henry James's veiled and subtle style suits his sense of ambiguity; Theodore Dreiser's heavy and troubled style embodies his view of life. Flaubert's search for the exact word transformed Madame Bovary from sentimental material into ironic tragedy.

Writing is a craft. Talent may appear early, but literary art also requires learning, practice, revision, control of language, and command of inherited forms.

## Genres

![[attachments/the_art_of_literature_literature_002.webp|420]]

*Title page of Shakespeare's First Folio*

The heroic epic ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epic_poetry)) arose from preliterate heroic ages, as in the Iliad, Odyssey, Beowulf, Nibelungenlied, and Irish Cu Chulainn stories. Literary epics such as Virgil's Aeneid and Milton's Paradise Lost belong to sophisticated written cultures.

Lyric poetry remains close to song. Many medieval and Renaissance lyrics now read silently were originally sung. Modern popular songs by writers such as Bertolt Brecht, Georges Brassens, Leonard Cohen, Bob Dylan, and Joni Mitchell can also have literary merit.

Satire ranges from mock epic to epigram and from immediate political attack to deep myth. Rabelais's Gargantua and Pantagruel, Cervantes's Don Quixote, and Murasaki Shikibu's Tale of Genji move beyond narrow satire into broad visions of human life.

Prose fiction developed later than poetry and drama. Classical romances were often extended adventures, while Petronius's Satyricon and Apuleius's Golden Ass show psychological, social, religious, and allegorical depth.

Drama ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drama)) has been stable yet highly experimental. Aristotle's dramatic categories shaped plays for centuries, but modern drama changed radically after innovators such as August Strindberg and under competition with cinema.

## Future and Scholarship

In the 20th century, poetry absorbed modernist, Cubist, Surrealist, dream, trance, and preliterate influences. Future literature may continue inventing multimedia forms or return to older forms with Neoclassical discipline.

Literary scholarship began as soon as texts accumulated. Ancient scholars preserved, edited, explained, and interpreted works such as the Epic of Gilgamesh. Later scholarship included textual criticism, historical criticism, philology, biography, theory, and comparative study.

Literary criticism can become a form of autobiography, philosophy, or social force. Critics such as Poe, Sainte-Beuve, Taine, Belinsky, Arnold, Pater, Brandes, Mencken, Edmund Wilson, Moto-ori Norinaga, I.A. Richards, Paul Valery, Susanne Langer, and Ernst Cassirer influenced more than literary judgment alone.

In the late 20th century, some Anglo-American criticism was criticized for jargon and for losing contact with literature as art. Yet criticism continues to shape how societies read, remember, and argue about meaning.

## Key Events & Developments

- Classical Greece: Aristotle's Poetics became a foundation of Western literary theory.
- 1611: The King James Bible became a classic English translation.
- 1850: Edgar Allan Poe's Poetic Principle argued for the short poem.
- 1922: James Joyce's Ulysses showed a highly structured modernist novel in one day.
- 1924: I.A. Richards's Principles of Literary Criticism shaped modern critical method.
- 1930: William Empson's Seven Types of Ambiguity made ambiguity central to criticism.
