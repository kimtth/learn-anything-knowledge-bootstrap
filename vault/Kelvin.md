## Introduction

![[attachments/kelvin_001.webp|420]]

*Portrait of Lord Kelvin*

William Thomson, Baron Kelvin, born in 1824 and dead in 1907, was a Scottish engineer, mathematician, and physicist. He helped found modern physics through work on thermodynamics, the absolute temperature scale, heat, electricity, magnetism, hydrodynamics, and Earth's age.

Kelvin's importance came from joining theory with engineering. His ideas on submarine telegraphy and his cable instruments helped Britain lead global communications in the 19th century. He also defended a mechanical view of nature, treating heat, magnetism, and electricity as effects of invisible matter in motion.

## Early Life And Education

Thomson was the fourth of seven children. His father James Thomson, a mathematics teacher and later Glasgow professor, trained him in advanced mathematics. Thomson entered the University of Glasgow at age 10 in 1834.

Fourier's heat theory ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heat_equation)) shaped him early. As a teenager, Thomson defended Jean-Baptiste-Joseph Fourier's mathematical study of heat flow and saw that similar methods could apply to electricity and fluids. He entered Cambridge in 1841, studied in Paris, and became professor of natural philosophy at Glasgow in 1846.

## Science And Theory

[[Principles of Thermodynamics (Thermodynamics)|Thermodynamics]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thermodynamics)) became Kelvin's central scientific field. He first hesitated to accept James Prescott Joule's claim that heat and mechanical motion were interchangeable, but by 1851 he publicly supported Joule's theory and gave an important version of the second law of thermodynamics.

The absolute temperature scale came from this work and later used the unit called the kelvin. Thomson also wrote on electricity and magnetism from his student years. His mathematical analogies helped James Clerk Maxwell, whose electromagnetic theory of light grew partly from Kelvin's earlier papers.

## Telegraphy And Engineering

Submarine telegraphy made Kelvin famous outside academic science. He argued that cable design had to follow mathematical analysis, while rival engineer Wildman Whitehouse trusted practical experience. Thomson still served as chief consultant on dangerous Atlantic cable expeditions.

Thomson patented the mirror galvanometer in 1858, making weak cable signals readable. After the Atlantic Telegraph Company adopted his advice and instruments, Queen Victoria knighted him in 1866. He later became wealthy through cable consulting firms.

## Later Work And Controversies

Kelvin's later interests included tides, Earth's shape, atmospheric electricity, ground heat, Earth's rotation, geomagnetism, and hydrodynamics. He also challenged the long timescale required by geology and Charles Darwin's evolution, arguing from thermodynamics that Earth and the Sun could not be indefinitely old.

Kelvin's age estimates were wrong, but the controversy mattered because he insisted that geology and biology had to respect physical law. In 1884 lectures at Johns Hopkins University, he also discussed problems in the wave theory of light, showing both the power and limits of Victorian mechanical physics.

## Honours And Legacy

Kelvin was elected to the Royal Society in 1851 and served as its president from 1890 to 1895. He published more than 600 papers, held many patents, received worldwide honours, died in Scotland, and was buried in Westminster Abbey.

Kelvin's legacy is synthesis. Like Isaac Newton and Albert Einstein, he prepared the ground for a major change in physics. Some theories failed, but his union of energy, measurement, mathematics, and technology remained influential.

## Core Equations

Physical models often begin by relating force, energy, waves, and conserved quantities:

$$\vec{F}=m\vec{a}$$

This is Newton's second law: force is the product of mass and acceleration, so a larger mass or a faster change in motion requires a larger net force.

$$E=h\nu$$

This relation says that photon energy is proportional to frequency; higher-frequency radiation carries more energy per photon.

$$E=mc^2$$

This expresses mass-energy equivalence: mass is a form of energy, with the speed of light squared as the conversion factor.
