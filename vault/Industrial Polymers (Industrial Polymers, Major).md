## Introduction

Industrial polymers are chemical compounds used to make plastics, elastomers, fibres, adhesives, surface coatings, sealants, and engineered materials. They can be classified by reaction type, heat response, fibre origin, or backbone composition. In this treatment, the main division is between carbon-chain polymers ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polymer)), or vinyls, whose backbones are carbon-carbon linkages, and heterochain polymers ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polymer)), whose backbones include atoms such as oxygen, nitrogen, sulfur, or silicon.

Commercial properties depend on monomer composition, chain architecture, crystallinity, branching, stereoregularity, copolymer ratios, plasticizers, fillers, cross-linking, and processing. Small structural changes can shift a material from rigid plastic to rubber, transparent sheet, fibre, adhesive, foam, coating, or high-temperature engineering component.

## Polyolefins and Vinyl Polymers

Polyolefins are the most important industrial polymers by volume. Olefin monomers such as ethylene, propylene, and butene come mainly from natural gas and petroleum fractions and contain carbon-carbon double bonds that open during chain-growth polymerization. The resulting materials include many commodity plastics and elastomers.

Polyethylene, or PE, is the largest single class of plastics. Hans von Pechmann observed a polyethylene-like precipitate in 1899, Eugen Bamberger and Friedrich Tschirner identified polymethylene in 1900, and Eric Fawcett and Reginald Gibson made waxy PE in 1935. Industrial low-density polyethylene appeared in 1939 at Imperial Chemical Industries and served during [[World Wars (World War II)|World War II]] as radar-cable insulation.

PE forms include branched low-density polyethylene, linear low-density polyethylene, high-density polyethylene, and ultrahigh molecular weight polyethylene. LDPE is flexible and used in packaging film, bags, agricultural mulch, squeeze bottles, toys, insulation, and housewares. HDPE and related linear forms are stiffer and stronger. Chlorinated and chlorosulfonated polyethylene can become ozone- and oxidation-resistant elastomers.

Karl Ziegler and Erhard Holzkamp produced linear HDPE in 1953 using low-pressure organometallic catalysis, and Ziegler shared the 1963 Nobel Prize in Chemistry. Phillips Petroleum Company introduced LLDPE in 1968. Catalyst choice became one of the decisive tools for controlling polymer structure.

Polypropylene, or PP, is a crystalline thermoplastic made from propylene. Its commercially important isotactic form was discovered in 1954 by Giulio Natta and Paolo Chini using Ziegler-type catalysts; Natta shared the 1963 Nobel Prize. PP is stiffer and has a higher melting temperature than PE. It is used in fibres, carpets, rope, nonwovens, medical and diaper fabrics, bottles, food containers, appliance housings, toys, battery cases, furniture, and living hinges.

Polystyrene, or PS, is a transparent, rigid, brittle thermoplastic made from styrene. Eduard Simon observed styrene polymerization in 1839, but commercial success came after Robert Dreisbach and colleagues at Dow Chemical purified the monomer and developed production by 1938. Foamed PS is used in insulation, packaging, cups, egg cartons, and trays; solid PS is used in utensils, cases, and packaging. High-impact polystyrene blends PS with polybutadiene.

Polyvinyl chloride, or PVC, is second only to PE among major plastics. Eugen Baumann prepared it in 1872, and Friedrich Heinrich August Klatte patented sunlight-initiated polymerization in 1913. Pure PVC is rigid and low-flammability, useful in pipe, conduit, siding, windows, and door frames. Waldo Lonsbury Semon's 1926 discovery of plasticized PVC made flexible products such as floor tile, hose, imitation leather, and shower curtains possible.

Polyvinylidene chloride, or PVDC, is valued for very low permeability to water vapour and gases. Copolymers such as saran make transparent food-wrap films. Polyacrylonitrile, or PAN, is mainly used in acrylic fibres containing at least 85 percent PAN. Acrylics imitate wool in knitwear and carpets, resist light, mildew, and moths, and also serve as carbon-fibre precursors, asbestos replacements, industrial filters, and battery separators.

Polymethyl methacrylate, or PMMA, is transparent, rigid, weather-resistant, and a common glass substitute. It is used in illuminated signs, skylights, aircraft canopies, instrument panels, luminous ceilings, headlights, and lighting covers. Related HEMA polymers absorb water and serve soft contact lenses, while cyanoacrylates polymerize in moisture and are sold as strong contact adhesives such as Super Glue.

Fluorinated polymers include PTFE, discovered in 1938 by Roy Plunkett at DuPont and marketed as Teflon cookware in 1960. PTFE's fluorine sheath gives chemical inertness, high-temperature stability, and low friction, making it useful in bearings, bushings, acid-resistant linings, high-temperature insulation, and nonstick surfaces. Fluoroelastomers withstand oxygen, ozone, heat, fuels, oils, and solvents in aerospace and industrial seals.

## Diene, Styrene, and Rubber Polymers

Diene polymers contain monomers with two double bonds and are central to elastomers. Polybutadiene has high resilience and abrasion resistance and is blended with natural rubber or styrene-butadiene rubber, especially in tires. Other uses include footwear, cable insulation, conveyor belts, and resilient products.

Polychloroprene, or neoprene, was prepared in 1931 by Arnold Collins in Wallace Hume Carothers's DuPont group. Its chlorine-bearing structure gives resistance to oil, heat, flame, ozone, oxidation, and abrasion. It is used in hoses, belts, springs, flexible mounts, and gaskets.

Polyisoprene includes natural rubber and synthetic isoprene rubber. Natural rubber is mostly cis-1,4 polymer, stretches repeatedly to seven or eight times its original length, crystallizes on stretching, and self-reinforces. Vulcanization and compounding overcome temperature sensitivity, oxidation, ozone attack, and oil swelling. Major uses include tires, rubber springs, mountings, footwear, and adhesives.

ABS, acrylonitrile-butadiene-styrene, is a graft copolymer patented in 1948 and introduced commercially by Borg-Warner Corporation in 1954. Butadiene supplies impact strength, acrylonitrile gives heat resistance, and styrene gives rigidity. ABS is used in appliance and telephone housings, luggage, helmets, pipe fittings, and automotive parts.

Styrene-butadiene rubber, or SBR, grew from synthetic-rubber research during natural-rubber shortages in the world wars. German Buna S, patented in 1933 by Eduard Tschunkur and Walter Bock, became American GR-S during [[World Wars (World War II)|World War II]]. SBR is the most important synthetic rubber and is heavily used in automobile and truck tires, carpet backing, belting, flooring, cable insulation, and footwear.

SAN and nitrile rubber are styrene- or acrylonitrile-based copolymers with specialized properties. SAN is rigid, transparent, and more heat- and solvent-resistant than PS, serving automotive parts, battery cases, kitchenware, appliances, furniture, and medical supplies. Nitrile rubber, or NBR, resists oil and is used in fuel hoses, gaskets, rollers, waterproofed textiles, and related products.

Butyl rubber, developed in 1937 by William Sparks and Robert Thomas at Standard Oil, is a copolymer of isobutylene and isoprene with very low gas permeability and strong ozone resistance. It replaced natural rubber in many inner tubes and is important in tubeless tire liners, automotive strips, curing bladders, and halobutyl copolymers.

## Aldehyde, Cellulose, and Fibre Polymers

Aldehyde condensation polymers come from formaldehyde reacting with phenol, urea, or melamine to form thermosetting networks. Adolf von Baeyer studied phenol-formaldehyde chemistry in 1872, but Leo Hendrik Baekeland controlled the reaction in 1907 and commercialized Bakelite. Bakelite helped launch the modern plastics industry because it was synthetic, heat-resistant, moldable, and less flammable than celluloid.

Cellulosics derive from cellulose, the natural polymer of cotton, flax, hemp, jute, ramie, sisal, wood, and paper. Native cellulose behaves like a thermoset because strong attractions between molecules prevent heat-softening without decomposition. In the 19th century, chemists learned to separate cellulose from lignin, regenerate it as fibre or film, and modify it into cellulose nitrate and cellulose acetate.

Rayon, named in 1924, is regenerated purified cellulose from plant sources and was the first semi-synthetic fibre. Joseph Wilson Swan exhibited nitrocellulose fibres in 1884 and 1885, while Louis-Marie-Hilaire Bernigaud, comte de Chardonnet, commercialized Chardonnet silk from 1891. Cuprammonium and viscose processes later made rayon safer and more important.

Polyamides contain amide groups in their chains. Nylon, announced by DuPont in 1938, was the first wholly synthetic fibre and was developed from Wallace Hume Carothers's 1935 nylon 6,6 synthesis with Julian Hill and coworkers. Nylon stockings appeared in 1939, wartime military uses followed, and nylon became central to hosiery, lingerie, sportswear, luggage, upholstery, carpets, tire cords, ropes, seat belts, parachutes, coated fabrics, bearings, gears, seals, and films.

Aramids are aromatic polyamides with exceptional strength, stiffness, heat resistance, and flame resistance. DuPont produced Nomex in 1961 and Kevlar in 1971. Stephanie Kwolek, Herbert Blades, and Paul W. Morgan developed Kevlar, which is about five times stronger per weight than steel and is used in bulletproof vests, radial tires, cables, aircraft composites, boats, sports equipment, flame-resistant garments, and asbestos replacements.

## Polyesters and Polyethers

*Plastic household items*

Polyesters form through ester linkages. The leading example is polyethylene terephthalate, or PET, made from ethylene glycol and terephthalic acid. Its benzene rings give stiffness and strength, especially when drawn into aligned fibres. PET is sold as Dacron, Terylene, and related fibres in durable-press fabrics, filling, carpets, tire yarns, belts, hoses, seat belts, nonwovens, diaper sheets, and medical garments. It is also a major bottle and film plastic.

Polycarbonate, sold under names such as Lexan and Merlon, is a transparent, impact-resistant engineering polyester made from bisphenol A and phosgene. It is used in water carboys, shatter-resistant windows, safety shields, safety helmets, and compact discs. Degradable polyesters such as PGA, PLA, PHB, and PCL can degrade by water or microorganisms and are useful in absorbable sutures, drug-release systems, bottles, and packaging films.

Alkyd resins are oil-modified network polyesters developed from General Electric research in the 1920s. They are important in paints because their oil side chains cross-link in air to form a tack-free coating. Unsaturated and oil-free polyesters also serve reinforced plastics, coatings, and other industrial materials.

Polyethers contain carbon-oxygen-carbon linkages. Polyacetal, or POM, is a high-strength, crystalline, low-friction engineering plastic marketed as Delrin and Celcon for gears, bushings, bearings, plumbing fixtures, appliances, zippers, and food-processing equipment. PPO, PEK, and PEEK are engineering plastics with strength, moisture resistance, thermal stability, chemical resistance, or radiation resistance.

Epoxy resins are commonly made from epichlorohydrin and bisphenol A into epoxide-terminated prepolymers that cure into polar thermosetting networks. Early patents by J. MacIntosh and E.Y. Walford appeared in 1920, and W.H. Moss advanced epoxy chemistry in 1937. Epoxies are strong adhesives, surface coatings, circuit-board materials, aerospace composites, laminates, and flooring systems.

## High-Performance Heterochain Polymers

Polyimides contain imide linkages and often aromatic rings. DuPont's Kapton is made through a polyamide intermediate that converts to insoluble, infusible polyimide above about 150 C. Polyimides retain high strength and temperature stability, with Kapton stable in inert atmospheres up to about 500 C. Uses include aircraft components, electronics, films, adhesives, sporting goods, and composites.

Polysiloxanes, or silicones, have silicon-oxygen backbones that rotate freely and resist heat, oxygen, and ozone. Silicone oils are lubricants and hydraulic fluids, while room-temperature-vulcanizing and high-temperature-vulcanizing silicone rubbers serve O-rings, heat-resistant seals, caulks, gaskets, electrical insulators, flexible molds, and surgical implants.

Polysulfides contain sulfur in their backbones. Polyphenylene sulfide is a high-strength crystalline engineering plastic with thermal and chemical resistance, used in automotive parts, machines, appliances, electronics, electrical equipment, and coatings. Polysulfide rubber, discovered in 1926 by Joseph Cecil Patrick, became Thiokol and is used as solvent- and oil-resistant sealant, though sulfur-sulfur bonds make it prone to flow under pressure.

Polyurethanes are made by reacting isocyanates with alcohols and can form foams, fibres, elastomers, coatings, and networks. Otto Bayer prepared many polyurethanes in the late 1930s, and foaming became commercially important after moisture-generated carbon dioxide was recognized in 1941. Bayer and Monsanto formed Mobay Chemical Corporation in 1953 to produce polyurethane in the United States.

Polyurethane foams are the largest market segment. Flexible foams use polyols, TDI, water, and carbon dioxide blowing to make upholstery, bedding, automobile seats, crash panels, carpet underlay, laminates, and sponges. Rigid foams use PMDI and polyether glycols for insulation, packaging, flotation, and lightweight furnishings. Polyurethane fibres and elastomers add stretch, abrasion resistance, coating performance, and specialty mechanical properties.

## Core Equations

Chemical and materials descriptions connect composition, reaction balance, and material response:

$$\mathrm{aA+bB \rightarrow cC+dD}$$

Read the left side as the reactants or starting materials and the right side as the products; the coefficients show the proportions needed to conserve atoms or charge.

$$\sigma=E\varepsilon$$

For an elastic material, stress is proportional to strain, with Young's modulus E measuring stiffness.

$$\Delta G=\Delta H-T\Delta S$$

The delta symbol marks a change in a quantity, so the equation tracks how a system shifts between states rather than only describing a static value.

## Key Events & Developments

- Polyethylene precursor: Hans von Pechmann observed a PE-like material in 1899.
- LDPE production: Imperial Chemical Industries made industrial PE in 1939.
- Ziegler catalyst: Karl Ziegler and Erhard Holzkamp produced linear HDPE in 1953.
- Isotactic polypropylene: Giulio Natta and Paolo Chini discovered the key commercial PP form in 1954.
- Plasticized PVC: Waldo Lonsbury Semon found flexible PVC in 1926.
- Teflon: Roy Plunkett discovered PTFE in 1938.
- Bakelite: Leo Hendrik Baekeland patented controlled phenol-formaldehyde resin in 1907.
- Nylon: DuPont announced nylon in 1938, and stockings appeared in 1939.
- Kevlar: DuPont introduced Kevlar in 1971.
- Polyurethane foams: Moisture-generated carbon dioxide made foam applications commercially important after 1941.

## Interesting Facts

- Backbone chemistry is one of the simplest ways to classify major industrial polymers.
- LDPE first became important as wartime radar-cable insulation.
- Kevlar is about five times stronger per weight than steel.
- PTFE does not melt below about 300 C and has very low friction.
- Natural rubber can stretch seven or eight times its original length.
- Cyanoacrylates polymerize with atmospheric moisture and adhere strongly to skin.
- Polyurethane foam owes its cellular structure to carbon dioxide generated during reaction.
- Silicone rubber has unusually low glass-transition temperature and high gas permeability.
