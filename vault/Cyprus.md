## Introduction

Cyprus ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cyprus)) is an island in the eastern Mediterranean Sea, known since ancient times for mineral wealth, produce, wine, natural beauty, and strategic position. It lies between Europe and Asia and has absorbed influences from conquerors, pilgrims, traders, and settlers.

The island’s main cities include Nicosia, Limassol, Famagusta, and Paphos. Cyprus became independent from Britain in 1960, but conflict between Greek Cypriots and Turkish Cypriots led to Turkish military intervention in 1974. A de facto Turkish Cypriot state was proclaimed in the north in 1975, and in 1983 it declared independence as the Turkish Republic of Northern Cyprus, recognized only by Turkey.

Cyprus ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cyprus)) lies about 40 miles south of Turkey, 60 miles west of Syria, and 480 miles southeast of mainland Greece. It is the third largest Mediterranean island after Sicily and Sardinia. Its coastline is rocky and indented, with many long sandy beaches.

The Kyrenia Mountains run parallel to the northern coast and include the five-fingered peak called Pentadaktylos. The Troodos Mountains dominate the south and southwest. Their highest point is Mount Olympus, or Mount Troodos, at 6,401 feet, or 1,951 metres. Between the two systems lies the Mesaoria Plain, the island’s main cereal-growing area and the location of Nicosia.

## Climate

![[attachments/cyprus_002.webp|420]]

*Sea caves Cape Greco 9*

Cyprus ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cyprus)) has a strong Mediterranean climate, with hot dry summers from June to September and rainy winters from November to March. Short spring and autumn seasons bring rapid change. Rainfall is variable and vital for agriculture and water supply.

Average annual precipitation is about 20 inches, or 500 millimetres, but it ranges from about 14 inches at Nicosia to 41 inches on Mount Olympus. The Troodos range has below-freezing winter nights and considerable snowfall. Vegetation includes olive, carob, citrus, pine, dwarf oak, cypress, cedar, vineyards, wildflowers, eucalyptus, acacia, and lowland pine. The protected agrino, a wild sheep related to the mouflon, is the island’s only surviving large wild animal.

## People

The people of Cyprus ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cyprus)) are mainly Greek Cypriots and Turkish Cypriots. Greek Cypriots make up nearly four-fifths of the population and descend from earlier inhabitants mixed with Greek immigrants from the Peloponnese after about 1200 BCE. Turkish Cypriots, about one-fifth of the population, descend from Ottoman soldiers and settlers after the conquest of 1571, with later immigration from Turkey after 1974.

Greek is the majority language, and Turkish is the minority language. Small Maronite and Armenian communities also exist, and many members are bilingual. English is widely spoken because of British rule and later international connections. Illiteracy is very low because of a strong educational system.

## Religion

Most Greek Cypriots are Eastern Orthodox Christians. The Church of Cyprus is autocephalous, meaning independent of any patriarch. This privilege was granted by the Byzantine emperor Zeno in 488. Under the Ottoman Empire, the Orthodox archbishop also gained secular responsibilities as ethnarch of the community.

Most Turkish Cypriots are Sunni Muslims. Cyprus also has Maronite, Armenian, Roman Catholic, and Anglican Christian communities. After the Turkish occupation in 1974, settlement patterns changed sharply, with many Greek Cypriots moving south and Turkish Cypriots moving north.

## Economy

Between 1960 and 1973, the Republic of Cyprus had a free-enterprise economy based on agriculture and trade, helped by the [[United Nations]], the World Bank, the International Monetary Fund, and foreign aid. GDP, income, agricultural output, industrial production, exports, and tourism all grew strongly.

The partition of 1974 dealt a severe economic blow. The Turkish occupation covered nearly two-fifths of the island and displaced about one-third of the population. Greek Cypriots lost land, property, Famagusta’s deepwater port, and Nicosia International Airport. The Greek Cypriot sector recovered through rapid growth after 1975, especially through tourism, services, and export-oriented activity.

## Manufacturing

Manufacturing ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manufacturing)) is limited by the island’s small raw-material base. Before partition, many small owner-operated plants served the domestic market, and many were in areas occupied by Turkish forces in 1974.

After partition, industries in the Republic of Cyprus were redirected toward exports, and many factories were built in the south. Heavy industries include petroleum refining, cement, asbestos-pipe manufacturing, and thermal electricity. Light industries include clothing, footwear, beverages, machinery, transport equipment, printing, and publishing.

## Services

![[attachments/cyprus_003.webp|420]]

*Boats near Paphos, Cyprus 1626p*

Tourism ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tourism)) became one of Cyprus’s main economic sectors after independence. Many hotels and resorts were in the north before 1974, especially around Kyrenia and Famagusta-Varosha, so the Greek Cypriot sector developed Limassol, Larnaca, and Paphos as replacement tourist centres.

Services became the largest source of foreign income for the Greek Cypriot sector from the mid-1980s. Trade, offshore finance, remittances from expatriates, receipts from British bases, and tourism helped offset chronic trade deficits. Labour unions remained strong, and unemployment stayed low except after the invasion.

## Transportation and Telecommunications

Road transport is the main inland system. The British built an extensive road network after 1878, and a narrow-gauge railway later proved uneconomical and closed in the early 1950s. Since then inland movement has depended on roads.

The Greek Cypriot sector continued to develop modern highways, ports, airports, and communications. Transport systems in the divided island reflect political control, with separate development in the Turkish Cypriot north and the Greek Cypriot south.

## Justice

The legal code of Cyprus ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cyprus)) is based on Roman law. In the Greek Cypriot zone, judges are appointed by the government, but the judiciary is independent of the executive branch. The Supreme Court is the highest court and final court of appeal.

The court system includes a Permanent Assize Court for islandwide criminal jurisdiction and district courts for criminal, civil, and admiralty matters. The Turkish Cypriot zone has a similar justice system, reflecting the island’s political division.

## Political Process

The oldest established party in the Republic of Cyprus ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cyprus)) is AKEL, the Progressive Party of the Working People, founded in 1941. It was a pro-Moscow communist party and controlled the main trade union federation, though its support declined after communism collapsed in Russia and eastern Europe.

Other [[Political Parties and Interest Groups (Political Party)|political parties]] include the Movement of Social Democrats EDEK and the Democratic Rally. In the Turkish Cypriot zone, important parties include the National Unity Party, the Communal Liberation Party, and the Republican Turkish Party. Political life remains closely tied to the unresolved division between the two communities.

## Security

Security in Cyprus is shaped by the island’s division. The Republic of Cyprus has a national guard of volunteers and conscripts, while the Turkish Cypriot north also requires military service. Both sides maintain close military links with Greece or Turkey.

[[Turkey and Ancient Anatolia (Turkey)|Turkey]] keeps a large garrison in northern Cyprus, and the [[United Nations]] maintains UNFICYP peacekeepers in the demilitarized zone. The [[United Kingdom]] also keeps two sovereign military bases on the island. This mixture of forces reflects the fragile peace after 1974.

## Health, Housing, and Education

Health standards in Cyprus are high because of favourable climate and organized public and private health services. Malaria was eradicated after World War II, and echinococcosis was later removed. Life expectancy is high, and infant mortality is low.

Housing ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Housing)) became urgent after the Turkish invasion displaced many Greek Cypriots. The government built low-cost housing, offered low-interest loans, used abandoned Turkish Cypriot homes temporarily, and provided rent subsidies and aid for low-income families.

[[Education]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Education)) is well developed in both sectors. In the Greek Cypriot sector, 12 grades of free education begin at age 5, and schooling is compulsory through age 15. The University of Cyprus opened in 1992, though many students study abroad. Turkish Cypriots also maintain strong public schools and specialized postsecondary institutions, and many students study in Turkey.

## Cultural Life

Cypriot culture is divided between the Greek south and Turkish north. Since 1974, the Turkish Cypriot community has promoted Turkish and Islamic culture, supported its own press, and changed many place-names to Turkish. The anniversary of the TRNC proclamation on November 15 is celebrated in the north.

Greek Cypriots speak a Greek dialect and maintain strong links with Greece, especially through higher education and popular culture. The island’s wider cultural life includes literature, music, religious festivals, art, painted churches, and the continuing influence of both separation and shared history.

## History

The earliest human activity on Cyprus is shown by artifacts and burned animal bones at Aetokremnos, dated to about 12,000 years ago. The first known settlement was at Khirokitia, about 9,000 years ago, where about 2,000 people lived in round stone houses. After some early settlements vanished, new villages appeared around 4500-4000 BCE.

The Bronze Age made Cyprus an international trading island. Middle Bronze Age pottery shows advanced craft, and imports came from Crete, Anatolia, Syria, and Egypt. In the Late Bronze Age, contacts extended from the Aegean to the Levant and Nile delta. Cyprus was linked with copper, Cypro-Minoan writing, fine jewelry, ivory carving, bronze figures, and the major port of Engomi.

Greek immigration after about 1200 BCE led to Greek kingdoms across much of the island, and Greek became dominant from the start of the 1st millennium BCE. Phoenicians founded Citium near modern Larnaca about 800 BCE, while Amathus retained an older indigenous identity for a time. Cyprus later came under Assyrian, Egyptian, Persian, Hellenistic, and Roman influence.

[[Christianity]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christianity)) reached Cyprus early. Paul and Barnabas, a native of the Cypriot Jewish community, preached there about 45 CE and converted the proconsul Sergius Paulus. Under Byzantine rule, the Church of Cyprus gained autocephalous status in 488. Between 688 and 965, Cyprus was a kind of condominium between Byzantium and the Caliphate before returning fully to Byzantine control.

In 1191, Richard I seized Cyprus during the Crusades. The island later passed through Latin, Lusignan, Venetian, Ottoman, and British rule. Britain administered Cyprus from 1878 and made it a crown colony in 1925. Greek Cypriot demands for enosis, or union with Greece, and Turkish Cypriot demands for protection or partition shaped the struggle before independence.

The Republic of Cyprus was created in 1960 with guarantees by Greece, Turkey, and Britain. Archbishop Makarios III became president and Fazl Kucuk vice president. Constitutional conflict, intercommunal violence, and the Green Line divided Nicosia from 1964. A Greek-backed coup in 1974 was followed by Turkish military intervention and control of the northern third of the island.

The Turkish Cypriot leadership proclaimed the Turkish Federated State of Cyprus in 1975 and the Turkish Republic of Northern Cyprus in 1983. The UN condemned the declaration and continued to support withdrawal of foreign troops and reunification efforts. Talks repeatedly failed over issues such as federation, central authority, troops, property, refugees, and territory.

In the 2000s, unification efforts continued. Dimitris Christofias and Mehmet Ali Talat opened a crossing at Ledra Street in Nicosia in 2008, a symbolic move because the street had represented division since 1964. Cyprus later became entangled in the euro-zone debt crisis because of bank exposure to Greek government bonds and requested help from the EU and IMF.
