# Atoms

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> Atoms are the smallest units that retain the identity of chemical elements, consisting of a dense nucleus of protons and neutrons surrounded by quantum-mechanical electrons. The development of atomic theory—from Greek atomism and Dalton's chemical laws through the electron, nucleus, spectroscopy, and the Standard Model—connects chemical bonding to modern particle physics.

## Introduction

The atom ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atom)) is the smallest unit of matter that still retains the characteristic properties of a chemical element. It is the smallest division of matter short of releasing electrically charged particles.

Modern atomic theory describes the atom as mostly empty space containing a tiny, dense, positively charged nucleus ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atomic_nucleus)) made of protons and neutrons, surrounded by negatively charged electrons. Because quantum mechanics governs electron behaviour, no single picture of the atom is fully adequate; different models highlight different properties.

In one picture, electrons behave like particles bound by electric attraction to the nucleus. In another, they behave like standing waves whose orbital patterns determine shell structure and chemical properties.

Atomic size and atomic mass are therefore governed by different parts of the atom. Nearly all the mass lies in the nucleus, while the electron cloud determines most of the volume and almost all ordinary chemical interactions. This contrast explains why atoms can be mostly empty space yet form solid, stable matter.

## Structure and Bonding

Electrons occupy distributions called orbitals, and their arrangement into shells determines much of an atom's chemical behaviour. Chemistry depends strongly on how outer electrons are organized.

Atoms combine into molecules ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Molecule)), and molecules can often be separated more easily than atoms themselves. Breaking chemical bonds usually requires more energy because it disrupts electron arrangements between atoms.

It explains three major ways atoms bond through outer electrons: ionic bonding, where electrons are transferred; covalent bonding, where electrons are shared between neighbouring atoms; and metallic bonding, where outer electrons are shared among many atoms in a material. These bonding patterns help explain salts, molecules, and metals.

## Greek Origins of Atomic Theory

The history of atomic thought goes back to ancient Greece. Leucippus and especially Democritus proposed that matter is made of indivisible particles called atomos. These atoms were imagined as solid, hard, eternal, and moving in empty space.

Epicurus later adapted these ideas in a materialist philosophy meant to free people from superstition. Although these early theories were speculative rather than experimental, they established a durable conceptual starting point. The Roman poet Lucretius preserved and spread Epicurean atomism in his poem De rerum natura, helping the idea survive into later centuries.

Greek atomism also tried to explain qualities such as taste and colour by differences in atomic shapes and arrangements, a speculative but important attempt to connect visible properties with hidden structure.

## From Early Modern Thought to Dalton

The broad outlines of Greek atomism survived into the early modern period. Isaac Newton, for example, still pictured matter as composed of hard, solid particles created by God.

The decisive shift toward scientific atomic theory came with chemistry. Joseph-Louis Proust's law of definite proportions showed that compounds always combine in fixed weight ratios, and John Dalton built on this to create the first modern atomic theory in the early 19th century.

## Dalton, Gay-Lussac, and Avogadro

Dalton used chemical laws to argue that matter is made of atoms that combine in definite numerical patterns. He also formulated the law of multiple proportions, helping turn atomism from philosophy into science. Gay-Lussac's work on gases and Amedeo Avogadro's hypothesis that equal gas volumes contain equal numbers of molecules helped distinguish between atoms ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atom)) and molecules ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Molecule)). Although Avogadro's ideas were neglected for decades, they became essential to modern chemistry.

## Electricity, Light, and Spectra

Atomic theory was transformed further once scientists linked matter to [[Electricity and Magnetism (Electricity)|electricity]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electricity)). [[Faraday]] suspected that atoms were associated with electrical powers, and [[Maxwell]]'s theory later showed that light itself is electromagnetic.

Maxwell's theory made a new problem visible: classical electromagnetic ideas could not explain why atoms emit only specific spectral lines or why radiating electrons do not simply lose all energy.

At the same time, spectral lines provided critical clues to atomic structure. Work by Fraunhofer, Kirchhoff, and others showed that each element has a characteristic pattern of emitted or absorbed light. These patterns revealed that the atom could not be a simple indivisible lump.

## Discovery of the Electron

The breakthrough came with studies of cathode rays. Work by Crookes and others led finally to J.J. Thomson's discovery of the electron in 1897. This demonstrated that the atom contains smaller charged parts and is therefore divisible.
This is a historic turning point. The ancient image of the atom as solid and indivisible could no longer stand. Thomson went on to propose a model in which negatively charged electrons were embedded in a sphere of positive charge, the so-called plum-pudding atom.

## Radioactivity and the Nucleus

Research on radioactivity added further evidence of atomic complexity. Becquerel discovered spontaneous penetrating radiation from uranium salts, and Pierre and Marie Curie discovered strongly radioactive elements such as polonium and radium.

[[Rutherford (Ernest Rutherford)|Ernest Rutherford]] showed that radioactive emissions include different kinds of particles and radiation. These studies prepared the way for a radically new image of atomic structure.

## Rutherford's Atomic Model

![[attachments/atoms_001.webp|420]]

*Rutherford planetary model of the atom*

In the famous gold-foil experiment, Rutherford and his collaborators found that most alpha particles passed through thin metal foil, while a very small number were strongly deflected. This led Rutherford to conclude that most atomic mass and positive charge are concentrated in a tiny central nucleus ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atomic_nucleus)).

This model explained the scattering results but created new problems, especially concerning how electrons could remain around the nucleus and how atomic structure related to chemical behaviour.

## Atomic Number and X-Rays

Henry Moseley's X-ray studies showed that each element is fundamentally identified by its atomic number, that is, the positive charge of its nucleus, rather than simply by atomic mass.

This discovery clarified the structure of the periodic table and confirmed that nuclear charge is central to atomic identity. It was one of the most important advances in early modern atomic physics.

## Wave-Particle Duality and Quantum Theory

![[attachments/atoms_002.webp|420]]

*Hydrogen atomic orbital shapes*

The atom could not be fully understood with classical mechanics alone. The Compton effect showed that light behaves as if it carries discrete packets of energy and momentum, and Louis de Broglie proposed that matter, too, has wave properties.

These ideas led toward quantum mechanics ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_mechanics)), in which electrons are described by wave functions and orbitals rather than tiny planet-like paths. Quantum theory is the only framework capable of explaining spectral lines and stable atomic structure.

## Subatomic Particles and the Standard Model

The later history of the atom opened into particle physics. Hundreds of subatomic particles were discovered during the 20th century, but patterns in their behaviour led to simpler underlying theories.

The modern [[Standard Model]] classifies matter into leptons and quarks. Protons and neutrons are hadrons built from quarks, while electrons belong to the leptons. Forces are carried by field quanta such as the photon and, in the electroweak theory, by particles such as the W, Z, and Higgs boson.

Quarks are not observed in isolation because the strong force confines them inside hadrons, but scattering experiments and particle patterns confirm their role.

## Conclusion

The atom ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atom)) is both the basis of chemistry and the gateway to modern physics. What began as a philosophical speculation about indivisible particles became a deeply evidence-based theory involving nuclei, electrons, quantum waves, and subatomic forces.

Its history is therefore also the history of modern science itself: a gradual shift from speculation to experiment, from solid particles to quantum structure, and from ordinary matter to the deeper architecture described by nuclear and particle physics.

## Core Equations

Atomic structure is organized by charge, mass number, and quantized energy:

$$A=Z+N$$

Mass number A is the total of protons Z and neutrons N in an atomic nucleus.

$$E=h\nu$$

This relation says that photon energy is proportional to frequency; higher-frequency radiation carries more energy per photon.

## Key Events & Developments

- Greek atomism begins with Leucippus and Democritus.
- Dalton turns atomism into a chemical theory in the early 19th century.
- Spectroscopy, cathode rays, and radioactivity reveal subatomic structure.
- Rutherford's nucleus and Moseley's atomic number reorganize atomic theory.
- Quantum mechanics and the Standard Model provide the modern framework.
