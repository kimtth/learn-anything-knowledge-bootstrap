# Modernization and Industrialization (Modernization)

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> Modernization is the broad transformation of social life associated with scientific habits, industrial economies, constitutional legitimacy, nationalism, urban growth, and new ideas of progress. The account situates industrialization within a longer transition from agricultural civilization to modern political and social institutions.

## Introduction

![[attachments/modernization_001.webp|420]]

*Industrialized city landscape*

Modernization ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modernization_theory)) is the transformation from traditional, rural, agrarian society to secular, urban, industrial society. Modern society is industrial society in a broad sense: industrialism changes technology, economy, politics, culture, family life, social structure, and ideas.

Modernization ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modernization_theory)) is continuous and uneven rather than a finished achievement. It creates growth and dynamism, but also backward regions, peripheral groups, rising expectations, conflict, and world instability because nations modernize at unequal speeds and with unequal power.

The modern industrial age is extremely recent in human history. For most of human existence, people lived as hunters and gatherers. Around 10,000 BC, agriculture and herding created settled villages, surplus food, cities, trade, government, laws, armies, and a division of labour. This Neolithic revolution ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neolithic_Revolution)) remained the basis of civilization until industrialism.

The Industrial Revolution ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Industrial_Revolution)) was the second great leap in social evolution. It began in northwestern Europe between the 16th and 18th centuries, especially in England, the [[Netherlands]], northern [[France]], and northern [[Germany]]. This was surprising because the region had once been less advanced than Mediterranean, Arabic, and Chinese civilizations.

Max Weber argued that [[Protestantism]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Protestantism)), especially Puritanism, helped form modern capitalism by valuing disciplined work, frugality, and rational enterprise. Later historians disputed the timing, but the fit between Protestant work ethic, capitalism, rational culture, and modern science remained important.

The scientific revolution ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scientific_Revolution)) in England, France, and the Netherlands during the 17th century created habits of observation, hypothesis, experiment, verification, skepticism, and provisional knowledge. Even before industry depended heavily on theory, this rational outlook shaped modern society and later social science.

Western expansion, colonization, science, and industry gave Europe and its offshoots global power. The idea of progress emerged when moralists and philosophers began to believe the modern world could equal or surpass Greece and Rome and show less-developed societies their possible future.

Modernity's political form came from the American Revolution and French Revolution. These made constitutional and democratic legitimacy central, even when dictatorships later claimed popular consent. The idea that political authority must be based on the will of the people became a norm of modern society.

The American Revolution also strengthened national self-determination. Legitimate states were increasingly imagined as nation-states, where a people sharing culture ruled a common territory. Nationalism later helped dissolve European empires and became central to liberal and radical movements.

The economic model of modernity came from Britain. Industrialization reduced agricultural employment, expanded industry, raised output, increased population, moved workers into cities, separated work from family life, and created factory towns. Observers such as Alexis de Tocqueville, Friedrich Engels, and Karl Marx treated Britain as a social laboratory for the future.

Modern society is partly defined by what it rejects from agrarian society. It emphasizes individualization, specialization, and abstraction: the individual replaces the kin group as the basic unit, institutions perform specialized tasks, and rules based on science and expertise replace custom, divine authority, and personal privilege.

Economic growth is the core feature of industrial society. Edward Anthony Wrigley and W.W. Rostow stressed the shift to sustained increases in real income and self-sustaining growth. Its components include machines, inanimate energy, free labour, factories, entrepreneurs, and eventually commercialized agriculture.

Industrialization breaks old limits of scarcity by raising productivity and producing food surpluses for urban populations. John Maynard Keynes even suggested that the economic problem might become solvable for the first time, though abundance remained uneven and uncertain.

Modernization transformed population. The Neolithic revolution raised world population to about 150 million by 1000 BC, but the Industrial Revolution caused a much faster second explosion. World population rose from about 500 million in the mid-17th century to billions by the 20th century.

The demographic transition begins when death rates fall because of medical knowledge, public health, and food supply, while birth rates remain high. Later, urban industrial populations voluntarily lower birth rates. Europe, Japan, and the Soviet Union showed versions of this pattern; many postwar developing countries saw death rates fall before fertility declined.

Modern life is urban life. Industrialization requires centralized production, exchange, credit, communications, transport, law, coinage, and administration. The [[United Kingdom]] became the first truly urban industrial society during the 19th century, and Europe, the United States, Japan, Asia, Africa, and Latin America later followed at different speeds.

Industrial urbanism differs from preindustrial urbanism because it becomes the dominant experience of society, not a minority culture. Cities concentrate workers, institutions, communication, creativity, and power, while the countryside becomes dependent on city-centred markets, finance, and administration.

City life has two faces. Georg Simmel and Louis Wirth stressed nervous overstimulation, reserve, isolation, fleeting fashions, and loneliness in crowds. Emile Durkheim saw cities as homes of progress, where new ideas, needs, and customs spread outward.

Industrialization also transformed the [[Family and Kinship (Family)|family]]. In preindustrial society, the family was a unit of production, consumption, socialization, and authority. Industry moved production to factories, enclosed commons, and made families dependent on wages and external economic structures.

At first, working families pooled wages and remained collective support systems. Over time, wages became individual, the state took over welfare functions, and the family shrank toward the nuclear household focused on child-rearing, emotional life, sexuality, recreation, and private identity.

[[Work and Employment (Work)|Work]] became a major source of identity in industrial society. Occupation replaced family or place as the common answer to who a person was. Yet the detailed division of labour fragmented tasks, as [[Adam Smith]] showed with pin-making and [[Henry Ford]] later developed through the assembly line.

This division of labour raised productivity but threatened meaning. John Ruskin said it was not labour but men who were divided. Mechanization and automation were already implied once tasks became simple enough for unskilled workers to repeat.

Industrial society created new class structures based on market position, property, education, training, and employment. Karl Marx expected capitalism to polarize society into bourgeoisie and proletariat, but older identities of race, religion, region, ethnicity, and nationality continued to overlap with economic class.

Modernization produces secularization, or the declining centrality of religious authority as an organizing principle. It replaces supernatural explanations with scientific ones, but it does not eliminate religion. Industrial societies may keep rituals, beliefs, churches, revivals, and private faith, as in the [[United States of America (United States)|United States]] and [[India]].

Secularization belongs to a broader process of rationalization. Weber saw rational law, administration, bureaucracy, scientific expertise, calculation, and impersonal rules as central to modern society. Bureaucracy can be impartial and efficient, but also despotic, rigid, and emotionally stifling.

Modern society brings abundance, mobility, freedom, and opportunity, but also social pathologies. Crowding, pollution, environmental destruction, tourism, loneliness, failure, crime, suicide, mental disorder, and anomie accompany the same forces that produce progress.

Marx described alienation in industrial work: workers are estranged from their activity, product, creativity, and human potential because labour is fragmented and controlled by others. Industrial growth also creates persistent poverty among those unable to compete by industrial rules.

The nuclear family carries heavy emotional pressure because it is one of the few remaining intimate face-to-face groups. Divorce, one-parent families, adolescent rebellion, and unstable remarriage show the strain of making family life repair the effects of impersonal work and mass society.

Advanced industrial societies also face postmaterialist values, political apathy, mass communications, and mass society. Leisure, environment, self-improvement, and quality of life become important, while centralized parties, media, and institutions may weaken civic participation and pluralism.

Non-Western modernization first used the Western industrial model because only industrial societies could act independently in the world system. [[Japan]] showed that a non-Western society could industrialize and become equal or superior to some Western powers.

There are several routes to modernity. Britain, France, Belgium, and the United States relied heavily on entrepreneurs and markets; Germany and Japan used stronger state direction; the Soviet Union, China, India, and many postcolonial states used planning, one-party guidance, protectionism, or developmental nationalism.

Late developers often placed the state at the centre of industrialization. The state directed investment, labour, unions, credit, transport, communication, education, and mass socialization to national ends, whether or not the economy was formally nationalized.

The modern world can be viewed through West-East, North-South, or world-system divisions. The West-East ideological divide declined after the Soviet collapse in the early 1990s. The North-South distinction highlights unequal wealth, while Immanuel Wallerstein's world-system model describes core, semi-peripheral, and peripheral positions in a single capitalist world economy.

Multinational corporations embody global interdependence. They move manufacturing to cheaper labour regions, keep planning and research in skilled core countries, and arrange profits through favourable tax locations. This creates a new international division of labour.

Postindustrial society shifts employment from manufacturing to services. Health, education, finance, government, leisure, entertainment, welfare, science, and technology expand, while white-collar and knowledge workers replace blue-collar factory workers as the typical industrial labour force.

Late industrial society spreads rather than ends urbanism. Suburbanization and exurbanization appear to escape the city, but they create larger metropolitan regions and megalopolises. Constantinos Apostolos Doxiadis imagined this trend leading to Ecumenopolis, a world-city linked by transport and electronic communication.

Modernization ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modernization_theory)) also creates reactions against itself. Movements for small scale, alternative technology, craft life, regional autonomy, ethnicity, religious revival, youth culture, and cultural difference resist bureaucracy, uniformity, scientific rationality, and centralized nation-states.

The final judgment on modernization remains unresolved. It brings material abundance, scientific power, global unity, and higher standards, but also magnifies failures, disasters, alienation, and social strain. To modernize is to accept both gains and losses; not to modernize is to lose a place in contemporary humanity.

## Key Events & Developments

![[attachments/modernization_002.webp|420]]

*Windmill and modern landscape*

- c. 10,000 BC: Agriculture and herding began the Neolithic revolution ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neolithic_Revolution)).
- 16th century: The Protestant Reformation contributed to cultural conditions later linked with capitalism.
- 17th century: The scientific revolution ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scientific_Revolution)) strengthened rational methods and modern knowledge.
- late 18th century: The American and French revolutions helped define democratic constitutional legitimacy.
- 18th and 19th centuries: The British Industrial Revolution provided the model for industrial society.
- 20th century: Late developers used state planning, protectionism, and national development strategies.
- early 1990s: The collapse of Soviet communism weakened the West-East model of modern world division.

## Key People

- Max Weber ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Max_Weber)): Sociologist who analyzed Protestantism, rationalization, bureaucracy, and modern capitalism.
- Karl Marx ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karl_Marx)): Theorist of industrial capitalism, class, alienation, and global historical development.
- Emile Durkheim ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C3%89mile_Durkheim)): Sociologist who analyzed anomie, urban progress, and modern social strain.
- Alexis de Tocqueville ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexis_de_Tocqueville)): Analyst of democracy, revolution, and modern political legitimacy.
- [[Adam Smith]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adam_Smith)): Economist whose pin-factory example illustrated the division of labour.
- Immanuel Wallerstein ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Immanuel_Wallerstein)): World-system theorist who described core, semi-periphery, and periphery in the capitalist world economy.
- Constantinos Apostolos Doxiadis ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constantinos_Apostolos_Doxiadis)): Planner who imagined world urbanization culminating in Ecumenopolis.

## Interesting Facts

- Modern industrial society occupies only the last tiny fraction of human social history.
- Industrialization can urbanize society even when people believe they are escaping cities through suburbs.
- The same processes that create abundance also create alienation, anomie, pollution, and new poverty.
- [[Japan]] showed that a non-Western society could modernize without simply copying every Western institution.
- Postindustrial society still belongs to modernization because services, knowledge, and science extend industrialism's logic.
