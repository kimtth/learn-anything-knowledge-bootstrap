## Introduction

East Asian arts ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asian_art)) means the visual arts, performing arts, and music of [[China]], [[Korea]], and [[Japan]]. The region can sometimes be defined more widely to include the Indochinese peninsula, nearby islands, and Mongolia, especially because many of these cultures shared Buddhist ties. But it gives special attention to China, Korea, and Japan because they were linked for several thousand years by a common written language, close cultural exchange, political contact, and sometimes conflict.

## Neolithic Vessels

Early Neolithic vessel traditions show how art, function, and social life grew together. Earthenware vessels had practical uses, especially for storage, but their shapes and decoration also reveal a strong decorative sense. As communities moved from nomadic and foraging life toward more settled crop-producing life, storage became more important, and vessel forms became more varied and refined.

[[Japan]]'s visual art shows the cycle of receiving outside influence and then asserting national style. Chinese styles were important at key points in Japanese history, especially in ink monochrome painting and calligraphy. Yet Japanese artists did not simply repeat Chinese models. They reshaped them and added a strong interest in human activity, story, design, and the fleeting moment.

## Dance and Theatre

![[attachments/east_asian_arts_003.webp|420]]

*Muzeul Vasile Grigore 16 - Asian ceramics*

The performing arts ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Performing_arts)) centred especially on dance and theatre. From ancient times, dance and theatre were important in China, Korea, and Japan, and they were often tied closely to religious belief. In China, records from about 1000 BCE describe costumed male and female shamans who sang and danced with music to bring heavenly spirits down to earth. By at least the 4th century BCE, performers were using makeup and costume to impersonate other characters.

## Performing and Visual Arts

There were several strong links between the performing arts ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Performing_arts)) and visual arts in China and Japan. During the Song dynasty, from 960-1279, China had Northern and Southern schools of painting. The Northern school used bold outlines and strong green, blue, and gold colors. The Southern school favored delicate monochrome ink landscapes. Opera schools of the same period showed similar contrasts: the Northern style was energetic and full of action, while the Southern style emphasized soft emotion and gentle singing.

## Form and Artifice

East Asian theatre often creates a deliberate tension between artistic aim and artistic method. In China, children could be moved like human puppets, while adults performed before lighted screens to create shadow plays. In Japan, puppets could perform realistic daily actions, while live Kabuki actors avoided ordinary realism. These contradictions were not mistakes. They were part of the audience's pleasure in form, convention, and controlled artifice.

## Key Events & Developments

- 14,000 BCE: Early earthenware vessels appeared in [[Japan]], giving the region some of the world's earliest dated pottery and showing that useful objects could also carry symbolic and design value.
- 3500 BCE: Earthenware vessels were documented in [[Korea]], showing the growth of Neolithic craft traditions on the peninsula.
- Around 2000 BCE: [[China]] developed a bronze culture that later supported the Shang dynasty's advances in bronze, stone, ceramics, jade, and pictograph-based writing.
- About 1600-1046 BCE: The Shang dynasty produced major advances in bronze, ceramic, jade, stone, and written culture, strengthening China's role as a reference culture in East Asia.
- From about 700 BCE: Bronze production and rice cultivation spread into Korea and later Japan, linking material culture with agricultural change.
- 206 BCE-220 CE: The Han dynasty encouraged the gradual spread of Chinese cultural elements into Korea and Japan and produced early written Chinese descriptions of Japanese society.
- 618-907: The Tang dynasty made China one of the great international powers of its age and deepened the prestige of Chinese cultural models.
- By the 5th century CE: A Chinese dynastic line adopted [[Buddha and Buddhism (Buddhism)|Buddhism]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buddhism)) as a state religion, helping Buddhist images, sects, and patronage become major forces in East Asian art.
- In the 7th century: Korean performers and Central Asian-linked traditions carried court dances, Buddhist masked dance plays, and puppet traditions into [[Japan]], where forms such as bugaku and gigaku developed.
- Until 668: The kingdom of Koguryo preserved court music and dances from Central Asia, Han China, Manchuria, and Korea for more than 700 years.
- In the late 13th century: Mongol forces twice failed to invade [[Japan]], helping Japan remain relatively isolated from foreign occupation for many centuries.
- After 1868: The Meiji Restoration encouraged Japanese study of Western performing arts, and this later helped transmit Western realism, ballet, and modern theatre to China and Korea.
- In the 17th-19th centuries: Large cities and merchant economies helped popular theatre grow in China and Japan, especially Chinese opera, Kabuki, and Bunraku.

## Key People

- Zeami ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zeami)): A Japanese Noh performer and theorist of 1363-1443 who described Noh as a total art joining imitation, dance, dialogue, narration, music, staging, and audience response.
- Mimaji: A Korean performer who brought a Buddhist masked dance play from the southern Chinese court of Wuhou to the Japanese court at Nara in the 7th century.
- Xuanzong ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Xuanzong)), also called Minghuang ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minghuang)): The 8th-century Tang emperor who founded palace schools in Chang'an for music, dance, and acting, including the Pear Garden.
- Toshusai Sharaku ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Toshusai_Sharaku)): An ukiyo-e artist who became known for portraits of Kabuki actors, showing the close link between Japanese popular theatre and wood-block print culture.
- Zhu Zaiyu ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zhu_Zaiyu)): A Chinese mathematician and musicologist of the late 16th century who discovered a formula like Western well-tempered tuning before it was accepted in Chinese practice.
- Chinese emperors, Korean kings, Japanese emperors, and shoguns: Rulers who supported court performance, helping music, dance, and theatre reach high artistic levels.
- Samurai ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samurai)): The Japanese military ruling class that cultivated Zen-influenced arts such as Noh, tea ceremony, ink painting, and rock-and-sand gardens in the 15th and 16th centuries.

## Interesting Facts

- Calligraphy was more than writing in East Asia. It was a visual art, a way to transmit values, and the foundation for painting because brush control mattered in both arts.
- [[Buddha and Buddhism (Buddhism)|Buddhism]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buddhism)) became the strongest long-term artistic patron in East Asia because its sects, images, and teaching mission moved across borders more powerfully than any one court or dynasty.
- Bugaku disappeared in China and Korea but survived in Japan for about 12 centuries and is still performed at the Imperial Palace in Tokyo.
- Kabuki and ukiyo-e both grew from lively 18th-century urban culture in Japan and shared bright color, verve, erotic energy, and interest in the passing moment.
- East Asian theatre often values stylized form over realism: Japanese puppets can act out daily realism while live Kabuki actors deliberately avoid copying daily life.
- Noh performances could last about 8 hours, and Kabuki could last up to 12 hours, so audiences expected a long sequence of music, speech, movement, and visual patterns.
- Chinese actors have long been called children of the Pear Garden because the Tang emperor Xuanzong founded an acting school by that name in Chang'an.
- East Asian music ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Music_of_Asia)) often has a pentatonic core, but it is not limited to the five black notes of the piano; authentic traditions use a much wider sound world.
- Transparency in East Asian music means that even in a large ensemble, individual instruments should remain clear to the listener instead of blending into one orchestral color.
- Music systems are not universal languages. Chinese music, Beethoven's music, and Indian music each make sense inside their own rules, but each can seem illogical when judged by another system.
