# Nerves and Nervous Systems (Nervous System)

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> Nervous systems coordinate stimuli and responses through cells that receive, conduct, and transmit signals. This comparative account follows diffuse nerve nets and centralized systems, neuron structure, ions and electrical impulses, synapses, and the increasing concentration of control in vertebrate brains and spinal cords.

## Introduction

![[attachments/nervous_system_001.png|420]]

*Neuron cell diagram*

Nervous systems ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nervous_system)) are organized groups of cells specialized for conducting electrochemical stimuli from sensory receptors to sites where responses occur. Living organisms must detect changes in light, temperature, sound, motion, odour, body position, and internal organs, then analyze and respond to them. Chemical communication alone was too slow for increasingly complex life, so nervous systems evolved to transmit electrical impulses rapidly along specialized nerve cells.

Two broad nervous-system types exist. Diffuse systems, found in lower invertebrates, lack a brain and distribute neurons in a netlike pattern. Centralized systems, found in higher invertebrates and vertebrates, concentrate coordination in a dominant region. Vertebrates show the most developed centralization, with a brain and spinal cord connected to the rest of the body by the peripheral nervous system.

## Stimulus-Response Coordination

The simplest stimulus-response relation is direct: a change in the environment produces a reaction. Single-celled organisms respond through irritability of cell fluid, while algae, protozoans, and fungi can move toward or away from stimuli in taxis. Larger organisms require controllers that coordinate responses across many body parts.

Multicellular control uses chemical regulation ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Regulation_of_chemicals)) and nervous regulation. Hormones produced by specialized cells diffuse or travel in blood to target cells, changing growth, form, reproduction, metabolism, and behaviour. Plants use hormonal controllers for tropisms, such as stems growing toward light and roots growing downward. Nervous regulation is faster and more precise, making rapid integrated behaviour possible.

## The Nerve Cell

![[attachments/nervous_system_002.png|420]]

*Synapse diagram*

The neuron theory began with Santiago Ramon y Cajal's 1889 observation that the nervous system is composed mainly of separate cellular units communicating by contiguity rather than direct continuity. Although not universally absolute, this idea remains the central principle of neuroscience. Nervous tissue contains neurons and neuroglial cells.

Neurons have individual identities based on location, properties, inputs, outputs, and secretions. The human brain contains roughly 85 billion to 200 billion neurons. Most neurons have a soma, an axon, and dendrites. Their ability to integrate signals and transmit information to selected targets is the foundation of nervous-system function.

## Soma

The soma, or cell body, contains the nucleus and much of the machinery needed for growth, metabolism, and protein synthesis. The plasma membrane is a lipid bilayer with embedded proteins, including ion channels that can open or close in different conformations. These channels determine neuronal excitability and electrical activity.

The neuron's cytoskeleton includes neurofilaments, actin-containing filaments, myosin-like filaments, and tubulin microtubules. It supports cell shape, helps move membrane proteins, and assists transport inside the cell. Membrane carbohydrates and glycoproteins act in adhesion, recognition, and chemical interaction with other cells.

## Nucleus

The nucleus defines the soma and contains chromosomes, the genetic material controlling protein synthesis, growth, and differentiation. It is surrounded by a double nuclear envelope with pores that allow communication with the cytoplasm. Neuronal proteins include enzymes, receptors, hormones, and cytoskeletal structures.

## Organelles

The endoplasmic reticulum and Golgi apparatus produce, modify, and package proteins. Rough ER, found in the soma, has ribosomes that synthesize proteins, while smooth ER connects the rough ER to the Golgi and can extend into the axon. The Golgi adds carbohydrates to proteins and packages glycoproteins into vesicles that enter the cell membrane.

## Axon

The axon arises from the axon hillock, where nerve impulses are generated, and conducts impulses away from soma or dendrites. Large axons may be myelinated by oligodendrocytes in the central nervous system or Schwann cells in peripheral nerves. Myelin is lipid-rich insulation interrupted by nodes of Ranvier, where the membrane contacts extracellular fluid.

Axon terminals release neurotransmitters at synapses. Enzymes needed for transmitter synthesis are made in the soma and transported by axoplasmic flow. Presynaptic terminals contain synaptic vesicles filled with neurotransmitter and active zones where vesicles fuse with the membrane during signalling.

## Dendrites and Neuroglia

Dendrites are usually shorter, unmyelinated branches that receive synaptic input. Many have dendritic spines that isolate small electrical changes and increase receptive surface. Although axons are usually output structures and dendrites input structures, dendrites can also form synapses with dendrites, axons, or somata, and some neurons lack axons.

Neuroglia outnumber neurons by at least 10 to 1. They lack axons, do not form synapses, and can continue dividing. Major types include astrocytes, oligodendrocytes, and sometimes microglia. Astrocytes support neuronal environments, oligodendrocytes form central myelin, and glial interactions help maintain the nervous system's structure and function.

## Water

Osmosis governs water movement across semipermeable membranes. Water diffuses from low osmotic pressure to high osmotic pressure, and pressure builds until equilibrium is reached. Because neuronal membranes are flexible, osmotic differences can change cell volume; if external osmotic pressure falls, water enters and the neuron swells.

## Ions

Ionic diffusion creates the electrical basis of nervous activity. Ions such as potassium and chloride move according to concentration gradients and electrical forces. If one ion crosses a membrane while its counterion cannot, charge separation creates a membrane potential. Neuronal plasma membranes use these potential differences to generate and transmit electrical impulses.

## Depolarization

Depolarization occurs when positive charge enters the neuron and makes the inside less negative. Local potentials are graded, but when threshold is reached, voltage-dependent sodium channels open fully. Sodium rushes in, polarity reverses, and an action potential begins. This all-or-none impulse propagates without losing amplitude.

## Repolarization

Repolarization follows when sodium channels inactivate and potassium channels open. Potassium leaves the cell, restoring the inside-negative membrane state. An absolute refractory period prevents a second action potential immediately afterward, followed by a relative refractory period in which a stronger stimulus is required.

## Conduction

Conduction is the traveling wave of depolarization and repolarization along a nerve fibre. Information is encoded mainly by the number and frequency of impulses, not by action-potential size. Conduction velocity increases with fibre diameter, temperature, and myelination. In myelinated fibres, saltatory conduction lets impulses jump from node to node along the axon.

## Chemical Synaptic Transmission

At a chemical synapse, an action potential reaches the axon terminal and causes calcium entry. Increased calcium raises the probability that synaptic vesicles will fuse with the terminal membrane and release neurotransmitter into the synaptic cleft by exocytosis. Vesicle membranes are later recovered by endocytosis, refilled, and reused.

Neurotransmitter release is quantal, because chemicals are packaged in similar vesicles that release discrete parcels. Each parcel contributes incrementally to the postsynaptic potential. This mechanism lets a presynaptic neuron translate action potentials into chemical signals that change the electrical state of a postsynaptic cell.

## Inactivation

Neurotransmitter inactivation lets postsynaptic cells accurately reproduce rapid sequences of arriving impulses. Transmitters are removed by diffusion from the cleft, uptake into the presynaptic terminal, or enzymatic breakdown into inactive compounds. Ion movement across membranes is maintained by protein channels and pumps, especially the sodium-potassium pump, which preserves disequilibrium needed for resting potential and action potentials.

## Acetylcholine

Acetylcholine was first studied at neuromuscular junctions and became central to understanding neurotransmission. Sir Henry Dale's principle stated in 1935 that a neurotransmitter released at one terminal of a neuron can be presumed to be released at other terminals of that same neuron. Motor neurons release acetylcholine at muscle and at collateral spinal synapses, and muscle membranes contain dense clusters of cholinergic receptors.

## Dopamine, Serotonin, and Amino Acids

Dopamine is a precursor of norepinephrine and acts as a neurotransmitter in brain synapses implicated in schizophrenia and Parkinson disease. D1 receptors stimulate cAMP synthesis, while D2 receptors inhibit it. Serotonin is linked to sleep, sexual drive, mood, and peripheral muscle priming. Amino-acid transmitters include excitatory glutamate and aspartate and inhibitory GABA and glycine.

## Evolution and Development of the Nervous System

The evolution of nervous systems involves older systems serving new functions and new systems meeting new requirements. Earlier theories proposed non-nervous effectors, then receptors, then protoneurons, but this sequence is no longer accepted. Non-nervous epithelial tissues in some simple animals can conduct electrical potentials, suggesting that nervous systems may have evolved from electrically coupled epithelial sheets.

Evolution requires heritable encoding, variation by mutation or recombination, and selection of favourable traits. As multicellular animals grew larger, internal communication became necessary. Two broad systems emerged: diffuse nervous systems and centralized nervous systems.

## Diffuse Nervous Systems

Diffuse nervous systems are primitive systems in which nerve cells are spread through the organism, often beneath the epidermis, without a large brain. They occur in cnidarians and ctenophores. Hydra nerve nets and the coordinated movement of sea anemones show that diffuse systems can still produce integrated behaviour and prolonged responses.

## Annelids

[[Annelids]], including earthworms and leeches, have relatively simple but centralized nervous systems. Earthworms have a bilobed brain above the pharynx, sensory nerves to the front of the body, and a ventral nerve cord with segmental ganglia. Giant axons allow rapid defensive contraction, while crawling depends on reflex arcs coordinating segmental muscle contraction and stretch receptors.

## Arthropods

Arthropod nervous systems have a brain divided into protocerebrum, deutocerebrum, and tritocerebrum, plus a ventral nerve cord with ganglia. The protocerebrum integrates visual and anterior sensory information; the deutocerebrum handles first antennae; the tritocerebrum handles second antennae and mouthpart/gut control. Insects have thoracic and abdominal ganglia, cuticular sensory hairs, compound eyes, and specialized chemoreceptors.

## Encephalization

Encephalization is the evolutionary concentration and elaboration of brain structures. Early vertebrates associated olfactory organs with forebrain, eyes with midbrain, and ears and related organs with hindbrain. The brainstem became a five-region brain: telencephalon, diencephalon, mesencephalon, metencephalon, and myelencephalon. Over evolution, functions shifted from lower brainstem regions toward higher cerebral cortex.

## Core Equations

Health and behavioural sciences often quantify risk, dose, change over time, and population variation:

$$C(t)=C_0e^{-kt}$$

This models concentration falling exponentially over time, often used for clearance, decay, or elimination processes.

$$z=\frac{x-\mu}{\sigma}$$

A z-score tells how many standard deviations a value lies above or below the mean.

$$\text{risk}=\frac{\text{cases}}{\text{population}}$$

This ratio turns the preceding idea into a measurable comparison, with the numerator as the quantity of interest and the denominator as the scale used for comparison.

## Key Events & Developments

- 1889: Santiago Ramon y Cajal formulated the neuron-theory observation of separate nerve-cell units.
- 1935: Sir Henry Dale stated Dale's principle about neurotransmitter release.
- 1944: Joseph Erlanger and Herbert Gasser received the Nobel Prize for work on electrical signs of nervous activity.
- 20th century: Ion channels, neurotransmitters, synapses, and evolutionary nervous-system models were increasingly clarified.

## Interesting Facts

- Saltatory conduction means action potentials jump between nodes of Ranvier.
- Neurotransmitter release is quantal because vesicles release chemical parcels.
- The sodium-potassium pump maintains ion disequilibrium across neuronal membranes.
- Diffuse nerve nets can coordinate behaviour without a brain.
