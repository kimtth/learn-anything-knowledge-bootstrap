# Nepal

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> Nepal is a Himalayan country between India and China whose mountains, climate, biological resources, and strategic position shape its society and development. Its modern history includes long isolation, the 1950 opening, multiparty politics, abolition of monarchy, and the democratic republic established in 2008.

## Introduction

Nepal is a landlocked Asian country on the southern slopes of the Himalayas, between [[India]] and the Tibet Autonomous Region of [[China]]. It extends about 500 miles east to west and 90 to 150 miles north to south, with Kathmandu as capital. Long isolated under hereditary prime ministers, Nepal opened after the palace revolt of 1950, joined the [[United Nations]] in 1955, adopted multiparty parliamentarism in 1991, and became a democratic republic in 2008 after monarchy was abolished.

Nepal's geopolitical position between India and China gives it strategic importance. It seeks balance between the two powers and marks the southern edge of China's Himalayan sphere. Geographic isolation and self-imposed isolation contributed to Nepal's low level of development, while India, China, the United States, the United Kingdom, Japan, Denmark, Germany, Canada, Switzerland, and others have provided foreign aid influenced partly by strategic concerns.

## Relief

![[attachments/nepal_002.webp|420]]

*Himalayan mountain landscape at Cholatse in Nepal*

Nepal has some of the world's most rugged mountain terrain, with about three-fourths of the country covered by mountains. From south to north it has four physical belts: the Tarai, a low fertile plain along India; the Churia foothills and Inner Tarai; the mid-mountain region between the Mahabharat Range and the Great Himalayas; and the Great Himalaya Range, rising above 29,000 feet. Kathmandu Valley lies north of the Mahabharat Range, enclosed by higher Himalayan systems.

## Drainage

Nepal's major [[Rivers|rivers]], including the Kosi, Narayani, and Karnali, flow south across Himalayan ranges and cut deep transverse valleys. Their watersheds often lie north of the highest peaks, in Tibet. The Kathmandu Valley is drained by the Baghmati River, while the Pokhara Valley is drained by the Seti and includes Phewa Lake. Rivers have major hydroelectric potential, though the Tarai suffers serious flooding in lowlands.

## Climate

![[attachments/nepal_003.webp|420]]

*Mount Everest in the Himalayas*

Nepal's [[Climate and Weather (Climate)|climate]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Climate)) changes sharply with elevation. The Tarai has subtropical monsoon conditions, the mid-mountain region is warm temperate, higher mountains are cool temperate, and high Himalayan slopes become Alpine and then permanently snow-covered. Eastern Nepal and mountain regions receive more rainfall than the drier west. Kathmandu receives most rain from June to September, while Pokhara is especially wet because monsoon winds release moisture against the Annapurna range.

## Plant and Animal Life

Vegetation ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vegetation)) follows climate and altitude. The Tarai and Churia have tropical deciduous forests with khair, sissoo, and sal. Higher regions have changing forest and alpine zones. Tarai forests support tigers, leopards, gaurs, elephants, buffalo, deer, and rhinoceroses, especially in the Lesser Rapti Valley. Central zones have fewer wild animals because of forest clearing, while alpine areas contain musk deer, tahr, goral, wild sheep, wolves, snow leopards, pheasants, and legendary Yeti traditions.

## The People

Nepal's population reflects migrations from Tibet and northern India. Indo-Aryan groups include Tarai peoples, Pahari, Newar, and Tharu and form the majority, while Tibeto-Nepalese groups include Tamang, Rai, Limbu, Bhutia, Sherpa, Sunwar, Magar, and Gurung. Nepali, or Gorkhali, is the official language and belongs to the Indo-Aryan branch, while many northern and eastern languages belong to the Tibeto-Burman family. Most Nepalese are Hindu, with Buddhist and other minorities.

Most Nepalese live in villages or small market centres. Outside Kathmandu, urbanization is limited, with smaller centres such as Biratnagar, Nepalganj, Birganj, and Pokhara. Indian and Tibetan cultural influences shape religion, language, settlement, and regional identity.

## Resources

Nepal's mineral resources are limited, scattered, and little developed, including lignite, iron ore, magnesite, copper, cobalt, pyrite, limestone, and mica. Its greatest natural economic potential lies in hydroelectric power from major river systems. If developed domestically and exported to India, hydropower could become a mainstay of the economy.

## Agriculture

Agriculture ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agriculture)) employs most of Nepal's population and provides much export income, but productivity is low because of limited irrigation, inadequate fertilizer and seed, and inefficient techniques. Rice, maize, wheat, potatoes, sugarcane, millet, cattle, buffalo, goats, and sheep are important. The Tarai has the greatest potential for increased production, while the mid-mountain region is nearly fully cultivated and vulnerable to erosion, deforestation, and landslides.

## Forestry

About one-third of Nepal is forested, mostly state-owned. Timber is one of the country's most valuable resources and an important source of Indian rupees through exports. Overcutting and poor management have damaged forests, while the Timber Corporation of Nepal supplies Kathmandu Valley with construction and furniture wood.

## Industry and Trade

Industry is small but growing, usually based on agricultural processing. Jute, sugar, sawmills, meat processing, rice and oil mills, brick and tile, construction materials, paper, food grains, cigarettes, cement, and beer are important. Manufacturing concentrates around Biratnagar, the Birganj-Hitaura corridor, and Kathmandu Valley. Tourism is small but expanding, centred on Kathmandu, Pokhara, Everest, and wildlife areas. Nearly all trade is with India because of geography and history.

## Transportation

Nepal has limited [[Transportation|transportation]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transport)) because mountains make road construction difficult. Footpaths have long been the main transport network, following valleys and river systems. Since the 1970s, road construction expanded with foreign aid. Narrow-gauge railways to India declined in importance as roads grew. Air service connects Kathmandu with India and other nearby countries, and domestic air routes expanded. The Kathmandu-Hitaura aerial ropeway, built by the United States in the 1950s, still carries goods.

## Government

Nepal's [[The Forms Of Government Their Historical Development (Government)|government]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Government)) moved unevenly from monarchy toward democracy. Reforms in the 1950s were reversed when the crown dissolved Parliament in 1960, banned parties, and ruled through panchayats under the 1962 constitution. Unrest forced King Birendra to accept multiparty parliamentary government in 1990. The new constitution reduced royal power and gave executive authority to a prime minister and Council of Ministers responsible to Parliament.

## History

![[attachments/nepal_001.webp|420]]

*Basantapur Palace in Kathmandu Durbar Square*

Nepal's early history combines Newar legends, Buddhist and Hindu traditions, Indian references, and archaeology. Lumbini, birthplace of Gautama Buddha, and Kathmandu Valley appear in Buddhist accounts. An Ashoka inscription at Lumbini and valley shrines show early Buddhist influence. A clearer dynastic history begins with the Licchavi dynasty in the 4th or 5th century CE, when rulers of plains Indian origin governed a mixed population.

The Licchavi period opened Himalayan trade and political links with Tibet and transformed Kathmandu Valley from a remote area into a regional cultural centre. Later history included Gorkha unification, Shah monarchy, Rana rule, isolation, constitutional experiments, and democratic movements.

## Recent Political Life

Since 1990, Nepal has experienced instability, party divisions, royal crisis, and insurgency. The royal massacre of 2001 killed the king and most of the royal family. Maoist rebels grew through the 1990s and early 2000s, creating civil war conditions. In 2006, the government and Maoists signed a UN-mediated peace accord, placing Maoists in temporary government, restricting rebel forces to camps, and requiring monitored arms storage. The monarchy was later abolished and a republic declared.

- 2008: Nepal became a democratic republic.
