# Museums (Museums, History of)

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> Museums developed from practices of collecting, ordering, preserving, and displaying material evidence of nature and human activity. Their history moves from ancient, medieval, and Renaissance collections through Enlightenment public institutions to national and global museums serving research, education, memory, and access.

## Introduction

![[attachments/museums_history_001.webp|420]]

*Ashmolean Museum*

The history of museums ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Museum)) is the history of institutions that preserve and interpret material evidence of humanity, human activity, and the natural world. Museums grew from an ancient and probably innate human desire to collect, order, explain, and display meaningful objects. Before modern public museums, rulers, churches, scholars, merchants, and private collectors built collections for prestige, devotion, learning, wealth, curiosity, and identity.

Modern public museums developed gradually from private and princely collections into institutions that serve education, research, national memory, civic pride, science, art, and public access. The historical sequence runs from the term museum's classical origins through ancient and medieval collecting, Renaissance Italy, royal Europe, Enlightenment public institutions, national museums, and global museum growth through the 20th century.

## Etymology

The word museum comes from the Greek mouseion, meaning seat of the Muses, a place of contemplation or philosophical activity. In Roman use, museum mainly meant a place for philosophical discussion. The Museum at Alexandria, founded by Ptolemy I Soter early in the 3rd century BC, was closer to a university with scholars and a library than to a modern collecting institution.

The word was revived in 15th-century Europe for the collection of Lorenzo de' Medici in Florence, where it suggested comprehensiveness. By the 17th century, museum described curiosity collections such as Ole Worm's collection in Copenhagen and John Tradescant's collection in Lambeth. When Elias Ashmole transferred the Tradescant material to Oxford, the public Ashmolean Museum opened in 1683, helping establish the modern sense of a public collection.

## Medieval Europe

![[attachments/museums_history_002.webp|420]]

*British Museum Reading Room*

In medieval Europe, collecting belonged mainly to princely houses and the church. Treasures such as those of [[Charlemagne]] had economic value and could finance war or state expenses. Churches preserved relics, and relic trade linked collecting to devotion and pilgrimage. Mediterranean ports in Lombardy and Tuscany connected Europe to antiquities and exotic material from the wider world.

Antiquities moved across Europe before the Renaissance. Henry of Blois, bishop of Winchester, reportedly bought ancient statues in Rome in 1151 and sent them to England. Venetian participation in the Fourth Crusade moved the bronze horses from Constantinople to San Marco in Venice, showing how conquest, trade, devotion, and collecting intertwined.

## Renaissance Italy

![[attachments/museums_history_003.webp|420]]

*Louvre museum gallery*

Renaissance Italy ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Italian_Renaissance)) produced the first great modern-style collections through renewed interest in classical antiquity and through merchant and banking wealth. Cosimo de' Medici formed an important collection in 15th-century Florence, and his descendants enlarged it until it was bequeathed to the state in 1743 for the people of Tuscany and all nations. The Uffizi Palace opened its upper floor to visitors in 1582, making display and public viewing part of collecting culture.

Royal and princely collections also developed across Europe. Matthias I of Hungary, Maximilian I of Austria, Augustus of Saxony, Ferdinand of Tirol, Rudolf II, Albert V of Bavaria, and Sigismund II Augustus of Poland kept collections of paintings, antiquities, scientific material, ethnographic objects, and luxury art. These collections encouraged arts, science, alchemy, astrology, and princely identity.

## The Ashmolean

The Ashmolean Museum ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ashmolean_Museum)) was the first case in which a corporate body accepted a private collection, built a structure for it, and opened it to the public. Elias Ashmole gave the Tradescant collection to the University of Oxford on condition that it be housed properly. The building opened in 1683, establishing a key model for university-based public museums.

## The British Museum

The British Museum ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_Museum)) opened in London in 1759, shaped by Enlightenment encyclopaedism, exploration, trade, and taste for the exotic. It preserved and displayed the collections of Sir Robert Cotton, Robert Harley, and Sir Hans Sloane. Cotton and Harley contributed manuscripts, while Sloane's material included natural history, Jamaican specimens, classical, ethnographic, numismatic, and art objects, plus William Courten's cabinet.

Public access at the British Museum ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_Museum)) was free from the beginning, though admission required limited daily tickets for many years. Its stated purpose was public instruction and benefit, not merely elite enjoyment. This made it a model for national museum responsibility: government accepted preservation duties and treated collections as resources for the learned, curious, and general public.

## The Louvre

The Louvre ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Louvre)) became a public museum through pressure to make French royal collections accessible. Louis XV exhibited paintings at the Luxembourg Palace in 1750, and Denis Diderot proposed a national museum in 1765. The Revolutionary government opened the Grande Galerie in 1793 as the Central Museum of the Arts, later transformed under Napoleon into the Musee Napoleon.

Napoleonic art appropriation moved many royal and noble collections to Paris, and the Congress of Vienna in 1815 required much looted material to be returned. Even so, the episode stimulated public interest in art and encouraged other collections to open. In Rome, the Capitoline Museum, Vatican collections, and Pio-Clementino Museum helped spread the Neoclassical museum model.

## Central Europe

In 19th-century central Europe, museums supported national consciousness. Hungary founded a national museum at Pest from Count Ferenc Szechenyi's gift, Prague opened noble natural-history collections in 1823, and museums appeared in Brno, Zagreb, Ljubljana, Graz, Innsbruck, Salzburg, Nurnberg, Poland, Wilanow, and Warsaw. Archaeological museums opened near the Black Sea, while Christian Jurgensen Thomsen developed the Stone, Bronze, and Iron Age system in Copenhagen.

## The United States

The Smithsonian Institution began from James Smithson's bequest for the increase and diffusion of knowledge. In 1846, the U.S. Congress created the institution, and the U.S. National Museum opened in 1858. American museum development also included historic house museums such as Hasbrouck House, purchased by New York in 1850, and large trustee-supported institutions such as the American Museum of Natural History and the Metropolitan Museum of Art.

## Europe

During the second half of the 19th century, European museums multiplied through civic pride and the free education movement. Britain opened about 100 museums in the 15 years before 1887, and Germany opened 50 in the five years from 1876 to 1880. Museums experimented with school specimen circulation, panoramas, habitat groups, gas lighting, and electric lighting to improve education and evening access.

## South America

South American museums expanded in capitals and provinces. Universities founded institutions such as the Geological Museum in Lima in 1891 and the Geographical and Geological Museum at Sao Paulo in 1895. Provincial museums appeared in Argentina, Brazil, Chile, and Ecuador, while specialist national, maritime, and memorial museums developed around public memory and regional identity.

## Asia

In [[Asia]], older institutions such as the Indian Museum in Calcutta and the Central Museum of Indonesian Culture in Jakarta were joined by new museums. Japan opened an industrial and natural-resource museum in 1872, later foundational for Tokyo's national museums. China founded the Nan-t'ung Museum in 1905, followed by institutions in Beijing and Tianjin. Museums also developed in Bangkok, Sri Lanka, Sarawak, and Peshawar.

## Africa

African museums developed in several regions by the early 20th century. Zimbabwe's national museums began in 1901, the Uganda Museum in 1908, and the National Museum of Kenya in 1909. Egypt's museum moved to a new Cairo building in 1902, and Islamic and Coptic museums followed. South African museums appeared in Grahamstown, Port Elizabeth, Bloemfontein, Durban, Pretoria, and Pietermaritzburg.

The South African sequence from Bloemfontein in 1877 through Durban, Pretoria, and Pietermaritzburg illustrates regional museum formation outside Europe and North America. These institutions fit a wider global pattern: local societies, colonial administrations, natural history interests, civic pride, and public education all helped build museums that preserved regional collections and shaped local identities.

## The 20th Century: Museums and Social Change

The 20th century forced museums to reassess their roles under the pressure of world wars, revolution, recession, public education, and political change. In Russia after the 1917 Revolution, collections and museums came under state control. [[Lenin]]'s belief that culture belonged to the people encouraged preservation and public access, and the number of museums tripled in 20 years. Revolutionary, memorial, artistic, historical, and scientific museums all expanded.

## Key Events & Developments

- 3rd century BC: Ptolemy I Soter founded the Alexandrian Museum.
- 1683: The Ashmolean Museum ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ashmolean_Museum)) opened to the public at Oxford.
- 1759: The British Museum ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_Museum)) opened in London.
- 1793: The Louvre ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Louvre)) opened publicly under the Revolutionary government.
- 1846: The Smithsonian Institution was established.
- 1917: The Russian Revolution transformed museum policy in Russia.

## Interesting Facts

- Mouseion originally meant seat of the Muses, not a public display building.
- The Uffizi began as offices before becoming a public display space.
- The British Museum was free from the start, though tickets were limited.
- Gas and electric lighting let museums open in the evening for working visitors.
