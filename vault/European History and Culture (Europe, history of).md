# European History and Culture (Europe, history of)

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> European history concerns a changing geographic, cultural, and political idea rather than a continent with uncontested boundaries. It traces recurrent projects of unity, conflict, empire, religion, and institutions from Rome to postwar voluntary cooperation.

## Introduction

![[attachments/europe_002.webp|420]]

*Satellite view of Europe*

European history ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_Europe)) is not simply the history of a continent with fixed natural borders. Europe is treated as a geographic expression, a cultural inheritance, and a political idea whose limits have always been debated. Its western and southern edges are relatively plain, but its eastern boundary, relation to Russia, and inclusion of places such as the British Isles, Malta, Cyprus, and lands around the Urals show that Europe is partly a historical construction.

European unity ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_integration)) appears as a recurrent hope and danger. The Roman Empire, [[Charlemagne]]'s empire, the Holy Roman Empire, and the Roman Catholic church each offered partial models of unity. Later thinkers imagined federal schemes, while Napoleon Bonaparte and Adolf Hitler tried to impose unity by conquest. Only after [[World Wars (World War II)|World War II]] did European leaders pursue durable unity through voluntary institutions and equality among states.

Europe's record is deliberately ambivalent. The continent produced major achievements in law, science, technology, social reform, literature, art, philosophy, religion, political institutions, and economic organization, but it also generated war, colonial domination, nationalism, persecution, and genocide. European civilization is therefore creative and destructive, not a simple story of progress.

Postwar integration supplies the moral frame for the whole article. World War I killed more than eight million Europeans, and World War II killed more than eighteen million Europeans in battle, bombing, and genocide, not counting deaths outside Europe. The second catastrophe convinced many leaders that France and Germany had to be reconciled, protectionist nationalism had to be avoided, and Europe needed institutions capable of managing interdependence.

Jean Monnet, Robert Schuman, Konrad Adenauer, Alcide De Gasperi, and Paul-Henri Spaak appear as practical founders of postwar cooperation. Their borderland or multilingual backgrounds mattered: Lorraine, the Rhineland, northern Italy, and bilingual Belgium represented zones where European rivalry had been especially intense and where reconciliation had special symbolic force.

Postwar cooperation also depended on turning memory of conflict into shared institutions, so reconciliation became a practical program rather than only a moral hope.

## Migration and Boundaries

European migration is not only a prehistoric or modern subject; it is one of the repeated mechanisms of European history. Neolithic farmers, Indo-European-speaking groups, Greeks, Romans, Germanic peoples, Huns, Slavs, Vikings, Jews, Muslims, merchants, soldiers, colonists, refugees, guest workers, and postcolonial migrants all changed where people lived and how communities imagined belonging. These movements created languages, kingdoms, diasporas, frontiers, minorities, and cultural mixtures, while also provoking exclusion, assimilation, persecution, and border-making.

The changing meaning of European boundaries matters just as much. Ancient frontiers, medieval Christendom, early modern confessional borders, imperial overseas networks, nation-states, Cold War blocs, and the European Union all defined Europe differently. This means European history cannot be reduced to a fixed geography. It is a history of shifting lines between inside and outside, centre and periphery, civilization and barbarism, nation and empire, east and west, and Europe and the wider world.

## Prehistory and Early Societies

Prehistory begins with anatomically modern humans in Europe about 35,000 BCE. Because written records do not exist for this period, reconstruction depends on archaeology, scientific dating, stratigraphy, typology, settlement remains, burials, pollen evidence, deep-sea cores, ice cores, tree rings, pottery sequences, and stone-tool analysis. This evidence is rich but difficult to interpret when materials have been disturbed by climate, farming, erosion, and modern development.

Late Paleolithic Europe was shaped by severe Ice Age environments and by human adaptation to cold, mobile, resource-stressed landscapes. Cave art at Lascaux and Altamira, carved figurines, long-distance flint movement, fossil shells, marine mollusks carried inland, and shared artistic styles show that hunter-gatherer groups were not isolated. Their networks carried objects, mates, seasonal knowledge, ritual meanings, and survival information across wide regions.

Mesolithic Europe followed warming after about 13,000 BCE and the retreat of the Scandinavian Ice Sheet after about 8300 BCE. Human groups adapted to new forests, coastlines, rivers, wetlands, animals, fish, birds, and plant foods. This period is a real age of local innovation rather than a passive interval between Ice Age hunting and farming.

Neolithic farming began in Greece around 7000 BCE and spread gradually and unevenly through the Balkans, central Europe, western Europe, Britain, Ireland, and Scandinavia. The evidence weighs explanations based on migrating farmers from the Middle East against explanations based on adoption by indigenous Mesolithic communities. The process varied by region, ecology, contact, and population.

The spread of farming also created different regional tempos. Linear Pottery communities in central Europe, Balkan tells, western megalithic landscapes, Mediterranean cave and coastal settlements, and later northern farming zones did not all follow one path. Some groups adopted crops and animals while retaining hunting and fishing; others built longhouses, cemeteries, and field systems that imply inherited land rights. This makes the Neolithic a long transformation of diet, labour, settlement, kinship, and ritual rather than a single agricultural revolution.

Early agriculture involved crops such as emmer wheat, einkorn wheat, barley, peas, and flax, all connected with Middle Eastern domestication. Animal husbandry included cattle, sheep, goats, pigs, dogs, and later horses. The dog had already been domesticated in Mesolithic Europe; cattle may have been domesticated in southeastern Europe; and the horse appears first domesticated on the Western Steppe.

Neolithic villages reorganized social life around houses, fields, storage, animals, pottery, polished stone tools, burials, and inherited claims to land. Farming did not instantly erase hunting, gathering, or seasonal movement, but it created permanent communities and new forms of memory, property, cooperation, and conflict. Settlement itself became a social institution.

Late Neolithic intensification added dairying, ox traction, wool use, manure, plowing, wheeled vehicles, irrigation in dry southeastern Spain, textile production, vines, olives, and the farming of less fertile soils. These changes adapted agriculture to Europe's varied environments and helped create surplus, exchange, specialization, and prestige goods.

Late Neolithic social change was uneven. Southeastern Europe saw breaks in pottery, ritual, and settlement around 3200 BCE; Greece experienced population expansion and authority centers; and Iberian sites such as Los Millares and Vila Nova de Sao Pedro had fortified settlements and rich cemeteries. These examples resist a single Europe-wide developmental model.

Indo-European languages pose a difficult historical problem. By the end of prehistory most European languages, except Basque and Etruscan, belonged to the Indo-European family, which also extended toward India and Central Asia. Competing explanations include invasion, migration, steppe-origin, and Anatolian-farming models, but language, ethnicity, and archaeological culture cannot be mechanically equated.

## The Metal Ages

Metal Ages ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Three-age_system)) Europe includes Copper, Bronze, and Iron Age societies across the 3rd, 2nd, and 1st millennia BCE. Older theories explained change mainly by eastern influence, invasion, or diffusion, but radiocarbon dating showed that many European developments were local and independent. Contact with the eastern Mediterranean and the steppe mattered, yet Europe was not simply a passive recipient of external civilization.

Copper Age metallurgy began unevenly and often served prestige before production. Copper was used for small tools, ornaments, and grave goods rather than for a complete technological revolution. In southeastern Europe and the Carpathian Basin, copper cultures appeared from the 5th and 4th millennia BCE, but chronology and meaning varied widely.

Los Millares in southeastern Spain illustrates Copper Age complexity. Fortifications, bastions, towers, stone walls, rich burials, and specialized spaces suggest defense, hierarchy, organized labor, and status display. The site shows that early metallurgy could accompany social ranking without proving the existence of a uniform state system across Europe.

Bronze Age Europe drew on Bell Beaker, Corded Ware, Aegean, Carpathian, western, and northern traditions. Bronze appeared in weapons, ornaments, tools, hoards, and burials, while social ranking became more visible through grave goods, settlement hierarchy, and control of exchange. Regional diversity rather than a single Bronze Age culture.

Bronze Age exchange joined distant environments. Tin, copper, amber, gold, salt, horses, weapons, textiles, and prestige vessels moved through river valleys, mountain passes, seaways, and elite gift networks. Hoards can mark wealth, ritual deposition, recycling stores, or crisis, so they have to be interpreted carefully. The period's chiefs and warrior elites depended on control of people and routes as much as control of metal itself, and many communities remained agricultural even while long-distance exchange became more visible.

Bronze Age settlement could be relatively egalitarian or strongly stratified. Lake Neuchatel villages had regular rows of similar houses, fences, paths, fireplaces, and craft spaces, with few signs of central administration. Spissky Stvrtok, by contrast, had an acropolis, rampart, ditch, different building techniques, and hidden stores of gold and bronze, suggesting social differentiation and defense.

Aegean civilization separated itself from much of temperate Europe through administration, writing, palace organization, urbanism, trade, differentiated power, and close Mediterranean contact. This is a major exception within prehistoric Europe: civilization developed earlier and more visibly in the Aegean than in many northern and western regions.

Iron Age Europe began when iron's practical properties were fully exploited for tools and weapons. Iron was more abundant than bronze and eventually replaced many stone, wood, flint, and bronze implements. Agricultural productivity increased, trade routes changed, and Mediterranean contacts helped transform south-central and western Europe.

Protohistoric Europe at the end of the Iron Age had tribal names, regional polities, proto-urban settlements, warrior elites, ritual centers, and contact with Greek and Roman writers. This was the transition from archaeology alone to a history in which external written records begin to identify peoples, places, and political formations.

## Greeks, Romans, and Post-Roman Transformations

![[attachments/ancient_greek_civilization_001.webp|420]]

*Parthenon on the Athenian Acropolis*

![[attachments/ancient_rome_001.webp|420]]

*Roman Forum, civic center of ancient Rome*

Greek civilization is one of Europe's classical foundations. Greek-speaking peoples emerged after Indo-European movements and fusion with earlier Mediterranean populations. Mycenaean culture, Dorian movement, the Greek Dark Age, colonization after about 800 BCE, and settlements around the Mediterranean and Black Sea spread Hellenic language, myth, politics, art, philosophy, drama, and historical thought.

Greek influence mattered beyond conquest. Greek culture transmitted and transformed older Near Eastern and Mediterranean inheritances while developing civic identity, speculative philosophy, artistic models, literary genres, and political vocabulary. Later Europeans repeatedly returned to Greek examples when discussing beauty, reason, democracy, empire, tragedy, science, and education.

Roman Europe began in a mixed Italian setting shaped by Italic peoples, Etruscans, Illyrians, Greeks, Celts, and others. Rome created unity through conquest, Latin language, citizenship, law, military organization, roads, urban institutions, colonies, taxation, and administration. By the 2nd century CE, its empire covered much of Europe, northern Africa, and western Asia.

Romanization was cultural and institutional rather than racial. Roman law, municipal life, architecture, roads, aqueducts, baths, armies, Latin education, citizenship, and imperial ideology transformed local elites and towns. Even where native languages and customs survived, Roman forms shaped later European ideas about law, city life, authority, citizenship, and empire.

Roman Europe also created deep regional differences. Provinces near the Mediterranean became densely urbanized and legally Roman, while frontier zones along the Rhine, Danube, and northern Britain remained heavily military. Villas, taxes, coinage, amphitheatres, baths, temples, and veteran colonies tied local elites to imperial culture. Yet Rome's success depended on compromise: indigenous cults, languages, and aristocracies often survived by being translated into Roman forms rather than erased outright.

Christianity in the Roman world connected empire and medieval Europe. The church inherited Roman administrative geography, Latin and Greek learning, urban networks, legal concepts, and imperial patronage. After Constantine and later Christian emperors, Christianity became embedded in the social and political structures that survived the western empire's collapse.

Germanic peoples and Huns reshaped the map of Europe. Germanic groups developed near the eastern North Sea and southern Scandinavia, expanded between the Weser and Vistula, and moved south under pressure from population, climate, steppe peoples, and Roman opportunity. Their migrations were often partial movements involving warriors, families, clients, and federate troops.

Migration-period kingdoms did not simply replace Rome with barbarism. Goths, Vandals, Franks, Burgundians, Lombards, Anglo-Saxons, and others adopted Roman law, Christianity, Latin literacy, court forms, taxation, and titles in varying degrees. Clovis's Catholic conversion, Theodoric's Ravenna rule, Visigothic law, Burgundian law, and the Lex Salica show accommodation and fusion.

Post-Roman Europe combined Roman, Christian, and Germanic elements differently by region. Germanic influence was strongest in central Europe and parts of Britain, less in highly Romanized southern regions, and mixed in Gaul. Medieval Romance, Germanic, and Celtic linguistic patterns preserve different balances of settlement, acculturation, and survival.

## The Middle Ages

Middle Ages conventionally means the period from about 500 to 1400 or 1500, but the period's boundaries are debated. The term was created by later scholars to describe the time between classical antiquity and their own age. Modern historians reject the old image of an entirely dark, stagnant millennium.

Late antiquity and the early Middle Ages reorganized political and religious life. Roman imperial forms declined in the West, Germanic kingdoms emerged, Christianity spread, bishops became civic leaders, monasticism gained prestige, and rural lordship expanded. Continuity and change were intertwined: Roman, Germanic, and Christian traditions all remained active.

Carolingian Europe gave the early Middle Ages a major revival. Charlemagne's empire, court learning, script reform, monasteries, capitularies, schools, liturgical reform, and imperial coronation renewed ideas of Christian rulership. The Carolingian renaissance drew on Irish, Anglo-Saxon, Iberian, Italian, Frankish, and Roman sources of learning.

High medieval expansion brought population growth, agricultural improvement, towns, trade, Gothic architecture, universities, law schools, scholasticism, papal monarchy, canon law, crusading, and new religious movements. This period is creative and expansive, even though hierarchy, violence, famine, and persecution remained powerful.

Agricultural growth underlay much of this high medieval expansion. Heavy plows, three-field rotations, horse collars, mills, drainage, forest clearance, and village consolidation increased output in many regions. More food supported towns, craft specialization, schools, cathedral building, and royal taxation, but it also tied peasants more firmly to rents, tithes, labour services, and seigneurial courts. Medieval dynamism therefore came from the countryside as much as from castles, cathedrals, universities, and courts.

Late medieval crisis included war, famine, plague, social unrest, fiscal pressure, religious anxiety, and political fragmentation. Yet the evidence does not treat crisis as collapse. Many institutions survived, adapted, or became foundations for later states, universities, parliaments, towns, guilds, legal traditions, and cultural forms.

The Black Death intensified these contradictions. Population loss could strengthen surviving workers' bargaining power, weaken landlords in some regions, and encourage statutes trying to freeze wages and mobility. It also produced fear, penitential movements, scapegoating, anti-Jewish violence, and new devotional practices. Late medieval Europe was therefore both wounded and inventive: demographic shock, war finance, peasant revolts, and church schism coexisted with stronger monarchies, vernacular writing, and commercial recovery.

Medieval historiography helped shape European identity. Christian writers such as [[Augustine]], Jerome, and Paulus Orosius interpreted history through biblical time, salvation, and world empires. The idea of translatio imperii moved imperial authority from ancient empires to Rome, the Franks, and later German emperors.

Periodization itself is historical. Petrarch and later humanists helped describe a middle age between antiquity and revival. Protestant and Catholic scholars used medieval history in confessional argument. Nineteenth-century historians then built medieval studies through archives, document editions, national histories, university seminars, and institutions such as the Monumenta Germaniae Historica.

Christian chronology was part of medieval intellectual life. Bede, adapting Dionysius Exiguus, used anno Domini dating, helping create the Common Era chronology. This system gradually displaced or supplemented Olympiads, Roman founding years, consular years, imperial regnal years, and indiction cycles.

## Medieval Christianity, Judaism, Islam, and Society

Christianization took many forms: royal conversion, missionary preaching, monastic discipline, saintly example, baptism, burial practice, law, liturgy, and patronage. Figures such as Martin of Tours, Patrick, Columba, Columban, Augustine of Canterbury, Boniface, and Clovis show how conversion joined politics and belief.

Medieval church organization grew more defined after 1100. Clergy and laity were distinguished; minor and major orders structured clerical status; ordination involved sacramental authority and chastity; popes, bishops, monks, secular clergy, rulers, and cathedral chapters struggled over discipline, appointments, law, and jurisdiction.

Monasticism ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monasticism)) and reform gave medieval society models of sanctity and learning. Monasteries preserved books, trained clergy, cultivated land, provided prayer, and sometimes became centers of wealth and reform. Later orders and movements renewed older ideals by emphasizing poverty, preaching, education, or stricter discipline.

Christianity, Judaism, and Islam interacted through coexistence, scholarship, trade, conquest, polemic, and violence. Christian anti-Judaism grew after the 11th century: pogroms during the First Crusade, accusations of ritual murder and host desecration, restrictions, moneylending pressures, massacres, and expulsions.

Medieval expansion also meant contact with Byzantium, Islam, Scandinavians, Hungarians, and eastern frontiers. Viking and Magyar raids gave way to Christianized Scandinavian and Hungarian polities. Western Europe then expanded demographically, agriculturally, commercially, and territorially while maintaining exchanges with parallel civilizations.

Universities and scholasticism joined faith and reason. The recovery of Aristotle, canon law, Roman law, theology, disputation, and commentary created a shared intellectual culture. Thomas Aquinas became emblematic of the attempt to reconcile Christian doctrine with philosophical inquiry.

## Renaissance and Humanism

The Renaissance ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Renaissance)) must be handled cautiously. A simple picture in which modernity, individualism, secularism, and classical rebirth suddenly replace medieval darkness is misleading. It still uses the term for a multifaceted period between medieval universalism and the transformations of the 17th century.

Italian cities made the Renaissance distinctive. Urban wealth, banking, trade, guilds, landed property, civic patriotism, rivalry, contado relationships, claims to Roman origins, and resistance to bishops or feudal lords created intense political and cultural life. Merchants, rentiers, nobles, craftsmen, and professionals shared and contested urban power.

[[Humanism]] used classical literature, rhetoric, history, philology, moral philosophy, and civic education. It did not abolish religion; lay piety and lay-sponsored reform remained strong. Humanist learning created new ideals of eloquence, historical criticism, public service, and moral self-fashioning.

Humanist method mattered because it trained readers to compare manuscripts, restore difficult texts, detect anachronisms, and ask how language changed over time. This philological habit affected theology, law, politics, and historical writing. It encouraged admiration for antiquity, but also made antiquity recoverable as something historically distant. The same tools that praised classical eloquence could criticize forged documents, corrupt church practice, and inherited scholastic habits.

Machiavelli and Guicciardini illustrate Renaissance political realism. Their Florentine experience, service under republican and Medici regimes, awareness of Italian weakness, and study of ancient and contemporary politics produced histories and analyses that treated power, fortune, corruption, military force, and civic order with unusual directness.

Renaissance Europe also involved vernacular literature, visual art, architecture, patronage, printing, scientific curiosity, state formation, and commercial expansion. Renaissance culture is both a continuation of medieval innovations and a transition toward early modern society.

## Early Modern Europe, 1500-1648

Early modern Europe after 1500 experienced economic expansion, population growth, geographic discovery, global trade, commercial integration, new commodities, capital accumulation, and new forms of production. These developments helped create western capitalism, but the period was also marked by crisis, inequality, bankruptcy, poverty, and serfdom.

Demographic growth was uneven. Europe's population outside Russia and the Ottoman Empire rose from about 61.6 million in 1500 to 78 million in 1600, then fell to 74.6 million by 1650. Northwestern Europe grew strongly, while Germany, Italy, and Spain suffered heavily from war, disease, and economic contraction.

Urbanization advanced slowly but decisively in capitals and commercial centers. Naples, Rome, Madrid, Paris, Vienna, Moscow, Amsterdam, and London grew for different reasons: court, administration, banking, shipping, manufacturing, consumption, and political centralization. Many smaller towns stagnated or declined, especially where guilds, tolls, and local privilege restricted growth.

Protoindustrialization linked town and countryside through rural cottage industry. Merchants supplied wool or other raw materials and collected finished goods from peasants who spun, wove, washed, or processed materials outside agricultural seasons. This putting-out system supplemented rural income, enlarged markets, and prepared some areas for later industrialization.

Technology improved in shipbuilding, firearms, mining, metallurgy, glassmaking, silk production, clocks, instruments, and coal use. Newcastle coal supplied London and industries such as glassmaking, brick baking, and brewing. Pressure on forests and the advantages of coal foreshadowed later English industrial leadership.

State formation changed diplomacy and politics. Permanent embassies, professional armies, bureaucracies, taxation, princely courts, secular diplomacy, overseas empire, and balance-of-power calculation increasingly replaced medieval forms. Yet estates, nobles, churches, local law, provincial liberties, city privileges, and fiscal limits constrained rulers everywhere.

The fiscal side of state formation was decisive. Rulers needed credit, taxes, offices, monopolies, customs, and loans to pay for war and administration, but raising revenue forced negotiation with estates, financiers, towns, nobles, and provincial bodies. Stronger states were often those that could borrow credibly and tax predictably, not merely those with the grandest claims. This helps explain why the Dutch Republic and Britain could compete with larger monarchies.

Reformation shattered western Christendom. Religious doctrine, princely interest, social grievance, humanist criticism, printing, church reform, and political rivalry all contributed. Protestant and Catholic camps crossed older dynastic alliances, and religious minorities often sought foreign protection, making domestic confession and international diplomacy inseparable.

The printing press gave the Reformation unusual speed and reach. Pamphlets, vernacular Bibles, catechisms, polemical woodcuts, sermons, and song spread arguments beyond universities and church councils. Catholic reform also used print, schools, missions, confessional discipline, and new orders to renew religious life. The result was not simple secularization but a more intensely organized confessional Europe, where doctrine, education, marriage, poor relief, and political loyalty became linked.

Wars of Religion produced different outcomes. Germany used cuius regio, eius religio; France's Edict of Nantes in 1598 granted Huguenots limited toleration and safeguards; the Dutch Republic became an independent Protestant federation; the southern Netherlands remained Catholic and Spanish. These settlements reduced some conflicts while leaving others unresolved.

## Thirty Years' War and Westphalia

Thirty Years' War began from crises in Bohemia and the Rhineland inside the Holy Roman Empire. The empire contained roughly 1,000 semi-autonomous political units, from Habsburg lands and electorates to tiny lordships, knights, and free cities. The Reformation and Counter-Reformation split these units into hostile confessional camps.

Bohemian revolt became European war when Ferdinand II, Frederick V, the Catholic League, Spain, the Papacy, England, the Dutch Republic, and German Protestant and Catholic powers became entangled. The Battle of White Mountain in 1620 crushed Frederick, but Habsburg ambitions and Dutch fears prolonged conflict.

Danish intervention under Christian IV failed after defeat at Lutter in 1626. Ferdinand's Edict of Restitution in 1629 attempted to recover Catholic church lands and restrict Calvinism, alarming German Protestants and even some Catholic princes who feared imperial overreach.

Swedish intervention under Gustav II Adolf began in 1630. Sweden defended Protestant interests, sought Baltic security, and tried to prevent Habsburg domination of Germany. Gustav Adolf's victories changed the war's course, but his death did not end Swedish involvement or the broader struggle.

French intervention after Nordlingen turned the war into an anti-Habsburg contest. Richelieu and Louis XIII subsidized Sweden, allied with the Dutch, and declared war on Spain in 1635. Catholic France supported Protestant powers because state interest outweighed confessional solidarity.

Peace of Westphalia came from negotiations at Munster and Osnabruck between 1643 and 1648. It strengthened German territorial rulers, granted amnesty, created an eighth electorate, abandoned the Edict of Restitution, recognized Calvinism, and gave gains to France and Sweden. It also restricted imperial authority.

Balance of power is 's key interpretation of Westphalia. The settlement did not end war or create modern sovereignty in a simple sense, but it resolved crucial German religious and constitutional issues and made the security of European states depend on equilibrium rather than universal monarchy.

## The Great Age of Monarchy

Great age of monarchy from 1648 to 1789 had more unity than its political fragmentation suggests. Diplomats, scholars, lawyers, scientists, religious writers, and rulers operated within shared European languages of Latin, French, Roman law, dynastic legitimacy, Christian inheritance, and balance-of-power diplomacy.

Hugo Grotius's De Jure Belli et Pacis in 1625 helped articulate law among states. European rulers continued to fight, but they increasingly justified and negotiated war through treaties, congresses, diplomacy, dynastic rights, succession claims, commercial interests, and legal arguments.

[[Population]] remained central in a preindustrial world because labor, demand, production, military power, and taxation all depended on people. The 1590s brought dearth and disorder; Spain lost more than a million people partly through plague; and later Europe continued to experience demographic vulnerability.

Little Ice Age conditions worsened agricultural risk. Fewer sunspots after 1645, rare auroras, thin tree rings, lower snow lines, advancing Alpine glaciers, cold winters, wet summers, droughts, frosts, and the winter of 1708-1709 threatened harvests and survival, especially in marginal regions.

[[War]] from 1618 to 1721 burdened states and societies. Large armies lived off the land, spread disease, damaged markets, increased taxation, and diverted resources. The Thirty Years' War devastated parts of Germany, while the War of the Spanish Succession, Great Northern War, and Ottoman-Habsburg conflicts extended strain.

Health reflected poverty, poor housing, contaminated water, open drains, animals under the same roof, lice, fleas, smoke, poor diet, dangerous childbirth, infant mortality, typhus, typhoid, measles, and limited medical knowledge. Many Europeans lived close to subsistence, where illness or crop failure could destroy a household.

Poverty was not marginal. If people lacking enough land or wages to replace tools and stock are counted as poor, at least one-quarter of Europeans fell below the poverty line. Crop failure, injury, disease, unemployment, death of livestock, fire, flood, taxes, rents, obsolete trades, and serfdom could all push families into destitution.

Urban growth favored capitals, ports, and administrative centers. London, Paris, Naples, Seville, Venice, Rome, Constantinople, Warsaw, St. Petersburg, Berlin, Vienna, and Turin grew through courts, trade, banking, manufacturing, government, military expenditure, refugees, and services. Yet many towns remained small and economically fragile.

Peasant society varied widely. Sweden had free peasants represented in the Riksdag; Denmark intensified serfdom; eastern Europe preserved rigorous lordship; England's enclosure created dependent laborers; France retained seigneurial rights, banalites, dues, lods et ventes, champart, hunting privileges, and other burdens even where peasants owned or sold land.

Commerce and finance developed despite tolls, tariffs, internal customs, and privilege. Bills of exchange became negotiable, banking expanded, the Bank of Amsterdam stabilized transfers, and the Bank of England joined a wider financial revolution. Reformers such as Turgot and Joseph II attacked barriers to internal trade.

## Absolutism and State Forms

Absolutism never meant identical government everywhere. Venice remained republican; Britain developed parliamentary government; the Dutch Republic combined federal and provincial sovereignty; France, Prussia, Denmark, Sweden, Spain, Portugal, Austria, and Russia built monarchies with different social bases and limits. Ceremony and divine right often exceeded practical administrative capacity.

[[France]] illustrates the tension between absolutist claims and local complexity. Kings claimed divine authority while preserving customary rights. Richelieu, Mazarin, Colbert, intendants, and later ministers battled parlementary resistance, noble privilege, uneven law, provincial exemptions, fiscal shortages, and the contradiction between taxation and economic development.

Prussia built military-bureaucratic power through war damage, Junker cooperation, taxation, disciplined finance, conscription, centralization, and service nobility. The Great Elector, Frederick I, Frederick William I, and Frederick II strengthened an army-state whose ruler was expected to serve state power even while ruling autocratically.

[[Sweden]] moved from great-power expansion to constitutional adjustment. Gustav II Adolf and Axel Oxenstierna joined military strategy with administrative reform; Queen Christina abdicated amid crisis; Charles XI imposed absolutism; Charles XII's wars against Poland and Russia ended with disaster at Poltava and declining Swedish power.

[[Denmark]] became absolutist after war and territorial loss. Frederick III was declared supreme above human laws in 1661. Legal reform, Copenhagen's growth, mercantilism, peasant burdens, schooling, Schleswig-Holstein acquisition, and Johan Struensee's brief reform program under Christian VII show the range of Danish absolutism.

[[Spain]] and [[Portugal]] show Iberian decline and reform. Spain struggled with bullion dependence, Castilian taxation, bankruptcies, Catalan and Portuguese revolts, Bourbon centralization, and Charles III's reforms. Portugal regained independence in 1668, allied with England, and under Pombal rebuilt Lisbon after the 1755 earthquake while attacking Jesuit power.

Britain passed through civil war, regicide, Cromwell, Restoration, Exclusion Crisis, Glorious Revolution, Bill of Rights, party politics, parliamentary finance, public credit, and commercial expansion. Britain is a state where monarchy survived by accepting a stronger parliamentary and legal framework.

[[Russia]] built autocracy through serfdom, compulsory service, military reform, bureaucracy, western technology, territorial expansion, and the Table of Ranks. Alexis, Peter I, and Catherine II used reform and coercion to make Russia a great power while leaving deep social tensions intact.

## The Enlightenment

Enlightenment ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Age_of_Enlightenment)) was a movement, a state of mind, and a European conversation about reason, reform, criticism, improvement, nature, law, society, and progress. Whether it was chiefly French and elite or broadly European is debated, but it took many national forms while sharing confidence in the mind's power to understand and improve the world.

Scientific thought supplied two models. [[Descartes and Cartesianism (Descartes)|Descartes]] used doubt, mathematics, and mechanism to create a rationalist framework, while [[Francis bacon|Francis Bacon]] advocated organized experiment and inductive knowledge. Kepler, Newton, chemistry, natural history, and practical investigation encouraged reformers to believe that society, like nature, could be studied.

Encyclopedie embodied Enlightenment ambition. Published in 17 volumes between 1751 and 1765 under Denis Diderot and Jean le Rond d'Alembert, it aimed to describe sciences, arts, and crafts while spreading critical knowledge. Censorship and religious hostility made the project politically charged.

French philosophes criticized ignorance, censorship, privilege, church authority, legal cruelty, inefficient institutions, and social prejudice. Voltaire, Rousseau, Montesquieu, Diderot, Quesnay, Turgot, and others differed sharply, but together they helped create a public language of reform and critique.

The Enlightenment also depended on institutions of sociability. Salons, coffeehouses, academies, Masonic lodges, journals, lending libraries, correspondence networks, and scientific societies made criticism social and transnational. Censorship could suppress books, but it also created clandestine trade, coded language, and a public appetite for forbidden works. Reform therefore moved through conversation, reputation, and print as much as through ministries or royal decrees.

German Aufklarung was more linked to universities, Lutheranism, Pietism, cameralism, administration, and service to rulers. Halle, Gottingen, and Vienna trained officials; Leibniz, Christian Wolff, Joseph von Sonnenfels, [[Frederick the Great]], and Immanuel Kant show a reformist but often authority-conscious Enlightenment.

Italian and Austrian reform was often practical, legal, anticlerical, patriotic, and utilitarian. Cesare Beccaria's Of Crimes and Punishments in 1764 attacked torture and irrational punishment and influenced Catherine II and Jeremy Bentham. Tuscany, Naples, Lombardy, and Habsburg lands became laboratories of enlightened policy.

Enlightened absolutism tried to use state power for rational reform. It could reduce church privilege, improve administration, codify laws, support education, and modernize taxation, but it also depended on rulers and bureaucracies that often feared popular participation. This made Enlightenment reform powerful yet politically fragile.

## Revolution and Industrial Society

![[attachments/industrialization_001.webp|420]]

*Boott Cotton Mills, representing factory industrialization*

Modern Europe from 1789 to 1914 is framed by the French Revolution and World War I. The period was united by broad cultural trends, economic transformation, science, diplomacy, and political ideologies, but it remained divided by state borders, nationalism, class conflict, uneven industrialization, and regional difference.

Industrial Revolution ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Industrial_Revolution)) began beneath the drama of revolution and war. Population growth, fewer epidemics, the potato, agricultural improvement, commercialization, capital, overseas markets, merchants, rural manufacturing, coal, iron, textiles, and new machines transformed production. Britain led first because it combined resources, markets, capital, institutions, and technology.

Britain had 620,000 horsepower in steam engines by 1840, compared with 860,000 for Europe as a whole. Belgium, northern France, the Ruhr, Germany, and other western areas followed, while southern and eastern Europe industrialized more slowly. Industrialization therefore widened regional inequalities even as it spread new methods.

Agricultural change supported industrial society through seed drills, crop rotation, nitrogen-fixing crops, better livestock, chemical fertilizers, specialization, and market supply. Old sectors did not disappear: craft work, rural production, small workshops, food processing, construction, and household economies survived alongside factories and mines.

Social consequences included urbanization, wage labor, owners and nonowners, changing family life, child labor, poor working conditions, factory discipline, worker protest, union formation, police control, consumer goods, and reduced older leisure customs. Industrialization made daily life more market-dependent and time-disciplined.

Industrialization also changed knowledge and infrastructure. Engineers, chemists, accountants, surveyors, managers, railway planners, and technical schools became central to economic power. Railways compressed distance, standardized time, moved food and coal, widened markets, and made military mobilization faster. Telegraphy made prices, news, and diplomatic messages travel quickly. These changes helped governments know and manage societies in ways that earlier states could not.

French Revolution grew from Enlightenment ideals, fiscal crisis, aristocratic resistance, bad harvests, middle-class demands, peasant grievances, and the calling of the Estates-General. It produced legal equality, representative claims, church land seizure, guild abolition, civil liberties, a constitutional monarchy, radicalization, war, Jacobin rule, and ultimately Napoleon.

Napoleon Bonaparte centralized administration, created legal codes, preserved male legal equality, confirmed church land sales, promoted education and bureaucracy, and spread reforms across conquered Europe. His empire also provoked nationalism, military reform, anti-French resistance, and state modernization in Prussia, Russia, Spain, Poland, and Britain.

Napoleonic rule exported both institutions and resentment. Civil codes, prefects, secular administration, Jewish emancipation in some regions, metric measures, abolished feudal dues, and reorganized courts made revolutionary change durable. At the same time, conscription, taxation, censorship, dynastic manipulation, and military occupation made many Europeans experience reform as domination. This double legacy explains why later nationalists could borrow Napoleonic state tools while defining themselves against French empire.

Congress of Vienna in 1814-1815 tried to restore stability without restoring the entire pre-revolutionary world. It restrained France, balanced great powers, confirmed German and Italian regional states, enlarged Prussia in western Germany, and gave Russia most of Poland. The postwar order then faced liberal, nationalist, conservative, and social pressures.

## Romanticism, Realism, and Nineteenth-Century Culture

Romanticism ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Romanticism)) grew from the revolutionary age. The year 1789 crystallized hopes and fears about individual rights, popular sovereignty, nationhood, and universal principles. Goethe, Burke, Blake, Kant, Goya, Beethoven, Holderlin, Schlegel, Wordsworth, Coleridge, Schiller, and Schleiermacher signaled new artistic, moral, and philosophical energies.

Cultural nationalism challenged the older idea of a cosmopolitan elite culture centered on French manners and classical taste. Each people was imagined as having its own language, history, poetry, spirit, and destiny. This idea drew strength from popular sovereignty and the belief that peoples, not dynasties, carried legitimacy.

Romantic art explored medieval worlds, exotic places, inner life, nature, the sublime, history, memory, national folklore, and emotional intensity. Byron, Chateaubriand, Goethe, Scott, Lamartine, Vigny, Musset, Hugo, Mickiewicz, Delacroix, Berlioz, Beethoven, Weber, Schumann, Schubert, Pushkin, Manzoni, and Leopardi gave Romanticism many national voices.

Music and painting expanded expressive range. Beethoven, Berlioz, Weber, Schubert, and Schumann linked sound to drama, poetry, landscape, and inwardness. Goya, Constable, Gericault, Delacroix, and Turner transformed visual realism, historical emotion, atmosphere, violence, and subjective vision.

Science and philosophy also changed in the 19th century. Conservation of energy, the mechanical equivalent of heat, cell theory, Dalton's atom, and later genetics reshaped knowledge. Kant answered [[Hume]], while Fichte, Hegel, Schopenhauer, Coleridge, and Victor Cousin developed post-Kantian thought.

Realism and Realpolitik followed disillusion after the revolutions of 1848. Artists and politicians looked more closely at the possible, visible, sordid, commonplace, and institutional. Flaubert, Dickens, Baudelaire, Wagner, and others inherited Romantic intensity while turning toward critique, social detail, irony, and moral ambiguity.

Victorian morality was a European ethos as well as a British one. It included earnestness, family order, religious revival, respectability, abolitionism, sexual restraint, antiaristocratic seriousness, bill paying, regular churchgoing, policing, and anxiety over salvation. Its critics attacked hypocrisy, conformity, materialism, and repression.

## A Maturing Industrial Society, 1850-1914

Second industrial revolution transformed Europe after 1850. Germany's heavy industry began to surpass Britain's by the 1870s, while the United States became a major competitor. Steel, chemicals, electricity, internal combustion engines, automatic machinery, and electrical industries reshaped production and everyday life.

Corporate organization changed as expensive equipment and large output encouraged joint-stock companies, big banks, cartels, state support, and economies of scale. Germany became especially associated with cartels and heavy industry. Older industrial centers continued to grow, but new sectors demanded more capital and technical training.

Labor and politics changed with industry. Semi-skilled operatives increased, work accelerated, wages and consumption rose in many areas, and workers organized through unions and socialist parties. Governments expanded education, health, labor regulation, tariffs, welfare, policing, and military mobilization.

Russian industrialization created revolutionary tension after 1900. St. Petersburg and Moscow workers became receptive to socialist doctrine, and Vladimir Ilich Lenin adapted Marxism to Russian autocracy through underground organization. Russia became a major producer but under severe social and political strain.

Imperialism and diplomacy after 1871 tied Europe to global competition and alliance systems. Postal cooperation, commercial law, humanitarian rules, the International Red Cross, and a World Court coexisted with naval races, nationalism, arms buildup, colonial rivalries, and tensions among Germany, Britain, France, Russia, Austria-Hungary, and Serbia.

Modern culture moved through Naturalism, Symbolism, Aestheticism, Post-Impressionism, Neoromanticism, and modernism. Baudelaire, Matthew Arnold, Henry Adams, Flaubert, Ruskin, Nietzsche, Wilde, Mallarme, Zola, Cezanne, van Gogh, Rodin, Puccini, Shaw, and Dreyfus-era debates reveal both confidence and crisis.

Cubist decade before 1914 produced anti-Romantic, anti-Victorian, constructivist, abstract, and experimental forms. Picasso, Stravinsky, Diaghilev, Wyndham Lewis, Ezra Pound, T.S. Eliot, Joyce, Lawrence, and Gide belonged to a generation determined to make art new before war transformed Europe.

## Europe Since 1914

![[attachments/world_wars_world_war_i_001.webp|420]]

*Battle of Verdun, 1916*

![[attachments/holocaust_001.webp|420]]

*Selection on the ramp at Auschwitz-Birkenau, 1944*

Europe since 1914 is marked by accelerated technological, social, cultural, political, and economic change. Electricity, telegraphy, telephones, radio, television, subatomic physics, oil, petrochemicals, plastics, jet engines, computers, telematics, bioengineering, longer education, women's opportunities, social mobility, cinema, mass leisure, and American influence transformed life.

Modern technology changed Europe's sense of time and possibility. The world of frock coats, top hats, early automobiles, and open-cockpit aircraft still looked close to the 19th century in 1914. After that, electrical power, broadcasting, motor transport, synthetic materials, and scientific industry made change feel rapid and often unsettling.

Mass society grew because more people stayed in school longer and more entered higher education. Women's access to opportunities once reserved for men widened, and class differences in clothing, manners, speech, and leisure became less sharp. Cinema, radio, and television spread shared models of behaviour, while older generations often saw these forms as cultural decline.

Americanization was one name for the new cultural sameness. the evidence does not treat it as simple imitation; it connects it with mass pleasure, consumer habits, media images, youth tastes, and the reduced distance between social classes. Europe remained more hierarchical than the United States, but its social surface became less visibly fixed.

[[World Wars (World War I)|World War I]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_War_I)) shattered the confidence of the prewar world. Trench warfare, the Somme, mass conscription, machine guns, artillery, poison gas, air raids, and disillusion overwhelmed assumptions about progress. More than eight million Europeans died, and many survivors returned physically or psychologically damaged.

The Great War also broke the cultural future imagined by prewar modernists. Writers such as Robert Graves, Siegfried Sassoon, Wilfred Owen, Henri Barbusse, and Erich Maria Remarque turned trench experience into literature of shock and disenchantment. The word glamour no longer fit war after the Somme, stalemate, wounds, gas, mud, and mass bereavement.

Total mobilization made the war a social crisis, not only a military one. Conscription drew millions into armies, while governments organized food, labour, transport, industry, censorship, and finance. Air raids and shortages reached civilians, and women entered new forms of work because the war economy needed labour outside older household patterns.

The war also altered legitimacy. Governments promised sacrifice would bring security, democracy, national self-determination, or social justice, but victory and defeat both produced disillusion. The Russian, German, Austro-Hungarian, and Ottoman empires collapsed; new borders created new minorities; veterans returned to unemployment or political violence; and debt, reparations, inflation, and grief made the peace settlement unstable from the beginning.

Postwar disillusion gave the years before 1914 a false golden glow. Europe had lost fewer than 4.5 million men in the whole century from the Napoleonic Wars to the Balkan Wars, but at least eight million died in four years. The scale of death made prewar confidence look naive.

Interwar Europe combined democratic hope, cultural experimentation, economic instability, revisionist nationalism, and authoritarian reaction. The League of Nations, Briand's federal plan, Stresemann's diplomacy, Locarno, the Wall Street crash, Weimar politics, paramilitary violence, and modernist culture all formed part of an unstable settlement.

European federal ideas were already active between the wars. Aristide Briand proposed a European federal union at the League of Nations, but many governments feared weakening the League, losing imperial responsibilities, or adding institutions during economic crisis. The plan was buried after debate in 1930, and Briand's death in 1932 ended that attempt.

Gustav Stresemann showed the better side of interwar diplomacy. Though he had been a nationalist during the war, he accepted that Germany had to respect the Versailles settlement while seeking peaceful revision. His work with Briand at Locarno in 1925 confirmed Germany's western borders and briefly suggested that reconciliation was possible.

Weimar Germany was born from defeat, revolution, mutiny, civil war, and social fear. It produced cosmopolitan culture, but it also faced political murder, extremist parties, inflation, reparations, unemployment, and weak loyalty from many elites. The depression after the Wall Street crash made these weaknesses much more dangerous.

Nazism rose from defeat, Versailles resentment, anti-Semitism, economic crisis, political violence, and the failures of Weimar legitimacy. Hitler's Munich coup in 1923, Mein Kampf, Nazi electoral gains by 1930, appointment as chancellor in 1933, emergency rule, and the Fuhrer state in 1934 led toward dictatorship and war.

Adolf Hitler used legality, propaganda, fear, and violence together. After the Reichstag fire in 1933, emergency decrees helped the Nazis destroy constitutional government. Rival centers of power were removed, and after Paul von Hindenburg died in 1934, Hitler joined the offices of chancellor and head of state under the title Fuhrer.

Nazi foreign policy won quick victories before war began. Germany reoccupied the Rhineland and allied with Benito Mussolini in 1936, joined Austria through Anschluss in 1938, took Czechoslovak territory in 1938-1939, and signed the German-Soviet Nonaggression Pact in 1939. These successes exposed the weakness of European appeasement.

The Phony Peace of the 1930s mixed ordinary prosperity with growing dread. Some middle-class Europeans still enjoyed servants, shops, holidays, and cultural life, but the Spanish Civil War, rearmament, dictatorships, and fear of another conflict made peace feel unreal. The decade is recovery shadowed by catastrophe.

[[World Wars (World War II)|World War II]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_War_II)) devastated Europe through invasion, occupation, bombing, deportation, genocide, forced labor, displacement, partisan war, siege, and ethnic expulsion. Germany, Italy, France, the Low Countries, the Balkans, Poland, the Baltic states, the Soviet Union, and many other regions suffered catastrophic destruction and civilian trauma.

The war radicalized choices inside occupied societies. Collaboration could mean ideological commitment, administrative survival, economic opportunism, fear, or anti-communism; resistance could mean intelligence work, sabotage, armed struggle, clandestine printing, rescue, or political preparation for liberation. Civil wars overlapped with foreign occupation in parts of eastern and southeastern Europe. Liberation therefore reopened questions of punishment, legitimacy, social reform, borders, and national memory.

Occupation Europe was not one experience. Some countries were conquered and administered directly; others had collaborationist governments, resistance networks, partisan war, civil conflict, or shifting fronts. Poland, the Balkans, the Baltic region, the Soviet Union, France, Italy, Germany, and the Low Countries all faced different mixtures of terror, accommodation, hunger, forced labour, and resistance.

The Holocaust stands at the moral center of the war's European destruction. The Nazi regime turned racial ideology, anti-Semitism, state bureaucracy, deportation, forced labour, camps, mass shootings, and industrial killing into genocide. the evidence connects genocide with the wider collapse of the old difference between civilian life and battlefield danger.

Displacement was enormous by the end of the war. At least 60 million European civilians were uprooted between 1939 and 1945; 27 million left their countries or were driven out; 4.5 million were deported by the Nazis for forced labour; and millions more moved through expulsions, transfers, and refugee flows after fighting ended.

Total war blurred the distinction between battlefield and home front. Air raids, rockets, blitzkrieg, commando raids, parachute drops, Resistance sabotage, guerrilla warfare, area bombing, and Nazi genocide made civilians direct targets. Between 1939 and 1945, at least 60 million European civilians were uprooted.

Europe's vulnerability was visible in ruined cities, destroyed bridges, bombed railways, blocked ports, burned countryside, and refugees moving through shattered borders. Berlin was described as a city of the dead. The war showed that frontiers and armies no longer protected civilians from modern violence.

Old great-power Europe was also weakened politically. After two wars, European states were no longer the main directors of world power. The global balance increasingly belonged to the United States and the Soviet Union, while Europeans became spectators or lesser actors in the nuclear rivalry that followed.

## Postwar Europe and Integration

Postwar institutions appeared with unusual speed: the United Nations, FAO, IMF, World Bank, UNICEF, International Court of Justice, GATT, International Refugee Organization, WHO, UNRWA, and ICFTU. They did not solve every problem, but they showed a serious effort to move beyond bilateral diplomacy and unmanaged rivalry.

Planning the peace was global as well as European. Statesmen tried to replace older national bargaining with permanent organizations for security, food, finance, refugees, health, law, trade, and labour. These institutions had limits, especially the United Nations, but their creation showed how deeply war had discredited unmanaged rivalry.

Postwar politics mixed longing for normality with pressure for reform. Europeans wanted demobilization, repatriation, reopened cultural life, consumer goods, and legal order, but they also wanted social justice after a democratic war against dictatorship. Resistance movements, socialist parties, Christian Democracy, Labour politics, and new constitutions reshaped western Europe.

Normal life meant law, food, homes, trains, theatres, clothes, and family return. The reopening of La Scala under Arturo Toscanini and Christian Dior's New Look symbolized a desire to live again after occupation and scarcity. Yet this restoration existed beside demands for a more just society.

Resistance politics gave reform moral force. In France, the newspaper Combat used the phrase From Resistance to Revolution, and similar feelings appeared among socialists and the Italian Action Party. Christian Democratic parties in Italy, Germany, the Netherlands, and France also revived or emerged with a more left-of-center tone than many later versions.

Britain followed a different party pattern but shared the reform mood. The Labour Party drew less on continental socialist theory than on nonconformist religion and trade unionism, yet it belonged to the wider postwar turn toward welfare, public responsibility, and democratic reconstruction. Across western Europe, politics moved away from prewar elite rule.

Marshall Plan aid and the OEEC saved western Europe from economic ruin while encouraging cooperation. Sixteen countries joined the Committee of European Economic Co-operation in 1947; the OEEC began in 1948; and the European Recovery Program provided $13.15 billion in grants and credits. By 1951, industrial output was 43 percent above prewar levels.

Marshall aid was practical and symbolic. Ships carried wheat, spiced ham, powdered eggs, tractors, machine tools, and many other goods. Projects included land reclamation in Italy and the Netherlands and an Austrian dam using water from melting glaciers, showing that recovery meant rebuilding production as well as feeding people.

OEEC cooperation mattered because the United States pressed Europeans to plan together. The Organisation for European Economic Co-operation reduced some quantitative and monetary barriers that had restricted trade. It did not create full integration, but it trained governments to consult regularly about recovery, payments, trade, and production.

By 1951, Marshall Plan recovery had lifted western European industrial output to about 43 percent above prewar levels. In practical terms, integration advanced through wheat shipments, machinery imports, rail and port repair, and shared payments coordination rather than through immediate political federation.

Cold War ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cold_War)) division hardened through the Berlin blockade, NATO, German partition, and Soviet-American rivalry. On April 4, 1949, Belgium, Britain, Canada, Denmark, France, Iceland, Italy, Luxembourg, the Netherlands, Norway, Portugal, and the United States signed the North Atlantic Treaty. Western Europe tied itself to the United States while eastern Europe remained under Soviet domination.

Eastern Europe followed a different postwar path under Soviet power. Coalition governments narrowed into communist monopoly, police forces suppressed opponents, land and industry were nationalized, churches and independent associations were pressured, and economies were reorganized around planning and Soviet priorities. The pattern varied by country, but limited sovereignty became the defining fact. Crises in Berlin, Hungary, Czechoslovakia, Poland, and later across the bloc exposed the gap between socialist legitimacy and coercive control.

German division became the clearest map of the Cold War. The Berlin blockade pushed western states toward firmer alliance, and within weeks of NATO's creation Germany was formally divided into two rival republics. The same country that had twice threatened Europe now became the main frontier between two systems.

Western security rested on American power after 1949. NATO promised that an attack on one member would be treated as an attack on all. This guarantee reassured western Europe, but it also confirmed that Europe could no longer organize its own security without the United States.

Affluence transformed western Europe. West Germany's currency reform, Ludwig Erhard's market reforms, the Wirtschaftswunder, rebuilt cities, televisions, Volkswagens, and rising incomes became symbols of recovery. France modernized through technocrats, electrified railways, nuclear projects, and planning; Italy and other western countries also experienced rapid growth.

West German recovery was dramatic because reform turned worthless paper money into usable currency and released goods from controls. By 1950, West Germany's gross national product had reached the level of 1936. Between 1950 and 1955, national income rose by 12 percent a year, exports grew faster, unemployment fell, and new homes were built in huge numbers.

[[France]] modernized through planning, elite technical training, electrified railways, nuclear projects, and industrial renewal. This is a different path from German market reform: more state-guided, more technocratic, and still aimed at making an old power competitive in a changed world.

Affluence had an underside because prosperity did not remove all tension. Consumer goods, television, cars, better housing, holidays, and higher education raised expectations, but they also fed criticism of materialism, bureaucracy, American influence, family change, and the loss of older social certainties.

Welfare states and social democracy changed ordinary life. Public health, pensions, unemployment protection, housing policy, education, and labour rights gave many western Europeans greater security than before the war. Christian Democrats, socialists, liberals, and trade unions disagreed over methods, but all worked in a world where mass democracy expected social protection.

Youth culture became more visible as education lengthened and prosperity gave young people more spending power. Music, cinema, clothing, student life, and television weakened older codes of deference. the evidence links this social leveling with mass media and with the fact that class signs became less blatant.

Women's opportunities widened slowly and unevenly. that women gained access to more areas previously monopolized by men, especially as education expanded. This did not erase hierarchy, but it made postwar social change more than a story of factories, treaties, and parties.

Decolonization and shrinking power formed part of postwar Europe's background, even if only briefly. Older European states, once world powers, were now too small alone beside the United States and the Soviet Union. Loss of imperial confidence helped make cooperation in Europe seem practical rather than merely idealistic.

Eastern Europe changed in the 1980s through Mikhail S. Gorbachev's glasnost and perestroika, Lech Walesa's Solidarity, reform elections, collapsing communist regimes, and national movements. The Berlin Wall was breached on November 9-10, 1989, and German reunification followed on October 3, 1990.

Communist collapse did not begin as a single revolution. In Poland, Solidarity gave workers and intellectuals a public language of opposition. In the Soviet Union, Gorbachev's glasnost weakened secrecy, while perestroika tried to replace rigid control with efficiency. Once Moscow no longer guaranteed repression, eastern European regimes lost authority with remarkable speed.

The Berlin Wall had been built in 1961 to stop East Germans from fleeing west. Its breach on November 9-10, 1989, therefore became more than a German event. It symbolized the end of a divided Berlin, a divided Germany, and a divided Europe.

European federalism drew on older ideas from Coudenhove-Kalergi, Aristide Briand, British Federal Union thinkers, Altiero Spinelli, Resistance networks, and postwar activists. The 1948 Congress of Europe at The Hague called for political and economic union, a European Assembly, and a European Court of Human Rights.

Federalist thought moved through many channels before institutions appeared. Coudenhove-Kalergi's Pan-European Movement, Briand's interwar plan, British Federal Union writing, Chatham House discussions, and Resistance circles all kept the idea alive. Altiero Spinelli, freed from Fascist imprisonment in 1943, carried federal ideas into Resistance planning.

Winston Churchill spoke of a kind of United States of Europe, and the Hague Congress gave the idea public force. The meeting included statesmen and writers such as Spaak, De Gasperi, Churchill, Schuman, Adenauer, Camus, Aron, Orwell, de Rougemont, and Silone. Its demands linked peace, rights, assembly government, and economic union.

Council of Europe was founded in 1949 and produced the European Convention for the Protection of Human Rights in 1950. But its consultative assembly and ministerial veto frustrated federalists. Monnet's opportunity came from disputes over German heavy industry and the need to reconcile German recovery with French security.

[[Human Rights|Human rights]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Human_rights)) were one early achievement of European cooperation. The Council of Europe did not satisfy federalists because it had little power, but the convention, court, and commission gave legal form to the postwar claim that states should be judged by common standards. This mattered after dictatorship, occupation, and genocide.

Jean Monnet chose a concrete economic route because direct political union was too difficult. German coal and steel were needed for recovery, but French leaders feared renewed German power. Pooling heavy industry made German recovery useful while placing it inside a shared authority.

European Communities grew from the European Coal and Steel Community to the European Economic Community and Euratom. The institutions merged in 1967, with a Council of Ministers, Commission, European Parliament, and Court of Justice. De Gaulle's veto of British entry and the 1965 voting crisis tested but did not stop integration.

Community institutions gave integration a partly federal shape. The Council of Ministers made key decisions, the Commission proposed policy, the European Parliament added legislative control, and the Court of Justice gave legal authority. The system was limited to economic and social affairs, but it could grow through crises.

Charles de Gaulle forced two major tests of the European Communities. He vetoed British entry in 1963, fearing British ties to the United States and a different European vision. In 1965, he also resisted wider majority voting, showing that national sovereignty remained powerful inside the integration project.

European Union ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_Union)) deepened and enlarged. Britain, Ireland, and Denmark joined in 1973, Greece in 1981, Spain and Portugal in 1986, and later Austria, Finland, Sweden, and many former communist countries. The Single European Act in 1987 pushed the common market; Maastricht in 1992 created the EU, EU citizenship, common policy areas, and the path to the euro.

Southern enlargement linked integration with democracy. Greece, Spain, and Portugal joined after periods of dictatorship, so membership became a sign of democratic normalization as well as economic cooperation. The common market widened from the original six to a broader western and southern European system.

Maastricht turned the European Community into the European Union. The treaty signed on February 7, 1992, created a common foreign and security policy, cooperation in domestic affairs, and EU citizenship. Citizens could vote and stand in local and European Parliament elections where they lived, even outside their country of nationality.

Monetary union required convergence in government spending, inflation, public debt, and exchange-rate stability. Some states failed to qualify at first, and Britain, Denmark, and Sweden stayed outside. Eleven countries began the transition to the euro on January 1, 1999, giving up control over exchange rates.

Yugoslav conflict showed the limits of collective European foreign policy. As Yugoslavia broke apart from 1991, EU members tried to act together to bring peace, but the violence exposed weakness in common diplomacy. Later disputes over Kosovo, Serbia, Russia, NATO, and missile defense showed that post-Cold War Europe still faced hard security questions.

Eastern enlargement transformed the EU after communism. Cyprus, Estonia, Hungary, Latvia, Lithuania, Malta, Poland, Slovenia, the Czech Republic, Slovakia, and other states joined in the 2000s, followed by Bulgaria and Romania in 2007. Most of these additions had belonged to the communist bloc, which made enlargement a symbol of Europe's attempted reunification.

Lisbon Treaty marked later institutional reform. Eleven countries began the euro transition on January 1, 1999. A constitutional treaty failed in French and Dutch referendums in 2005, but the Lisbon Treaty entered into force on December 1, 2009, after a difficult ratification process.

EU legitimacy remained contested. Danish voters needed two referendums for Maastricht, French approval was narrow, and the constitutional treaty failed in France and the Netherlands in 2005. Ireland first rejected the Lisbon Treaty in 2008 and then approved it in a second referendum in 2009, showing both popular doubt and institutional persistence.

European integration had three linked purposes by the late 20th century. It aimed to reconcile former enemies and prevent war, avoid the economic nationalism that had deepened the Great Depression, and give small or medium-sized European states collective influence in a world dominated by larger powers.

## Expanded Source Coverage by Era

Prehistoric interpretation in the evidence repeatedly warns against treating archaeological cultures as fixed peoples. Pottery styles, burial forms, house plans, tool types, and metal objects may show contact, fashion, exchange, imitation, or status display rather than migration or ethnic replacement. This caution matters because older European history often tried to map later nations onto prehistoric cultures.

Neolithic and Metal Age change also shows how technical innovations altered social relations. Plows required animals, fields, and cooperation; wool production required sheep management, spinning, and weaving; wheeled vehicles changed movement and exchange; metalworking required ore, fuel, skill, and trade. These innovations were social systems, not isolated inventions.

Classical inheritance gave later Europe a double legacy. Greek civilization offered philosophy, drama, history, aesthetics, civic ideals, and models of inquiry, while Rome offered law, roads, cities, Latin, administration, imperial memory, citizenship, and military organization. Medieval and modern Europeans repeatedly argued about whether they were recovering, continuing, rejecting, or surpassing this classical world.

Christian Europe formed through gradual conversion rather than one event. Kings could order baptism, but missionaries, monks, bishops, law, burial custom, saints' cults, church calendars, liturgy, and local patronage made Christianity part of everyday life. This process also absorbed pre-Christian practices, altered kinship and marriage rules, and gave rulers new sacred legitimacy.

Medieval social order depended on land, lordship, church authority, custom, and personal obligations. Peasants, nobles, clergy, townspeople, monks, merchants, Jews, and rulers lived under overlapping jurisdictions. The same society could produce universities and persecution, cathedral building and famine, devotional reform and crusading violence.

Medieval learning was not isolated from society. Schools, scriptoria, cathedral chapters, monasteries, universities, and courts trained clerks who served church and state. The recovery of Aristotle and Roman law changed theology, politics, jurisprudence, and administration, while scholastic method made disputation and commentary central intellectual tools.

Renaissance urbanism linked culture to power. Patronage by merchant families, guilds, princes, popes, republics, and courts supported art, architecture, scholarship, and public display. The Renaissance city was not merely beautiful; it was a competitive arena where wealth, lineage, civic memory, and political authority were staged.

Early modern expansion connected European history to world history. Voyages, colonization, trade companies, silver flows, plantation economies, missionary activity, and maritime warfare enriched European states and merchants while also creating conquest, slavery, disease, extraction, and cultural disruption outside Europe. European change cannot be separated from this global setting.

Reformation-era politics turned conscience into a matter of state. A ruler's confession could determine worship, law, officeholding, education, foreign alliance, and military security. Religious refugees, minority communities, printing networks, and polemical literature made ideas mobile even when governments tried to control them.

Westphalian Europe preserved German fragmentation while restraining imperial universalism. France and Sweden gained influence, German princes gained security, Calvinists gained recognition, and the emperor lost direct control over many imperial affairs. The result was not peace forever but a diplomatic system in which coalition politics checked hegemonic ambition.

Seventeenth-century monarchy grew in the shadow of crisis. Climate stress, plague, harvest failure, tax resistance, noble revolt, urban unrest, religious division, and war forced rulers to bargain even when they spoke the language of divine right. Absolutism was therefore a project of administration, finance, military force, and persuasion, not a simple fact.

Peasant experience varied more than court-centered history suggests. Some peasants held land and communal rights; others faced serfdom, labor services, seigneurial courts, rents, tithes, taxes, enclosure, or forced military burdens. 's social sections make clear that European history includes household survival and rural labor as much as diplomacy and kings.

Enlightenment reform often moved through officials, academies, salons, books, universities, courts, and learned societies. Its language of reason could support religious toleration, penal reform, economic liberalization, administrative centralization, public education, and scientific investigation. It could also remain paternalistic, assuming that enlightened rulers or experts should reform society from above.

Revolutionary change after 1789 did not remain French. Legal equality, citizenship, constitutions, secular administration, conscription, national armies, civil codes, and nationalist resistance spread across Europe. Even opponents of the revolution had to answer its questions about legitimacy, representation, rights, and the nation.

Industrial society reorganized time and space. Railways, steamships, mines, factories, banks, newspapers, telegraphs, schools, and cities made markets faster and governments more intrusive. Work discipline, wage dependency, class organization, urban poverty, middle-class respectability, and mass consumption changed how Europeans understood social order.

Nineteenth-century nationalism was cultural before it was purely political. Language revival, folklore collection, national histories, music, monuments, schools, and literature created imagined communities. These movements could demand liberation from empire, unity of fragmented peoples, or superiority over neighbors, making nationalism both emancipatory and dangerous.

Modern ideologies developed in response to revolution and industry. Liberalism defended rights, property, constitutions, and markets; conservatism defended order, hierarchy, religion, and continuity; socialism criticized exploitation and private ownership; nationalism identified sovereignty with peoples; Christian social thought reacted to poverty and secularism; anarchism and revolutionary socialism attacked the state itself.

These ideologies became mass politics through newspapers, clubs, parties, unions, churches, schools, veterans' groups, and electoral reform. They were rarely pure. Liberals could support empire, nationalists could be democratic or authoritarian, conservatives could adopt social welfare, and socialists could choose parliament or revolution. Their flexibility made them powerful vocabularies for workers, middle classes, peasants, women activists, ethnic minorities, and states seeking loyalty.

Late nineteenth-century imperialism fed European rivalry while offering domestic prestige and economic opportunity. It intensified racial thinking, military planning, naval competition, missionary activity, commercial networks, and diplomatic crises. 's account of prewar diplomacy shows that international cooperation and militarized competition grew at the same time.

Prewar culture already sensed rupture. Symbolism, Aestheticism, Decadence, Post-Impressionism, early abstraction, psychoanalytic interests, Nietzschean critique, new music, and experimental fiction challenged Victorian confidence. The catastrophe of 1914 did not create modernist anxiety from nothing; it confirmed and deepened tensions already visible.

[[World Wars (World War I)|World War I]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_War_I)) transformed states and societies through mass mobilization. Governments controlled labor, food, transport, censorship, finance, and industry; women entered new work roles; soldiers experienced mechanized slaughter; and civilians endured shortages and grief. The war destroyed empires and left unresolved border, minority, debt, and security problems.

Interwar instability came from the interaction of democracy, social fear, revisionist nationalism, reparations, inflation, depression, paramilitary politics, and ideological extremism. Some states preserved parliamentary government, but others moved toward dictatorship because liberal institutions could not manage economic crisis, social polarization, and fear of revolution.

Fascism and Nazism promised order, national rebirth, anti-communism, leadership, and violent unity. Nazi rule added biological racism, anti-Semitic conspiracy, terror, propaganda, expansionism, and genocide. 's account of Hitler's rise makes dictatorship appear as both a political failure and a social mobilization.

World War II aftermath left Europe physically destroyed and morally shocked. Bombed cities, displaced persons, refugees, prisoners, deportees, destroyed railways, broken ports, food shortages, currency collapse, ethnic expulsions, and the revelation of genocide made reconstruction a social and ethical task as well as an economic one.

Cold War Europe divided recovery into western and eastern paths. In the west, Marshall aid, NATO, Christian Democracy, social democracy, welfare states, consumer growth, and European integration created new stability. In the east, Soviet power, communist parties, planned economies, secret police, and limited sovereignty shaped political life until the crises of the 1980s.

Western affluence changed cultural expectations. Housing, cars, television, tourism, higher education, social mobility, youth culture, and welfare provision widened access to comfort. Yet prosperity also brought debates over Americanization, technocracy, consumerism, family change, immigration, decolonization, and the limits of national sovereignty.

Affluence also altered political expectations. Citizens increasingly judged governments by growth, employment, housing, education, health care, pensions, transport, and consumer stability. This made welfare states and managed economies part of postwar legitimacy, even for parties that disagreed about nationalization, markets, taxation, or planning. Prosperity was therefore cultural, social, and administrative at once.

Post-1914 social change was not only a matter of wealth. the evidence links mass education, higher education, women's widened opportunities, cinema, radio, television, and shared leisure to a slow leveling of class signs. Europeans still lived with hierarchy, but speech, dress, manners, and entertainment no longer marked rank as sharply as before.

Postwar reconstruction joined ordinary needs with moral claims. Repatriation, demobilization, currency stability, food imports, rebuilt railways, reopened cultural life, and new housing answered immediate damage. At the same time, Resistance prestige, Labour politics, Christian Democracy, and socialist pressure made welfare and social justice part of democratic legitimacy.

Reconstruction also depended on memory management. Trials, purges, amnesties, resistance myths, collaboration scandals, refugee settlement, and the return of prisoners all shaped postwar politics. Societies needed to rebuild bridges and factories, but they also needed usable accounts of occupation, defeat, complicity, victimhood, and liberation.

European integration advanced through practical sectors before broader political identity. Coal and steel cooperation addressed the problem of German industry; the common market addressed trade and growth; the European Court created legal authority; the Parliament gradually gained significance; monetary union pushed states toward shared economic discipline.

Integration also changed the meaning of sovereignty. States did not simply disappear into a federation; they pooled authority in selected fields while keeping national governments, elections, armies, languages, welfare systems, and political memories. This partial character explains both the success and unease of the European project. It could stabilize trade, law, and diplomacy, yet every treaty or enlargement reopened questions about democratic accountability and national choice.

Post-1989 Europe revived old hopes and problems. German reunification, NATO enlargement, EU enlargement, postcommunist transition, Russian anxiety, Balkan conflict, migration, constitutional debate, and the euro all showed that Europe's division had ended without ending arguments about power, borders, sovereignty, democracy, and identity.

The end of communism also made economic transition uneven. Privatization, new parties, constitutional reform, inflation, unemployment, foreign investment, corruption, EU accession rules, and NATO security promises reshaped central and eastern Europe at different speeds. For some countries, integration offered recovery and protection; for others, it revived fears of dependency or lost sovereignty. The post-1989 order was therefore hopeful but not settled.

Late European unity therefore remained unfinished. The EU grew from coal, steel, common markets, courts, elections, citizenship, and currency plans, but referendums and treaty crises showed public hesitation. the evidence closes by treating integration as necessary and difficult: it answered war, depression, and lost power, yet it kept raising questions about democracy, national choice, and common authority. Enlargement after communism made this problem wider because more states now had to balance national recovery with shared European rules.

## Historical Patterns and Legacies

European history ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_Europe)) is not the history of one continuous state or people. It is the history of a connected but divided peninsula where mountains, seas, rivers, and competing political centres encouraged both exchange and fragmentation. The Mediterranean linked southern Europe to North Africa and western Asia, the Atlantic opened routes to the Americas and Africa, and the great northern plain enabled migration, trade, and invasion. Political diversity often produced war, but it also created competition among courts, cities, universities, merchants, and religious institutions.

The legacy of [[Rome]] gave later Europe shared languages, law, roads, urban ideals, and Christianity, but the western empire’s collapse did not end cultural continuity. Germanic kingdoms, the Byzantine Empire, the Islamic world, monasteries, and trading cities preserved and remixed older traditions. Medieval Europe developed a distinctive relationship among kings, nobles, towns, churches, and legal institutions. Feudal obligations, manor agriculture, guilds, universities, and representative assemblies did not operate identically everywhere, but together they formed many of the institutions from which later European states developed.

The Renaissance ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Renaissance)), Reformation, scientific revolution, and Enlightenment changed how Europeans understood authority and knowledge. Printing accelerated the circulation of religious arguments, scientific claims, maps, political pamphlets, and vernacular literature. The Reformation divided western Christianity and linked belief to state power, while the growth of science encouraged systematic observation, experiment, and mathematical explanation. Enlightenment ideas about rights, sovereignty, commerce, and reason later influenced revolutions and reform movements, even when European governments remained authoritarian.

Industrialization transformed Europe by moving production from households and workshops toward factories, coalfields, railways, and large cities. Industrial capitalism increased output and wealth but also created crowded housing, dangerous labour, class conflict, and environmental damage. It helped European powers project military and commercial influence around the world, yet imperial expansion depended on unequal power, coercion, and colonial extraction. The world wars exposed the destructive potential of nationalism, industrial warfare, and racial ideology; postwar reconstruction, welfare states, decolonization, and European cooperation were attempts to create greater security from that history.

## Key Events & Developments

- 35,000 BCE: Anatomically modern humans appeared in Europe.
- 13,000 BCE: Postglacial warming began reshaping human adaptation.
- 7000 BCE: Farming began in Greece and later spread unevenly across Europe.
- 3200 BCE: Copper Age societies in southeastern Europe and the Carpathian Basin showed prestige metallurgy and social complexity.
- 2300-700 BCE: Bronze Age societies developed new metallurgy, settlement forms, burials, and exchange.
- 700-1 BCE: Iron Age Europe saw iron tools, proto-urban centers, and clearer historic identities.
- 800 BCE: Greek colonization began spreading Hellenic culture around the Mediterranean and Black Sea.
- 2nd century CE: Rome ruled much of Europe, northern Africa, and western Asia.
- 5th century CE: Germanic kingdoms replaced western Roman rule while adopting Roman and Christian forms.
- 774: [[Charlemagne]] conquered Lombardy and strengthened Frankish ties with Rome.
- 800: Charlemagne's imperial coronation renewed western imperial ideology.
- 1100 onward: High medieval universities, canon law, Gothic art, papal power, and scholastic learning matured.
- 1190: The York massacre illustrated intensified medieval anti-Judaism.
- 1494-1559: Italian and Habsburg-Valois wars reshaped diplomacy and monarchy.
- 1517: Martin Luther's challenge helped launch the Protestant Reformation.
- 1555: The Peace of Augsburg established cuius regio, eius religio in the Holy Roman Empire.
- 1598: The Edict of Nantes gave French Huguenots limited toleration.
- 1618-1648: The Thirty Years' War devastated central Europe and reshaped diplomacy.
- 1648: The Peace of Westphalia strengthened territorial rulers and balance-of-power politics.
- 1701-1714: The War of the Spanish Succession tested dynastic claims and European equilibrium.
- 1751-1765: The Encyclopedie appeared as a major Enlightenment project.
- 1789: The French Revolution redefined sovereignty, rights, and political legitimacy.
- 1799: Napoleon Bonaparte came to power in France.
- 1814-1815: The Congress of Vienna rebuilt the diplomatic order after Napoleon.
- 1848: Revolutions exposed the force and limits of liberalism, nationalism, and social protest.
- 1856: The Bessemer converter symbolized the second industrial revolution.
- 1871: German unification changed Europe's balance of power.
- 1914-1918: World War I shattered Europe's prewar confidence.
- 1933: Adolf Hitler became German chancellor and moved toward dictatorship.
- 1939-1945: World War II devastated Europe through occupation, bombing, genocide, and displacement.
- 1948: The OEEC and Hague Congress advanced recovery and unity.
- 1949: NATO and the Council of Europe were founded.
- 1950: The European Convention on Human Rights and the Schuman Plan advanced integration.
- 1957: The Treaties of Rome founded the EEC and Euratom.
- 1967: European institutions merged into the European Communities.
- 1987: The Single European Act pushed the common market.
- 1989: The Berlin Wall was breached.
- 1992: Maastricht created the European Union.
- 1999: Eleven countries began transition to the euro.
- 2004-2007: Major eastern enlargements brought many former communist states into the EU.
- 2009: The Lisbon Treaty entered into force.

## Interesting Facts

- [[Europe]] is treated as a debated historical idea, not just a physical continent.
- European unity ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_integration)) moved from empire and church to voluntary postwar institutions.
- Prehistoric Europe must be reconstructed from archaeology and scientific dating rather than documents.
- Neolithic farming spread unevenly and cannot be explained by one simple migration model.
- Metal Ages ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Three-age_system)) developments once attributed to eastern influence are now often seen as local and independent.
- Westphalia mattered because it made balance of power central to European diplomacy.
- Little Ice Age climate conditions affected harvests, demography, poverty, and migration.
- Absolutism was limited by law, privilege, finance, nobles, churches, and local institutions.
- Enlightenment ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Age_of_Enlightenment)) reform took different forms in France, Germany, Italy, Austria, Britain, and Russia.
- Industrial Revolution ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Industrial_Revolution)) changed agriculture, transport, finance, cities, family life, and class relations, not only factories.
- Romanticism ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Romanticism)) reshaped nationalism, selfhood, music, literature, art, and historical imagination.
- Victorian morality appears as a broader European ethos of discipline and respectability.
- [[World Wars (World War I)|World War I]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_War_I)) and [[World Wars (World War II)|World War II]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_War_II)) undermined Europe's old global dominance and pushed states toward integration.
- Marshall aid helped economic recovery while encouraging permanent consultation among western European states.
- European Union ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_Union)) integration was designed to prevent war, avoid protectionist nationalism, and give European states collective influence.
