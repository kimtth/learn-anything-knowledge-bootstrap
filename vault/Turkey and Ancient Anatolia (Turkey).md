## Introduction

Turkey ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turkey)) is a country lying partly in Asia and partly in Europe. It occupies a strategic position between the Balkans, Caucasus, Middle East, eastern Mediterranean, Black Sea, Aegean, and Mediterranean worlds.

Most of the country is in [[Turkey and Ancient Anatolia (Anatolia)|Anatolia]], or Asia Minor, while Turkish Thrace lies in southeastern Europe. Turkey has often acted as both bridge and barrier between continents.

Its capital is Ankara, while Istanbul is the largest city and main seaport. The Turkish straits, including the Bosporus, Sea of Marmara, and Dardanelles, are the Black Sea's only outlet and a major factor in foreign relations.

Turkey's long boundary is mostly maritime, and the Aegean boundary with Greece has been a repeated source of dispute since World War II. Only Gokceada and Bozcaada remain Turkish among many Aegean islands near the coast.

## Ancient Anatolian Layers

[[Turkey and Ancient Anatolia (Anatolia)|Anatolia]] was a meeting ground long before the modern Turkish republic. Hittite, Phrygian, Lydian, Greek, Persian, Hellenistic, Roman, Byzantine, Armenian, Kurdish, Arab, Seljuk, and Ottoman histories all left traces in settlement, religion, language, law, architecture, trade, and memory. This layering matters because Turkey is not simply a later nation-state placed on empty ground; it occupies a peninsula where imperial roads, frontier zones, Christian and Muslim sacred landscapes, port cities, upland pastoralism, and agricultural basins repeatedly changed hands.

The arrival of Turkic-speaking peoples did not erase the older Anatolian world. Seljuk and later Ottoman rule joined Central Asian, Persianate, Islamic, Byzantine, Balkan, and Mediterranean elements. Modern Turkish nationalism then reinterpreted this complex inheritance through secular citizenship, language reform, archaeology, and state history. The result is a country whose identity is built from both Anatolian depth and republican reinvention.

Turkey is mostly mountainous. True lowlands are limited to coastal fringes, and about one-fourth of the surface lies above 4,000 feet.

The highest peak is Mount Ararat ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mount_Ararat)), near the borders with Armenia and Iran. Steep slopes, high plateaus, folded ranges, volcanic areas, and young geological structures shape climate, agriculture, and settlement.

Flat or gently sloping land forms only a small share of the country, limiting easy cultivation. The east-west folded mountain zone includes sedimentary rocks, intrusions, volcanic materials, and active faulting, so relief and geology combine to make regional development uneven.

Major structural regions include the Pontic Mountains in the north, the Taurus system in the south, the central Anatolian massif, and the Arabian platform in the southeast.

## Rivers, Lakes, and Soils

Turkey has several drainage basins. The Euphrates and Tigris flow south into Syria and Iraq; the Aras flows toward the Caspian; other rivers drain to the Black, Marmara, Aegean, and Mediterranean seas.

Important rivers include the Sakarya, Kizil, Yesil, Gediz, Buyukmenderes, Ceyhan, Seyhan, Euphrates, and Tigris.

Major lakes include saline Lake Van and Lake Tuz, plus freshwater lakes such as Egridir, Beysehir, Aksehir, and Iznik. Dams on major rivers have created large artificial lakes and hydroelectric reservoirs.

Soils vary with relief, climate, and parent material. Brown and reddish brown soils support dryland grain in semiarid interior regions, while forest soils occur in more humid mountain belts.

Red and gray-brown podzolic soils, brown forest soils, chestnut soils, rendzinas, grumusols, and alkaline serozems form a complex soil pattern. Irrigation can make interior brown soils more productive, while coastal and mountain soils reflect wetter climates and calcareous rocks.

## Climate and Vegetation

Turkey's climate is a dry, semicontinental Mediterranean variant shaped by surrounding seas and mountains. Coastal areas are milder, while the interior has colder winters and hotter, drier summers.

The Black Sea coast is the wettest region, with rain throughout the year. The Aegean and Mediterranean coasts have Mediterranean climates with winter rain and summer drought.

The southeast is hot and dry in summer, while the Anatolian interior has large seasonal temperature contrasts and relatively low precipitation.

Vegetation includes [[The History of the Eurasian Steppe (Steppe)|steppe]] grasslands in central and southeastern regions, humid deciduous and Pontic forests near the Black Sea, Mediterranean maquis, pine, fir, cedar, oak, juniper, and alpine grasslands.

Human action has heavily modified natural vegetation through lumbering, clearance, and grazing. The richest woodland is the Pontic or Colchian forest of the eastern Black Sea coast, where heavy rainfall supports hornbeam, sweet chestnut, oriental spruce, alder, rhododendron, laurel, holly, hazel, and walnut.

## People and Settlement

*Ancient Anatolian Regions*

Most people live in towns and cities, though Turkey was largely rural before the mid-20th century. Population density is highest around the Marmara, Aegean, and Black Sea regions.

The constitution uses Turk as a political term for citizens, regardless of race or religion. Most people speak Turkish as a mother tongue; significant minorities speak Kurdish or Arabic.

Kurds form a large minority, especially in eastern and southeastern Anatolia. Other groups include Greeks, Armenians, Jews, Circassians, Georgians, and Laz.

Settlement patterns show strong regional contrast. The Marmara, Aegean, and Black Sea regions occupy less than half the land but hold more than half the population, while the Anatolian interior and southeast are more lightly settled. Seasonal movement between plain and mountain survived among herders.

More than nine-tenths of the population is Muslim, but Turkey is constitutionally secular. Secularism has been a defining state principle and a recurring source of political conflict.

The secular republic tried to place religion ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Religion)) under state supervision rather than remove it from society. This produced continuing debates over religious education, public dress, political Islam, minority rights, and the proper legacy of Ataturk's reforms.

## Economy

Since 1923 Turkey has had a mixed economy with both state and private enterprise. It shifted from mainly agriculture toward industry and services.

Early republican development relied heavily on state investment in railways, ports, mining, metallurgy, chemicals, food processing, textiles, and basic manufacturing.

After World War II, foreign aid, mechanized agriculture, private enterprise, and foreign trade became more important. In the 1980s policy shifted toward foreign investment, joint ventures, exports, and a more open economy.

The postwar opening also made growth vulnerable to inflation, unemployment, and foreign-trade deficits. State planning continued through five-year plans and loans to private firms, but the economy no longer remained as self-contained as in the early republic.

Economic change also widened regional contrasts. Western and coastal zones benefited more from industry, trade, tourism, and transport links, while eastern and southeastern regions remained more rural, mountainous, and poorer. Migration from villages and small towns fed the growth of Istanbul, Ankara, Izmir, Adana, and other cities, creating informal settlements, new labour markets, and pressure on urban services.

Natural resources include coal, lignite, small oil fields, hydroelectric power, iron, chromite, manganese, zinc, lead, copper, and bauxite.

Agriculture uses about one-third of the land. Major products include wheat, barley, corn, rice, cotton, sugar beets, tobacco, potatoes, fruits, nuts, vegetables, olives, tea, hazelnuts, livestock, and livestock products.

Manufacturing includes [[Textile Industries (Textile)|textiles]], clothing, food, tobacco, chemicals, steel, engineering, vehicles, oil refining, cement, machinery, and consumer goods. Major industrial zones include Istanbul-Marmara, Izmir-Aegean, Adana, and Ankara.

## Trade and Transportation

Foreign trade expanded after World War II. Exports shifted from mainly agricultural and mineral products toward textiles, clothing, steel, machinery, and manufactures.

Imports include machinery, chemicals, petroleum products, transport equipment, and consumer goods. Europe, especially Germany, is a major trading partner, while Middle Eastern states are important for oil and manufactured exports.

Transportation policy first emphasized railways, then roads and highways after the 1950s. The Marmaray Project sought to improve rail around Istanbul and included a rail tunnel beneath the Bosporus.

## Government and Law

The Republic of Turkey began under Mustafa Kemal Ataturk and the Republican People's Party. Multiparty democracy was introduced in 1950, though interrupted by military governments in 1960-61, 1971-73, and 1980-83.

The 1982 constitution created a parliament, the Grand National Assembly, elected by universal adult suffrage. Executive authority was shared between prime minister and president under the system described.

The assembly had 550 members elected for five-year terms by a modified proportional system. Parties needed at least 10 percent of the national vote to enter parliament, and extremist parties of both left and right were barred.

Legal reforms in 1926 replaced religiously based civil law with new codes influenced by Swiss and Italian models. Courts include constitutional, criminal, civil, administrative, and superior judicial bodies.

## Education, Health, and Culture

Education is organized by the state, with compulsory primary schooling, secondary schools, technical and vocational institutions, and universities in cities such as Istanbul, Ankara, Izmir, and elsewhere.

Health services include state and private systems, social security programs, village health houses, health units, and hospitals, though facilities are concentrated in urban areas.

Turkish culture mixes East and West. The republic inherited Anatolian, Byzantine, Seljuq, Ottoman, Islamic, European, and modern national influences.

Daily life includes rural work rhythms, markets, crafts, family roles, religious practices, traditional and Western clothing, urban commuting, and modern factories, offices, and shops.

## War of Independence and Republic

After World War I, resistance grew in Anatolia against Allied demands and the Greek occupation of Izmir. Mustafa Kemal landed at Samsun in 1919 and organized nationalist resistance.

Congresses at Erzurum and Sivas formed national organizations. The Grand National Assembly met at Ankara in 1920 and claimed sovereignty for the nation.

The republic abolished the sultanate, defeated opponents, and created a new political order. Ataturk's Republican People's Party dominated until 1950.

Ataturk's six principles were republicanism, nationalism, populism, statism, secularism, and revolution. Reforms changed politics, law, education, language, dress, economy, and public culture from above.

## After Ataturk

After Ataturk's death in 1938, Ismet Inonu became president. Turkey stayed neutral through most of World War II and joined the Allied side only in February 1945.

Postwar Soviet pressure over the straits and eastern territory led Turkey to seek U.S. aid, beginning military assistance in 1947 and economic aid in 1948.

Multiparty politics brought the Democratic Party to power in 1950, but military coups and constitutional changes shaped later decades.

The Kurdish issue became especially violent after the PKK launched armed struggle in 1984. The conflict caused deaths, displacement, martial law, cross-border operations, and deep political strain.

In 2002, the Justice and Development Party, or AKP, won elections. Recep Tayyip Erdogan became prime minister in 2003 after a constitutional change removed his ineligibility.
