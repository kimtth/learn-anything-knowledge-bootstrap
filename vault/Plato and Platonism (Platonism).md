# Plato and Platonism (Platonism)

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> Platonism is the family of philosophies inspired by Plato that locate value and intelligibility in eternal realities beyond changing sensible things. The account follows early Greek, Neoplatonic, Jewish, patristic, Augustinian, and medieval forms while showing how readings of Plato shaped ethics, religion, and politics.

## Introduction

Platonism ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Platonism)) is any philosophy ultimately inspired by [[Plato and Platonism (Plato)|Plato]]. Although ancient reports mention unwritten doctrines, Platonism has usually been based on readings of the dialogues. These readings vary greatly, but most forms share concern for human life, ethics, religion, politics, and belief in eternal realities or Forms independent of changing sensible things.

Platonism holds that absolute values ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Absolute_value)) are rooted in an intelligible order that causes the existence of things and gives value and meaning to life. This distinguishes it from pre-Socratic naturalism, Sophistic relativism, and [[Aristotle and Aristotelianism|Aristotle]]'s more this-worldly correction of Plato.

## Early Greek Platonism

Plato's own late metaphysics must be inferred from dialogues and testimony by [[Aristotle and Aristotelianism|Aristotle]] and others. His doctrine of Forms appears to have had a mathematical character, perhaps influenced by Pythagoreanism. [[Aristotle and Aristotelianism|Aristotle]] rejected transcendent Forms but preserved elements of Platonism in his emphasis on intelligible form, rational definition, eternal intellects, and the highest realities.

Plato's immediate successors shaped the Academy. Speusippus emphasized numbers and rejected Forms, while Xenocrates identified Forms and numbers and debated whether the creation account in the Timaeus was chronological or expository. Crantor was allegedly the first commentator on the Timaeus.

## Neoplatonism

Neoplatonism ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neoplatonism)) arranged reality into descending levels of being derived from superior levels through timeless dependence. Each level returns toward its source through contemplative desire, and each lower level images its archetype above. Degrees of being are degrees of unity, while multiplicity, separation, and limitation increase downward toward the physical world.

The highest principle is the One, also called the Good, beyond being, determination, attributes, division, and ordinary knowledge. Human knowledge of it must differ from knowledge of objects. Reality moves through outgoing and return, and the soul's goal is reunion with its source.

## Iamblichus and Proclus

Later Neoplatonism moved the One into ineffable transcendence and introduced henads, identified with pagan gods, throughout reality. Iamblichus developed complex triadic structures, though many works are lost. Proclus codified later Platonism in works such as Elements of Theology and Platonic Theology, combining metaphysical hierarchy with pagan theology and defending old religion in an increasingly Christian world.

## Early Jewish Platonism

Jewish Platonism appears most clearly in Philo of Alexandria, who used Greek philosophy to interpret and defend Jewish scripture. Through allegorical commentaries, especially on Genesis, Philo argued that the revelation to [[Moses]] accorded with the best Greek philosophy. He preferred Middle Platonism and strongly influenced Greek-speaking Christian thinkers.

## Patristic Platonism

Christian Platonists used Platonic philosophy to understand and defend revelation. Justin Martyr, Clement of Alexandria, and Origen treated Platonism as the best philosophical instrument for expressing Christian faith. They rejected pagan polytheism but accepted much Platonic theology, morality, and metaphysics as compatible with Christianity.

## Augustinian Platonism

[[Augustine]] was deeply influenced by Plotinus and Porphyry, yet created a distinctive Augustinianism. He held the soul superior to the body, emphasized divine illumination, and treated truth as encountered through God's action on the mind. Unlike pagan Neoplatonists, he insisted that spiritual beings are mutable, that sin and fall are real, and that divine grace is necessary.

Medieval Christian Platonism flowed through Calcidius's Timaeus commentary, Boethius, Augustine, the School of Chartres, and Anselm. John Scotus Erigena transmitted Greek Christian Platonism from Gregory of Nyssa, Pseudo-Dionysius, and Maximus the Confessor. Medieval thought was never simply Plato versus Aristotle, since Middle Platonism, Neoplatonism, Byzantine theology, Islamic philosophy, and Scholasticism blended Platonic and Aristotelian elements.

## Evaluation

The central issue in Platonism ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Platonism)) is whether there is an intelligible or spiritual reality independent of the visible world and serving as the source of existence and value. Opponents, beginning with Aristotle, argue that such doctrines duplicate reality, posit unsupported entities, and fail to explain their relation to sensible things. The dispute remains unsettled, but Platonism has been central to European thought.

## Key Events & Developments

- 4th century BCE: Plato and early Academy members formulated key doctrines.
- 3rd century CE: Plotinus founded classical Neoplatonism.
- 410-485: Proclus codified later Neoplatonic metaphysics.
- Patristic era: Christian thinkers used Platonism to express theology.
- Middle Ages: Augustinian and Dionysian Platonism shaped Western theology.
- Renaissance and modern era: Platonic traditions reappeared in humanism, Cambridge Platonism, and later religious thought.

## Interesting Facts

- The One in Neoplatonism is beyond ordinary being and description.

- Christian Platonism and Christian Aristotelianism were often intertwined.
