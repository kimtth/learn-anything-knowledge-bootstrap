# Mexico City

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> Mexico City is Mexico's capital and one of the Western Hemisphere's oldest and largest urban settlements, founded by Spaniards on the ruins of Aztec Tenochtitlan. Its central political and economic role coexists with a vast metropolitan scale, rich cultural institutions, inequality, congestion, pollution, and environmental strain.

Mexico City, or Ciudad de Mexico, is the capital of [[Mexico]] and is closely identified with the Federal District. The name can also mean the wider metropolitan area, which extends beyond the district into the surrounding state of Mexico.

Spanish conquistadors founded the city in 1521 on the ruins of Tenochtitlan, the island capital of the Aztec empire. Its inland position in the Valley of Mexico, also called Anahuac, made it a crossroads between northern Mexico, the Gulf coast, the Pacific coast, and the south.

The city is one of the oldest continuously inhabited urban settlements in the Western Hemisphere and one of the world's largest metropolitan areas. Its power comes from long-standing centralism: politics, religion, trade, roads, services, schools, hospitals, and government agencies have converged there for centuries.

The capital is also a city of contrasts. Its historic centre, colonial churches, museums, Chapultepec, Paseo de la Reforma, modern towers, and shopping districts coexist with congestion, pollution, inequality, shantytowns, and lower-working-class neighbourhoods.

## Landscape

Mexico City sits in the highland Valley of Mexico, enclosed by mountains of the Cordillera Neo-Volcanica. Downtown lies at about 7,350 feet, while the wider district averages above 8,000 feet.

Ancient lakes once covered the basin floor, and lacustrine plains still underlie much of the city. Southern volcanic slopes, lava beds called pedregales, the Sierra de las Cruces, and peaks such as Ajusco shape the district's rugged edges.

The built-up area grew from an island near Lake Texcoco through land reclamation, canal building, drainage, and urban expansion. These transformations drained most basin lake water and reduced forests, grasslands, and the fragile plant communities on southern lava flows.

## Climate

Mexico City has a cool tropical highland climate shaped by elevation, mountain barriers, tropical air, and cold northern fronts. The mean annual temperature is about 59 F, and the rainy season runs from late May to early October.

The city became warmer and drier after the lakes were drained and the paved built environment expanded. Roads, buildings, and machines created an urban heat island, while mountain-enclosed air circulation and temperature inversion trap polluted air above the valley.

The historic layout includes Tepeyac, Cerro de la Estrella, Chapultepec, the Zocalo, the Metropolitan Cathedral, the National Palace, Torre Latinoamericana, Paseo de la Reforma, and Insurgentes Avenue. Middle-class, upper-class, and squatter settlements spread unevenly across old lake beds and slopes.

## People

Mexico City attracts migrants from across Mexico and from abroad. Native-born residents with long family roots are called chilangos, but the city contains sharp class and ethnic divisions.

Ideas about raza affect social mobility and acceptance. People with indigenous or mestizo ancestry often live in middle- and lower-class areas, while criollos and people claiming European ancestry are more common in wealthy zones and better-paying jobs.

Religion remains important to many residents. [[Roman Catholicism]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catholic_Church)) dominates the cityscape through neighbourhood churches and shrines, while Jewish and Protestant communities also contribute to trade, professions, and urban religious life.

## Economy

The Mexico City region produces nearly one-fourth of national GDP. Services dominate income and employment, manufacturing remains important, and the informal economy includes street vendors, scavengers, day labourers, domestic workers, and unlicensed service providers.

The city depends on supplies from far beyond the basin. Food, water, and fuel must travel long distances, and tens of thousands of tons of food arrive daily to sustain the metropolitan population.

## Manufacturing

Mexico City remains the country's principal manufacturing centre because of transportation networks and a large skilled and semiskilled workforce. Its share of manufacturing employment has declined, however, as services grew and border maquiladoras expanded.

Heavy industry lies mainly around the metropolitan ring, and some plants were forced to move or close in the 1990s because of air pollution. Clothing, paper products, consumer electronics, chemicals, plastics, cement, processed foods, refined metals, and metal products are important outputs.

## Transportation

Mexico City has been a transportation hub since ancient trade routes crossed the highland basin. Modern roads, railways, beltways, expressways, and superhighways connect the capital with Cuernavaca, Toluca, Morelia, Queretaro, Tlaxcala, Puebla, and Pachuca.

Traffic is intense because millions of vehicles use streets already crowded by vendors, pedestrians, and protest marches. Vehicle numbers nearly doubled from the late 1970s to the early 1990s, and by the early 21st century about four million circulated in the city.

Public transport includes buses, taxis, trolleys, peseros, light rail, and the Metro. The Metro has about 125 miles of rail on 11 routes and carries about four million passengers daily, while the international airport handles national and international flights.

## Government

Mexico City is the seat of federal power, so local and national politics overlap strongly. The National Palace on the Zocalo is the president's official seat, and crowds gather there each September 15 for the Grito de Dolores.

The Federal District includes administrative areas such as Magdalena Contreras, Miguel Hidalgo, Milpa Alta, Tlahuac, Tlalpan, Venustiano Carranza, and Xochimilco. The wider metropolis also includes more than two dozen self-governing municipios in the state of Mexico.

For much of its history, residents did not elect local leaders; the president appointed the mayor. Since 1997, the mayor has been chosen by popular vote, and since 2000 the delegaciones have elected their heads directly.

Municipal services vary sharply by class and neighbourhood. Wealthier colonias have developed-world utilities and entertainment, while poorer ciudades perdidas may lack safe water, electricity, sewerage, paved streets, and durable housing for years.

Freshwater and drainage have been central problems since Aztec times. Deep wells, tunnels, distant reservoirs, and pumping systems supply the city, but groundwater extraction has caused subsidence, and the 1985 earthquake badly damaged the telephone system.

## Health

Mexico City's public health problems reflect uneven sanitation, pollution, overcrowding, and class inequality. Gastrointestinal disease affects poorer children, respiratory illness follows polluted air, and pepenadores risk toxic exposure while scavenging waste.

Health care is also a major service industry. Prosperous areas contain large hospitals, research institutes, clinics, private facilities, and government complexes such as the General Hospital and Medical Centre, drawing patients from across Mexico.

## Education

Most Mexico City residents are literate, and the capital has Mexico's strongest concentration of schools and higher education. The National Autonomous University of Mexico, or UNAM, was founded in 1551 and is among the world's largest universities.

## The National Polytechnic Institute and the Metropolitan

The National Polytechnic Institute and the Metropolitan Autonomous University are major public institutions. Private universities include the Ibero-Americana University and Anahuac University, and the College of Mexico is a leading research institution.

## The UNAM Campus

The UNAM campus links education with architecture and public art. Built on southern lava flows in 1949 to 1952 and opened in 1954, it became a UNESCO World Heritage site in 2007.

## Muralism

David Alfaro Siqueiros created murals on the UNAM campus in 1952 to 1953. These works belong to the city's broader mural tradition, which joins modern public buildings with Mexican history and social themes.

## Administration

The Palace of Fine Arts, built between 1904 and 1934, houses art and performances. Public buildings such as the National Palace, the Central Library, and the Rectoria display murals by Juan O'Gorman, Siqueiros, Diego Rivera, and others.

The historic centre became a UNESCO World Heritage site in 1987, including more than 1,400 buildings from the 16th to 19th centuries. The Templo Mayor, Metropolitan Cathedral, Tepeyac basilicas, Xochimilco canals, and National Museum of Anthropology preserve religious, archaeological, and cultural memory.

Football is the leading sport, and Mexico City hosted the World Cup championship match in 1970 and 1986. Chapultepec Park, national parks, Plaza Mexico, Olympic facilities from 1968, publishing, television, radio, cinema, and lucha libre all shape urban recreation.

## Weekend Excursions

Residents often leave the city on weekend excursions to Puebla, Cuernavaca, Acapulco, Nevado de Toluca, Desierto del Carmen, and Zoquiapan. These trips show how the capital's recreation depends on nearby highlands, historic towns, and coastal resorts.

## History

The Aztec-Mexica reached the Valley of Mexico by the early 14th century after leaving Aztlan. They used chinampas, fishing, hunting, gathering, and lake resources to support a dense island city.

## Tenochtitlan

Tenochtitlan was founded in 1325 after a sacred sign associated with Huitzilopochtli: an eagle with a snake perched on a prickly pear cactus. The symbol became central to the Mexican national flag.

The city had levees, aqueducts, canals, causeways, drawbridges, temples, schools, markets, palaces, ward districts called calpulli, and neighbourhoods organized by trade or origin. By the early 16th century, it held perhaps 100,000 to 200,000 inhabitants and was the political and economic hub of a region with more than 1,000,000 people.

## The Tlatelolco Market

The Tlatelolco market, located where the Plaza of the Three Cultures stands today, impressed Hernan Cortes. He described tens of thousands of buyers and sellers, food, precious metals, medicinal herbs, birds, cloth, services, and organized urban commerce.

After Cortes first entered Tenochtitlan, the Spaniards were driven out on June 30, 1520, later called La Noche Triste. They returned with indigenous allies, weapons, horses, dogs, and European diseases, then destroyed the city during a 75-day siege in 1521.

Spanish Mexico City was chartered in 1522 and recognized by 1535 as the preeminent city of the Americas. It became the seat of the Viceroyalty of New Spain, with influence extending from northern Spanish territories to Panama and across the Pacific to the [[Philippines]].

Colonial administrators expanded drainage and filled canals while the city grew. Churches, convents, hospitals, guild workshops, seigniorial homes, Baroque palaces, and Neoclassical buildings made the capital wealthy and impressive, but poverty and epidemics persisted around its edges.

## After Independence

After independence, Mexico City suffered political instability, protest, class restrictions on voting, and church property dominance. The 1847 U.S. capture of the city, the Ninos Heroes at Chapultepec, and Benito Juarez's 1856 expropriation of church property all reshaped civic memory and urban form.

French intervention brought Maximilian and the expansion of Paseo de la Reforma. Under Porfirio Diaz, modernization followed Parisian models: railways, streetcars, lighting, drainage, factories, foreign investment, and elite western neighbourhoods contrasted with poor eastern and downtown tenements.

The 1910 to 1920 revolution brought intense fighting, including La Decena Tragica in 1913. Yet the capital drew migrants and refugees, growing beyond 600,000 people by 1921, 1,000,000 by 1930, and 1,500,000 by 1940.

In the second half of the 20th century, the city became a megalopolis. Metropolitan population grew from 3.1 million in 1950 to 14 million by 1980, driven by migration, industrialization, and import-substitution policy.

The 1968 Olympics were meant to display national progress, but the Tlatelolco Massacre occurred ten days before the games when security forces fired on student protesters. Economic crisis in the 1980s, the 1984 San Juanico explosions, and the 1985 earthquake further damaged confidence in government.

Neoliberal reforms in the 1990s brought foreign investment, hotels, malls, gated communities, privatization, and industrial relocation, but poor residents saw fewer benefits. Popocatepetl became active again in 1994, and concerns over pollution, earthquakes, crime, corruption, and overcrowding slowed growth.

By the early 21st century, Mexico City remained Mexico's political and economic centre and a microcosm of the country. Its streets and plazas continued to host contested politics, while the city legislature passed progressive measures such as same-sex unions and abortion legalization.

## Key Events & Developments

- 1325: The Aztec-Mexica founded Tenochtitlan on an island in Lake Texcoco.
- 1521: Hernan Cortes and allied forces destroyed Tenochtitlan after a long siege and began Spanish Mexico City.
- 1535: The city became the leading seat of the Viceroyalty of New Spain in the Americas.
- 1847: U.S. forces captured the city after fighting at Chapultepec and other sites.
- 1907: Major drainage works were completed during the modernization drive under Porfirio Diaz.
- 1968: The Tlatelolco Massacre preceded the Summer Olympic Games.
- 1985: A severe earthquake killed thousands and exposed the danger of soft lakebed soils.
- 1997: Residents began electing the mayor by popular vote.

## Key People

- Tenoch ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tenoch)): Legendary priest-leader linked to the founding sign and name of Tenochtitlan.
- Hernan Cortes ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hernan_Cortes)): Spanish conqueror who described and then destroyed the Aztec capital during the conquest.
- Cuauhtemoc ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cuauhtemoc)): Last Aztec emperor, whose world was broken by conquest and disease.
- Benito Juarez ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Benito_Juarez)): Liberal president whose reform movement expropriated church property and changed the city.
- Maximilian ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maximilian)): Emperor under French auspices who expanded the city limits and built what became Paseo de la Reforma.
- Porfirio Diaz ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Porfirio_Diaz)): President whose modernization policies transformed the capital but deepened class contrasts.
- David Alfaro Siqueiros ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Alfaro_Siqueiros)): Muralist whose public art is part of the city's modern cultural identity.

## Interesting Facts

- Mexico City is one of the few major world cities not built beside a river, because it grew in an inland lake basin.
- Chinampas were raised agricultural fields surrounded by water, not truly floating gardens.
- Nezahualcoyotl grew from about 10,000 residents in the late 1950s to about 1,200,000 a half-century later.
- The Metro carries about four million passengers daily but sells tickets for only a fraction of its operating cost.
- The Metropolitan Cathedral sank unevenly into lakebed soils until a major stabilization project was completed in 2000.
