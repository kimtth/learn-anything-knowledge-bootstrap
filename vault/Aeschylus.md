# Aeschylus

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> Aeschylus was an early classical Athenian dramatist who expanded Greek tragedy through poetic language, religious and political conflict, and ambitious trilogies such as the Oresteia.

## Introduction

Aeschylus, born in 525/524 BC and died in 456/455 BC at Gela, Sicily, was the first of classical [[Athens]]' great dramatists and raised the young art of tragedy ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tragedy)) to new heights of poetry and power. He fought the Persians at Marathon in 490 BC, where his brother was killed, and again at Salamis in 480 BC; he competed at Athens' Great Dionysia ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dionysia)), where each poet staged three tragedies and a comic satyr play ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Satyr_play)), and won his first victory in 484 BC.

## Persians

![[attachments/aeschylus_001.webp|420]]

*Herma bust of Aeschylus*

Persians ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Persians)), presented in 472 BC, is unique among surviving tragedies in dramatizing recent history rather than ancient myth. It treats the Persian defeat at Salamis in 480 BC: a messenger brings the news to the queen in the enemy capital, the loss is blamed on Greek courage and the gods' punishment of Persian overreach, and the broken king Xerxes returns home at the end.

## Seven Against Thebes

Seven Against Thebes ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seven_Against_Thebes_%28play%29)), the only surviving play of a connected trilogy from 467 BC, completes the doom on the house of Laius, whose son Oedipus had killed his father and cursed his own sons Eteocles and Polyneices. Eteocles defends Thebes against his brother's army, posting defenders at six gates but fighting at the seventh himself, where he and Polyneices kill each other and end the royal line.

## Suppliants

Suppliants, the only surviving play of a trilogy probably from 463 BC, was long thought early because of its archaic shape, in which the chorus of Danaus' daughters leads the action, though it likely dates from mid-career. The Danaids, born in [[Egypt]] of Greek descent, flee to Argos to escape forced marriage with their cousins, and King Pelasgus grants them asylum, the trilogy praising domestic love over both rigid virginity and violent lust.

## Oresteia

The Oresteia ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oresteia)), presented in 458 BC, is a trilogy of three linked plays often ranked among the greatest of Greek tragedies. In Agamemnon, the king returns from Troy with the prophetess Cassandra and is murdered by his wife Clytemnestra, who acts from grief for her sacrificed daughter Iphigenia, love for Aegisthus, and the curse of Atreus. In Libation Bearers, her son Orestes, with his sister Electra, avenges his father by killing Aegisthus and then his mother at Apollo's command, after which the Erinyes pursue him in guilt.

## Prometheus Bound

Prometheus Bound ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prometheus_Bound)), disputed in date and authorship but often placed among Aeschylus' last works, shows the god Prometheus chained to a crag for defying Zeus by giving fire to humanity. Visited by Oceanus, the cow-headed Io, and Hermes, he refuses to reveal a secret that could threaten Zeus and is hurled into the underworld; his portrait of Zeus as a tyrant made him a lasting symbol of defiance, echoed in Shelley's Prometheus Unbound of 1820.

## Key Events & Developments

- 490 BC: Aeschylus fights at Marathon, where his brother is killed.
- 484 BC: He wins his first victory at the Great Dionysia ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dionysia)).
- 472 BC: Persians ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Persians)) wins first prize.
- 467 BC: Seven Against Thebes ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seven_Against_Thebes_%28play%29)) is produced.
- 458 BC: The Oresteia ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oresteia)) is presented.

## Key People

- Aeschylus ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aeschylus)): The tragic poet who reshaped Greek tragedy.
- Xerxes: The Persian king whose defeat is dramatized in Persians ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Persians)).
- Clytemnestra ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clytemnestra)): The queen who murders Agamemnon in the Oresteia ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oresteia)).
- Prometheus ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prometheus)): The defiant god of Prometheus Bound ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prometheus_Bound)).

## Interesting Facts

- Persians ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Persians)) tells of a recent battle as tragedy from the defeated side's point of view.
