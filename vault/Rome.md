# Rome

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> Rome is Italy's capital and a layered modern city built around the historical memory of the Roman Republic and empire, papal authority, Renaissance and Baroque art, and national unification. Its Tiber setting, hills, monuments, ruins, basilicas, and institutions make it a central site of European political, religious, architectural, and cultural history.

## Italian Roma

![[attachments/rome_001.webp|420]]

*The Colosseum in Rome illuminated at dusk*

Rome, Italian Roma, is the historic capital of Italy, of Lazio, and of Roma province. It stands on the Tiber River about 15 miles, or 24 km, inland from the Tyrrhenian Sea. It was the capital of an ancient republic and empire, the physical and spiritual seat of the Roman Catholic Church, and one of the great centres of European art, architecture, law, and memory.

Rome is called the Eternal City because it remains both a modern political capital and a memorial to the ancient and Christian past. Its city area is about 496 square miles, and its population was more than 2.6 million in the early 2010s. Its urban identity combines ruins, basilicas, palaces, fountains, traffic, film, government, tourism, and daily neighbourhood life.

The city's character reflects a long tension between papal power and Italian state power. Rome joined unified Italy only in 1870, after Italian troops entered the city and the pope withdrew into the Vatican. The Lateran Treaty later settled relations with Vatican City, leaving Rome both Italy's capital and the setting for Catholic world authority.

## Landscape

Rome grew at a defensible crossing of the Tiber where an island helped passage over the river. Its ancient core formed around the traditional Seven Hills: Palatine, Capitoline, Quirinal, Viminal, Esquiline, Caelian, and Aventine. The surrounding Campagna was settled later than some other parts of central Italy.

The city layout joins ancient and modern systems. The old centre contains 22 rioni, while 35 urban sectors and several suburbs surround it. A belt highway lies about 6 miles, or 10 km, from the centre and links ancient roads such as the Appian Way, Via Aurelia, and Via Flaminia.

Rome's ancient walls still shape the city. The Servian Wall, linked by tradition to Servius Tullius but probably built after the Gallic attack of 390 BC, enclosed much of the early city. The Aurelian Wall, begun in AD 270, ran about 12.5 miles and still largely survives.

## Climate

Rome has hot, dry summers often cooled by the ponentino, a west wind from the Tyrrhenian Sea. Annual precipitation is about 30 inches, or 750 mm, with spring and autumn as the rainiest seasons. Winters are mild, with occasional frost or light snow and cold tramontana winds from the north.

## Modern Monuments and Streets

The Vittoriano, completed in 1911 for Victor Emmanuel II, marks the Corso with a huge Neo-Baroque monument of Brescian marble. It contains the tomb of the unknown soldier and a museum of the 19th-century cultural revival. Romans mockingly call it the wedding cake or typewriter.

The Via Vittorio Veneto, laid out in 1887, became famous during the peak of Italian filmmaking from about 1950 to 1965. International celebrities filled its cafes and hotels, and Federico Fellini's La dolce vita made its glamour part of modern Rome's image.

## The Palatine

The Palatine is tied to Rome's founding legend of Romulus and Remus, twin sons of Mars, abandoned on the flooding Tiber and suckled by a she-wolf. Ancient Romans preserved shrines linked to the myth, including the Lupercal and Romulus's House. Archaeology has found Iron Age hut sites from the 8th-7th century BC.

The hill became an elite residential district by the 3rd century BC and later an imperial palace zone. [[Augustus]], born there in 63 BC, kept a private dwelling known as the House of Livia. Later imperial structures spread over nearly the whole hill, leaving columns and ruins that shaped romantic visions of Rome.

## The Capitoline

The Capitoline Hill was fortress, asylum, and sacred centre. Its northern peak held the Temple of Juno Moneta, source of the word money through its association with the mint. Its southern crest held the Temple of Jupiter Optimus Maximus, dedicated in 509 BC, which became central to Roman religion and triumphal ceremony.

The Capitoline is still the seat of Roman [[The Forms Of Government Their Historical Development (Government)|government]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Government)). Its modern form largely follows [[Michelangelo]]'s 16th-century design, one of the early examples of modern town planning. Santa Maria d'Aracoeli, ancient pavements, and museum collections link pagan, medieval, Renaissance, and civic Rome.

## The Aventine and Caelian

The Aventine preserves a quieter older Rome despite modern buildings. Santa Prisca contains a well-preserved Mithraic basilica beneath it, and Santa Sabina, little changed since the 5th century, has 24 Corinthian columns taken from a pagan building. The Knights of Malta Square was designed by Giambattista Piranesi.

The Caelian includes Villa Celimontana, early churches, medieval abbey walls, and remains of the Temple of Claudius. San Stefano Rotondo may have been modeled on the Holy Sepulchre in Jerusalem. Behind the hill, the Baths of Caracalla, built about AD 206-216, became monumental ruins and a summer opera setting.

## The Esquiline, Viminal, and Quirinal

The Esquiline contains remains of Nero's Golden House, or Domus Aurea, built after the fire of AD 64. Nero's successors erased it by draining its lake for the Colosseum, building baths above it, and covering its rooms. Renaissance artists later discovered its painted spaces and imitated their style as grotesque decoration.

The Viminal and Quirinal lie in modern Rome's busy centre. The Baths of Diocletian, built about AD 298-306, were enormous; [[Michelangelo]] transformed part of them into Santa Maria degli Angeli. The Quirinal Palace, begun by Gregory XIII in 1574, later served popes, kings, and the Italian presidency.

## Parks and the Janiculum

The Galleria Nazionale d'Arte Moderna, founded in 1883, holds major 19th- and 20th-century Italian art, while the Bioparco zoological garden was established in 1911. Across the Tiber, the Janiculum became a park in 1870 honouring Giuseppe Garibaldi's defense of the Roman Republic of 1849.

## The Forum

![[attachments/rome_002.webp|420]]

*Panoramic view of the Roman Forum ruins*

The Roman Forum was the religious, civic, and commercial centre of ancient Rome. It began as a small valley ringed by hills, with the Comitium for politics, the social Forum, shops, priestly precincts, Vestals, and temples. Emperors expanded and monumentalized it, but its main poles of activity remained stable.

Fires, earthquakes, and invasions repeatedly buried and rebuilt the Forum. Medieval Romans called it Campo Vaccino, or Cow Field. Excavations from the late 19th century removed accumulated debris to levels known in Julius Caesar's time. Structures such as the Cloaca Maxima, Temple of Vesta, Regia, and temples of Janus and Saturn record archaic and republican Rome.

## Castel Sant'Angelo and Trastevere

Hadrian began his mausoleum in AD 135. Incorporated into the Aurelian Wall in 271, it became a fortress and then the Castel Sant'Angelo after a plague-ending vision of the archangel Michael attributed to Gregory I. Popes used it as castle, refuge, prison, and storehouse, connected to the Vatican by a fortified passage.

Trastevere, across the Tiber, has narrow cobbled lanes, small piazzas, churches, palaces, cafes, and a bohemian reputation. Santa Maria in Trastevere is claimed by some as Rome's oldest church and is largely 12th-century Romanesque, with a mosaic facade and traditions reaching back to early Christian worship.

## Campus Martius and the Pantheon

![[attachments/rome_003.webp|420]]

*Interior dome and oculus of the Pantheon in Rome*

The Campus Martius, once marshy and lightly built, became a grand district under Marcus Vipsanius Agrippa in the 1st century BC. Remains of Domitian's stadium survive in the form of Piazza Navona. The Ara Pacis and Pantheon are among its greatest monuments.

The Pantheon, rebuilt by Hadrian after earlier Augustan work, is one of the West's great buildings. Its dome is about 142 feet, or 43 metres, in diameter and height, lit by the open oculus. It survived because it became a church in 608, though popes later stripped some bronze.

The area also contains Renaissance ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Renaissance)) and Baroque palaces: Montecitorio, Madama, Spada, Braschi, Pamphili, Firenze, Sapienza, Cancelleria, Farnese, and Massimo alle Colonne. These buildings show Rome as a city of papal families, embassies, archives, parliament, museums, and learned institutions.

## St. Peter's and the Major Basilicas

Rome's four major papal basilicas are St. Peter's, San Giovanni in Laterano, San Paolo Fuori le Mura, and Santa Maria Maggiore. The first three began under Constantine I. The Lateran Treaty gave the Holy See extraterritorial authority over these and other sites.

St. Peter's Basilica stands over the traditional burial place of Peter. Constantine built the first basilica around a shrine in the 4th century. In 1506, Julius II ordered a new basilica; Bramante, Raphael, Antonio da Sangallo, and [[Michelangelo]] shaped its design. Bernini's baldachin later magnified Constantine's canopy idea.

San Giovanni in Laterano was Constantine's cathedral basilica and later rebuilt many times; Francesco Borromini remade its interior in 1646-50. San Paolo Fuori le Mura preserves early basilical character after restoration from an 1823 fire. Santa Maria Maggiore, founded in 432, is Rome's first great Marian church.

## Other Churches, Fountains, and Baroque Rome

Rome's churches include San Lorenzo Fuori le Mura, Santa Croce in Gerusalemme, San Pietro in Vincoli, Gesu, Santa Maria della Vittoria, and Sant'Agostino. They preserve relics, mosaics, ancient columns, Renaissance art, Jesuit architecture, and Baroque theatricality.

Gian Lorenzo Bernini's Ecstasy of St. Teresa in Santa Maria della Vittoria is staged like theatre, with the Cornaro family in marble boxes and hidden light illuminating the saint and angel. Sant'Agostino includes works by Caravaggio, Raphael, and Jacopo Sansovino.

Rome is also a city of fountains, with more than 300 monumental examples. The Trevi Fountain, designed by Nicola Salvi after a 1732 competition, combines allegorical figures and natural rock forms. Its water came from the ancient Acqua Vergine, and legend promises return to Rome for those who toss coins into it.

## People

To be a Romano di Roma, a Roman Roman, still carries prestige. Trastevere residents traditionally considered themselves especially Roman. Rome also contains old papal aristocracy, white nobility, working-class neighbourhoods, immigrants, and many religious communities.

Rome's Jewish community has lived in the city for thousands of years. Major persecution began when Paul IV forced Jews into a ghetto in the 16th century. Restrictions lasted, with interruptions, until Rome joined unified Italy in 1870. Later redevelopment destroyed much of the ghetto, though traces remain.

Population grew quickly in the 1950s and 1960s, rising from just over 1,960,000 in 1951 to more than 2,610,000 in 1967. Since the 1970s, immigrants have arrived from the Philippines, Romania, Poland, Peru, Egypt, China, Sri Lanka, Bangladesh, Morocco, Senegal, Albania, Ecuador, and other countries, sometimes facing racism and violence.

## Economy

Rome is not mainly an industrial city, though it has engineering, electronics, chemical, printing, clothing, and food-processing industries. Many factories closed or relocated in the 1980s and 1990s. Construction, publishing, public administration, tourism, cinema, church-related employment, commerce, and services dominate the economy.

The city is a major tourist, cultural, shopping, and conference centre. Cinecitta Studios, built in 1937, made Rome important in film. Football clubs AS Roma and Lazio also contribute economically and culturally through intense local loyalties.

## Transportation and Government

Rome's traffic is a chronic problem, with more than a million cars and motor scooters producing congestion, noise, pollution, and damage to monuments. Private vehicles were banned from parts of the ancient centre beginning in 1973. Subway construction has always faced archaeological obstacles.

The first subway line from Stazione Termini opened by 1955, and a second line was completed in 1980. Mayor Francesco Rutelli expanded subway and tram systems in the 1990s. Rome is served by Leonardo da Vinci, or Fiumicino, Airport and Ciampino Airport.

The comune of Rome is governed by an elected council, executive committee, and directly elected mayor. Roma province and Lazio region have their own elected councils and executives. Local government manages services such as police protection, health, transport, public assistance, housing, and education.

Rome's government and economy are also shaped by its unusual relation to Vatican City. The city is the capital of the Italian state, the administrative centre of Lazio, and the urban setting for the papacy's most visible institutions. Pilgrimage, diplomacy, church administration, museums, religious festivals, and the major basilicas all add to Rome's public life. This dual role makes Rome both a national capital and a world religious centre, with local services constantly interacting with international crowds, ceremonial spaces, and protected historic fabric.

## Housing, Education, and Modern Growth

Rome has long suffered a housing shortage. After Italian unification in 1870, population growth and building fever transformed open spaces inside the walls. Poorer residents were pushed into borgate on the rural-urban edge, and Mussolini's grand boulevards destroyed central neighbourhoods.

Mid-20th-century migrants crowded into the borgate, while new housing rose at Tiburtino and EUR. Much construction was hasty and substandard. Rome's universities, schools, ministries, libraries, museums, and cultural institutions helped keep it a national centre of learning and administration.

## History

Rome's site was occupied as early as the Bronze Age, around 1500 BC, but continuous settlement began near the start of the 1st millennium BC. Villages appeared on the Palatine, Aventine, Esquiline, and Quirinal, involving Latins, Sabines, and perhaps other groups. By the 7th century BC, valley settlements began to merge.

Ancient Romans dated Rome's founding to 753 BC, but political unification probably occurred in the early 6th century BC under Etruscan influence. Kings, traditionally seven, ruled before the republic. Social distinctions between patricians and plebeians emerged in this period.

## The Early Roman Republic

The Roman Republic ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_Republic)) began after the overthrow of the last king in 509 BC or somewhat later. Two patrician consuls led the state. Economic crisis, grain shortages, and political struggle followed, leading to the Law of the Twelve Tables in 451-450 BC and the creation of plebeian tribunes.

Rome expanded over neighbouring peoples and captured Veii in 396 BC, but a Gallic army attacked in 390 BC. Only the Capitoline held out, and Rome was left in ruins after the Gauls were bought off. Rebuilding was rapid and irregular, but for eight centuries no foreign invader breached Rome's walls.

## Papal, Fascist, and Postwar Rome

Papal Rome was transformed by figures such as Sixtus V, who restored aqueducts, laid out roads, built the Vatican Library, advanced St. Peter's dome, rebuilt papal palaces, reerected obelisks, and restored fountains. By 1600, Rome was again a prosperous cosmopolitan city with more than 100,000 inhabitants.

In the 17th and 18th centuries, noble families built palaces and patronized art while papal influence declined. After unification, Rome became Italy's political capital. Under Fascism and after World War II, the city was reshaped by building projects, corruption, migration, cinema, traffic, and modern politics.

Postwar Rome ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rome)) hosted the Treaties of Rome in 1957, which helped create institutions later forming the European Union, and the Olympic Games in 1960. Film directors such as Roberto Rossellini, Vittorio De Sica, Federico Fellini, and Pier Paolo Pasolini made Rome a centre of cinematic imagination.

Political conflict intensified after 1968. Student protests, extremist violence, coup attempts, terrorism, and the kidnapping and murder of Aldo Moro by the Red Brigades in 1978 marked the era. In 1981, John Paul II was shot in St. Peter's Square, shocking Rome and the world.
