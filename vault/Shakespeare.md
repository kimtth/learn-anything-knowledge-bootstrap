# Shakespeare

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> William Shakespeare was an English poet, actor, and dramatist whose theatrical writing explores human emotion, ambition, love, power, conflict, and moral choice with exceptional verbal and dramatic force. The source relates his Stratford life and commercial theatre career to the enduring global performance and reinterpretation of his plays and poems.

## Introduction

![[attachments/shakespeare_001.webp|420]]

*Chandos portrait of William Shakespeare*

William Shakespeare, baptized on April 26, 1564 and dead on April 23, 1616, was an English poet, dramatist, and actor from Stratford-upon-Avon. He is often called the Bard of Avon and is treated as England's national poet. His reputation is unusual because his plays, first written for a small repertory theatre in late the 16th century and early the 17th century, are now read and performed across many countries.

His greatness rests on several combined powers. Shakespeare had intellectual quickness, perceptive feeling, poetic force, and a special command of words and images. He applied these gifts not to remote subjects but to human beings under pressure: grief, delight, love, ambition, jealousy, aging, loyalty, fear, and moral choice.

The stage made his art especially vivid. Drama ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drama)) allowed imagined people to appear as living bodies before an audience, inviting sympathy and shared experience. Because his situations are deeply human, his plays can survive translation and cultural distance even when some language is difficult.

## Life

The factual record for Shakespeare is large for a person of his social rank, but much of it is official: baptisms, marriages, burials, wills, property records, lawsuits, and payments. Contemporary references to him as a writer and actor give more human detail, though many private questions remain unanswered.

Shakespeare was baptized in Holy Trinity Church at Stratford-upon-Avon on April 26, 1564; his birthday is traditionally kept on April 23. His father, John Shakespeare, was a burgess, alderman, and bailiff, and his mother, Mary Arden, came from an old Warwickshire family. Stratford had a strong grammar school, where Shakespeare likely studied Latin authors rather than attending university.

At age 18, he married Anne Hathaway. A marriage bond dated November 28, 1582 survives, and their daughter Susanna was born on May 26, 1583. Twins, Hamnet and Judith, were christened on February 2, 1585. Hamnet died in 1596, an event that left Shakespeare without a male heir.

By 1592, Shakespeare was known in the London literary world. Robert Greene mocked him as an "upstart crow," but the attack shows that Shakespeare had already become important enough to provoke jealousy. Soon afterward, his poems Venus and Adonis and The Rape of Lucrece ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Rape_of_Lucrece)) were dedicated to Henry Wriothesley, the 3rd earl of Southampton.

From about 1594, Shakespeare was a leading member of the Lord Chamberlain's Men, later the King's Men. He worked not only as a playwright but also as an actor and shareholder. This made him part of a commercial theatre enterprise, sharing both risk and profit with colleagues such as Richard Burbage, John Heminge, and Henry Condell.

His rising prosperity is visible in property records. A coat of arms was granted to John Shakespeare in 1596, probably through William's initiative, and in 1597 Shakespeare bought New Place, a large Stratford house. He later bought London and Stratford properties and a share in Stratford tithes, helping explain his burial in the church chancel.

The later records show a man with local ties and financial interests. In 1604, he walked in royal livery at the coronation of James I. In 1612, he gave evidence in a Mountjoy family lawsuit in London. His will, made on March 25, 1616, left most property to heirs through Susanna and famously gave Anne his second-best bed.

## Sexuality

Shakespeare's sexuality remains uncertain because private evidence is limited. His marriage to Anne Hathaway, who was eight years older, was followed quickly by Susanna's birth, suggesting an unplanned premarital pregnancy. After the twins, the couple had no more children, and Shakespeare lived mainly in London for many years while Anne stayed in Stratford.

The will's brief mention of Anne Hathaway has often been read as a sign of emotional distance, but the evidence is not decisive. The legal and social world of the period made divorce nearly impossible, and surviving documents rarely reveal ordinary feeling inside a marriage.

The sonnets provide more suggestive but still uncertain evidence. They describe a deep relationship between the speaker and a well-born young man, marked by devotion, jealousy, absence, rival poets, and pain. They also describe desire for a dark-haired woman. Scholars disagree about how directly these poems reflect Shakespeare's own life.

The plays repeatedly explore sexual ambiguity. Disguised heroines such as Viola/Cesario, Rosalind/Ganymede, Portia, and Imogen test the boundaries between male and female roles. Because female parts were played by boys in Shakespeare's theatre, these scenes had extra layers of gender play for original audiences.

## Early Documentation and Memory

After Shakespeare's death, his family or friends built a monument in Stratford, apparently by 1623. Its Latin epitaph compared him with Nestor, [[Socrates]], and [[Virgil]], showing how Stratford contemporaries wished him remembered: wise, intellectually gifted, and poetically great.

His memory also survived in the theatre. Ben Jonson once said Shakespeare "wanted art," but in the 1623 First Folio he praised him as "not of an age, but for all time." Jonson also remembered the impact of the plays on Elizabeth I and James I at court performances.

John Heminge and Henry Condell, fellow actors in the King's Men, helped preserve the plays by preparing the First Folio. Their dedication described Shakespeare as a worthy friend and colleague, not merely as a famous author. Later anecdotes, gathered by antiquaries, mixed possible memories with gossip and uncertain tradition.

## Shakespeare the Poet and Dramatist

Shakespeare wrote during a period when medieval ideas of order still mattered but were under pressure. Elizabeth I was viewed as God's deputy, yet religious unity had been broken by the Reformation, royal power was challenged in Parliament, capitalism was changing society, education expanded, and new wealth came from overseas discovery.

This mixture of old and new ideas shaped the plays. Hamlet ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hamlet)) reflects skepticism about man, belief, political decay, and a disordered age. The translation of [[Montaigne]]'s Essays in 1603 widened such questioning. Shakespeare's works after 1603 show a darker Jacobean distrust under James I.

The English language itself was changing. Writers, schoolmasters, courtiers, travellers, classical learning, and cheaper printed books expanded vocabulary and helped standardize grammar and spelling. Shakespeare worked in a language becoming more flexible, ambitious, and ready for lasting literary use.

His literary debts were many. Raphael Holinshed's Chronicles supplied material for several histories and tragedies, including Macbeth and King Lear ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/King_Lear)). Plautus, Ovid, Seneca, morality drama, Christopher Marlowe, Italian comedy, folk materials, and the court masque also shaped different works. Over time these influences became absorbed into his own dramatic method.

## Theatre, Performance, and Dating

![[attachments/shakespeare_002.webp|420]]

*The Globe Theatre in London*

The Lord Chamberlain's Men and King's Men performed at court, in public theatres, in private indoor theatres, at Inns of Court, universities, and great houses. During plague closures they could tour the provinces. By early 1613, the King's Men could present fourteen plays, showing the strong demand for repertory drama.

From 1608-1609, the King's Men regularly used the private indoor Blackfriars theatre, where high admission prices drew a more select audience. Shakespeare's experience as actor and sharer gave him practical knowledge of staging, audience response, roles, clowns, boy actors, music, and the limited rehearsal conditions of the period.

Dating the plays is difficult. Scholars use external documents, internal evidence, style, theme, and the likely pace of no more than about two plays a year. The narrative poems can be dated firmly to plague years: Venus and Adonis in 1593 and The Rape of Lucrece ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Rape_of_Lucrece)) in 1594. The sonnets are usually placed mainly between 1593 and 1600.

## Publication

![[attachments/shakespeare_003.webp|420]]

*Title page of Shakespeare's First Folio*

Renaissance acting companies needed new plays but were not eager to sell successful scripts to publishers. Once a company sold a play, the author had no modern intellectual property control. Plays usually appeared as quartos, though some were octavos, and some publications were unauthorized or shortened.

Textual history is therefore complex. Romeo and Juliet and Hamlet ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hamlet)) appeared in different early versions, with later quartos claiming correction and expansion. Some shorter texts may have been reconstructed from memory by actors; others may preserve theatrical versions with their own value.

The 1623 First Folio, prepared by Heminge and Condell, collected 36 plays under comedies, histories, and tragedies. It printed many plays for the first time but omitted the poems, Pericles, The Two Noble Kinsmen, and other possible collaborations. Modern editors still compare folio and quarto texts, especially for King Lear ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/King_Lear)), Hamlet ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hamlet)), Othello, and several histories.

## Early Plays and Histories

Shakespeare probably reached London in the late 1580s. His early plays show apprenticeship through imitation of successful London drama and classical models. Titus Andronicus, his first full tragedy, draws heavily on revenge tragedy, especially Thomas Kyd's The Spanish Tragedy and Senecan models of violence, oath-bound revenge, feigned madness, and bloodbath endings.

The early comedies quickly formed a recognizably Shakespearean romantic comedy. Plays such as The Two Gentlemen of Verona, The Comedy of Errors, Love's Labour's Lost, and The Taming of the Shrew use courtship, disguise, mistaken identity, verbal wit, and struggles over love and power. Female figures often show loyalty, intelligence, and self-possession.

The early histories created a powerful English dramatic genre. The three parts of Henry VI and Richard III dramatized civil war, faction, weak kingship, national danger, and the rise of Tudor order. Shakespeare freely shaped facts for drama, making Richard III a brilliant theatrical villain and ending the cycle with providential movement toward Elizabethan stability.

## Poems, Mature Comedies, and Later Histories

During plague closures in 1592-1594, Shakespeare published Venus and Adonis and The Rape of Lucrece ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Rape_of_Lucrece)), both dedicated to Southampton. The sonnets explore attachment, rivalry, separation, poetry's power to preserve memory, and destructive jealousy involving the so-called Dark Lady.

The mature comedies deepen the earlier pattern. A Midsummer Night's Dream interweaves lovers, fairies, artisans, and marriage politics to show love as dream, conflict, and renewal. The Merchant of Venice contrasts romantic wooing with near-tragedy. Much Ado About Nothing, As You Like It, Twelfth Night, and The Merry Wives of Windsor explore wit, disguise, self-knowledge, social correction, and more equal relations between men and women.

The second history sequence moves from Richard II through Henry IV and Henry V. It studies political legitimacy, rebellion, maturity, and the cost of kingship. Prince Hal's rejection of Falstaff is both necessary for royal authority and emotionally painful, while Agincourt makes Henry V a heroic national figure.

Romeo and Juliet stands between comedy and mature tragedy. It celebrates young love but frames it as sacrifice to family hatred, misunderstanding, chance, and Romeo's own decision to answer Mercutio's death with violence. Its emotional effect differs from the later tragedies because the lovers are ordinary young people caught in a hostile world.

## Problem Plays and Roman Transition

Around 1599-1604, Shakespeare turned toward darker problems: revenge, sexual jealousy, ethical compromise, and death. All's Well That Ends Well, Measure for Measure, and Troilus and Cressida are difficult to classify. They use comic devices such as substitutions and marriages, but they test desire, law, forgiveness, cowardice, and moral corruption.

Julius Caesar, written around 1599-1600, marks a transition toward major tragedy. Roman history gave Shakespeare a world where divine purpose is unclear. Brutus seeks republican liberty but is proud and stubborn; his character becomes his fate. The play treats history as rise and fall rather than providential progress.

## Major Tragedies

Hamlet ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hamlet)) uses the form of revenge tragedy but turns it inward. The ghost, feigned madness, spying, and play within the play remain, yet Hamlet's moral questioning makes revenge difficult. He meditates on friendship, memory, corruption, desire, habit, and death, making the play far more than a revenge plot.

The great tragedies explore mature human crises. Othello studies sexual jealousy in marriage; King Lear ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/King_Lear)) studies aging, ingratitude, and family cruelty; Macbeth studies ambition and moral collapse; Antony and Cleopatra studies midlife crisis, desire, public duty, and the unstable dream of heroic greatness.

In King Lear ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/King_Lear)), father-daughter bonds become central after the wife is absent from the dramatic picture. Lear rejects Cordelia, Gloucester misjudges Edgar, and both old men suffer through the evil of ungrateful children. The play tests whether goodness can survive in a world where gods seem silent.

Macbeth is especially unsettling because Macbeth understands that ambition is morally wrong and still chooses murder. The Weird Sisters tempt him, Lady Macbeth drives him, but responsibility remains his. Antony and Cleopatra offers a different tragic vision: worldly failure and sexual weakness become mixed with a grand image of lovers seeking freedom from Roman control.

## Romances and Late Works

In the late plays, Shakespeare returned to comedy through romance or tragicomedy. These works present wandering, separation, loss, and eventual reunion. Pericles follows travel, apparent death, threatened daughterhood, and restoration. The Winter's Tale turns jealousy and family destruction toward repentance and recovery.

Cymbeline again uses a virtuous daughter, exile, jealousy, and eventual reconciliation. The Tempest gathers many late concerns in Prospero, a wifeless father and stage-managing figure who educates Miranda, arranges Ferdinand's arrival, confronts betrayal, and finally chooses forgiveness and release.

Henry VIII, probably written with John Fletcher, is both history and romance because it turns Henry's marital crisis and conflict with the papacy toward the providential birth of Elizabeth I. The Two Noble Kinsmen also involved Fletcher and adapts Chaucer's Knight's Tale. Other possible links include Edward III, Sir Thomas More, and the lost Cardenio.

## Reputation and Criticism

Shakespeare's reputation grew through performance, publication, editing, biography, and criticism. Early collectors such as Nicholas Rowe tried to form a connected life from documents and tradition. Later scholars discovered wills, records, and theatrical evidence, gradually replacing legend with archival knowledge.

Critical approaches changed over time. The 18th century admired Shakespeare while often judging him by classical rules. Romantic critics valued imagination and organic genius. Later criticism studied character, imagery, language, theatre conditions, textual variants, politics, gender, psychoanalysis, New Criticism, deconstruction, film, opera, and cultural history.

The lasting result is that Shakespeare remains both a historical playwright of Renaissance London and a writer repeatedly remade by later readers, actors, editors, and audiences. His plays survive because they are rooted in theatrical practice yet open to new questions about power, language, identity, love, family, politics, and mortality.

## Key Events & Developments

- 1564: William Shakespeare was baptized in Stratford-upon-Avon.
- 1582-1585: Shakespeare married Anne Hathaway, and their children Susanna, Hamnet, and Judith were born.
- 1592: Robert Greene's attack shows that Shakespeare was already known in London's literary world.
- 1593-1594: Venus and Adonis and The Rape of Lucrece ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Rape_of_Lucrece)) appeared during plague disruption of theatre.
- 1594 onward: Shakespeare became a major member of the Lord Chamberlain's Men, later the King's Men.

- 1616: Shakespeare died in Stratford.

## Key People

- William Shakespeare ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Shakespeare)): Poet, actor, shareholder, and dramatist whose works became central to world literature.
- Anne Hathaway ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anne_Hathaway_%28wife_of_Shakespeare%29)): Shakespeare's wife and mother of Susanna, Hamnet, and Judith.
- John Shakespeare ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Shakespeare)) and Mary Arden: Shakespeare's parents, linked to Stratford civic life and Warwickshire family status.

- Ben Jonson ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ben_Jonson)): Contemporary dramatist who both criticized and famously praised Shakespeare.
- John Fletcher: Later King's Men playwright and collaborator on late works.
- David Bevington ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Bevington)) and Terence John Bew Spencer: Modern scholarly voices associated with major interpretive sections on Shakespeare.

## Interesting Facts

- No letters written by Shakespeare survive, but a letter addressed to him by Richard Quiney was preserved in Stratford records.
- The First Folio printed about half of the plays for the first time.
- Female roles in Shakespeare's theatre were performed by boys, adding complexity to plays about disguise and gender.
- Quarto and folio versions of some plays differ enough that they may preserve different stages of theatrical life.
- New Place, bought in 1597, showed Shakespeare's financial success and restored family status in Stratford.
