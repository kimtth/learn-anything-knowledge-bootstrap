# Chaucer

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> Geoffrey Chaucer was a poet, courtier, diplomat, and civil servant whose Canterbury Tales uses a pilgrimage frame to bring medieval social types, voices, morality, humour, and narrative experimentation into English verse. His absorption of French, Latin, and Italian models helped establish English as a major literary language.

## Introduction

Geoffrey Chaucer was the outstanding English poet before Shakespeare and one of the central founders of English literary tradition. He was a poet, courtier, diplomat, and civil servant whose greatest work was The Canterbury Tales.

Chaucer's writing is praised for its variety, humour, tolerance, and philosophical depth. He wrote about love, morality, religion, and human folly, and he helped shape English as a literary language.

## Early Life and Court Career

![[attachments/chaucer_001.webp|420]]

*Portrait of Geoffrey Chaucer from Hoccleve's Regiment of Princes*

Chaucer came from a family of London merchants with growing court connections. He appears early in royal service, was taken captive in France during military service, and later served in diplomatic work on the continent. By the 1370s he had become a trusted courtier and a man with a secure place in public administration.

His career included customs work in London, royal grants, diplomatic missions to France, Italy, and elsewhere, and positions that linked him closely to the political life of Edward III and Richard II. These duties gave him access to courts, books, and international culture.

## Literary Formation

Chaucer read deeply in French, Latin, and Italian literature. His Italian journeys brought him into contact with [[Dante]], Petrarch, and Boccaccio, whose works strongly influenced his own development as a poet.

His early and middle poems show his growing skill with dream vision, allegory, narrative complexity, and psychological detail. Works such as Troilus and Criseyde reveal his mastery of tragic love, philosophical reflection, and controlled narration.

## The Canterbury Tales

![[attachments/chaucer_002.webp|420]]

*Ellesmere Chaucer manuscript page with an illustration of Chaucer*

The Canterbury Tales ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Canterbury_Tales)) is Chaucer's greatest achievement. A group of pilgrims gather at the Tabard Inn and agree to tell stories on their way to Canterbury, creating a frame narrative that allows Chaucer to present many voices, styles, and social types.

The work has an unfinished but unified design, dramatic links between tales, and extraordinary variety. Chaucer used the pilgrimage frame to explore human character, social relations, humour, irony, devotion, and the tensions between ideal and ordinary life.

## Later Career and Final Years

Chaucer's later years were politically difficult. He lost office, faced debts and legal problems, and withdrew from active public work. Even so, he continued writing and completed or continued major works in the 1390s.

He died in 1400 and was buried in Westminster Abbey, an honour that marked his growing importance. His death is the end of a career that had already begun to reshape English poetry and prose.

## Reputation and Legacy

His reputation grew steadily after his death, especially because of The Canterbury Tales, which preserved a rich cross-section of medieval life and displayed an unusually flexible and humane imagination.

Chaucer's influence was immense and lasting. Later English poets, translators, scholars, and critics all treated him as a founder of literary English, and his works remained central to the study of medieval and early modern literature.

## Conclusion

Chaucer emerges as a writer whose public career and literary achievement were closely linked. His verse turned courtly, philosophical, and social experience into enduring art, and his place in English literature is foundational.
