## Introduction

![[attachments/anatolia_001.webp|420]]

*Relief map of Anatolia*

Anatolia ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anatolia)), also called Asia Minor, is the peninsula that forms the Asian part of modern Turkey. It lies where Asia and Europe meet and has long been a crossroads of migration, trade, conquest, and cultural exchange.

Geographically, Anatolia is bounded by the Black Sea ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black_Sea)), the Taurus Mountains, the Mediterranean, the Aegean, and the Sea of Marmara. Culturally, it also includes links with the eastern Aegean islands.

Ancient Anatolia's history includes prehistoric settlements, Neolithic farming communities, Chalcolithic villages, Bronze Age cultural provinces, the Hittite empire, Greek coastal cities, Phrygians, Lydians, Persians, Romans, Byzantines, and Seljuq Turks.

## Prehistoric Anatolia

![[attachments/anatolia_002.webp|420]]

*Reconstruction of a Neolithic Catalhoyuk room*

Early evidence includes cave sites and rock art from the Paleolithic. Rising sea levels after the last Ice Age and deep alluvial deposits may have hidden much early settlement evidence.

Yarmburgaz Cave near Istanbul preserves Lower Paleolithic traces, while caves near Antalya contain animal engravings linked to western European Upper Paleolithic art. Rock shelters with layered occupation debris may eventually clarify the transition from cave life to farming villages.

The beginnings of farming were long sought around the Fertile Crescent, but Anatolian excavations changed older ideas that the peninsula was largely empty before the Chalcolithic.

At Hacilar, occupation about 8000 BCE included mud-brick houses with plastered walls and floors. Later phases showed advanced agriculture, pottery, arranged houses, and striking figurines.

Catalhuyuk, occupied from the mid-8th to mid-7th millennium BCE, transformed ideas about Neolithic life. Its houses were built close together, entered from roofs, and sometimes decorated as shrines with horns, animal heads, and murals.

The close-packed roofs created communal space above the houses, while repeated replastering preserved layers of painted designs. Some murals echoed cave art, and the shrines suggest ritual life embedded inside ordinary domestic architecture rather than separated in monumental temples.

## Chalcolithic and Early Bronze Age

The transition to the Chalcolithic was gradual. Painted pottery became a major visible innovation, especially in western Anatolia, south-central Anatolia, and Cilicia.

Settlements such as Hacilar, Can Hasan, and Mersin show defensive architecture, contiguous houses, circuit walls, fortification, and early signs of military organization.

Metallurgy developed slowly but significantly. Copper pins, tools, mace-heads, silver, and a tin-bronze seal show increasing metalworking skill.

The Early Bronze Age began in the mid-4th millennium BCE. Anatolia contained several cultural provinces, including areas represented by Troy, Alisar Huyuk, Alaca Huyuk, Beycesultan, Mersin, and sites in southeastern Anatolia.

Economic growth came from grape cultivation, wine production, the wheel, pottery manufacture, improved transport, seafaring, and advanced metallurgy. Finds such as Priam's Treasure and Alaca Huyuk grave goods show high technical skill.

Early Bronze Age metalworkers used casting in closed molds, lost-wax processes, inlay, soldering, hammering, repousse, granulation, filigree, and even cloisonne. Gold, silver, copper, bronze, electrum, iron, and meteoric iron all appear in elite contexts, showing both local skill and wide access to valued materials.

Early Bronze Age society also shows strong fortification and elite display. Troy had heavy stone walls, mud-brick superstructures, a sloping glacis, and projecting gates. Other sites used ditches, ramparts, radial house plans, citadels, and defended palaces. Weapons, ritual standards, bronze disks, precious vessels, jewelry, and cist graves at Alaca Huyuk suggest ruling families, military display, ancestor ritual, and control over wealth.

Trade helped spread styles across Anatolia ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anatolia)). Wheel-made pottery, two-handled drinking vessels, western architectural forms, metal goods, and imported materials moved between regions. Some scholars once linked destruction levels to Indo-European migration, but wider contact through trade and transport may also explain the growing similarity of styles.

## Middle Bronze Age and Assyrian Trade

The Middle Bronze Age, beginning about 2000 BCE, was prosperous. Assyrian merchants established trading colonies linked to Anatolian cities and mineral wealth.

The main colony, or karum, was at Kultepe, ancient Nesa. Assyrian archives show trade, taxation, transport, prices, debts, intermarriage, and relatively close relations with local Anatolian rulers.

Written records in Akkadian, cylinder seals, palace archives, and merchant documents made this period historically visible in a new way.

The karum at Kanesh functioned almost like a merchant institution, regulating prices, debts, taxes, and transport while coexisting with local rulers. Its archives reveal Assyrian families, Anatolian partners, intermarriage, lawsuits, credit, textiles, metals, and caravan movement. The destruction of Nesa, Acemhoyuk, Hattusas, and other centres ended this trading system, but its documents preserve one of the clearest pictures of Middle Bronze Age Anatolian urban life.

## Hittite Origins

The first evidence of the Hittites in central Anatolia appears in Kultepe tablets containing Indo-European personal names among Assyrian and Hattian names.

The Hittites called their language Nesite, after Nesa, though it is usually called Hittite. Other Indo-European languages in Anatolia included Luwian and Palaic.

Their arrival was probably not a simple military invasion. Evidence suggests peaceful penetration, gradual mixing with indigenous Anatolians, and eventual political dominance.

## Old Hittite Kingdom

The Old Hittite Kingdom lasted roughly from 1650 to 1500 BCE. Hittite memory began with Labarnas and Hattusilis, whose dynasty shifted power toward Hattusas.

Hattusilis expanded from the Kizil River region toward the Lower Land and northern Syria. Campaigns reached Alalakh and areas near the Euphrates.

The Edict of Telipinus later contrasted early prosperity with later disorder and tried to regulate royal succession. Telipinus is usually treated as the last king of the Old Kingdom.

## Middle Kingdom and Empire

![[attachments/anatolia_003.webp|420]]

*Hittite relief at Yazilikaya*

After Telipinus, Hittite power declined during a less documented Middle Kingdom. Syria passed through Hurrian, Mitannian, and Egyptian influence, and the Hittites faced a strong Mitanni state to the south.

In the empire period, Tudhaliyas II conquered Arzawa, Assuwa, Aleppo, and Mitanni territories, while Kaska tribes threatened the north. Hattusas itself was burned during this troubled era.

Suppiluliumas I restored and expanded Hittite power in the 14th century BCE. He strengthened Hattusas, attacked Mitanni, and reasserted influence in Syria.

His rebuilding of Hattusas probably included an expanded wall circuit enclosing more than 300 acres, a sign that imperial recovery required defensive architecture as well as campaigns. He also attacked Syria by a less expected route through Malatya and the Euphrates valley.

The Battle of Kadesh between Hittites and Egypt was militarily inconclusive, but the Hittites maintained Syrian power afterward. Under Hattusilis III, peace with Ramses II produced a famous treaty in 1259 BCE.

At Kadesh, concealed Hittite chariotry struck the Egyptian column as it was making camp near the Orontes. The Egyptians escaped disaster, but Hittite control in Syria survived, which is why the later treaty mattered more than battlefield propaganda.

Queen Puduhepa played an important political role alongside Hattusilis III. The later empire enjoyed periods of prosperity but eventually collapsed around the wider upheavals of c. 1200 BCE.

## After the Hittites

The end of the Hittite empire coincided with disruption by Sea Peoples and wider eastern Mediterranean upheaval. Anatolia's geography made it a bridge between the Greek world and eastern empires.

Greek city-states appeared on the western coast, including Miletus, Priene, and Ephesus. Ionia, Aeolis, and Dorian settlements developed urban leagues, trade, and cultural contact.

The Phrygians occupied much of western and central Anatolia. The Cimmerians later destroyed Gordium, and Lydia rose in western Anatolia with its capital at Sardis.

The Lydians, whose language belonged to the Anatolian branch of Indo-European, were credited by ancient writers with inventing coinage. Their king Croesus became famous for wealth and expansion.

Neo-Hittite states survived in southeastern Anatolia and northern Syria until Assyrian expansion absorbed or destroyed many of them in the 8th century BCE.

## Roman and Byzantine Anatolia

In the later Roman period, Anatolia was divided into provinces and dioceses under the eastern imperial administration based at Constantinople.

By the 4th to early 7th century CE, the peninsula contained 24 provinces grouped mainly in the dioceses of Pontica and Asiana, with some districts tied to Oriens. This administrative network belonged to the praetorian prefecture of the East, headquartered at Constantinople.

Settlement was densest in coastal plains, especially the Aegean, northern, and southern regions. The plateau remained more pastoral, while coastal regions produced cereals, vegetables, vines, and olives.

From the 7th century, Anatolia was transformed by [[War|war]], Arab raids, social dislocation, urban contraction, military reorganization, and Byzantine frontier defense.

## Seljuq Anatolia

Turkmen groups began entering eastern Anatolia from Central Asia by the 10th century. They pressed into Armenian and Byzantine frontier lands as mounted warriors armed with bow and lance.

Great Seljuq rulers redirected Turkmen energies into Anatolia ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anatolia)), gaining land, power, and pressure against Byzantium while limiting disorder in Muslim territories.

The decisive turning point was the Battle of Manzikert in 1071, where Alp-Arslan defeated and captured the Byzantine emperor Romanus IV Diogenes.

After Manzikert, Turkmen settlement expanded widely. Dynasties such as the Mangujakids and Saltuqids appeared in eastern and central regions, and Anatolia gradually became a Turkish and Islamic land.

## Key Events & Developments

- c. 8000 BCE: early Neolithic settlement at Hacilar.
- mid-8th to mid-7th millennium BCE: Catalhuyuk flourished.
- c. 2000 BCE: Assyrian trading colonies linked Anatolia to Mesopotamia.
- c. 1650-1500 BCE: Old Hittite Kingdom developed.
- 1259 BCE: Hattusilis III and Ramses II made a famous peace treaty.
- 1071 CE: Seljuq victory at Manzikert opened Anatolia to wider Turkmen settlement.
