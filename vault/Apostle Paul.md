# Apostle Paul

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> Paul the Apostle was an early Christian missionary and theologian whose letters transformed Christian thought about faith, law, Christ, community, ethics, and the inclusion of Gentiles. His work helped move Christianity from its Jewish origins into a wider Mediterranean world.

## Introduction

Paul the Apostle ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_the_Apostle)), originally Saul of Tarsus, is one of the central figures of the first generation of Christianity and often regarded as the second most important person in Christian history after Jesus. His career combined greatness with a difficult position in his own lifetime: he had to defend his authority against rivals and detractors, and he was probably not honoured as highly by some contemporaries as Peter and James were.

Paul's enduring importance rests chiefly on his surviving letters, which profoundly shaped later Christian theology, ethics, and church life. He was not only a missionary organizer but also one of the early Christian movement's great religious thinkers.

## Sources

The main sources for Paul's life are his own letters and the Acts of the Apostles ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acts_of_the_Apostles)). Of the 13 New Testament letters attributed to him, only seven are accepted as wholly authentic: Romans, 1 Corinthians, 2 Corinthians, Galatians, Philippians, 1 Thessalonians, and Philemon. These are treated as the most reliable evidence for both his life and thought.

Acts is recognized as important but secondary and sometimes inconsistent with the letters, so the picture of Paul rests mainly on the authentic correspondence, using Acts with caution.

## Early Life and Background

Paul was a Greek-speaking Jew from Tarsus in Cilicia. He had learned a manual trade, probably tent making or leatherworking, and this practical skill later shaped his missionary career by allowing him to support himself while traveling. His language and social position suggest that he was not an aristocrat, though neither was he a simple labourer.

Before his conversion Paul belonged to the Pharisees. This helps explain several lasting features of his thought, including belief in life after death, dedication to Jewish traditions, and intense concern with the law. His later Christian theology did not erase his Jewish formation; rather, it transformed it.

## Persecutor and Conversion

Before becoming a Christian missionary, Paul had persecuted followers of Jesus. His own testimony is more reliable here than later narrative expansions. His life changed when, on the road to [[Damascus]], he had a revelation of Jesus as the risen Lord.

This experience convinced Paul that Jesus truly was God's chosen messiah and that Paul himself had been appointed for a mission. After the revelation he went to [[Arabia]], returned to Damascus, and only later met leading figures in Jerusalem. The conversion was not merely a moral change but a revelatory call that determined Paul's entire vocation.

## Mission to the Gentiles

*Rembrandt painting of the Apostle Paul*

Paul believed that God had appointed him specifically as an apostle to the Gentiles. His vision of history was shaped by Jewish expectations that in the final age the nations would come to worship the God of Israel. Paul reversed the expected order: first the Gentiles would be brought in, and then Israel itself would be saved.

Over roughly two decades, he founded churches across [[Syria]], Asia Minor, and parts of [[Europe]], including Corinth. His missionary methods likely combined work, personal contact, and public teaching rather than formal eloquence. His role was not isolated; he worked within a broader Christian missionary movement, but he understood his own calling as unique and divinely assigned.

## Paul and the Jerusalem Apostles

Paul's mission created tension with Christians who thought Gentile converts should become Jews and accept circumcision. A political and theological settlement was reached with leaders in [[Jerusalem]]: Peter would focus on Jews, Paul on Gentiles, and Paul would raise money for the Jerusalem church.

This agreement did not eliminate conflict. Paul remained sharply opposed to missionaries who insisted that Gentiles must adopt Jewish identity markers. His letters to the Galatians especially reveal how fierce that dispute became and how central it was to his self-understanding.

## The Core of Paul's Gospel

Paul's message centred on several linked claims: God sent his Son, the Son was crucified for the benefit of humanity, the Son was raised, and he would return soon to save believers and judge the world. Paul's preaching was therefore both theological and apocalyptic.

Believers were called not only to accept these claims but also to live morally transformed lives in the present. Paul expected very high standards of conduct from his converts, precisely because he believed they already belonged to the new age inaugurated by Christ.

## Churches and Community

Paul's converts were mostly Gentiles, many of whom had formerly worshipped local gods. Conversion required a break with public cults and civic religious life, which left them socially vulnerable and isolated, and this must have been very difficult in cities where public festivals, meals, and social occasions were tied to pagan worship.

As a result, Christian communities had to provide a strong new social world. Paul encouraged mutual support, shared identity, patience under suffering, and reliance on spiritual gifts such as prophecy, healing, and speaking in tongues. Women also played major roles in this missionary network, several of whom are named.

## Moral Teaching

Paul demanded what he regarded as near blamelessness. He believed that believers were already united with Christ and ought therefore to live accordingly. His ethics largely matched the strictest moral expectations of Greek-speaking Jewish communities, especially in sexual conduct.

He opposed prostitution, homosexual activity, sex before marriage, and divorce, while also accepting marital sexual relations and showing a preference for celibacy. Some of these teachings had enormous later influence in Christian and Western moral tradition. Paul also taught obedience to governing authorities, though later political thought often modified or rejected this implication.

## Monotheism and the Spiritual Universe

Paul remained a monotheist in the Jewish sense: the God of Israel was the only true God. Yet he also imagined the universe as populated by many spiritual beings and powers, including angels, demons, rulers, principalities, and Satan. The world was therefore the scene of a cosmic struggle.

Despite the existence of hostile powers, Paul believed that God's final victory was certain. Christ's mission would defeat sin, death, and the forces of darkness. This cosmic outlook gives his theology both its urgency and its grandeur.

## Christology

Paul freely used titles such as Christ and Son of God for Jesus, often treating Christ almost as part of Jesus' name. He did not give a later metaphysical doctrine of Christ's two natures, but he did present Jesus as central to God's saving action.

Some Pauline passages seem to describe Christ as preexistent and heavenly, while others sound more like adoption language focused on resurrection. Paul's thought did not settle these later theological debates, but it provided much of the language and many of the problems that shaped Christian doctrine afterward.

## Faith, Law, and Justification

One central theme is Paul's argument that Gentiles are accepted by God through faith in Christ and not through works of the law. In context, this does not mean a rejection of good actions in favour of mere inward belief. Rather, it means that Gentiles need not become Jews by taking on circumcision and the law as covenant markers.

Paul's language of justification and righteousness by faith is therefore tied to the inclusion of Gentiles in God's people. Later Christianity often broadened or altered this issue, but it must be read in its original missionary setting.

## The Body of Christ

Paul understood believers not only as individuals but as members of the body of Christ. Baptism joined them to Christ's death and life, and communal worship expressed that organic union. Ethical life followed from this identity: members of Christ's body should not unite themselves with immorality or idolatry.
This idea is one of Paul's most creative contributions. It gave a powerful way to think about the church, moral transformation, sacramental life, and spiritual unity.

## The Return of the Lord and Resurrection

Paul expected Jesus to return and to raise the dead. In 1 Thessalonians, he describes the dead in Christ rising and the living greeting the Lord in the air. Yet this teaching created difficulties, especially among converts shaped by Greek ideas of the soul and body.

To address this, Paul taught that the resurrection body would be a spiritual body, not simply ordinary flesh restored. He also sometimes spoke as though believers who died would be with the Lord before the final resurrection. Later Christian doctrine worked to systematize these different strands.

## Achievement and Influence

Paul's influence on Christian history came above all through his letters. They helped shape doctrine about Christ, faith, grace, church, ethics, and the relation between Christianity and the Hebrew Bible. His mission to the Gentiles also helped shift Christianity from a Jewish movement into a largely Gentile religion, though this was not his stated intention.
Paul is one of early Christianity's two greatest theological minds, alongside the author of the Gospel of John. His combination of total commitment, intellectual force, missionary energy, and willingness to suffer gave his words unusual authority for later generations.

## Conclusion
Paul is a Jewish apostle whose revelation of the risen Christ transformed him into the great missionary theologian of Gentile Christianity. He remained deeply shaped by Jewish monotheism and apocalyptic hope, yet he reinterpreted both around Jesus' death and resurrection.

His legacy lies in the churches he founded, the letters he left, and the theological language he bequeathed to Christianity. Faith, union with Christ, the body of Christ, justification, resurrection, and the worldwide mission to the nations all bear the lasting mark of Pauline thought.

## Key Events & Developments

- c. AD 33: Paul's revelation on the road to Damascus changes his life.
- c. mid-30s to mid-50s: Paul carries out major missions among Gentiles.
- A Jerusalem agreement recognizes separate missions to Jews and Gentiles.
- Paul's authentic letters are written during his missionary decades.
- c. AD 62-64: Paul dies, probably in Rome.
