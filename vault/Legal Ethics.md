# Legal Ethics

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> Legal ethics are the professional conduct standards that guide lawyers in representing clients, managing conflicts, charging fees, and serving legal institutions. They developed because specialized legal work requires public trust, accountability, and norms beyond ordinary market judgment.

Legal ethics ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Legal_ethics)) are the conduct principles expected of lawyers in practice. They grew with the legal profession because lawyers needed standards once their work became socially important and difficult for non-specialists to judge.

## Background

Legal specialists emerged when law became too complex for ordinary people to master. Without qualifications or controls, some practitioners charged excessive fees, failed to perform promised work, and delayed or obstructed proceedings.

Governments and courts answered with qualification rules, expulsion from practice, criminal penalties, and professional standards. Written codes are useful because they make duties visible, but ethical principles may also exist through shared professional understanding and bar committee opinions.

Lawyers in democratic systems such as the [[United States of America (United States)|United States]], [[Canada]], the European Union, and [[Japan]] have a dual responsibility: they represent clients, yet also serve the fair administration of justice as officers of the court.

Conflict of interest rules prevent a lawyer from being both for and against a client, undoing earlier work for a client, or using confidential information from a former client. Client consent may help only after full disclosure and may still fail when public interest is harmed.

Confidential communications are generally protected in Anglo-American systems and also appear in Japan, Germany, and Russia. Some systems that stress state interests may require disclosure when the state is considered to benefit.

Lawyer advertising was once widely forbidden because it seemed to lower professional tone and encourage exaggerated claims. In 1977, the U.S. Supreme Court ruled that lawyers could not be barred from advertising fees, leading to revised American ethics rules.

## Fees

Legal fees should be reasonable. Countries have tried to regulate them through statutes, court-imposed fees, advisory schedules, and disciplinary rules; in 1975, the U.S. Supreme Court rejected enforced minimum fee schedules under antitrust law.

The profession also recognizes a duty to serve poor clients, leading to legal aid and public defenders. Contingent fees are common in U.S. negligence cases but are generally prohibited elsewhere and barred in some American matters.

Criminal practice creates special ethical duties. Prosecutors must seek justice, disclose information that may exonerate or mitigate, and avoid unfair tactics; defense lawyers must require proof beyond a reasonable doubt but may not knowingly use false testimony.

## Globalization

[[Globalization and Culture (Globalization, Cultural)|Globalization]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Globalization)) has increased contact between different ethical traditions. U.S.-trained lawyers may face European confidentiality rules for in-house counsel, while work in [[China]] may involve different ideas about a lawyer's public obligations.

International practice and new communication technologies let lawyers advise distant clients across different jurisdictions. The Council of Bars and Law Societies of Europe works toward common principles in the European Union.
