## Introduction

Torts ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tort)) are civil wrongs independent of contract. They include harmful conduct such as physical attack, damage to property, interference with land, harm to reputation, invasion of privacy, and some economic injuries.

The word comes from Latin tortum, meaning twisted or crooked. Different legal systems use different terms, such as unlawful acts, delicts, quasi-delicts, or extra-contractual civil responsibility.

Tort law is mainly concerned with liability for behaviour that the legal order treats as socially unacceptable. Remedies usually involve damages paid to the injured person, though injunctions may sometimes be used.

## Legal Systems and Method

![[attachments/torts_001.webp|420]]

*Historic courtroom interior*

Common-law and civil-law systems often deal with similar facts and reach similar practical results, but they organize tort law differently.

German law tends toward abstraction and system, reflecting Roman-law and university origins. Common law is more case-based and judge-made. French-inspired codes often use broad, manifesto-like provisions.

These differences affect method, but the core problems remain similar: when should one person pay for another's injury, and how should fault, causation, risk, and compensation be defined?

## Functions of Tort Law

![[attachments/torts_002.webp|420]]

*A jury scene in a courtroom*

Tort law has several functions: compensation, deterrence, loss spreading, corrective justice, and sometimes public expression of blame.

Compensation is the most important modern function. Tort law tries to restore the victim, as far as money can, to the position before the injury.

Modern [[Insurance|insurance]] makes compensation easier because it can pay victims without financially destroying defendants. But insurance can also weaken the personal deterrent effect of damages.

## Deterrence

![[attachments/torts_003.webp|420]]

*United States Supreme Court building*

Deterrence aims to reduce accidents by imposing costs on unsafe conduct. Specific deterrence depends on the warning effect of liability, but this is limited when insurance covers the defendant.

Traffic accidents show the limits of tort deterrence. Many harms result from momentary inattention, and no award of damages can fully prevent such lapses.

Guido Calabresi argued for general deterrence: activities should bear their accident costs, allowing people and markets to decide whether the activities are worth those costs.

Economic approaches raise questions about rationality. They assume people can respond to costs in useful ways, but real human behaviour may not always be so calculable.

## Compensation and Loss Spreading

Tort law compensates serious personal injury but can be expensive, slow, and unpredictable. Critics have called it a forensic lottery because results depend on proof, witnesses, lawyers, delay, and finding a solvent wrongdoer.

The welfare state and insurance often provide broader accident compensation than tort law. Some systems have created special schemes, especially for automobile accidents.

Loss spreading shifts the cost of accidents away from a single victim or wrongdoer and distributes it through insurance premiums, prices, taxes, or social programs.

## Negligence

Negligence is the most important modern tort. It usually requires conduct that is careless, unjustified, legally causative of harm, and below the required standard of care.

Legal systems use objective standards such as the reasonable person or bonus pater familias. Courts adjust the standard by considering risk, cost of precautions, social utility, age, skill, and circumstances.

Common-law systems historically hesitated to impose liability for inaction. Some modern statutes and courts have moved toward duties to assist, especially in traffic-accident contexts.

French law and some other systems recognize a broader duty to aid persons in physical danger when help can be given without risk to the rescuer. Such rules may be more educational than frequently litigated.

Good Samaritan rules often encourage help by lowering the standard of care for rescuers, reducing fear of later lawsuits by those helped.

## Causation and Harm

Tort liability requires causation in a legal sense, not merely a factual connection. Courts must decide whether the defendant's conduct is close enough to the injury to justify responsibility.

Rules about remoteness limit compensation. Even when a wrong caused some harm, not every later loss is recoverable if it is too indirect, unforeseeable, or unreasonable.

## Property and Chattels

Tort law protects chattels, or movable property. Owners may recover goods removed from them or claim damages for intentional or negligent damage.

Most modern chattel cases involve damaged movables, such as automobiles. Claims may include repair costs, substitute transport, and reasonable related expenses.

Courts may reject claims that look like pure economic loss rather than property damage. Difficult cases arise when access is blocked, use is delayed, or no physical damage occurs.

Reasonableness remains important. A victim cannot usually replace a damaged economy car with a luxury hire at the wrongdoer's expense.

## Key Events & Developments

- 19th century: civil-law codifications shaped broad approaches to extra-contractual responsibility.
- 20th century: insurance and welfare systems changed tort law's compensation role.
- 1970: Guido Calabresi's The Cost of Accidents gave an influential economic account of accident law.
- 1978: England's Royal Commission examined civil liability and compensation for personal injury.

## Key People and Concepts

- Guido Calabresi: developed a major theory of accident costs and general deterrence.
- Reasonable person: common-law standard for judging negligent conduct.
- Bonus pater familias: civil-law-style figure used to express reasonable care.
- Tortfeasor: a person who commits a tort.

## Interesting Facts

- Tort law covers civil wrongs outside contract, even when the same conduct might also be criminal.
- [[Insurance]] helps victims receive money but can reduce the personal sting of liability.
- Good Samaritan laws often protect rescuers so people are less afraid to help.
