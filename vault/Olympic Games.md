# Olympic Games

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> The Olympic Games are the leading international athletic festival, rooted in ancient Greece and revived in the late nineteenth century. Their modern history includes the International Olympic Committee, Winter Games, professionalization, political protest, commercialization, corruption, boycotts, tragedy, and changing eligibility.

## Introduction

Olympic Games ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Olympic_Games)) are the world's foremost athletic festival, originating in ancient Greece and revived in the late 19th century. Once officially restricted to amateurs, the Games gradually opened to professionals during the 1980s. The Summer Games have included dozens of sports, and the Winter Games, sanctioned in 1924, cover sports played on snow or ice.

## Ancient Origins

Organized athletic contests existed in Greece nearly 3,000 years ago. By the late 6th century BCE, four major classical games were prominent: Olympic, Pythian, Nemean, and Isthmian. The Olympic Games were held every four years at Olympia in honour of Zeus. The first recorded champion was Coroebus of Elis, who won the sprint in 776 BCE. Later events included additional races, wrestling, boxing, equestrian events, and the pentathlon.

The ancient Olympiad ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Olympiad)) became a way to measure time because the festival returned on a fixed four-year cycle between August and September. The earliest race, the stade, covered one length of the track at Olympia, about 192 metres, and later gave English the word stadium. The program grew from one footrace to include the diaulos, longer races, combat sports, and contests that tested both athletic skill and social prestige.

## Modern Revival

![[attachments/olympic_games_001.webp|420]]

*Panathenaic Stadium during the opening of the 1896 Olympic Games*

Pierre, baron de Coubertin led the modern Olympic revival, arguing that international sport could support peace. At a 1894 Paris conference, delegates approved the revival, and the first modern Games opened in Athens in 1896. Dimitrios Vikelas became the first president of the International Olympic Committee ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Olympic_Committee)), and the Games soon became a regular international competition.

The revival did not begin with broad enthusiasm. Coubertin's appeal for international sport first produced little action, and even the later Paris congress supported the plan partly out of courtesy to him. The first modern Games were originally expected for Paris in 1900, but the date and site shifted to Athens in April 1896 after Coubertin and Vikelas overcame Greek political resistance.

## International Olympic Committee

The IOC controls and develops the Olympic Games, maintains their regular celebration, promotes sport, and chooses host cities. During World War I, Coubertin moved IOC headquarters to Lausanne, Switzerland. IOC members are supposed to be independent ambassadors of the committee rather than delegates of their governments. Reforms in 1999 limited membership, clarified categories, imposed retirement rules, and strengthened governance.

The committee is a permanent self-electing body. After the 1999 reforms, it could have up to 115 members, including individual members, active Olympic athletes, national Olympic committee presidents, and international federation presidents. Members serve renewable eight-year terms and normally retire at age 70, while IOC presidents serve an eight-year term with possible four-year renewals.

An executive board manages regular IOC work, while the full committee meets annually and can be called into session when enough members request it.

## Awarding and Governing the Games

The honour of hosting the Olympic Games ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Olympic_Games)) is awarded to a city, not a country. Candidate cities apply with national-government support and promise nondiscrimination, freedom of entry for competitors, and restrictions on political demonstrations in Olympic sites. National Olympic committees organize participation, transport, housing, and support for athletes and must remain autonomous, not-for-profit, and independent of political or commercial control.

Each participating country needs a recognized national Olympic committee, and by the early 21st century there were more than 200 of them. These committees must include national sport federations linked to international federations. Their stated purpose is to develop the Olympic movement, but in practice they also handle the difficult logistics of sending teams to host cities.

## Corruption, Politics, and Commercialization

The Games have repeatedly faced political pressure, including Nazi propaganda in Berlin 1936, Cold War medal rivalry, boycotts, disputes over China and Taiwan, and protests over apartheid and invasions. In 1998, Salt Lake City bribery allegations led to expulsions, resignations, a 50-point reform package, and an independent Ethics Commission. Commercialization grew through television rights, apparel endorsements, sponsorships, and the financially successful Los Angeles Games of 1984.

## Sports, Eligibility, and Mascots

The Olympic program includes summer and winter sports negotiated between the IOC and international federations. Eligibility shifted away from strict amateurism after 1971, and by 1986 federations could decide whether professionals were allowed. Mascots began informally with Schuss at Grenoble 1968 and officially with Waldi at Munich 1972, after which host nations created figures tied to local symbols or culture.

The program has changed repeatedly as sports were added, removed, or reconsidered. In 2005, the IOC voted to remove baseball and softball from the 2012 Games, while rugby and karate failed to gain enough support. Later votes added women's boxing to London 2012 and golf and rugby sevens to the 2016 program, showing that Olympic eligibility applies not only to athletes but also to sports themselves.

## Early Modern Summer Games

The 1896 Athens Games had about 280 male athletes from 12 countries and featured the first modern marathon, won by Spyridon Louis. Paris 1900 and St. Louis 1904 were poorly organized around world fairs, though women first competed in 1900. Athens 1906 introduced the parade of nations but is not officially recognized. London 1908 and Stockholm 1912 improved organization, technology, and international standards.

The first modern marathon followed the legendary route connected with Pheidippides and became the emotional centre of Athens 1896. Paris 1900 suffered from confused schedules and weak venues, yet it also introduced or displayed sports such as football, rowing, equestrian events, golf, rugby, cricket, and croquet. St. Louis 1904 was even more lopsided because most competitors came from the United States and some events were folded into a fairground anthropological exhibition.

## Between the World Wars

Antwerp 1920 introduced the Olympic flag after World War I. Paris 1924 marked a coming of age, with stronger federations and national trials. Amsterdam 1928 added women's track-and-field and gymnastics and debuted the Olympic flame. Los Angeles 1932 created the first Olympic Village and used automatic timing. Berlin 1936 combined advanced media coverage and the torch relay with Nazi propaganda; Jesse Owens won four gold medals.

The interwar Games also reflected exclusion, recovery, and changing gender policy. Antwerp did not invite the defeated countries of [[World Wars (World War I)|World War I]], while Paris showed that international federations were becoming strong enough to standardize rules and raise competition quality. Amsterdam's women's 800-metre controversy delayed longer women's running events for decades, even as women proved they could compete at a high level.

## Postwar and Cold War Summer Games

London 1948 revived the Games after World War II. Helsinki 1952 brought the Soviet Union into Olympic competition. Melbourne 1956 faced boycotts and introduced mixed-national closing ceremonies. Rome 1960 was fully covered by television and featured Abebe Bikila, Wilma Rudolph, and Cassius Clay. Tokyo 1964 used computers and added judo and volleyball.

## Protest, Tragedy, Boycott, and Professionalism

Mexico City 1968 was marked by student killings, Black-power protests, drug testing, and high-altitude records. Munich 1972 was overshadowed by the killing of Israeli team members by Palestinian terrorists. Montreal 1976 faced African boycotts, steroid suspicions, and debt. Moscow 1980 suffered the largest boycott after the Soviet invasion of Afghanistan; Los Angeles 1984 faced communist boycotts but made a large profit. Seoul 1988 saw professionalism expand and doping scandals intensify.

These Games also changed Olympic management: security became tighter after 1972, and Los Angeles showed that sponsorship, television, and private organizing could remake Olympic finance.

## Recent Summer Games

Barcelona 1992 had no boycott, welcomed post-Cold War teams, returned South Africa, and featured the U.S. basketball Dream Team. Atlanta 1996 was privately funded, highly commercialized, and marred by a bombing. Sydney 2000 highlighted Aboriginal athlete Cathy Freeman. Athens 2004 returned the Games to Greece. Beijing 2008 showcased China's global ambitions, Michael Phelps's eight golds, and Usain Bolt's sprint records. London 2012 became the first city to host three modern Games.

## Winter Games

The first Winter Olympics were recognized from Chamonix 1924, originally International Winter Sports Week. Early Winter Games developed figure skating, speed skating, skiing, bobsled, skeleton, and ice hockey traditions. St. Moritz 1928, Lake Placid 1932, Garmisch-Partenkirchen 1936, St. Moritz 1948, Oslo 1952, Squaw Valley 1960, Innsbruck 1964, and Grenoble 1968 expanded technology, television, women's events, and winter-sport specialization.

Early winter competition often turned on weather, equipment, and eligibility rules. St. Moritz 1928 was disrupted by warm foehn winds, Lake Placid 1932 struggled with Depression-era costs and speed-skating format disputes, and Garmisch-Partenkirchen 1936 became another stage for Nazi display. Chamonix introduced young Sonja Henie, while later Games made figures such as Clas Thunberg, Thorleif Haug, Hjallis Andersen, and Jean-Claude Killy central to Winter Olympic memory.

Later Winter Games also became stages for politics, commercialization, and changing sport programs. Sapporo 1972, Innsbruck 1976, Lake Placid 1980, Sarajevo 1984, Calgary 1988, Albertville 1992, Lillehammer 1994, Nagano 1998, Salt Lake City 2002, Turin 2006, Vancouver 2010, and Sochi 2014 reflected expanding television audiences, new snow and ice events, professional athletes, national spectacle, and increasingly complex security and finance.

## Athletic Symbols and Records

The Games repeatedly produced symbolic performances that outgrew sport itself. Paavo Nurmi, Emil Zatopek, Fanny Blankers-Koen, Abebe Bikila, Nadia Comaneci, Carl Lewis, Michael Johnson, Cathy Freeman, Michael Phelps, and Usain Bolt became markers of eras. Olympic records also tracked changes in timing, training, medicine, media, altitude, professionalism, and technology.

## Tensions in Olympism

Olympism combines international idealism with recurring tensions. The Games seek peaceful competition and global participation, yet they have been shaped by nationalism, discrimination, propaganda, terrorism, doping, gender policy, media pressure, corporate sponsorship, and host-city debt. Their history shows that Olympic sport is never isolated from world politics, economics, and culture.

The continuing expansion of Olympic participation also changed who could compete. Women's events widened from a few early sports to track, gymnastics, boxing, wrestling, weightlifting, pole vault, football, and more. New nations entered as empires dissolved, apartheid ended, and former Soviet and Yugoslav republics appeared under their own flags. The Olympic program kept evolving with each host cycle.

## Key Events & Developments

- 776 BCE: Coroebus of Elis became the first recorded Olympic champion.
- 1894: The IOC was created at Paris.
- 1896: The first modern Olympics opened in Athens.
- 1924: Winter Games were sanctioned.
- 1936: Berlin introduced the torch relay and large-scale political spectacle.
- 1986: Professional eligibility decisions moved to sport federations.
- 1999: IOC reforms followed corruption allegations.
