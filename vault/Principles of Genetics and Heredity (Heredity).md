# Principles of Genetics and Heredity (Heredity)

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> Heredity explains both the continuity of species and individual variation through genes, chromosomes, gene-environment interaction, and population change. The account connects Mendelian patterns, chromosomal variation, molecular genetics, and evolutionary mechanisms while distinguishing inherited genotype from environmentally shaped phenotype.

## Introduction

Heredity ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heredity)) is the sum of biological processes by which characteristics pass from parents to offspring. It explains both the constancy of a species across generations and the variation among individuals within that species. These two features are not opposites; both arise from genes.

A species has a characteristic set of genes, which provides continuity. Individuals vary because genes can occur in different forms. The set of genes inherited from both parents is the genotype, while the phenotype is the visible and functional result of those genes interacting with internal and external environments.

The genotype remains largely constant through life, but the phenotype changes as environments change. Genetic study therefore asks how much of an observable trait comes from gene pattern and how much comes from environment. This is the biological basis of the nature-nurture problem.

## Misconceptions About Heredity

Older ideas about [[Inheritance and Succession (Inheritance)|inheritance]] included acquired traits, prepotency, maternal impressions, and telegony. Jean-Baptiste Lamarck is associated with the idea that acquired traits could be inherited, but modern genetics rejects this. Genes can be changed by mutagens or selection, but acquired physique or intellect is not passed directly to children.

Beliefs such as telegony, which claimed that earlier mates could affect later offspring, go back to Aristotle and survived into animal breeding. Even Darwin discussed an alleged zebra-horse case in 1868. Modern evidence shows that such claims are incompatible with the mechanisms of heredity.

## Allelic Interactions

Mendelian inheritance is often more complex than simple dominance and recessiveness. In four-o'clock plants or snapdragons, red and white varieties can produce pink F1 hybrids. When those hybrids are crossed, red, pink, and white flowers appear in a 1:2:1 ratio, showing incomplete dominance rather than blending inheritance.

Dominance ranges from complete dominance to incomplete dominance and overdominance, in which the heterozygote is more extreme than either homozygote. Codominance occurs when the heterozygote expresses both alleles, as in the human MN blood group system.

Complementation is interaction between nonallelic genes. Two deaf parents may have hearing children if each parent's deafness is caused by mutation in a different gene. The child is heterozygous at both genes and hears normally, so complementation can test whether mutations are alleles of the same gene.

## Polygenic Inheritance and Heritability

Many traits vary continuously rather than in clear categories. Milk yield, egg-laying capacity, stature, head shape, blood pressure, and intelligence can range across degrees and are often strongly affected by environment. Such traits are usually polygenic, controlled by many genes of small effect.

Heritability asks how much observed diversity is due to genetic variation and how much to environment. In plants and animals this can be tested by raising related organisms in different environments or different genotypes in the same environment. In humans, the problem is harder because controlled crosses are impossible and environments are socially complex.

The general conclusion is that nearly every physical, physiological, or behavioural trait depends partly on genes and partly on environment. Similar genotypes tend to resemble one another more than different genotypes in the same environment, but similar genotypes can develop differently in different environments.

## Chromosomes and Genes

*Diagram of meiosis stages showing chromosome separation*

Gregor Mendel proposed particulate inheritance without knowing the physical basis. Modern biology shows that genes reside on chromosomes. Mitosis gives daughter cells a full copy of hereditary material, while meiosis produces gametes with one member of each allele pair.

Chromosomes meet the requirements for carrying genes because they replicate and are passed to daughter cells. In meiosis, homologous chromosomes pair and separate, matching Mendel's first law. Walter S. Sutton observed this in grasshopper sperm formation in 1902.

Each species has a characteristic diploid chromosome number: humans have 46, house mice 40, Drosophila 8, corn 20, tomatoes 24, and potatoes 48. Gametes carry the haploid number, half the diploid set, and fertilization restores the double dose.

Recombination during meiosis allows genetic mapping. Crossovers exchange chromosome segments and create new allele combinations. Human chromosome mapping was difficult because experimental crosses cannot be made, but recombinant DNA technology later mapped hundreds of genes and linkages.

## Sex Linkage

In many animals, sex chromosomes are X and Y in males and two X chromosomes in females. Sperm carry either X or Y, while eggs carry X. X-bearing sperm produce XX females, and Y-bearing sperm produce XY males.

Genes on the X chromosome show sex-linked inheritance. Because the Y chromosome carries far fewer genes, recessive alleles on a male's X chromosome can be expressed as if dominant. Morgan's 1910 work on white eyes in Drosophila became the classic example of this crisscross inheritance.

## Chromosomal Aberrations

Chromosome sets are usually stable, but errors can alter chromosome structure or number. These changes often cause disease, sterility, or death, though rarely they create adaptive opportunities and contribute to evolution.

A deletion is loss of part of a chromosome and is usually serious because it removes genetic material and creates imbalance. In humans, cri-du-chat syndrome is caused by deletion at the tip of the short arm of chromosome 5, producing a catlike cry, severe intellectual disability, and small head size.

A duplication creates an extra copy of a chromosome region. It can be harmful because of imbalance, but if duplicated material becomes stable, one copy may acquire new function through mutation. Inversions rotate a chromosome segment 180 degrees; they may have little direct effect but can produce abnormal meiotic products.

Translocations move segments between nonhomologous chromosomes. A carrier may have a complete gene set and be viable, but meiosis often produces gametes with deletions or duplications, making translocation heterozygotes semisterile.

## Polyploids and Aneuploids

Polyploidy means additional whole chromosome sets. Triploids have three sets and are usually sterile because chromosomes cannot pair evenly, which is why triploid watermelons are seedless. Tetraploids and other even polyploids can be fertile if chromosomes pair two by two.

Hybrid plants can become fertile if chromosome sets duplicate, creating allotetraploids. Bread wheat is hexaploid because of natural hybridizations and chromosome doubling. Polyploidy is common in plant evolution but lethal or nearly lethal in humans.

Aneuploidy is an abnormal number of chromosomes that is not a whole set. It often arises through nondisjunction during meiosis. Down syndrome results from trisomy 21. Turner syndrome involves females with one X chromosome, while Klinefelter syndrome involves males with an XXY pattern or related sex-chromosome variants.

## Molecular Genetics

Molecular genetics explains gene structure and function at the DNA level. A gene is a functional chromosome region that can make a transcript when regulatory signals are present. Genes include transcribed DNA and adjacent control regions needed for correct developmental expression.

During transcription, RNA polymerase separates DNA strands and synthesizes RNA from one template strand. In eukaryotes, RNA polymerase I makes ribosomal RNA, RNA polymerase III makes transfer RNA and small RNAs, and RNA polymerase II makes messenger RNA for protein-coding genes.

Translation uses ribosomes to read mRNA codons and build amino acid chains. Transfer RNA carries specific amino acids to ribosomes. Proteins used inside the same cell are made on free ribosomes, while exported proteins are made on ribosomes attached to the endoplasmic reticulum and processed through the Golgi apparatus.

## Repetitive and Extranuclear DNA

Eukaryotic genomes contain much repetitive DNA. Some repeats occur in gene families, tandem repeats, dispersed repeats, satellite DNA near centromeres, microsatellites, minisatellites, and transposons. Many repeats have unknown function, but transposons can copy themselves and move to new genome locations before becoming inactive.

Not all hereditary DNA is nuclear. Extranuclear DNA occurs in mitochondria and chloroplasts. Chloroplast DNA contains genes involved in photosynthesis and organelle protein synthesis. Mitochondrial DNA contains genes involved in ATP production and mitochondrial protein synthesis. Bacterial plasmids can carry useful genes such as antibiotic resistance.

## Heredity and Evolution

Charles Darwin and Alfred Russel Wallace placed variation and natural selection at the centre of evolution, but 19th-century science did not yet know the genetic mechanism. Modern genetics explains evolution as changes in allele frequencies through mutation, selection, nonrandom mating, random change, and other forces.

Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium, formulated by Godfrey Harold Hardy and Wilhelm Weinberg in 1908, shows that populations do not automatically change allele frequencies. If mating is random, populations are large, and selection or other forces are absent, genotype frequencies settle at p2, 2pq, and q2.

Selection changes allele frequencies when genotypes differ in fitness, meaning ability to produce fertile offspring. Dominant harmful alleles are exposed to selection efficiently, while recessive harmful alleles can hide in heterozygotes. Mutation is the ultimate source of hereditary variation, and mutational pressure can balance selection.

Nonrandom mating also changes genotype proportions. Inbreeding increases homozygosity and can expose deleterious recessive alleles inherited from common ancestors. Outbreeding increases variation and can provide evolutionary flexibility, while sexual selection favours traits that help individuals obtain mates.

## Microevolution, Synteny, Polyploidy, and Human Evolution

Microevolution is small-scale evolutionary change in natural populations. Examples include melanic peppered moths in industrial areas, resistance to DDT, warfarin, and myxomatosis, habitat-adapted Caribbean anoles, Galapagos finch beak changes, and the apple maggot's shift from hawthorns to apples.

Synteny is the conservation of blocks of genes in the same relative positions across related species. Humans share syntenic gene blocks with other mammals such as apes, cows, and mice. Studying synteny shows how genomes are cut and pasted during evolution.

Genomic analysis shows that polyploidy has been important in evolution, especially plants. Spontaneous doubling after hybridization can create fertile new species. Wheat evolved through unions of wild grasses and chromosome doubling.

Human evolution is supported by DNA comparison. Humans and chimpanzees are about 99 percent identical at the DNA sequence level, and gene trees place humans and apes on closely related branches. Fossils such as Australopithecus, Homo habilis, Homo erectus, and Homo sapiens show intermediate stages in hominin evolution.

## Core Equations

Allele frequencies in a two-allele system satisfy:

$$p+q=1$$

For a two-allele model, the two allele frequencies add up to the whole population.

A classic Hardy-Weinberg genotype distribution is:

$$p^2+2pq+q^2=1$$

Hardy-Weinberg proportions describe expected genotype frequencies when allele frequencies are p and q under idealized assumptions.

## Key Events & Developments

- 1902: Walter S. Sutton connected chromosome behaviour with Mendelian inheritance.
- 1908: Hardy and Weinberg formulated population equilibrium.
- 1910: Thomas Hunt Morgan described sex-linked inheritance in Drosophila.
- 1868: [[Darwin]] still discussed telegony, later rejected by genetics.
- 1996: Genomic and cloning-era techniques reflected the expansion of heredity research into modern biotechnology.

## Interesting Facts

- Identical twins are the exception to the rule that no two individuals have exactly the same genetic traits.
- Triploid watermelons are seedless because odd chromosome sets disrupt meiosis.
- Down syndrome is caused by an extra chromosome 21.
- Microsatellite DNA consists of short tandem repeats spread through genomes.
- Inbreeding can reveal harmful recessive alleles by increasing homozygosity.
