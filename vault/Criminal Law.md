# Criminal Law

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> Criminal law defines offenses against public order and establishes the rules for accusation, trial, defenses, and penalties. It coordinates police, prosecutors, courts, and correctional sanctions.

## Introduction

Criminal law defines offenses, regulates arrest and trial, and sets penalties for convicted offenders. It differs from tort law because a crime is treated as an offense against the public, even when the immediate victim is an individual.

Modern criminal law is only one part of social control. Family, school, religion, office rules, police regulation, and civil remedies all help organize conduct, but criminal law is the system that uses public authority to punish and deter serious wrongdoing.

## Principles of criminal law

The traditional view treated crime as moral wrong that deserved retribution. The modern view, shaped by Enlightenment writers such as Beccaria, Montesquieu, [[Voltaire]], and Bentham, puts more emphasis on prevention, public protection, and the reform of offenders.

Criminal law has steadily moved toward clearer codes, fairer procedures, and more rational sentencing. Even so, the legal systems of different countries still vary in how they define offenses and in how much weight they give to punishment, correction, and social defense.

## Legality

The principle of legality is the cornerstone of criminal law. It means that there can be no crime without law, that criminal statutes should be interpreted strictly, that they should not be applied retroactively, and that they should give fair warning through clear language.

Legality limits judicial creativity and supports the rule of law. At the same time, the law still has to be interpreted in a practical way, so courts often balance strict wording against the evident purpose of the statute.

Responsibility concerns the mental condition needed for criminal liability. Serious mental disorder may excuse a defendant, but legal tests differ widely, and psychiatrists often find it difficult to translate medical knowledge into legal categories.

The Model Penal Code, the older M'Naghten rule, and related doctrines of diminished responsibility all address this. The key issue is how to decide when a person lacks enough control or understanding to deserve full criminal liability.

## Conspiracy

Conspiracy ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Criminal_conspiracy)) is usually defined in common law as an agreement by two or more people to commit an unlawful act or to use unlawful means. This idea is broad and sometimes uncertain, especially in Anglo-American law.

Civil-law systems usually treat conspiracy more narrowly. Continental law tends to punish group conduct mainly when the offense itself is committed or when the agreement is tied to state security.

## Attempt

Attempt ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Attempt)) is one of the most important inchoate offenses because guilt can exist even when the planned crime is not completed. The hard boundary between preparation and attempt shows why that line matters for punishment.

Impossibility, voluntary desistance, and the wide range of approaches in different legal systems also matter. The main point is that criminal law often punishes dangerous intention before the final harm occurs.

## Modern reform and comparison

Criminal codes were revised in many countries during the 20th century. The Model Penal Code influenced reforms in the United States, while Europe and other regions also modernized rules on theft, homicide, probation, and responsibility.

The comparison makes clear that there is no single criminal law model. Even where legal systems differ in detail, they all try to balance fairness, social protection, and the need to define crime with enough precision to govern conduct.

## Key Events & Developments

- medieval common law: Criminal law grows out of royal justice, local custom, and the authority of the courts.
- 18th century: Codification and reform ideas begin to replace older, harsher practices.
- modern period: Criminal law becomes more procedural, with stronger rules for evidence, defense, and appeal.

## Key People

- Sir Edward Coke ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sir_Edward_Coke)): Helped define the common-law tradition of criminal justice.
- William Blackstone ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Blackstone)): His legal writing shaped later understanding of criminal law.
- Cesare Beccaria ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cesare_Beccaria)): Influenced criminal-law reform through arguments for proportionate punishment.
- Judges and lawyers: They interpret criminal law and apply its rules in court.

## Interesting Facts

- Criminal law ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Criminal_law)) deals with offenses against the state or public order, not only private harm.
- Procedure matters as much as the definition of offenses.
- In modern systems, guilt must usually be shown with strong proof before punishment can follow.
