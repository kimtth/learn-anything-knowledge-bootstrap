# Development of Human Behaviour (Human Behaviour)

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> Human behaviour develops through the interaction of biological maturation, learning, family, culture, emotion, cognition, and social experience across the life course.

## Introduction

![[attachments/human_behaviour_003.webp|420]]

*Graphic showing stages in child development*

Human development is the changing capacity for physical, mental, and social activity across the life span, but the first 12 years matter most, because growth is then fastest and the effects of change are easiest to see. Development is a series of linked phases, from prenatal life to infancy, childhood, adolescence, adulthood, and old age, and it stresses that a child is not shaped by one cause alone. Theories by Freud, Erikson, and Piaget all try to explain how children move from early instincts and simple reactions toward more stable thought and behaviour, while the broader debate about nature and nurture asks how much comes from inheritance and how much comes from experience. The key idea is that children actively develop through their own growth, their relationships, and their environment, so development is always a mix of biology, learning, and social influence.

## Perception

Perceptual development begins very early, because even the fetus and newborn can respond to touch, taste, smell, light, and sound. In the first months of life, infants become much better at seeing and hearing, and they quickly show special attention to human faces, facial expressions, and voices. Babies do not simply receive the world passively; they also sort and compare information, such as different sounds, smells, and sights, in ways that grow more precise over time. By the end of infancy, perception is already helping the child recognize people, follow movement, and notice the important patterns that make social life possible.

## Judgment

![[attachments/human_behaviour_001.webp|420]]

*Infant in a visual cliff experiment testing depth perception*

Very young infants can make surprisingly complex judgments about distance, shape, movement, and danger. They do not only react to a stimulus; they use earlier sensory experience to guess what an object is and whether it can be reached or should be avoided. The visual cliff example shows this clearly, because children begin to avoid apparent depth when crawling starts and they can link sight with body position and safety. Judgment is therefore not a late skill but an early sign that infants are already building practical understanding from [[Human Perception (Perception)|perception]] and movement.

## Infant Memory

Infant memory improves quickly during the first year, first through recognition and then through recall. At first, babies remember only for a very short time, but soon they can recognize objects, actions, and familiar events after longer delays, and they begin to remember where something was hidden and how an action caused a result. This growth supports object permanence and makes it possible for the child to connect the present with the recent past. This change is linked to brain maturation, showing memory as a foundation for anticipation, learning, and early social expectation.

## Vocalizations

The earliest infant sounds are cries, but by about two months cooing begins, followed later by babbling and then by meaningful words. At first, vocal sounds often express excitement or discomfort, and environmental influence is limited, but over time the child’s sounds become more shaped by the voices and responses of other people. The first year also brings rapid physical growth, when body and brain develop quickly and motor skills appear in a clear order, such as reaching, grasping, sitting, and crawling. This combination of sound, movement, and growth shows that early development is a whole system, not a set of separate abilities.

## Attachment

Attachment is the strong emotional bond that forms between an infant and the main caregiver. It is one of the most important achievements of early personality development because it gives the child food, comfort, protection, and emotional security, while also teaching the child that other people respond in stable ways. A secure attachment makes children less fearful in unfamiliar situations and more likely to explore with confidence, while weak or unstable care can lead to less social responsiveness and later emotional difficulty. The social smile and the child’s growing preference for particular people show how attachment becomes a core social relationship in the first years of life.

## Temperament

Temperament refers to the child’s basic style of response, including fearfulness, activity level, irritability, and readiness to adapt to new situations. Some traits are probably tied to inherited differences in brain chemistry, but many early differences do not last unchanged into later life. One lasting pattern is the contrast between inhibited and uninhibited children: inhibited children are shy and restrained in unfamiliar settings, while uninhibited children are more open, sociable, and spontaneous. Temperament matters because it shapes how children meet challenge, how they react to stress, and how their early style interacts with caregiver behaviour and social experience.

## Language

Language grows quickly after the first birthday, as children move from sounds to words and then to short sentences. Children understand many words before they can say them, and that early speech is often compressed, with one word or two words standing for a longer adult sentence. As vocabulary expands, children learn grammar, sentence order, and more complex expression, often with little direct teaching. Language depends on both biology and interaction: children need the capacity for speech, but they also need rich contact with speakers who answer, encourage, and shape their attempts to communicate. Language is therefore a social skill as well as a mental one.

## Memory in School-Age Children

Later memory development is described mainly in school-age children, where working memory becomes larger, recall becomes faster, and children get better at choosing what is worth remembering. Younger children often rely on recognition, but older children become more skilled at recall, strategy, rehearsal, and organization, which lets them handle more information at once. They also learn that meaning matters, so they can store and retrieve information more effectively when they group ideas, use patterns, or build on familiar knowledge. In this sense, memory is not just storage; it is an active thinking process that improves as children learn how to learn.

## Childhood Personality Problems

Common childhood personality problems divide into internalizing problems, such as anxiety, withdrawal, and fearfulness, and externalizing problems, such as disobedience, aggression, and property destruction. Some children are more vulnerable because of temperament, while others develop problems because of harsh, inconsistent, or permissive parenting and stressful surroundings. Aggression becomes more serious in later childhood than it is in the preschool years, and very aggressive children are more likely to carry these patterns into adulthood. The main message is that behaviour problems are shaped by both inner disposition and family environment, so prevention and guidance need both care and consistency.

## Adolescence

The adolescence section describes puberty as a period of rapid change in the body, thoughts, feelings, and social relations. Boys and girls move through prepubescent, pubescent, and postpubescent phases as height, weight, muscle, fat distribution, and sexual characteristics change. These bodily changes do more than alter appearance; they also force the young person to rethink the self, relationships, and future roles. Adolescence is a time when development becomes especially visible because the child must now manage a new body and a new place in society, and that makes identity a central concern.

## Adolescent Cognition

![[attachments/human_behaviour_002.webp|420]]

*Jean Piaget speaking in Ann Arbor*

Adolescent thinking becomes more abstract, logical, and experimental than the thinking of childhood. This links to Piaget’s formal-operational stage, in which adolescents can reason about possibilities, test hypotheses, and think about their own thinking. They can solve problems more systematically, imagine several outcomes at once, and separate reality from assumption in a more mature way. This shift matters because cognition now supports science-like reasoning, social reflection, and the ability to compare ideas instead of just handling immediate facts. It is a clear example of how mental growth changes the whole style of behaviour.

## Personality

Adolescence also brings a major personality challenge, because the young person must form a coherent identity while physical and social expectations are changing. The identity crisis is a serious but normal task: the adolescent has to decide who he or she is, what values matter, and what social role to adopt. This requires balancing personal wishes with the demands of family and society, and the result is not just self-description but commitment to a life direction. When the process works well, the adolescent gains a more stable sense of self; when it fails, confusion and uncertainty grow. Identity in this stage is therefore a psychosocial achievement, not just a private feeling.

## Cognition in Later Adulthood

In later adulthood, cognition changes more slowly, and a useful distinction separates learning speed from memory for meaning. Older adults often learn more slowly than younger adults, especially when tasks move quickly, but they can do better when they are allowed to control the pace or when the task gives them clear structure. A further distinction separates primary memory from secondary memory: short, immediate processing stays relatively strong, while organizing and retrieving meaning from larger amounts of information becomes harder with age. At the same time, older adults often keep strong knowledge about the world and can remember general information well, which shows that aging does not mean simple loss but a shift in how mental work is done.

## Conclusions

Human development is unified even when we study it in separate parts. Physical, perceptual, cognitive, linguistic, personality, and social growth all influence one another, and none of them can be understood alone. Development comes from the long interaction of biology, experience, and culture, and that interaction produces both continuity and change across the life span. The main aim of developmental study is to describe and explain where behaviour comes from, how it stays stable, and how it changes over time, while always remembering that human growth is a complex whole rather than a set of isolated parts.

## Key Events & Developments

- early psychology: Human behaviour is explained through instinct, environment, and learning.
- 20th century: Developmental psychology and ethology show that behaviour grows through both biology and experience.
- modern period: Researchers study infancy, family life, culture, and cognition as parts of behaviour.
