# Biblical Literature and Its Critical Interpretation (Biblical Literature)

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> Biblical literature encompasses the Hebrew Bible, intertestamental writings, the New Testament, and related apocrypha across genres including law, prophecy, poetry, wisdom, gospel, and apocalypse. Its canon formation, textual witnesses, translations, and critical interpretation show how Jewish and Christian communities preserved sacred memory while profoundly shaping Western worship, ethics, languages, and literary imagination.

## Introduction

Biblical literature ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biblical_canon)) comprises four bodies of written works: the Old Testament writings according to the Hebrew canon; intertestamental works, including the Old Testament Apocrypha; the New Testament writings; and the New Testament Apocrypha.

The range of biblical literature includes law, historical narrative, prophecy, poetry, wisdom, gospel, letter, apocalypse, translation, liturgy, and interpretation. Its importance lies both in religious belief and in the way these writings shaped cultural memory, ethical ideals, language, political thought, and literary imagination.

## In Judaism

The Hebrew Bible ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hebrew_Bible)) helped the Jewish people survive conquest, exile, and the loss of political independence after the fall of Israel in 722 BCE and Judah in 587/586 BCE. Its law, history, prophecy, and poetry preserved memory, shaped worship, organized communal life, and sustained Jewish identity after many communities remained outside Palestine.

After the Temple in [[Jerusalem]] was destroyed in 70 CE, Jewish religion could no longer centre on priestly sacrifice. Scripture, synagogue reading, prayer, teaching, family practice, and calendar observance became portable forms of religious continuity across the Diaspora.

## In Christianity

The Hebrew Bible ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hebrew_Bible)) is foundational for Christianity as well as Judaism. The New Testament grew from its language, stories, theology, covenant ideas, and moral vision; without the Old Testament, the figure of Jesus and the early Christian movement could not be understood in the same way.

The Old Testament ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_Testament)) supplied Christianity with themes of creation, promise, judgment, deliverance, covenant, kingdom, and final hope. Early Christians used these writings to interpret Jesus, shape worship, defend doctrine, and connect the new movement with Israel's sacred past.

## Influences

Biblical literature ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biblical_canon)) carried its ideas about God, humanity, creation, justice, history, and hope into the major languages and cultures of the West. Greek, Latin, German, English, and other translations shaped worship, theology, literature, political ideals, moral assumptions, and the development of national languages.

The Septuagint made Jewish Scripture available in the Hellenistic world and helped provide the language of the New Testament and early Christian theology. The Latin Bible shaped Western thought for a thousand years, while vernacular translations such as Luther's German Bible and the English Authorized Version influenced national languages and literary style.

Biblical ideas also shaped nonreligious Western assumptions about human dignity, justice, the claims of widows, orphans, strangers, and the poor, and the idea that governments and societies can be judged by moral standards. Western literature repeatedly used biblical characters, plots, symbols, and views of crisis, choice, suffering, and hope.

## Old Testament Canon, Texts, and Versions

The Old Testament canon developed as a recognized body of sacred writings and as a standard for faith and practice. The Hebrew canon became known as TaNaKh, from Torah, Neviim, and Ketuvim, while Christian traditions arranged and counted many of the same writings differently.

The Torah ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Torah)) contains Genesis, Exodus, Leviticus, Numbers, and Deuteronomy. The Neviim include the Former Prophets and Latter Prophets, and the Ketuvim include Psalms, Proverbs, Job, the Five Megillot, Daniel, Ezra-Nehemiah, and Chronicles. The Hebrew count of 24 books differs from many Christian counts because several scrolls are divided differently.

Canon formation was gradual, communal, and liturgical. Writings became authoritative through public reading, teaching, copying, citation, and use in worship, not simply through one council or decree. Judaism eventually distinguished Torah, Prophets, and Writings, while Christian churches inherited the Septuagintal order and often included deuterocanonical books between Old and New Testaments. These differences matter because they shape how readers encounter law, history, prophecy, wisdom, and apocalypse.

## The Book of the Twelve

The Book of the Twelve ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Twelve_Minor_Prophets)) gathered the shorter prophetic books on one scroll: Hosea, Joel, Amos, Obadiah, Jonah, Micah, Nahum, Habakkuk, Zephaniah, Haggai, Zechariah, and Malachi. In the Hebrew canon this collection counted as one book, while Christian Bibles usually present the prophets separately.

## The Torah

The Torah ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Torah)), or Pentateuch, was accepted as the most revered part of the Hebrew canon, but its canonization must be distinguished from the earlier formation of its diverse legal and narrative materials. The discovery and public reading of a Torah book under Josiah in 622 BCE and later readings in the period of Nehemiah show how law, covenant, and communal instruction became central.

The Torah's authority joined story and law. Creation, ancestry, Exodus, wilderness, revelation, and Moses' final speeches explain who Israel is, while legal collections define worship, justice, purity, social responsibility, and covenant loyalty. Critical scholarship asks how older narrative, priestly, legal, and Deuteronomic materials were combined; religious tradition asks how the completed Torah functions as instruction for a people before God.

## The Neviim

The Neviim, or Prophets, were probably gathered and ordered under the influence of the Pentateuch as a sacred model. The Babylonian Exile in 587/586 BCE and the later restoration increased the prestige of prophetic figures and encouraged preservation of their teachings, while the absence of Greek influence suggests the prophetic canon closed before the Hellenistic period.

In the Hebrew arrangement, the Former Prophets are not mainly prediction but interpreted history. Joshua, Judges, Samuel, and Kings tell the story of land, monarchy, temple, apostasy, reform, and disaster through a Deuteronomic theology of covenant. The Latter Prophets preserve poetic oracles, symbolic actions, laments, lawsuits, visions, and hopes for restoration. Together they make history morally intelligible without reducing it to simple chronicle.

## The Ketuvim

The Ketuvim ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ketuvim)), or Writings, formed later and more gradually than the Torah and Prophets. The lack of a stable early name, debates over books such as Esther, Ecclesiastes, Proverbs, and Song of Songs, and discussions around 100 CE show that this third division remained fluid before becoming a closed collection.

The Writings gathered the Bible's broadest literary range. Psalms shaped prayer and Temple memory; Proverbs, Job, and Ecclesiastes tested wisdom against experience; the Megillot connected story and poetry to festival reading; Daniel joined court tale and apocalypse; Ezra-Nehemiah remembered restoration and reform; and Chronicles retold Israel's past from a priestly, Davidic, and Temple-centred perspective. Their diversity shows that biblical authority included debate, lament, praise, and questioning.

## The Samaritan Pentateuch

The importance of the recension known as the Samaritan Pentateuch ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samaritan_Pentateuch)) lies in the fact that it constitutes an independent Hebrew witness, written in a late and developed form of the paleo-Hebrew script.

The Samaritan Pentateuch ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samaritan_Pentateuch)) matters for textual criticism because it can be compared with the Masoretic text, the Septuagint, and Qumran fragments. Many of its variants are spelling, grammatical, interpretive, or sectarian, but it still preserves evidence for a Hebrew textual tradition outside the later Jewish mainstream.

## The Aramaic Targums

The Aramaic Targums arose because Aramaic became the official language of the Persian Empire in the 5th and 6th centuries BCE and later a common Jewish language. Synagogue translators first rendered Torah and prophetic readings orally, but repeated liturgical use gradually fixed phrases that were later written down.

## The Septuagint (LXX)

The Septuagint began with the Greek translation of the Pentateuch, traditionally explained by the Letter of Aristeas and connected with Ptolemy II Philadelphus. Although that account is historically unreliable, the translation probably began in Alexandria around the middle of the 3rd century BCE and later became crucial for Hellenistic Judaism and early Christianity.

The Septuagint was more than a translation project. It gave Greek-speaking Jews a scriptural language for diaspora worship, education, and apologetic, and it later became the Bible most often quoted by New Testament writers. Because its readings sometimes differ from the later Masoretic text, it preserves evidence for Hebrew textual forms and interpretive choices that circulated before medieval standardization.

## Origen's Hexapla

The multiplication of versions doubtless proved to be a source of increasing confusion in the 3rd century. This situation the Alexandrian theologian Origen, working at Caesarea between 230 and 240 CE, sought to remedy.

Origen's Hexapla placed the Hebrew text, a Greek transliteration, and several Greek versions in parallel columns. It compared Aquila, Symmachus, the Septuagint, and Theodotion, marking differences between Hebrew and Greek readings with critical signs.

## The Old Latin version

The existence of a Latin translation can be attested in North Africa and southern Gaul as early as the second half of the 2nd century CE, and in Rome at the beginning of the following century.

## The Vulgate

The Vulgate ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vulgate)) grew from the work of St. Jerome, whose knowledge of Latin, Greek, and Hebrew made him the leading Christian biblical scholar of his age. By 405 he had translated the Old Testament from Hebrew, and although his work first met suspicion because it differed from the Greek and Old Latin traditions, it eventually became the common Bible of Western Christendom.

## The Thomas Matthew version

The Thomas Matthew version appeared under royal license in the same period as Coverdale's authorized Bible. It was mainly the work of John Rogers, who combined translations by William Tyndale and Miles Coverdale and added editorial work of his own.

## The Great Bible

In an injunction of 1538, Henry VIII commanded the clergy to install in a convenient place in every parish church, one book of the whole Bible of the largest volume in English.

## The Geneva Bible

The Geneva Bible was produced by Protestant exiles after the reign of Mary I halted Bible printing and public reading in England. Published in 1560, it became a popular family Bible because of its clarity, notes, and technical innovations, and it strongly influenced the later King James Version.

## The Bishops' Bible

The Bishops' Bible was created because the Great Bible failed to match the popularity of the Geneva Bible, while Geneva's marginal notes seemed too partisan for official church use. Matthew Parker, archbishop of Canterbury, organized the revision, which appeared in 1568 and became an authorized church Bible in practice.

## The DouaiReims Bible

The Roman Catholics addressed themselves affirmatively to the same problem faced by the Anglican Church: a Bible in the vernacular.

## The King James (Authorized) Version

King James I approved the project in 1604, and 47 scholars worked in six companies at Westminster, Oxford, and Cambridge before publication in 1611.

## The English Revised Version

The English Revised Version addressed weaknesses in the King James Version, including uneven translation, limited knowledge of Hebrew tense, and dependence on a poor Greek New Testament text. Committees formed in 1870, with parallel American participation, and produced the New Testament in 1881 and the Old Testament in 1885.

## The American Standard Version

The American Standard Version incorporated into the main text the preferred readings and renderings of American revisers that had appeared only in an appendix to the English Revised Version. The New Testament appeared in 1900, and the Old Testament followed in 1901.

## The Revised Standard Version

The Revised Standard Version grew from the American Standard Version and from a desire for a modern, scholarly American revision. The New Testament appeared in 1946, the complete Bible in 1952, and the Apocrypha in 1957; it also made early use of evidence from the Dead Sea Scroll of Isaiah.

The idea of a completely new translation into British English was first broached in 1946.

The New English Bible was not simply a revision of the King James tradition. Its translators worked from the original languages and aimed at contemporary British English, producing the New Testament in 1961 and the Old Testament and Apocrypha in 1970.

## Old Testament History

Old Testament history presents Israel's past as the story of a real people in specific lands and empires, but it interprets events through divine presence, covenant, judgment, and restoration. The prosperity under Jeroboam II and Uzziah, the Assyrian destruction of Israel, the Babylonian capture of Jerusalem in 586 BCE, the exile, and the Persian restoration under Cyrus all became major turning points in biblical memory.

The Babylonian Exile divided Israelite history into preexilic and postexilic periods. In exile, Sabbath, circumcision, prayer, law, and the preservation of tradition gained new weight. The return under Persian rule allowed the Temple to be rebuilt, but the restored community was more religious than politically independent.

Biblical history is therefore both memory and theology. Assyria, Babylon, Persia, Hellenistic kingdoms, and Rome provide the imperial background, but biblical writers organize events around election, covenant, temple, land, kingship, prophecy, exile, and hope. Archaeology, inscriptions, comparative Near Eastern law, and external chronicles can illuminate setting and language, while the biblical books themselves preserve Israel's effort to interpret catastrophe and survival religiously.

## The Torah (Law, Pentateuch, or Five Books of Moses)

Composition and authorship The Torah, or Pentateuch (Five Scrolls), traditionally the most revered portion of the Hebrew canon, comprises a series of narratives, interspersed with law codes, providing an account of events from the beginning of the world to the death of Moses.

Modern scholarship treats the Pentateuch as a composite work built from several traditions rather than a simple single-author text. The documentary hypothesis distinguishes sources such as J, E, P, and D, while the traditional Jewish and Christian view associates the books with [[Moses]].

The critical issue is not only authorship but structure. Repeated stories, double names, changes in divine title, alternate legal emphases, genealogies, ritual regulations, and shifts in style suggest long transmission and editorial shaping. Priestly materials emphasize sanctuary, sacrifice, holiness, genealogical order, and cosmic structure; Deuteronomic materials stress covenant choice, centralized worship, and obedience; narrative traditions preserve ancestral promise, conflict, migration, and divine guidance.

## Genesis

Genesis ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Book_of_Genesis)), called Bereshit in Hebrew, tells of creation, early humanity, and the ancestors of Israel. Its primeval history includes creation, Eden, disobedience, flood, and Babel, while its patriarchal narratives follow Abraham, Isaac, Jacob, and Joseph as bearers of divine promise, covenant, migration, family conflict, and survival.

## Exodus

The title (in the Greek, Latin, and English versions) means a going out, referring to the seminal event of the liberation of Israel from Egyptian bondage through the wondrous acts and power of God.

Exodus joins liberation from Egypt with revelation at Sinai. It presents Moses' call, the divine name YHWH, the plagues, the crossing of the Sea of Reeds, wilderness guidance, covenant law, apostasy, renewal, and the building of the Tabernacle.

## Legislation

The legislation in Exodus connects liberation with covenant law. The Covenant Code follows the Decalogue and includes ritual, civil, social, and justice rules, while the Tabernacle instructions describe priestly clothing, sacred furniture, the ark, altars, and the sanctuary where divine presence is represented among the people.

## Leviticus

The cultic and priestly laws presented in Exodus are expanded to take up virtually the whole of Leviticus, the Latin Vulgate title for the third of the Five Books of Moses, which may be translated the Book (or Manual) of Priests.

Leviticus is mainly priestly law, but it also links ritual order with ethical seriousness. Offerings, priestly ordination, purification rules, holiness laws, vows, and tithes are presented as ways Israel lives before a holy God, including the command to love one's neighbour.

## The Holiness Code

Next (chapters 1726) comes what has been designated the Holiness Code, or Law of Holiness, which scholars regard as a separate, distinctive unit within the P material (designated H), possibly from the exilic period, as some commentators have assumed. The Holiness Code concludes: These are the statutes and ordinances and laws which the Lord made between him and the people of Israel on Mt. Sinai by Moses, connecting these precepts with the primal revelation in Exodus.

## Numbers

Numbers, called Bemidbar in Hebrew, follows Israel in the wilderness between Sinai and Canaan. Census lists, Levite duties, complaints, rebellions, spies in Canaan, Balaam traditions, settlement east of the Jordan, and rules for land division present the wilderness years as a time of divine care, testing, punishment, and preparation.

## Deuteronomy

Greek translation of chapter 17, verse 18, referring to a copy of this law: the implication being that the book is a second law or an expanded version of the original law for the new generation of Israelites about to enter Canaan. The final chapters are concerned with the last words and acts of Moses: directing Joshua to lead Israel after his death, writing down this law, calling for a sabbatical renewal ceremony of it on the Feast of Booths, ordering that it be put beside the ark of the Covenant, and uttering two poems.

Deuteronomy is framed as Moses' address in Moab before Israel enters Canaan. It retells the wilderness past, urges exclusive loyalty to Yahweh, gives laws for life in the land, and ends with the Song of Moses, the Blessing of Moses, and Moses' death outside the promised land.

Deuteronomy strongly influenced later biblical history. Its themes of one God, one chosen place of worship, blessing and curse, memory of slavery, care for the poor, and national covenant shaped the language of Joshua-Kings and later reform traditions. The book makes obedience a historical question: Israel's future depends on whether memory, law, and worship become public life.

## The Neviim (Prophets)

The Hebrew canon of the Old Testament known as the Nevi'im, or the Prophets, is divided into two sections: the Former Prophets and the Latter Prophets. Amos, Obadiah, Jonah, Micah, Nahum, Habakkuk, Zephaniah, Haggai, Zechariah, and Malachi. Thus, in the Hebrew canon of the Prophets there are, in effect, eight books.

## Joshua

The Book of Joshua takes its name from Joshua son of Nun, Moses' successor and leader of the Hebrew tribes. The book presents entry into Canaan, conquest traditions, tribal allotments, Levitical cities, and covenant renewal at Shechem, with Joshua portrayed as a leader who obeys Yahweh and fulfills the promise of land.

## Judges

The Book of Judges explains the period between Joshua and the monarchy through a Deuteronomic cycle: obedience brings peace, prosperity leads to disobedience, disobedience brings oppression, and oppression leads to repentance and deliverance. Judges such as Deborah, Gideon, Jephthah, and Samson arise through divine charisma rather than hereditary office.

The book also shows Israel's incomplete settlement among Canaanite peoples and the religious danger of fertility cults. Stories of the shibboleth test, Samson's conflict with the Philistines, Micah's shrine, and the violence against Benjamin create a grim portrait of tribal disorder before kingship.

## Isaiah

The Book of Isaiah is a major prophetic work spanning judgment, hope, exile, and restoration. First Isaiah reflects the 8th century BCE prophet of Judah, condemning empty ritual, social injustice, drunkenness, bribery, and failed trust in Yahweh, while later Isaianic material speaks to exilic and postexilic communities.

Isaiah's vision of divine holiness, his Immanuel oracle, and his hope for a peaceful Davidic ruler made the book central for both Jewish and Christian interpretation. Later sections connect the fall of Babylon, the return from exile, and renewed creation with the saving power of Yahweh.

## Jeremiah

The prophet Jeremiah began to prophesy about 626 BCE during the reign of the Judaean king Josiah.

## Ezekiel

The Book of Ezekiel comes from the prophet-priest Ezekiel, who lived around the Babylonian Exile of 586 BCE. Its symbolic actions, visions, temple imagery, judgment speeches, and restoration hopes made it one of the most vivid prophetic books and helped shape later apocalyptic expectation that God would intervene dramatically to save a faithful remnant.

The prophetic books often preserve layered collections rather than stenographic records. Oracles against Israel and Judah, oracles against foreign nations, biographical narratives, temple sermons, symbolic acts, laments, salvation promises, and later editorial links may stand side by side. This complexity explains why prophets could be read as witnesses to specific crises and as continuing voices for later communities seeking warning, consolation, and hope.

## Hosea

The Book of Hosea, the first of the canonical Twelve (Minor) Prophets, was written by Hosea (whose name means salvation, or deliverance), a prophet who lived during the last years of the age of Jeroboam II in Israel and the period of decline and ruin that followed the brief period of economic prosperity.

## Joel

The Book of Joel, the second of the Twelve (Minor) Prophets, is a short work of only three chapters. The dates of Joel (whose name means Yahweh is God) are difficult to ascertain.

## Amos

The Book of Amos, the third of the Twelve (Minor) Prophets, has been one of the most significant and influential books of the Bible from the time it was written (8th century BCE) down to the 20th century.

## Obadiah

The Book of Obadiah, the fourth book of the Twelve (Minor) Prophets, contains only 21 verses. Nothing is known about the prophet as a person or about his times.

## Jonah

The Book of Jonah, containing the well-known story of Jonah in the stomach of a fish for three days, is a narrative about a reluctant prophet.

## Micah

The Book of Micah, the sixth book of the Twelve (Minor) Prophets, was written by the prophet Micah in the 8th century BCE.

## Nahum

The Book of Nahum, seventh of the Twelve (Minor) Prophets, contains three chapters directed against the mighty nation of Assyria.

## Habakkuk

The Book of Habakkuk, the eighth book of the Twelve (Minor) Prophets, was written by a prophet difficult to identify.

## Zephaniah

The Book of Zephaniah, the ninth book of the Twelve (Minor) Prophets, is written in three chapters.

## Haggai

The Book of Haggai, the 10th book of the Twelve (Minor) Prophets, is a brief work of only two chapters. Written about 520 BCE by the prophet Haggai, the book contains four oracles.

## Zechariah

The Book of Zechariah, the 11th book of the Twelve (Minor) Prophets, dates from the same period as that of Haggaiabout 520 BCE.

## Malachi

The Book of Malachi, the last of the Twelve (Minor) Prophets, was written by an anonymous writer called Malachi, or my messenger.

## Overview

The Ketuvim (the Writings or the Hagiographa), the third division of the Hebrew Bible, comprises a miscellaneous collection of sacred writings that were not classified in either the Torah or the Prophets.

## Psalms

The Psalms (from Greek psalmas, song) are poems and hymns, dating from various periods in the history of Israel, that were assembled for use at public worship and that have continued to play a central role in the liturgy and prayer life of both Jews and Christians. consists of two parts with three stresses to each part (3/3); thus: Have-mercy-on-me,/O-God, in-your-goodness; in-your-great-tenderness/wipe-away-my-faults. (Ps. 51:1)

Lines with three or four parts and parts with two, four, or five stresses also occur. (Ps. 38:1)

In antithetic parallelism the second part presents the same idea as the first by way of contrast or negation. (Ps. 1:6)

Synthetic parallelism involves the completion or expansion of the idea of the first part in the second part. As a doe longs for running streams, so longs my soul for you, my God. (Ps. 42:1)

Synthetic parallelism is a broad category that allows for many variations, one of which has the picturesque name staircase parallelism and consists of a series of parts or lines that build up to a conclusion. (Ps. 29:12)

Although it is evident that Hebrew poetry groups lines into larger units, the extent of this grouping and the principles on which it is based are uncertain.

## Psalm Numbering in the Hebrew and Septuagint-Vulgate Traditions

Although Roman Catholic versions in the past have used the Septuagint-Vulgate way of numbering the Psalms, recent translations have followed the Hebrew tradition, which divides and counts several of the psalms differently.

## Proverbs

Proverbs is probably the oldest extant document of the Hebrew wisdom movement, of which King Solomon was the founder and patron. chapter of Proverbs is an example of a simple antithetic saying: He who spurns his father's discipline is a fool, he who accepts correction is discreet. Other forms of the mashal, such as parables, riddles, allegories, and ultimately full-scale compositions developed later.

## Job

The Book of Job is not only the finest expression of the Hebrew poetic genius; it must also be accorded a place among the greatest masterpieces of world literature. this direct encounter with Yahweh: I had heard of thee by the hearing of the ear, but now my eye sees thee; therefore I despise myself, and repent in dust and ashes.

## The Megillot (the Scrolls)

The five books known as the Megillot or Scrolls are grouped together as a unit in modern Hebrew Bibles according to the order of the annual religious festivals on which they are read in the synagogues of the Ashkenazim (central and eastern European Jews and their descendants). appearance usually follows the order of the liturgical calendar: The five books have little in common apart from their roles in the liturgy.

## Ruth

The Book of Ruth is a beautiful short story about a number of good people, particularly the Moabite great-grandmother of David.

## Ecclesiastes

The book of Ecclesiastes is a work of the Hebrew wisdom movement, associated by its title and by tradition with King Solomon.

## Daniel

The Book of Daniel presents a collection of popular stories about Daniel, a loyal Jew, and the record of visions granted to him, with the Babylonian Exile of the 6th century BCE as their background. The conflict between the religion of the Jews and the paganism of their foreign rulers is also the basic theme of the Book of Daniel.

## Ezra, Nehemiah, and Chronicles

The final books of the Hebrew Bible are the books of Chronicles and EzraNehemiah, which once formed a unitary history of Israel from Adam to the 4th century BCE, written by an anonymous Chronicler.

## Intertestamental Literature

Intertestamental literature names Jewish writings from roughly the Persian, Hellenistic, and early Roman periods, especially the 2nd and 1st centuries BCE and the 1st and 2nd centuries CE. The term is Christian and chronological, because Judaism did not experience these writings as merely between two testaments. They preserve prayer, wisdom, legend, apocalypse, rewritten Scripture, martyrology, history, ethical instruction, and sectarian interpretation.

This literature is indispensable because it shows the religious world in which both rabbinic Judaism and Christianity emerged. The Maccabean crisis, Hellenistic rule, Temple piety, diaspora life, messianic hopes, resurrection belief, angelology, demonology, martyrdom, and debates over law all appear with new intensity. Some books entered Catholic and Orthodox Old Testament canons; others survived in Greek, Latin, Syriac, Ethiopic, Armenian, Slavonic, or fragments because Christian communities copied them.

## Definitions

A vast amount of Jewish literature written in the intertestamental period was preserved, for the most part, through various Christian churches. Scholars distinguish Apocrypha, deuterocanonical books, and Pseudepigrapha, though the boundaries vary by religious tradition. The categories are modern aids, not neutral ancient shelves: a book could be canonical in one community, useful but noncanonical in another, and suspect in a third.

## Apocalypticism

In intertestamental Jewish literature a special trend developed: namely, apocalypticism. Apokalypsis is a Greek term meaning revelation of divine mysteries, both about the nature of God and about the last days. Apocalyptic writings often use heavenly journeys, interpreting angels, symbolic beasts, periodized history, persecution, judgment, resurrection, and final vindication. They respond to crisis by insisting that history has a hidden divine order not visible to the oppressed.

## Esdras

The Greek Ezra, sometimes named I (or II or III) Esdras, enjoyed considerable popularity in the early church but lost its prestige in the Middle Ages in the Latin Church.

## Judith

The book of Judith is similar to the biblical Book of Esther in that it also describes how a woman saved her people from impending massacre by her cunning and daring.

## Tobit

The other Jewish short story possibly dating from Persian times is the book of Tobit, named after the father of its hero.

## The Story of Ahikar

According to the book of Tobit, Ahikar, the cupbearer of the Assyrian king Esarhaddon, was Tobit's nephew; he is a secondary personage in the plot, and his own story is mentioned.

## Baruch

## Qumran literature (Dead Sea Scrolls)

Discovery of the Dead Sea Scrolls in caves near Qumran in the 1940s transformed knowledge of the Hebrew Bible, Second Temple Judaism, and Jewish sectarian life. The scrolls include biblical manuscripts, commentaries, rule books, hymns, calendars, rewritten biblical texts, apocalypses, legal works, and community documents. They pushed the evidence for Hebrew biblical texts back about a millennium before the medieval Masoretic manuscripts.

The scrolls show that Jewish textual tradition before rabbinic standardization was diverse but disciplined. Some manuscripts resemble the later Masoretic text, some agree more closely with the Septuagint or Samaritan Pentateuch, and some preserve independent readings. The sectarian writings also reveal a community organized around purity, covenant renewal, eschatological expectation, scriptural interpretation, and conflict with rival Jewish authorities.

## Pesharim

An important source of knowledge about the Dead Sea sect is the pesharim, or commentaries. They read prophetic texts as coded references to the community's own time, leaders, enemies, and final expectations. Pesher interpretation assumes that Scripture contains mysteries whose true meaning is disclosed to the inspired teacher and fulfilled in the sect's history.

## Hodayot

One of the most important Essene works is the Hodayot, or Thanksgiving Psalms. These hymns praise God's mercy, lament human weakness, describe persecution, celebrate revealed knowledge, and present the worshiper as rescued from corruption by divine grace. They show that sectarian discipline and apocalyptic hope were joined to intense personal prayer.

New Testament canon, texts, and versions concern how early Christian writings became Scripture, how their Greek text was copied, and how they were translated into the languages of expanding churches. The 27-book New Testament was not produced at one moment; it emerged through worship, apostolic memory, teaching authority, antiheretical controversy, circulation among churches, and comparison with rival writings.

## Background

The historical background of the New Testament and its times must be viewed in conjunction with the Jewish matrix from which it evolved and the Hellenistic (Greek cultural) world into which it expanded during a period of Jewish religious propaganda.

This background includes Roman rule, Herodian politics, the Temple, synagogues, diaspora communities, apocalyptic expectation, Pharisaic and priestly debates, Greek language, and imperial roads and cities. Christianity began inside Jewish debates about Scripture, law, kingdom, resurrection, and messiah, then spread through Greek-speaking urban networks where Gentile converts raised new questions about circumcision, food, idolatry, and communal identity.

## The Pharisees

The Pharisees (possibly spiritual descendants of the Hasidim [Pious Ones], who were the exponents of Maccabean revolt) were strict adherents to the Law.

## The Sadducees

The Sadducees, more conservative and static, consisted mainly of the old priesthood and landed aristocracy and, perhaps, some Herodians. They were collaborators with Rome.

## The Zealots

The Zealots were revolutionaries who plotted actively against the Roman oppression.

## The Essenes

Though the Essenes of the Dead Sea Scrolls are not mentioned in the New Testament, they are described by Philo, Josephus, and Eusebius, a 4th-century Christian historian.

## Astrology

There were elements in the Greek world that may have come from the East, partly Egyptian and Babylonian, which gave rise to astrology.

## The Synoptic Gospels

The Gospel According to Mark: background and overview The Gospel According to Mark is the second in canonical order of the Gospels and is both the earliest gospel that survived and the shortest.

The Synoptic Gospels share a broad narrative pattern: John the Baptist, Jesus' Galilean ministry, teaching, healings, conflict, journey toward Jerusalem, passion, death, and resurrection proclamation. Yet each Gospel shapes the story differently. Mark stresses urgency, secrecy, suffering, and discipleship; Matthew emphasizes fulfillment, teaching, church order, and Jewish Scripture; Luke highlights universal salvation, the poor, the Spirit, prayer, women, and the continuity between Gospel and Acts.

Matthew is the first in order of the four canonical Gospels and is often called the ecclesiastical Gospel, both because it was much used for selections for pericopes for the church year and because it deals to a great extent with the life and conduct of the church and its members.

## The Catholic Letters

As the history of the New Testament canon shows, the seven so-called Catholic Letters (i.e., James, I and II Peter, I, II, and III John, and Jude) were among the last of the literature to be settled on before the agreement of East and West in 367.

They are called Catholic Letters because most are addressed broadly rather than to one local church. Their themes include endurance under trial, true wisdom, faith and works, false teachers, household and community conduct, apostolic memory, love, truth, hospitality, and judgment. Their late canonical settlement reflects both their usefulness and the difficulty of proving apostolic authorship or universal reception in every region.

## The Letter of James

The Letter of James, though often criticized as having nothing specifically Christian in its content apart from its use of the phrase the Lord Jesus Christ and its salutation to a general audience depicted as the twelve tribes in the dispersion (the Diaspora), is actually a letter most representative of early Christian piety.

## The First Letter of Peter

The purpose of the First Letter of Peter is exhortation directed to the exiles of the Dispersion in Asia Minor in order that they stand fast in God's grace in the face of persecution. feed their flocks humbly and faithfully. Thus, there is a typical testament form: Peter has failed and repented; and the church is warned, admonished, and strengthened as by the Apostle, who, on the analogy of Jesus' Passion and death in innocence, exhorts the church to share in the vocation of innocent suffering and to do good in innocence.

## The Johannine Letters: I, II, and III John

The three epistles gathered under the name of John were written to guide and strengthen the post-apostolic church as it faced both attacks from heresies and an ever increasing need for community solidarityalong with the concomitant love and ethics necessary to such unity.

Their central problem is the connection between confession, ethics, and fellowship. I John insists that belief in Jesus Christ, love of fellow believers, and obedience cannot be separated. II John compresses the warning into a short note about truth, love, and refusal to support deceivers. III John shows the same world from the angle of traveling missionaries, local authority, and conflict between hospitality and control.

## The First Letter of John

I John assumes a knowledge of the Johannine Gospel ( of I John may be the ecclesiastical redactor of the Gospel According to John) and adds ethical admonition and instruction regarding the well-being of the church as it confronts heresy and stresses the lack of moral concern that springs from it.

## The Second Letter of John

II John warns a specific church (or perhaps churches), designated as the elect lady and her children, against the influence of the Docetic heresy combatted in I John, whose proponents lured Christians from following the truth, just as we have been commanded by the Father.

## The Third Letter of John

III John, addressed to Gaius, shows that its author is concerned about and has responsibility as presbyter for the missionaries of the church.

## The Letter of Jude

The Letter of Jude, after a salutation that attributes it to Jude, the brother of James, and addresses itself to the church as a whole, develops the theme of the short lettera polemic against heretics who have abandoned the transmitted traditional faith and who will thus be judged by the Lord.

## The Revelation to John

Purpose and theme The Revelation (i.e., Apocalypse) to John is an answer in apocalyptic terms to the needs of the church in time of persecution, as it awaits the end-time expected in the near future.

Revelation uses letters, throne visions, seals, trumpets, bowls, beasts, symbolic numbers, hymns, judgment scenes, and the New Jerusalem to interpret imperial pressure and Christian endurance. It stands within Jewish apocalyptic tradition but gives that tradition a specifically Christian centre in the slain and victorious Lamb. Its imagery has made it one of the most influential and contested books in Christian interpretation.

## New Testament Apocrypha

The title New Testament Apocrypha may suggest that the books thus classified have or had a status comparable to that of the Old Testament Apocrypha and have been recognized as canonical. These records can be classified in relation to their literary forms: gospels, acts, letters, and apocalypses.

These writings show how early Christians extended apostolic memory beyond the canonical books. Some supplied childhood stories of Jesus, speeches of apostles, missionary adventures, martyrdom scenes, ascetic ideals, heavenly tours, or secret revelations. Church leaders often rejected them because of late origin, legendary expansion, doctrinal suspicion, or limited use, but they remained influential in art, devotion, folklore, and later imagination.

## Texts, Communities, and Interpretation

Biblical literature ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biblical_canon)) is a library rather than a single book. It includes law, narrative, poetry, prophecy, wisdom writing, gospel, letter, apocalypse, genealogy, hymn, and parable. These forms were written, edited, copied, translated, collected, and interpreted over many centuries by Jewish and Christian communities. Reading a psalm, a prophetic oracle, a gospel story, and an apostolic letter in the same way can therefore obscure their different purposes and historical settings.

The Hebrew Bible ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hebrew_Bible)), called the Old Testament by Christians, was largely written in Hebrew with portions in Aramaic. Its traditional Jewish divisions are Torah, Prophets, and Writings. The Christian New Testament was written in Greek and centres on Jesus, the early Christian movement, and letters addressed to communities facing practical, theological, and ethical disputes. Different Jewish, Catholic, Orthodox, and Protestant communities recognize somewhat different canons, especially concerning books commonly called deuterocanonical or apocryphal.

Canon formation was a process of communal recognition, use, debate, and authority rather than a single moment when every book was selected. Religious communities valued texts for connection to founding figures, consistency with received belief, liturgical use, moral or theological power, and wide acceptance. Manuscripts also differ in wording because copying by hand introduces mistakes, corrections, explanations, and occasional deliberate changes. Textual criticism compares manuscripts, ancient translations, and quotations to reconstruct the earliest recoverable wording.

Historical criticism asks when, where, by whom, and for what situation a text was produced. Literary criticism examines genre, structure, imagery, narrative voice, and the relationships among texts. Form criticism considers oral or social settings behind smaller units; redaction criticism studies how editors arranged inherited material; archaeological and social-historical research relates texts to ancient political, economic, and religious life. These methods do not automatically determine belief, but they make interpretation more accountable to evidence.

Biblical interpretation has shaped law, worship, art, music, language, politics, and moral debate across many societies. Translation broadens access but also requires choices, because no two languages match perfectly in grammar, idiom, metaphor, or cultural associations. Interpretation therefore remains active: readers ask how ancient texts should inform modern questions about justice, gender, violence, community, ecology, faith, and public life, while recognizing that the texts have been read differently by different traditions.

- The Hebrew Bible ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hebrew_Bible)) became foundational for Christianity because the New Testament depends on its creation stories, covenant ideas, prophetic language, moral vision, and hope for God's future rule.
- The Genesis ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Book_of_Genesis)) creation stories influenced Western thought less as scientific explanation than as a theological framework for understanding God, humanity, and the universe.
- The Covenant people are presented not as chosen for privilege alone but as chosen to serve God's will toward all nations.
- Biblical ethics give special attention to widows, orphans, strangers, and the helpless, making social justice a central part of the Bible's moral imagination.
- The Old Testament ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_Testament)) contains many literary forms, including law, narrative, prophecy, poetry, wisdom sayings, love story, lament, philosophical reflection, and national memory.
- The New Testament also uses varied forms: gospels, historical narrative, letters, and apocalypse, with letters forming the largest single class of writings.
- A Gospel is not ordinary biography; it arranges remembered acts and sayings of Jesus to call readers toward faith.
- The Septuagint gave Greek-speaking Jews and early Christians access to the Hebrew Scriptures and strongly shaped New Testament language and early Christian theology.
- The Latin Bible shaped Western thought for about a thousand years, while later vernacular translations helped develop languages such as German and English.
- Martin Luther's Bible translation in the 16th century is linked with the rise of modern German literary language.
- The Authorized Version of 1611, or King James Version, appeared when English prose was reaching a high point of literary maturity.
- The Vulgate ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vulgate)) remained the chief Bible of Western Christendom for centuries, even though it began as a controversial revision and translation project.
- The King James Version ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/King_James_Version)) preserved familiar English biblical names while drawing on Hebrew, Greek, earlier English translations, and the scholarship available in 1611.
- The Dead Sea Scrolls supplied ancient manuscript evidence that helped scholars compare Hebrew textual traditions older than medieval copies.
- Biblical influence continued into the modern secular age through assumptions about dignity, moral choice, justice, suffering, and the worth of ordinary human lives.
- Western literature often uses biblical themes to show that any person, not only a noble or ruler, can experience tragedy, dignity, moral crisis, and hope.
- The Torah ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Torah)) became the most revered part of the Hebrew canon, but its legal and narrative materials developed before the five-book collection was fully accepted as sacred.
- The Book of the Twelve ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Twelve_Minor_Prophets)) counted as one book in the Hebrew canon because the shorter prophetic writings were preserved together on a single scroll.
- The Ketuvim ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ketuvim)) closed later than the Torah and Prophets, as debates continued around books such as Esther, Ecclesiastes, Proverbs, and Song of Songs.
- Origen's Hexapla compared Hebrew and Greek biblical traditions side by side, showing how textual criticism began as an effort to manage multiple ancient versions.
