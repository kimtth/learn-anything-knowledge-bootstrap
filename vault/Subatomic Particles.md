## Introduction

![[attachments/subatomic_particles_001.png|420]]

*Standard Model of elementary particles diagram*

Subatomic particles ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Subatomic_particle)), also called elementary particles, are self-contained units of matter or energy that form the basic constituents of matter. Familiar examples include electrons, protons, and neutrons, but particle physics has revealed many more particles, including quarks, muons, neutrinos, positrons, mesons, hyperons, photons, and gluons.

The electron is negatively charged and almost massless compared with the atom, yet it accounts for much of the atom's size. Protons and neutrons form the dense atomic nucleus, but they are themselves made of quarks. The positron is the antimatter counterpart of the electron.

Particle physics expanded through cosmic-ray studies and [[Particle Accelerators|particle accelerators]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Particle_accelerator)). High-energy collisions convert energy into matter, creating unstable particles that may exist for less than a millionth of a second. More than 200 subatomic particles have been detected through cosmic rays and accelerator experiments.

Modern particle physics also changed the understanding of forces. Forces are described through exchange particles such as photons and gluons. The Standard Model organizes known particles and the electromagnetic, weak, and strong forces, though gravity and some deeper questions remain outside its full reach.

## Size

Subatomic size is best understood by comparison. An atom is about $10^{-10}$ metre across, but most of that volume is empty space occupied by electrons around the nucleus. An average nucleus is about $10^{-14}$ metre across, and a nucleon such as a proton or neutron is about $10^{-15}$ metre across.

The distance $10^{-15}$ metre is called a fermi, named for Enrico Fermi. Sizes of atoms, nuclei, and nucleons are measured by scattering beams of electrons from targets. Higher-energy electrons penetrate deeper before being deflected, revealing smaller structures.

At low energies, electrons scatter mainly from atomic electrons. At hundreds of megaelectron volts, they penetrate atoms and scatter from nuclei. At still higher energies, scattering experiments reveal internal nucleon structure and the presence of quarks.

## Spin

Spin is an intrinsic angular momentum possessed by subatomic particles. It emerged from quantum mechanics in the 1920s when Samuel Goudsmit and George Uhlenbeck proposed that electrons have an internal angular momentum with two possible orientations.

Spin helped explain atomic spectra, especially those of alkali metals such as sodium. An electron behaves like a tiny magnet with a magnetic moment that can orient parallel or antiparallel to the magnetic field inside an atom.

Spin classifies particles into two groups. Fermions have half-integer spin, such as $1/2$, and include matter particles such as quarks and leptons. Bosons have integer spin, such as 0, 1, or 2, and include force-carrying particles.

## Antiparticles

P.A.M. Dirac gave electron spin a relativistic quantum theory and predicted antiparticles. His equation allowed positive and negative energy solutions, implying a particle like the electron but with positive charge. In 1932, Carl Anderson discovered the positron.

Antiparticles have the same mass and spin as corresponding particles but opposite charge or related quantum properties. When a particle meets its antiparticle, they can annihilate, converting mass into energy, often gamma-ray photons.

Cosmic rays naturally create particle-antiparticle pairs in Earth's atmosphere. Particle accelerators reproduce such high-energy collisions. In 1955, a team led by Emilio Segre and Owen Chamberlain found the antiproton using the Bevatron accelerator.

## Gravity

[[Gravitation (Gravity)|Gravity]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gravity)) is the weakest fundamental force but acts on all mass and energy, including subatomic particles. Isaac Newton described it as an inverse-square force between masses, with strength proportional to the product of the masses and inversely proportional to the square of distance.

At atomic scales, gravitational effects are negligible compared with other forces. Yet gravity is long-ranged and dominates large masses such as planets, stars, and galaxies. Einstein's general relativity refined Newtonian gravity, but gravity remains outside the Standard Model's quantum framework.

## Electromagnetism

Electromagnetism ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electromagnetism)) acts between electric charges and can be attractive or repulsive. Charles Coulomb showed that electrostatic force follows an inverse-square law similar to Newtonian gravity. Like charges repel, while opposite charges attract.

Electricity and magnetism were unified through experiments by Hans Oersted, Andre-Marie Ampere, and Michael Faraday, then synthesized by James Clerk Maxwell. Maxwell predicted electromagnetic waves travelling at light speed.

Quantum theory introduced photons, particles of electromagnetic radiation. Max Planck's work on blackbody radiation led to quantization, and the quantum picture made electromagnetic force an interaction involving photon exchange.

## Hadrons

Hadrons are particles made from quarks and affected by the strong force. Protons and neutrons are the most familiar hadrons and form atomic nuclei. The proton appears stable, with lifetime greater than $10^{32}$ years if it decays at all, while a free neutron lives about 15 minutes.

Some hadrons live about $10^{-10}$ to $10^{-8}$ second and are called stable compared with still shorter-lived resonances. Relativity helps experiments detect them because fast-moving particles experience time dilation and leave tracks in detectors.

Very short-lived resonant hadrons last about $10^{-23}$ second and decay through the strong force. Their discovery greatly expanded the list of known particles and required new classification schemes.

## Strangeness

The discovery of strange particles began after the pion was found in 1947. Clifford Butler and George Rochester observed particles heavier than pions or muons but lighter than protons. These particles were created through strong interactions but decayed slowly, which seemed strange.

Murray Gell-Mann and Nishijima Kazuhiko proposed a new quantum number called strangeness. Strong interactions conserve strangeness, so strange particles are produced in pairs with net strangeness zero. Weak interactions do not conserve strangeness, allowing the particles to decay.

Strangeness helped bring order to the growing number of particles. It showed that conservation laws depend on which fundamental force governs a process.

## Quarks and Colour

![[attachments/subatomic_particles_002.png|420]]

*Proton quark structure diagram*

The realization that protons, neutrons, and pions are made from quarks changed the understanding of nuclear force. Yukawa's meson-exchange picture still worked at nuclear scale, but quark-level binding required a deeper explanation.

The property called [[Colour|colour]] was introduced to solve a problem with the Pauli exclusion principle. Some particles seemed to contain quarks of the same flavour and spin state, which should not be allowed. Colour provided three possible states, called red, green, and blue, plus anticolours for antiquarks.

Hadrons are colourless overall, just as atoms are electrically neutral overall. Baryons contain three quarks with balanced colours, while mesons contain quark-antiquark pairs. The strong force between coloured quarks is described by quantum chromodynamics.

## Testing the Standard Model

The [[Standard Model]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Standard_Model)) combines electroweak theory with quantum chromodynamics. It classifies known particles and explains electromagnetic, weak, and strong interactions through gauge theories. It has matched many experiments extremely well.

One major missing piece before 1994 was the top quark. It was needed to complete the set of six quarks. Evidence appeared at Fermilab's Collider Detector Facility in 1994 and was confirmed in 1995 by CDF and DZero experiments. The top quark's mass was about 170 to 190 GeV.

The top quark's discovery completed an important chapter but left questions. Why are there six quarks? Why do particles and antiparticles differ subtly? What explains particle masses and generations? The Standard Model organizes data but does not answer all deeper questions.

## Theory

Quantum chromodynamics, or QCD, describes the strong force. At very short distances or high energies, the strong force weakens, allowing calculations from few gluon exchanges. This is called asymptotic freedom.

At larger distances, strong interactions become harder to calculate because many gluon exchanges matter. This makes hadron properties, especially masses, difficult to derive. Since the 1980s, supercomputers have made progress through lattice QCD, which approximates space-time by a lattice of points.

The [[Standard Model]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Standard_Model)) remains incomplete. It does not incorporate quantum gravity, and many features appear ad hoc. It works well experimentally but leaves open the search for deeper unification, the origin of mass patterns, and the full relation between particle physics and cosmology.

## Core Equations

Physical models often begin by relating force, energy, waves, and conserved quantities:

$$\vec{F}=m\vec{a}$$

This is Newton's second law: force is the product of mass and acceleration, so a larger mass or a faster change in motion requires a larger net force.

$$E=h\nu$$

This relation says that photon energy is proportional to frequency; higher-frequency radiation carries more energy per photon.

$$E=mc^2$$

This expresses mass-energy equivalence: mass is a form of energy, with the speed of light squared as the conversion factor.

## Key Events & Developments

- 1925: Goudsmit and Uhlenbeck proposed electron spin.
- 1932: Carl Anderson discovered the positron.
- 1947: The pion was discovered, followed by strange particles.
- 1953: Gell-Mann and Nishijima proposed strangeness.
- 1955: Segre and Chamberlain's team found the antiproton.
- 1964-1965: Greenberg, Nambu, and colleagues introduced colour.
- 1994-1995: Fermilab experiments found and confirmed the top quark.

## Interesting Facts

- A fermi is $10^{-15}$ metre, about the size scale of a nucleon.
- Antimatter annihilates with matching matter to produce energy.
- [[Colour]] in particle physics is not visual colour but a quantum property.
- The top quark is almost as massive as a lead nucleus.
