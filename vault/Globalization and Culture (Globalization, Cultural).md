# Globalization and Culture (Globalization, Cultural)

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> Cultural globalization is the cross-border diffusion of commodities, ideas, media, travel, and everyday practices. It does not create one uniform culture; global influences are adapted through local traditions, identities, institutions, and unequal transnational networks.

## Introduction

Globalization is described as a process in which everyday life is increasingly shaped by the diffusion of commodities, ideas, media, and travel across national borders. This does not produce a single world culture, but rather a mix of standardization and local adaptation. It stresses that while global influences are powerful, they are filtered through existing traditions, identities, and social conditions.

## Global Subcultures and Elite Networks

*Early migrations mercator*

Some groups, especially highly educated professionals in business, media, and diplomacy, appear to share a recognizable transnational lifestyle, a cultural formation often called a global subculture and associated with gatherings such as the World Economic Forum at Davos. As Samuel Huntington and Peter L. Berger have argued, such networks are not limited to the wealthy, since academic and professional communities also form international circles shaped by common values and practices. These networks remain narrow, however, compared with the full range of human experience.

## Travel, Clothing, and Everyday Convergence

Tourism, hotels, business dress, and modern consumer habits create a familiar global environment. International travel has made Western-style accommodation and clothing widely recognizable, even when local customs continue to shape how they are used; clothing can signal modernity, prestige, or independence, while local forms of dress often remain important as markers of identity.

Routine global travel depended on air-conditioning, standardized hotels, international news channels, and predictable services that reduced the shock of weather, food, noise, and local living arrangements.

## Media, Entertainment, and Cultural Influence

Media and entertainment are presented as major channels of globalization. Television, films, sports, and other mass-cultural forms can spread ideas about beauty, lifestyle, and social behaviour across borders. These influences can be powerful, but audiences interpret them in local ways. The result is not simple imitation, but a negotiated blend of imported images and existing cultural meanings.

This local filtering is central to cultural globalization. Imported programs, brands, sports, fashions, and music may be accepted, resisted, parodied, translated, or mixed with local values. The same global image can signal sophistication in one setting, moral danger in another, and raw material for new hybrid styles in a third.

Collective viewing also matters: in many towns or villages, one television set in a bar, teahouse, restaurant, or viewing room could connect dozens of viewers to global programming.

## Food and Cultural Exchange

Food is one of the oldest and most durable forms of cultural exchange. From 1493 onward, crops from the Americas—peppers, maize, sweet potatoes, and peanuts—transformed diets and agriculture across many regions of the world, and food still carries identity, memory, and social meaning, so what circulates globally is not just a product but a set of tastes, traditions, and symbols.

## Key Events & Developments

- 1971: The World Economic Forum meetings in Davos became associated with a global elite culture.
- mid-1960s onward: Cheaper international air travel expanded global mobility.
- 1980s–1990s: Television, video, and global sports events intensified cultural exchange.
- 1493 onward: The spread of New World crops reshaped food cultures worldwide.
