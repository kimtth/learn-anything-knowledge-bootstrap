## Introduction

![[attachments/thought_001.webp|420]]

*Illustration of the brain as an organ of mind*

Thought ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thought)) can be described as covert symbolic responses to internal or external stimuli. It mediates between inner activity and the outside world, using symbols, memories, concepts, images, language, and problem-solving processes.

In everyday speech, thinking can mean believing, attending, remembering, imagining, or having something in consciousness. Psychologists usually focus on thinking as intellectual effort aimed at answering a question or solving a problem.

Definitions vary. Some psychologists treat thought as modification of cognitive structures; others see it as internal problem-solving behaviour or as a sequence of covert symbolic responses representing absent events.

## Directed Thinking

Directed thinking is symbolic activity aimed at a specific solution. It includes reasoning, planning, problem solving, and the organized search for an answer.

Reasoning pieces together earlier learning to produce a new pattern of behaviour or conclusion. It contrasts with mnemonic thinking, which simply recalls a chain of past events.

Historically, thought was associated with conscious experience. Modern psychology also studies unconscious or partly conscious processes, because many problem-solving steps occur without full awareness.

## Trial, Error, Insight, and Heuristics

Behaviourists emphasized trial and error, where solutions are found by trying responses and learning from consequences. Gestalt psychologists emphasized insight, where a solution appears as a meaningful whole.

The contrast is too simple. Insight often follows groping, failed attempts, and reorganization, while trial-and-error behaviour is rarely completely random.

Allen Newell, Herbert Simon, and J. Clifford Shaw stressed heuristics, or useful search strategies. Heuristics guide problem solving by exploring promising paths first, setting subgoals, and sometimes working backward from the goal.

## Motivation and Thinking

Thinking is shaped by [[Human Motivation (Motivation)|motivation]]. Laboratory tasks often give subjects a problem and starting point, but real thinking depends on interest, need, anxiety, curiosity, reward, values, and the decision that something is worth solving.

[[Human Motivation (Motivation)|Motivation]] affects what problems people notice, how long they persist, how they allocate attention, and whether they accept or reject possible answers.

## Problem Solving

Problem solving is a systematic search through possible actions to reach a goal. It includes divergent thinking, which generates many alternatives, and convergent thinking, which narrows alternatives toward one answer.

Robert J. Sternberg described a problem-solving cycle with several steps: problem identification, problem definition, resource allocation, representation, strategy construction, monitoring, and evaluation.

This cycle is often recursive. Solving one problem can create another, as when choosing a restaurant leads to deciding how to get there, what to order, or whether the result was satisfactory.

Problem solving depends on how the problem is represented. A poor representation can hide a solution, while a better one can make relevant relations clear.

## Deductive Reasoning

![[attachments/thought_002.webp|420]]

*Syllogism and deductive reasoning diagram*

Deduction draws conclusions that are logically implicit in premises. A valid deductive argument guarantees that if the premises are true, the conclusion cannot be false.

Conditional reasoning uses if-then statements. Modus ponens affirms the antecedent and validly affirms the consequent. Modus tollens denies the consequent and validly denies the antecedent.

Two common invalid forms are affirming the consequent and denying the antecedent. These can lead to false conclusions even when the argument seems persuasive.

Deductive validity depends on form, not real-world truth. An argument can be valid even if its premises and conclusion are false, as long as the conclusion follows necessarily from the premises.

Syllogisms are common tools for studying reasoning. Categorical syllogisms relate classes, while linear syllogisms compare quantities or ordered relations such as height.

## Inductive Reasoning

Induction moves from part to whole, observed to unobserved, past to future, or particular cases to general claims. Unlike deduction, it supports probability rather than certainty.

Major forms include causal inference, categorical inference, and analogical inference. Hearing piano music may suggest someone is playing a piano, but another cause remains possible.

Analogical reasoning applies knowledge from one domain to another. It can be useful, but it fails when the compared situations are not similar enough.

## Judgment and Decision Making

Judgment is realistic thinking directed toward discriminating objects or information, such as distinguishing a lion from a tiger. It underlies decision making.

Accuracy and efficiency depend on information, presentation rate, expectations, and number of alternatives. Redundant cues can help; a lion can be identified by colour, mane, body shape, and lack of stripes.

Decision making becomes harder when information is incomplete, alternatives are numerous, expectations are misleading, or emotional stakes are high.

## Concept Attainment

Concept attainment is the ability to identify or use classes of items, such as recognizing triangles among other shapes. It links objects by shared properties and gives them a general name.

Concept learning involves noticing dimensions, forming hypotheses, testing rules, and deciding whether new cases fit a category. Objects can differ in shape, size, colour, location, emotional meaning, and other features.

Successful concept formation allows people to classify new information and use rules flexibly rather than treating every object as entirely new.

## Creative Thinking

Creative thinking is often linked to divergent thinking because it generates new ideas, unusual associations, and multiple possible solutions. It is important in invention, mathematics, art, science, and everyday adaptation.

Creativity still needs evaluation. Many ideas may be produced, but useful creative thought also tests, selects, revises, and applies them.

## Core Equations

This models concentration falling exponentially over time, often used for clearance, decay, or elimination processes.

$$z=\frac{x-\mu}{\sigma}$$

A z-score tells how many standard deviations a value lies above or below the mean.

$$\text{risk}=\frac{\text{cases}}{\text{population}}$$

## Key Events & Developments

- 1910: John Dewey's How We Think became a classic study of reflective thought.
- Mid-20th century: Gestalt, behaviourist, and information-processing approaches debated trial, error, insight, and problem solving.
- 1972: Newell and Simon's Human Problem Solving advanced computer-influenced models of thinking.

## Key People

- John Dewey ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Dewey)): important theorist of reflective thinking.
- Max Wertheimer ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Max_Wertheimer)): key Gestalt thinker on productive thinking.
- Allen Newell ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Allen_Newell)), Herbert Simon, and J. Clifford Shaw: pioneers of heuristic and computer-simulation approaches.
- Robert J. Sternberg ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Sternberg)): described a problem-solving cycle and studied complex cognition.

## Interesting Facts

- Thinking ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thought)) can occur without being fully conscious.
- Insight often looks sudden, but it may follow many failed attempts.
- Deduction can be valid even when its statements are factually false.
- Induction is essential for everyday life because people constantly reason beyond what they directly observe.
