## Introduction

Hebrew literature ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hebrew_literature)) is the body of writing produced in Hebrew, distinct from Jewish literature written in other languages. It reaches from its earliest roots in the biblical period to its modern role as the official language of Israel. It emphasizes that Hebrew survived for long stretches as a literary language even when it was no longer widely spoken, and that its development was shaped by religious, historical, and cultural change.
Hebrew literature is not identical with Jewish literature as a whole. It includes works by the Samaritans and by later Protestant writers, while many important Jewish texts were written in Greek, Aramaic, Arabic, Yiddish, and other languages. This broader context helps explain why Hebrew literature remained a distinct but not isolated tradition.

## Early Hebrew and the Biblical Period

Hebrew has a long early history as a language of religious and literary expression. Biblical Hebrew developed from a simple, earthy idiom into a language capable of complex religious thought and poetic grandeur. Later, Mishnaic Hebrew emerged as a distinct spoken and literary form used by Pharisaic teaching in the 2nd century BC. Even after Hebrew ceased to be a common spoken language around AD 200, it continued to function as a literary medium.

## Piyyutim and Medieval Developments

During the medieval period, Hebrew literature expanded through religious poetry and scholarship. Especially important were the piyyutim, liturgical poems composed by paytanim, which used biblical, Mishnaic, and Aramaic vocabularies and often reflected intense messianic devotion. It also notes that new Arabic-inspired metrical forms entered Hebrew poetry and influenced secular writing.

In Europe, writers such as Rashi, Gershom ben Judah, and others helped shape the Hebrew tradition through commentaries, poetry, and scholarship. The Crusades and later expulsions from Europe drove many Jews eastward, where new literary centres developed and the tradition continued in changed forms.

## Medieval Philosophy and Mysticism
Hebrew literature also engaged with philosophy and mysticism. Works such as those of Moses Maimonides connected Hebrew thought to Aristotelian and Neoplatonic ideas. The rise of Kabbalah, especially in the 16th century, further expanded the range of Hebrew literature by introducing mystical and theosophical themes. The Zohar ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zohar)) became one of the most influential works in this tradition.

## Modern Hebrew Literature

In the modern period, Hebrew literature entered a renewed phase through the Haskalah, or Jewish Enlightenment, and later Romantic and nationalist currents. Writers such as Abraham Mapu, Judah Leib Gordon, and Micah Judah Lebensohn helped revive Hebrew prose and poetry, while the first Hebrew novel and new forms of social criticism emerged. Modern Hebrew literature increasingly reflected the tensions of Jewish life under imperial and social change.

The final section turns to Israel, where Hebrew became the official language of the new state. After 1948, literature in Hebrew developed on a large scale, shaped by the experiences of war, immigration, and the search for national identity. Writers of this period explored themes of alienation, memory, catastrophe, and renewal.

## Key Events & Developments

- Hebrew was spoken in Palestine from the 12th century BC to about AD 200.
- Mishnaic Hebrew became important in Pharisaic teaching during the 2nd century BC.
- The Arabic metrical system influenced Hebrew poetry in the 10th century.
- Kabbalah flourished in the 16th century, especially through the Zohar ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zohar)).
- Hebrew became the official language of the State of Israel in the 20th century.
