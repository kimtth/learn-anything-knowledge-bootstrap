# Johannesburg

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> Johannesburg grew from an 1886 gold-mining camp into South Africa's leading industrial and financial metropolis. Its urban form and social contrasts reveal the enduring effects of labor migration, segregation, apartheid, and unequal access to housing, education, and services.

## Introduction

![[attachments/johannesburg_001.webp|420]]

*Johannesburg skyline*

Johannesburg ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Johannesburg)), in Gauteng province, is South Africa's chief industrial and financial metropolis. Founded in 1886 after the discovery of gold, it grew from a mining camp into one of the youngest major cities in the world. Its geography and society also show the long effects of racial planning, segregation, and apartheid.

The city is marked by sharp contrasts: skyscrapers and shantytowns, major universities and persistent illiteracy, wealth and poverty. Although apartheid legally ended in 1994, older spatial inequalities continued to shape residential life and public services.

## Landscape And Climate

![[attachments/johannesburg_002.webp|420]]

*Johannesburg skyline*

Johannesburg's site lies on the Highveld and straddles the Witwatersrand, or Rand. Its elevation is about 5,700 to 5,930 feet, and it is the world's largest city not on a navigable body of water. Its location was determined instead by the gold-bearing Witwatersrand Main Reef.

Gold mining created the city, though most local mines had stopped operating by the 1970s. At its height, the Witwatersrand gold industry produced more than 40 percent of annual world output.

The climate ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Climate)) is temperate, with summer temperatures around 75 F and winter temperatures around 55 F. Rain falls mainly in summer, often in late-afternoon electrical storms, while droughts are common. Winter air pollution is severe in dense townships where coal is still used for fuel.

## City Layout And People

Central Johannesburg keeps the rectangular street grid laid out in 1886. Rapid growth and little preservation left a mixture of surviving Victorian fragments, Beaux Arts public structures, mining-house offices, Art Deco influence, and later high-rise blocks.

Greater Johannesburg covers more than 200 square miles and includes over 500 suburbs and townships. The 1950 Group Areas Act made each area racially designated, and although the law was repealed in 1991, segregation remained strong in places such as Soweto, Alexandra, and Lenasia.

The population is mostly black, with smaller white, Coloured, and Asian or Indian communities. At least a dozen languages are used daily, and most residents are Christian.

## Economy, Transport, And Services

The economy ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economy)) rests on mining headquarters, manufacturing, engineering, and finance. The Chamber of Mines, mining houses, major banks, insurers, and the Johannesburg Stock Exchange make the city South Africa's financial centre.

[[Transportation]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transport)) connects Johannesburg locally, nationally, and internationally. Railroads, freeways, the Gautrain, O.R. Tambo International Airport, buses, and taxis link suburbs, townships, Pretoria, and overseas destinations.

Local government rests with the City of Johannesburg Metropolitan Municipality, which includes former black townships such as Soweto and Alexandra. The post-apartheid municipal structure was meant to equalize revenue and services, but disparities remained in housing, health, education, and safety.

Public health shows apartheid's deep legacy. Child mortality and tuberculosis were far worse in black townships and migrant hostels than in white neighbourhoods, while Baragwanath near Soweto became Africa's largest hospital.

## Education And Culture

[[Education]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Education)) ranges widely in quality, and racial separation persisted in practice after legal segregation ended. The University of the Witwatersrand began in 1896 as the South African School of Mines, and the University of Johannesburg was formed in 2005.

Cultural life ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Culture)) is rich and divided, combining orchestral music, jazz, galleries, theatre, museums, libraries, and sport. The Johannesburg Art Gallery and Market Theatre are among the best-known institutions.

## Gold Boomtown

![[attachments/johannesburg_003.webp|420]]

*Gold mining heritage at Gold Reef City in Johannesburg*

Johannesburg's early history began with gold finds. In 1853, Pieter Jacob Marais found alluvial gold near the Jukskei River, but the decisive discovery came in 1886, when George Harrison found an outcrop on Langlaagte. By mid-1886, diggers had rushed to the Witwatersrand.

The Transvaal government sent Christiaan Johannes Joubert and Johann Rissik to choose a city site, and Johannesburg was apparently named in their honour. By 1896, it had about 100,000 people and had become the last great boomtown of the 19th century.

Deep mining required huge capital and tight labour control. By the mid-1890s, a few mining houses run by Randlords such as Alfred Beit, Barney Barnato, and J.B. Robinson dominated the Witwatersrand industry. Their need for cheap labour shaped later racial laws and political pressure on the Transvaal.

## War, Segregation, And Labour

Mining interests helped draw Britain into conflict with the Boer republic. After the failed Jameson Raid of 1895, Britain used the voting rights of white foreign miners, or uitlanders, as a cause. The South African War began in 1899, British troops entered Johannesburg in 1900, and the mines resumed by the end of 1901.

Early segregation appeared through forced removals and pass controls. In 1904, black residents near the centre were relocated to Klipspruit under a sanitation pretext. The 1923 Natives Urban Areas Act helped define urban blacks as temporary workers.

Labour conflict shaped the city. Black workers organized petitions, anti-pass campaigns, and a 1920 mineworkers' strike involving 70,000 workers. White mineworkers also fought mineowners, and the 1922 Rand Revolt was crushed by troops and bombardment.

## Apartheid And After

Industrial growth in the 1930s and 1940s drew large numbers of black workers into Johannesburg. By the early 1940s, manufacturing surpassed mining and agriculture, and wartime labour needs doubled the city's black population to more than 400,000. Overcrowded townships fostered boycotts, unionism, and the ANC Youth League.

Apartheid became central after Daniel F. Malan's National Party won in 1948. Laws fixed where people could live, work, and study by race. Sophiatown was removed from 1955, and between 1960 and 1980, close to four million black South Africans were forcibly relocated.

Resistance exploded on June 16, 1976, when police fired on Soweto students protesting the use of Afrikaans in black schools. The uprising spread nationally. Township unrest returned in 1984, trade unions revived, strikes disrupted the Witwatersrand economy, and sanctions helped push white rulers toward negotiations and democratic elections in 1994.

After apartheid, the ANC government passed the Municipal Structures Act in 1998 to integrate and regulate local government. Crime became especially acute in the 1990s and early 2000s, driving many businesses from the city centre to safer suburbs.

## Key Events & Developments

- 1853 Pieter Jacob Marais: Alluvial gold was found near the future city.
- 1886 George Harrison: The Witwatersrand Main Reef discovery triggered Johannesburg's founding.
- 1896 Boomtown: Johannesburg reached about 100,000 residents.
- 1899-1902 South African War: Britain defeated the Boer republics and took control of the Transvaal.
- 1904 Klipspruit removal: Black residents near the centre were forcibly relocated.
- 1922 Rand Revolt: White mineworkers seized the city before military suppression.
- 1948 Daniel F. Malan: The National Party won power and imposed apartheid.
- June 16, 1976 Soweto uprising: Police shootings of students sparked national resistance.
- 1994 Democratic elections: ANC victory ended white-minority rule.
- 1998 Municipal Structures Act: Post-apartheid local government was further integrated.
