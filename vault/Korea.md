# Korea

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> Korea traces the history of the Korean peninsula from early settlement and ancient kingdoms through Koryo, Choson, Japanese colonial rule, post-1945 division, and the 1953 armistice. Its development reflects indigenous institutions and sustained interaction with neighboring Northeast Asian powers.

## Introduction

Korea ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Korea)) covers the history of the Korean peninsula from prehistoric settlement to the 1953 armistice ending the Korean War. Archaeological, linguistic, and legendary evidence points to Tungusic-speaking peoples moving from Manchuria and Siberia into coastal and river-valley settlements. They formed the main ethnic foundation of the Korean people and developed the Korean language.

Early Korean culture was closely linked with neighbouring northeastern Asian cultures. Neolithic comb pottery, bronze daggers, belt hooks, and knobbed mirrors show regional connections, while local communities developed agriculture, fishing, metal use, clan organization, and eventually tribal states.

## The Stone Age

![[attachments/korea_001.webp|420]]

*Korea 2003-05-01 NASA MODIS Terra 250m*

Paleolithic remains include chipped-stone tools, round fireplaces, dwellings, and carved pebbles from sites such as Kulp'o-ri and Sokch'ang. By about 3000 BCE, the Neolithic Period was established, marked by comb pottery, shell mounds, stone spears, flint arrowheads, bone hooks, and fishing weights.

Late Neolithic farming appears in stone plows and sickles. People lived in dugouts grouped into villages that probably formed clan communities. Bronze appeared by about the 8th century BCE, bringing undecorated pottery, hillside settlements, rice farming tools, bronze weapons, and dolmen tombs for prominent people.

## The Three Kingdoms

Tribal federations developed beyond Old Choson. Puyo arose in the Sungari basin, while Qin south of the Han River divided into Mahan, Chinhan, and Pyonhan. These federations evolved into the rival kingdoms of Koguryo, Paekche, and Silla.

The Three Kingdoms centralized through war, military organization, training institutions, stronger kingship, hereditary monarchy, aristocratic ranks, and provincial administration. Silla's bone-rank system and Council of Nobles show how high aristocrats controlled politics. Buddhism entered as a state religion meant to protect and strengthen each kingdom.

## Unified Silla

Silla, with Chinese support, conquered Paekche in 660 and Koguryo in 668, then drove out Chinese forces by 676. Survivors of Koguryo founded Parhae in northern Manchuria, creating a southern Silla and northern Parhae age, though Korean history usually focuses on Silla.

Unified Silla strengthened absolute monarchy, reduced aristocratic land control, and organized administration through provinces, prefectures, and counties. Buddhism supported both monarchy and aristocracy, while Pure Land Buddhism appealed to common people. Silla art survives in Pulguk Temple, Sokkuram, and the Pongdok Temple bell.

Silla decline came from succession struggles, aristocratic rebellion, provincial magnates, castle lords, peasant unrest, and new regional powers. Kyonhwon founded Later Paekche in 892, and Kungye founded Later Koguryo in 901, producing the Later Three Kingdoms.

## Koryo

Wang Kon founded Koryo at Songdo in 918 and unified the peninsula in 936. Koryo claimed succession from Koguryo, absorbed people from defeated states, fought the Khitan, and expanded to the Yalu River. Its ruling class consisted of provincial castle lords and former Silla aristocrats.

Koryo society was aristocratic. High families gained office through lineage, marriage, and privilege, while politics centred on the Three Chancelleries, Royal Secretariat, and Supreme Council of State. Aristocrats used land as wealth, embraced Buddhism for spiritual life, and used Confucianism for political and ethical order.

Military rule began after discrimination against military officials led to a coup in 1170. Ch'oe Ch'ung-hon established a regime that lasted about 60 years, leaving the king as a figurehead. Peasant rebellions spread, Buddhism retreated into mountain Son communities, and the Chogye sect became central.

Mongol invasion began in 1231, and the Ch'oe regime resisted for nearly 30 years. Civilian leaders overthrew the regime in 1258 and made peace. Later Koryo retained identity under Mongol interference, but large manors, serfdom, weak tax revenue, and landholding abuses weakened the state.

Neo-Confucian scholar-officials rose through examinations and opposed Buddhist privilege and aristocratic land control. With support from Yi Song-gye, they introduced land reform and ended Koryo in 1392, founding the Choson dynasty.

## The Choson (Yi) Dynasty

Choson ruled from 1392 to Japanese annexation in 1910. Hanyang, now Seoul, became the capital. The dynasty adopted Confucian ethics, displaced Buddhism, and created a yangban society in which hereditary civil and military aristocrats controlled office, land, and culture.

Sejong the Great oversaw major cultural achievements. Movable-type printing supported publications in medicine, astronomy, geography, history, and agriculture. The Hall of Worthies was founded in 1420, and the Korean phonetic alphabet Hangul was completed under Sejong's direction in 1443.

Centralized Choson government grew under Sejo, with eight provinces, appointed officials, codified law, and a State Council. Confucian scholarship deepened in the 15th and 16th centuries, and private academies produced thinkers such as Yi Hwang and Yi I.

Foreign invasions devastated Korea. Toyotomi Hideyoshi invaded in 1592 and again in 1597. Admiral Yi Sun-shin won great naval victories, Ming China aided Korea, and Japan withdrew after Toyotomi's death. In the 17th century, Manchu invasions forced Korea to submit and shift tribute from Ming to Qing China.

Silhak and popular culture transformed the 17th and 18th centuries. Improved rice transplantation, irrigation, cash rent, markets, tobacco, ginseng, commerce, and Kaesong merchants changed the economy. Practical Learning scholars emphasized reform, industry, technology, classics, Korean history, geography, and language. Hangul fiction, p'ansori, genre painting, and popular pottery widened culture beyond the elite.

[[Roman Catholicism]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catholic_Church)) entered through Korean contacts with missionaries in China. Some silhak scholars converted by the late 18th century, and commoners later found hope in Christian equality and afterlife belief. The government persecuted Catholics in 1801, 1839, and 1866 because Catholic rejection of ancestor rites clashed with Confucian order.

Ch'ondogyo, or Tonghak, was founded by Ch'oe Che-u in 1860. It mixed reformist ideas with traditional belief and appealed strongly to peasants. Popular unrest grew as boy kings, powerful maternal relatives, weak government, and peasant uprisings destabilized the country.

## Contact With World Powers

King Kojong came to the throne in 1864, while his father, the Taewon-gun, ruled as regent. The Taewon-gun tried to strengthen monarchy and close Korea to foreign powers, resisting American and French approaches and reinforcing exclusionism.

Opening Korea began after the Taewon-gun lost power in 1873. Japan forced a treaty of commerce and friendship, opening Pusan, Wonsan, and Inch'on. Qing China then increased interference, while Korea signed treaties with the United States, Britain, Germany, Russia, and France.

[[Modernization and Industrialization (Modernization)|Modernization]] began unevenly through missions to Japan and China, new schools, newspapers, and reform plans. A radical coup in 1884 lasted only three days before Chinese troops crushed it. The Li-Ito Convention of 1885 tried to preserve a Sino-Japanese balance in Korea.

The Tonghak Uprising of 1894 united peasants and Ch'ondogyo followers against injustice. Chinese and Japanese intervention triggered the Sino-Japanese War. Japan's victory and the Treaty of Shimonoseki in 1895 recognized Japanese dominance in Korea and led to reforms that abolished civil service examinations and class discrimination.

Imperial rivalry intensified after Japan's rise. Japan engineered Queen Min's assassination in 1895, while King Kojong took refuge in the Russian legation. So Chae-p'il organized the Independence Club and promoted sovereignty and civil rights. After the Russo-Japanese War of 1904-1905, Japan forced Korea into protectorate status, disbanded the army, and annexed Korea in 1910.

## Korea Under Japanese Rule

Japanese colonial rule deprived Koreans of assembly, association, press, and speech freedoms. Schools promoted Japanese language and assimilation while excluding Korean language and history. Land surveys dispossessed many farmers, and transport, communications, finance, and commerce were built to serve Japanese rule.

The March First Movement began on March 1, 1919, after former emperor Kojong's death. A Declaration of Independence was read in Seoul, and about two million people joined peaceful demonstrations. Japan violently suppressed them, arresting about 47,000 Koreans and killing about 7,500.

Korean resistance continued through the provisional government in Shanghai, newspapers, cultural nationalism, student movements, and exile networks. Japan relaxed some open military controls but kept exploitation. Rice-production programs served Japanese needs, forcing many Koreans to live on poorer imported grains.

Wartime Japanization intensified after 1931, 1937, and 1941. Koreans were forced to worship at Shinto shrines, adopt Japanese-style names, and give labour and military service. Korean-language publications and research groups were banned, and many Korean women were forced into military sexual slavery.

## Division Of Korea

![[attachments/korea_003.webp|420]]

*1894JoseonSeoul*

The Cairo Declaration of 1943 promised Korean independence in due course, but no clear plan followed. At Yalta in 1945, [[Franklin D. Roosevelt]] proposed trusteeship, and at Potsdam the Allies repeated the promise of independence. After Soviet entry into the war, Japanese surrender was divided at the 38th parallel: Soviets in the north, Americans in the south.

The southern zone became politically confused after liberation. The U.S. military government refused to recognize the People's Republic of Korea or the returning provisional government. Advisory and legislative bodies were created, but trusteeship caused strong opposition.

The northern zone came under Soviet control through Korean communists placed in key positions. Kim Il-sung arrived in P'yongyang in 1945, was presented as a national hero, and consolidated power through the Provisional People's Committee and later the Supreme People's Assembly.

Two republics emerged after U.S.-Soviet negotiations failed. UN-supervised elections were held only in the south in 1948. The Republic of Korea was inaugurated on August 15 with Syngman Rhee as president. The Democratic People's Republic of Korea was proclaimed on September 9 with Kim Il-sung as premier.

## The Korean War

![[attachments/korea_002.webp|420]]

*War Memorial of Korea main building*

War preparations were unequal. South Korea had about 98,000 lightly armed troops by June 1950, while North Korea had about 135,000 troops, Soviet equipment, a tank brigade, advisers, and Korean veterans transferred from China. North Korea invaded on June 25, 1950.

UN intervention followed quickly because the Soviet delegate was boycotting the Security Council and could not veto action. Harry S. Truman ordered U.S. air and naval forces on June 27 and ground forces on June 30. Seoul fell on June 28, but a UN command under Douglas MacArthur formed with forces from 16 countries, mainly supplied by the United States.

The war's first phase swung sharply. UN forces held a perimeter around Pusan, then MacArthur's Inch'on landing on September 15 trapped North Korean forces. UN forces crossed the 38th parallel, entered P'yongyang, and reached the Yalu River before Chinese intervention in November forced them back.

Chinese intervention brought massive forces under Peng Dehuai. Seoul was evacuated again in January 1951, but UN forces recovered to the 38th parallel by March. MacArthur's public call to expand the war to China led Truman to dismiss him in April, replacing him with Matthew B. Ridgway.

Armistice talks began in July 1951 and stalled over foreign troops, boundaries, and prisoners. The prisoner issue was hardest because many communist prisoners refused repatriation. After Stalin's death, exchanges resumed, Rhee resisted division, and the United States promised South Korea aid and a security pact.

The armistice was signed on July 27, 1953. The battle line became the border, and a demilitarized zone was created by pulling forces back on each side. The war lasted three years and one month, caused about four million casualties, destroyed two-fifths of Korea's industrial facilities, and devastated one-third of its homes.
