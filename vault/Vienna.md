# Vienna

## German Wien, Czech Viden, Hungarian Becs

![[attachments/vienna_001.webp|420]]

*Vienna skyline with St. Stephen's Cathedral*

Vienna is both the capital of Austria and a federal state. It is Austria's smallest state by area but its largest by population. From 1558 to 1918 it was an imperial city: first connected with the Holy Roman Empire ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holy_Roman_Empire)), then with the Austro-Hungarian Empire ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Austria-Hungary)). After [[World Wars (World War I)|World War I]] it became the capital of a smaller republic; from 1938 to 1945 it was part of Nazi Greater Germany; and after the State Treaty of 1955 it again became the capital of independent Austria.

The central Innere Stadt ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Innere_Stadt)) remains one of the least spoiled old capitals of western Europe. Its skyline is still marked by St. Stephen's Cathedral and the Ferris wheel in the Prater. Though bombing caused serious damage near the end of [[World Wars (World War II)|World War II]], rebuilding preserved much of the city's pre-1914 character. Viennese Lebenskunst, or art of living, survives in concert halls, theatres, coffeehouses, wine taverns, parks, shopping streets, and slow public habits supported by strong health and welfare services.

## Site

Vienna lies in northeastern Austria, between the foothills of the Alps and the Carpathians. Most of the city stands on the right bank of the Danube, Europe's second-longest river. The Vienna basin became important because it joined north-south movement along the amber route with east-west movement along the Danube.

Its position gave Vienna military and commercial value near regions bordering [[Czech and Slovak Republics (Slovakia)|Slovakia]], the [[Czech and Slovak Republics (Czech Republic)|Czech Republic]], and [[Hungary]]. This geography helped make the city a central European meeting point for trade, armies, empire, and later diplomacy.

## Climate

Vienna has a generally temperate climate because the Vienna Woods soften some Alpine effects. Northern winds can bring cool summers and milder winters, while southeastern winds can bring summer heat and winter cold. The yearly average temperature is above 50 F (10 C).

Rainfall is moderate, about 26 inches (66 cm) a year, mostly in summer downpours. The local Lufterl, a light breeze from the northwest and west, gives welcome relief during hot summer evenings.

## Layout and Architecture

![[attachments/vienna_003.webp|420]]

*Schonbrunn Palace in Vienna*

Vienna reaches across the Danube and rises toward the Vienna Woods, including Kahlenberg and Hermannskogel. The Danube was straightened in the 19th century, and the Danube Canal became a flood-control channel. Former floodlands became a long recreational island, while the Lobau and Vienna Woods remained protected greenbelts.

The city has 23 districts. The Innere Stadt ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Innere_Stadt)) contains many famous monuments and is encircled by the Ringstrasse, a boulevard lined with grand public buildings and parks. Leopoldstadt, assigned to Jews in 1622, also contains the Prater, an aristocratic preserve opened as a public park in 1766. Beyond the Gurtel are outer suburbs and the Central Cemetery.

St. Stephen's Cathedral is a major Gothic church with Romanesque remains, later medieval rebuilding, and post-[[World Wars (World War II)|World War II]] restoration. Other important churches include St. Ruprecht's, the Church of the Augustinians, Maria am Gestade, St. Peter's, the University Church, and the Capuchin church with the Habsburg crypt. Baroque became the city's leading style in the 17th and 18th centuries, visible in churches, palaces, and noble houses.

The Hofburg is a large imperial complex containing apartments, museums, the imperial treasury, the Austrian National Library, the Albertina, the Spanish Riding School, and presidential offices. The Ringstrasse added 19th-century historicist landmarks such as the University of Vienna, City Hall, Burgtheater, Parliament, the museums, the Neue Hofburg, and the Vienna State Opera. Schonbrunn Palace, the Belvedere, the Church of St. Charles, Theater an der Wien, the Plague Column, and Joseph's Fountain connect imperial residence, music, religion, and civic memory.

## People, Ethnicity, and Language

Vienna lost population around [[World Wars (World War II)|World War II]] through Jewish emigration and murder, boundary changes, and movement away from the Soviet-occupied east. Between 1934 and 1951 the population fell by about 15 percent. Immigration from eastern Europe in the late 1980s and early 1990s helped restore the population to more than 1,600,000.

The Viennese are a mixed population shaped by ancestors from Bohemia, [[Hungary]], [[Poland]], the Balkan Peninsula, Germany, and other Austrian regions. Wienerisch, the local speech, contains Roman, Italian, Spanish, French, Magyar, Slavic, Turkish, and Yiddish traces and helps mark local identity apart from northern German speech.

## Religion

Vienna is the seat of a Roman Catholic archbishop and a Protestant bishop. Most residents have historically been Roman Catholic, but the city is strongly secular and active religious practice is much lower.

The prewar Jewish community numbered more than 160,000 and was culturally and economically important. About two-thirds escaped Nazi rule, while most who remained were murdered, except for a small number who survived in hiding or camps. Jews later formed less than 1 percent of the population.

## Economy

Vienna depends on commerce, industry, public administration, services, and tourism. It provides about one-fourth of Austria's jobs and almost one-third of its gross national product. Tourism brings about two million visitors each year, and public institutions, including theatres, are major employers.

The city produces more than half of Austria's capital goods and almost half of its consumer goods. Machinery, electrical products, chemicals, metals, oil processing, cement, brickmaking, textiles, ceramics, jewelry, scientific and musical instruments, furniture, paper, and carpets all appear in its industrial base. Banking and other service industries are especially important, and the Vienna international trade fair supports trade with Europe and overseas markets.

## Transportation

In the 19th century, immigrants from the Austro-Hungarian Empire arrived through six major railway terminals. Later the South Railway Station and West Railway Station remained the main stations, while Schwechat airport connected the city with more than 30 airlines.

Freight movement on the Danube links Vienna with the Balkan states, Romania, the Black Sea, and northwestern Europe through the Rhine-Main-Danube route. After [[World Wars (World War II)|World War II]], Vienna kept and modernized its tramway system and expanded the underground, so public transport and walking became the usual ways to move within the city.

## Government

Vienna is both a capital city and a federal state, so the same elected representatives act in separate municipal and state roles. Its 23 districts are managed by appointed magistrates. A mayor, two deputies, and a 100-member city council govern the city, and the mayor also serves as state governor.

The city sends 28 members to the National Council and 11 to the Federal Council. It also operates Vienna Holding, a mixed public-private enterprise with restaurants, publishing, insurance, shopping centres, cinemas, the Stadthalle, the restored Theater an der Wien, and the revived porcelain factory.

Vienna's health and welfare system has long been admired. Joseph II founded the General Hospital in 1784, and 19th-century Viennese medicine became famous through figures such as Ignaz Philipp Semmelweis, Theodor Billroth, Karl Landsteiner, and Sigmund Freud. The city has many doctors, more than 40 hospitals, geriatric facilities, public welfare, social insurance, and clean mountain-spring water.

## Education

Vienna has a higher share of high-school and university graduates than other Austrian states. Five of Austria's 12 universities are in the city: the University of Vienna, the University of Technology, the University of Agriculture, the University of Veterinary Medicine, and the University of Economics.

The city also has academies of music, drama, fine arts, and applied arts, plus scientific societies, research institutes, and the Austrian Academy of Sciences. Training in [[The Art of Music (Music)|music]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Music)), medicine, law, and the arts attracts foreign students, who make up about 10 percent of the student population.

## Cultural Life

Vienna is Austria's main cultural centre and one of the world's great music capitals. Opera, concerts, and theatre have shaped city life for centuries. The Society of Friends of Music, founded in 1812, the Vienna Boys' Choir, founded in 1498, and the Vienna Philharmonic Orchestra preserve this musical role.

The State Opera, People's Opera, Burgtheater, and Academy Theatre are major institutions. The State Opera opened in 1869 with [[Mozart]]'s Don Giovanni, was destroyed in [[World Wars (World War II)|World War II]], and reopened in 1955 with [[Beethoven]]'s Fidelio. The Burgtheater, founded in 1776, remains one of Europe's leading German-language theatres.

Vienna's [[Museums (Museums, History of)|museums]], libraries, and historic houses preserve art and memory. The Albertina, Kunsthistorisches Museum, Academy of Fine Arts, Imperial Treasury, natural history and technology museums, Roman remains, St. Stephen's catacombs, the Habsburg Imperial Vault, Schonbrunn rooms, and homes linked with Haydn, [[Mozart]], [[Beethoven]], Schubert, Johann Strauss, and [[Freud]] connect culture with the cityscape.

The coffeehouse has been a Viennese institution for about three centuries, functioning as a second living room for coffee, pastries, newspapers, cards, and conversation. Heuriger taverns in districts such as Grinzing, Nussdorf, and Sievering serve young local wine, often with small musical groups.

## History

Human settlement at Vienna reaches back to the Paleolithic Period. Illyrians and Celts later lived there, and Roman Vindobona became a strategic garrison and trade point. Marcus Aurelius is said to have died there in AD 180. After 5th-century invasions, the Roman remains formed the nucleus of the medieval city.

The Babenberg dukes ruled from 1156 to 1246, making Vienna a trading centre and a supply point for Crusaders. After the last male Babenberg died, Otakar II of Bohemia took control but was defeated by Rudolf I of the Habsburg dynasty in 1278. The Habsburgs then held the region for more than 600 years.

During the Renaissance, Vienna was important in science, art, and humanism, but Ferdinand I reduced its rights after a rebellion. Turkish forces besieged the city in 1529 and failed. The Reformation brought conflict, and many Protestants left because Vienna had to remain Roman Catholic. A plague in 1679 killed nearly a third of the population, and a second Turkish siege in 1683 was defeated with help from John III Sobieski. Afterward, Prince Eugene of Savoy helped drive the Turks from Hungary.

The end of the Turkish threat led to a great Baroque building period between 1700 and 1730. Architects such as Johann Lucas von Hildebrandt and J.B. Fischer von Erlach shaped palaces, churches, and noble houses, while immigration and factories moved the city from trade toward manufacturing.

Maria Theresa, ruling from 1740 to 1780, made primary education compulsory, put the university under state control, and reorganized the economy, army, and courts. Joseph II continued Enlightenment reform with religious toleration in 1781, fewer restrictions on Jews in 1782, humanitarian measures, and support for education and the arts. By 1790, Vienna had 300 factories and 235,000 people.

Vienna's music and politics grew together. Gluck, Haydn, and [[Mozart]] began a golden age continued by [[Beethoven]] and Schubert. Francis II declared himself emperor of Austria in 1804 and ended the Holy Roman Empire in 1806. [[Napoleon]] occupied the city in 1805 and 1809, but the Congress of Vienna in 1814-15, led by Prince Metternich, made it central to Europe's restoration.

The revolution of 1848 ended Metternich's rule, though later revolt was crushed by Francis Joseph's army. Modern Vienna expanded after the inner walls were removed in 1857 and the Ringstrasse opened in 1865. Better water, Danube regulation, gas and electric works, free trade, city self-government in 1861, suburban incorporation in 1890, and the Gurtel helped form the modern city.

Around 1900, Vienna had nearly two million people and became a centre of modern culture and ideas. Music and operetta flourished through Brahms, Anton Bruckner, Mahler, the younger Johann Strauss, and others. Adolf Hitler absorbed nationalist and anti-Semitic ideas there; Theodor Herzl developed Zionism; and Sigmund Freud and Alfred Adler shaped psychology. The Sezessionstil movement included Otto Wagner, Adolf Loos, Josef Hoffmann, Gustav Klimt, Oskar Kokoschka, and Egon Schiele.

After [[World Wars (World War I)|World War I]], the Habsburg monarchy collapsed and a republic was proclaimed in 1918. Social Democrats won the city council in 1919 and made Red Vienna famous for housing, education, welfare, and health reforms. In 1922, Vienna became a federal province. Economic crisis and street violence weakened the First Republic; Engelbert Dollfuss dissolved Parliament in 1933, and the attack on workers' apartment blocks in 1934 deepened the sense that democracy had failed.

On March 12, 1938, German troops occupied Austria, and Adolf Hitler proclaimed the Anschluss. Nazi rule suppressed Austrian identity, persecuted Jews and political enemies, and brought war damage. Soviet troops liberated Vienna in April 1945. The city was divided among four Allied powers, with the first district as an international zone, and became a Cold War ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cold_War)) espionage centre.

Nearly a quarter of Vienna's buildings were damaged or destroyed, and the population fell to 1,322,000. Mayors Theodor Korner and Franz Jonas led reconstruction. The Austrian State Treaty, signed in the Belvedere on May 15, 1955, ended occupation. Though 1918, 1938, and 1945 weakened Vienna's geopolitical position, it remained an East-West meeting place and later hosted OPEC, the International Atomic Energy Agency, the United Nations Industrial Development Organization, and other bodies at the Vienna International Centre.
