# Protozoa (Protozoan)

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> Protozoan is an informal name for diverse single-celled, usually heterotrophic eukaryotes rather than a natural evolutionary group. The source explains their locomotion, symbioses, parasitism, reduced energy organelles, cysts, mixotrophy, disease relevance, and replacement by phylogenetic classification.

## Introduction

Protozoans ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Protozoa)) are usually single-celled, heterotrophic eukaryotes that live in moist or aquatic habitats. They have a true nucleus, lack fungal hyphae, and occur worldwide from the South Pole to the North Pole. Many live freely in water or soil, while others are symbionts or parasites of animals, plants, and other organisms.

The term protozoan is now mostly informal. It once meant animal-like protists that could move and feed like small animals, and older biology often treated them as ancestors of animals. Modern ultrastructural, biochemical, and genetic evidence shows that protozoans do not form one natural lineage. Because the group is paraphyletic, the formal subkingdom Protozoa ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Protozoa)) is considered obsolete.

Noctiluca scintillans illustrates the older habit of grouping diverse protists by visible animal-like traits. Such organisms can be motile and heterotrophic, but their evolutionary relationships may be distant from one another. This helps explain why protozoan remains useful as a descriptive word but not as a precise taxonomic rank.

## Amoeba

Amoebas, paramecia, dinoflagellates, and the malaria parasite Plasmodium are common examples of organisms often called protozoans. They differ greatly in movement, feeding, cell structure, and life history. Their shared label depends mainly on being nonfilamentous heterotrophic protists, not on descent from one common protozoan ancestor.

## Representative

Representative protozoans show how unstable the category is. Many are heterotrophs, yet some are mixotrophs that combine heterotrophy with photosynthesis or benefit from photosynthetic partners. Paramecium bursaria carries eukaryotic algae, while Paulinella chromatophora appears to have acquired autotrophy through a relatively recent cyanobacterial endosymbiosis.

Motility is also common but not unique to protozoans. Many use flagella, cilia, or pseudopodia, but other non-protozoan organisms also produce flagella during parts of their life cycles. Protozoans are not truly multicellular, although colonial forms such as Dictyostelium discoideum can show cell specialization close to multicellularity.

## Photomicrograph

Several protozoans are important parasites. Trypanosoma cruzi, the cause of Chagas disease, has intracellular amastigote stages. Related Leishmania species infect macrophages and are spread by sand flies; cutaneous forms remain near the infection site, visceral forms enlarge the spleen and liver in kala-azar, and mucocutaneous forms can damage the nose, mouth, and throat.

Plasmodium causes malaria and remains difficult to control despite mosquito-control measures and antimalarial drugs. The five species have broadly similar life cycles, but disease severity, attack frequency, and relapse vary. Control is complicated by insecticide resistance in mosquitoes and drug resistance in parasites, though the absence of strong antigenic variation is promising for vaccine work.

Other parasitic protozoans include Cryptosporidium, which infects humans and other animals, and forms associated with intestinal or urogenital disease. These examples show that protozoan biology is not only a question of classification but also of public health, host relationships, and specialized life cycles.

## Trichomonas

Trichomonas vaginalis causes the sexually transmitted disease trichomoniasis. Trichomonads are anaerobic parasites and are related to hypermastigotes, which are obligate symbionts of wood-digesting insects. Oxymonads are another large group of anaerobic symbionts in such insects.

Some anaerobic protozoans are free-living. Sulfur-associated ciliates such as Metopus, Plagiopyla, and Caenomorpha live in sulfide-containing sediments and carry bacterial symbionts. These bacteria may reuse metabolic products released by the ciliates for growth and energy production.

## Hydrogenosomes

Many anaerobic protozoans lack typical aerobic mitochondrial metabolism. Instead of using the tricarboxylic acid cycle and electron transport chains, they rely on substrate-level phosphorylation to make ATP. The hydrogenosome is a double-membraned organelle that partially oxidizes pyruvate to acetate, producing carbon dioxide, molecular hydrogen, and ATP.

Hydrogenosomes ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrogenosome)) occur in trichomonads, hypermastigotes, and some euglenids, and they are thought to have evolved from mitochondria. Mitosomes are another reduced organelle found in diplomonads such as Giardia and first described in Entamoeba histolytica. Blood parasites such as Trypanosoma brucei use glycosomes, peroxisome-related organelles that contain glycolytic enzymes.

## Mixotrophy

All protozoans are heterotrophic in the broad descriptive sense, but some combine heterotrophy with autotrophy. Mixotrophs may rely heavily on symbiotic algae, temporarily keep plastids from prey, or switch between photosynthesis and feeding depending on light and available substrates. This makes nutrition a spectrum rather than a simple animal-like trait.

Many protozoans form photosynthetic partnerships. Stentor and Acanthocystis can form temporary associations with Chlorella, while Paramecium bursaria forms a longer-lasting symbiosis but must reacquire the alga in each generation. Euglenids and cryptomonads may use photosynthesis in light and feed on acetate, fatty acids, or alcohols when light is absent.

Some amoebae and ciliates practice plastid retention rather than keeping whole algal cells. The marine ciliate Tontonia appendiculariformis may contain thousands of plastids taken from flagellate prey. Because these plastids do not reproduce inside the host, they must be replaced continually through feeding.

## Adaptations

Parasitic protozoans often live in stable host environments with steady temperature, moisture, and food. Free-living forms face changing temperature, acidity, food supply, moisture, and light. Many survive bad conditions by encysting, building a thick wall and entering a quiet state similar to hibernation.

Cysts help protozoans disperse by wind and survive in soil, temporary ponds, pools, and seasonal climates. During encystment, flagella or cilia may disappear, contractile and food vacuoles vanish, organelles are reorganized, and cell volume may shrink. Excystment reverses these changes when conditions improve.

Some life cycles are seasonally timed. The tintinnid Helicostomella subulata excysts in June in temperate waters, becomes common from July through October, and then encysts again in October, sinking to sediments until the next year. Even laboratory populations can keep the same seasonal rhythm.

## Classification

Protozoan classification ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taxonomy)) changed strongly after Carl Woese and colleagues introduced the three-domain system in 1990. Based on rRNA sequences, this system divided life into Eukarya, Bacteria, and Archaea. It replaced much of the older five-kingdom model of Robert H. Whittaker, which relied more on morphology and nutrition.

Within Eukarya, classification moved away from fixed Linnean ranks at higher levels and toward phylogenetic supergroups. Animals, plants, and fungi are now placed within broader eukaryotic groups that also include protists. This nested picture shows why protozoans appear across many lineages rather than forming one kingdom or subkingdom.

## Archaeplastida

Archaeplastida consists mostly of photosynthetic algae descended from a heterotrophic ancestor that acquired a plastid by primary endosymbiosis of a cyanobacterium. Plastids usually have two membranes. The monophyletic Plantae arose from an archaeplastidan ancestor, linking unicellular algae and land plants.

## Glaucophyta

Glaucophyta live in fresh water and contain blue-green plastids called cyanelles, with peptidoglycan remnants between their two membranes. Red algae lack motile cells, have special pit plugs, and may have biphasic or triphasic life cycles. Chloroplastida, or green algae, store starch in plastids and share chlorophylls and carotenoids with land plants.

## Excavata

Excavata are mainly heterotrophs with a suspension-feeding groove and a recurrent flagellum. Placement of Heterolobosea and Euglenozoa remains debated because morphology and genetic evidence do not always agree. The group contains many small flagellates and anaerobic lineages.

## Fornicata

Fornicata possess a distinctive B fibre against one microtubular root. This structural trait helps separate them from other excavate groups and reflects the importance of fine cell architecture in modern classification.

## Carpediemonas

Carpediemonas are biflagellated free-living unicells with a broad cytostome and a posterior-directed flagellum. Their feeding groove connects them to the excavate pattern of suspension feeding.

## Eopharyngia

Eopharyngia lack typical mitochondria and possess a single kinetid and nucleus. Their reduced energy organelles reflect adaptation to low-oxygen or specialized environments.

## Diplomonadida

Diplomonadida are binucleate and have duplicated flagellar apparatuses. Some descendants are mononucleate with one flagellar apparatus, showing how cell organization varies within related anaerobic groups.

## Retortamonadida

Retortamonadida include two genera marked by a nuclear papillum or lapel connected to the flagellar apparatus. They do not possess typical mitochondria, which links them to other reduced excavates.

## Malawimonas

Malawimonas possess mitochondria, two kinetosomes, and a single ventral flagellar vane. Their traits are important because they combine excavate-like feeding structures with retained mitochondria.

## Parabasalia

Parabasalia have a unique parabasal Golgi body. Their two major groups are trichomonads and hypermastigotes, both important in anaerobic symbiosis or parasitism.

## Oxymonadida

Oxymonadida have a unique microtubule-based articulate axostyle. They are known only as symbionts of wood-digesting insects, and some attach to the insect gut with a holdfast called a rostellum.

## Trimastix

Trimastix are free-living quadriflagellates with a broad cytostome and posterior-directed flagellum. Their mitochondria are replaced by small dense organelles, showing another reduced-energy adaptation.

## Jakobida

Jakobida possess tubular mitochondrial cristae and a multilayered structure associated with basal bodies. Their mitochondrial genome is described as ancestral, making them important for understanding early eukaryotic evolution.

## Euglenozoa

Euglenozoa have a paraxial rod associated with at least one flagellum, two functional basal bodies, tubular extrusomes, and discoidal mitochondrial cristae. They include autotrophic and heterotrophic taxa. Their placement within Excavata is based on genetic similarity, though it remains debated.

## Euglenida

Euglenida move with pellicle strips that create euglenid metaboly. Their tubular extrusomes are reduced to mucocysts between the pellicle strips, giving them a distinctive surface organization.

## Kinetoplastea

Kinetoplastea contain a kinetoplast, a large DNA mass in the mitochondrion. The group includes free-living bodontids and parasitic trypanosomes, making it biologically and medically important.

## Diplonemea

Diplonemea are heterotrophic, and their vegetative phase lacks paraxial rods. They are treated as part of the wider euglenozoan-related diversity.

## Heterolobosea

Heterolobosea often show amoeboid, flagellated, and encysted forms. Their pseudopodia differ from those of Amoebozoa. Genetic evidence places them near Excavata, and they are often grouped with euglenozoans in Discicristata.

## Chromalveolata

Chromalveolata are described as descendants of a heterotrophic ancestor that acquired a red algal plastid by secondary endosymbiosis. Some subgroups, such as ciliates, later lost the plastid. Autotrophic members usually contain chlorophyll c.

## Alveolata

Alveolata have alveolar sacs beneath the plasma membrane, transverse cell division, and tubular mitochondrial cristae. The alveoli may contain rigid material that gives the cell surface a distinctive texture.

## Ciliophora

Ciliophora possess many duplicated kinetids, true ciliation, and nuclear dimorphism with a diploid micronucleus and a polyploid macronucleus. Dinoflagellates have longitudinal and transverse flagella that create spiraling motion and unusual mesokaryotic genome organization.

## Apicomplexa

Apicomplexa are parasites with an apical complex used in host colonization. This group includes important disease-causing protozoans and shows how cell structures can be specialized for invasion.

## Haptophyta

Haptophyta are photosynthetic and possess a haptonema, a third flagellum-like structure used in feeding in many mixotrophic forms. Coccolithophores produce calcium carbonate scales called coccoliths, while other clades include pavlovaleans and Prymnesiales.

## Cryptophyceae

Cryptophyceae are motile unicells that may be autotrophic, heterotrophic, or mixotrophic. They contain a nucleomorph in the periplastidal space, starch storage products, and flagella inserted into a depression called a vestibulum.

## Stramenopiles

Stramenopiles include heterotrophic and mainly autotrophic clades. Their derived trait is a tubular tripartite flagellar hair, and autotrophic forms often use fucoxanthin as the dominant accessory pigment. The group includes many secondarily heterotrophic lineages.

## Labyrinthulomycetes

Labyrinthulomycetes are absorptive heterotrophs that live within ectoplasmic membranes. Their fungus-like nutrition shows why older schemes often misplaced some protists.

## Peronosporomycetes

Peronosporomycetes are absorptive heterotrophs with coenocytic hyphae, diploid life cycles, oogamous reproduction, and glucan-cellulose walls. Their biflagellate zoospores are heterokont, linking them to stramenopile organization.

## Bicosoecida

Bicosoecida are small biflagellate ingestive heterotrophs without plastids. They often attach to surfaces by the posterior flagellum, live in loricae, and occur as solitary or colonial forms in marine and freshwater habitats.

## Hypochytriales

Hypochytriales are absorptive heterotrophs that grow as chytridlike unicells or sometimes hyphae. Golden algae, or chrysophyceans, are mostly freshwater, often mixotrophic, and commonly produce stomatocysts and siliceous coverings.

## Synurales

Synurales produce stomatocysts, lack chlorophyll c2, and have a unique flagellar root system. These traits separate them from other golden-algal relatives.

## Eustigmatales

Eustigmatales are small coccoid unicells in the vegetative phase. They lack fucoxanthin and chlorophyll c, appear yellow-green, and their motile cells contain a prominent eyespot.

## Pelagophyceae

Pelagophyceae include autotrophic, heterotrophic, and mixotrophic taxa. Most are marine, and silicoflagellates form a successful group of marine phytoplankton.

## Raphidophyceae

Raphidophyceae are flagellated unicells with peripherally aligned trichocysts and chloroplasts. Some contain many plastids and can form palmelloid and cyst stages.

## Xanthophyceae

Xanthophyceae occur mostly in fresh water and soil and lack fucoxanthin. Nearby stramenopile groups include brown algae, diatoms, heliozoans, and other flagellates with diverse shells, skeletons, pigments, and storage products.

## Bolidomonas

Bolidomonas are naked unicellular flagellates. Their chloroplast endoplasmic reticulum connects directly to the nuclear envelope, and their plastid DNA has a ring-type genophore.

## Dictyochophyceae

Dictyochophyceae may be solitary or colonial flagellates or amoebae. They can be naked, scaled, or silica-skeleton-bearing, and their flagellated cells have a paraflagellar rod.

## Opalinata

Opalinata have multiple cilia with a double-stranded transitional helix, and these cilia differ from true cilia. They are usually binucleate or multinucleate parasites of amphibians, reptiles, and fish.

## Phaeothamniophyceae

Phaeothamniophyceae may be filamentous, coccoid, capsoid, or palmelloid. Their chloroplasts have girdle lamellae, eyespots are present, and flagellated cells have two flagella with tripartite hairs on the anterior one.

## Pinguiochrysidales

Pinguiochrysidales may be flagellated or coccoid, naked or enclosed in mineralized loricae. Their chloroplasts have girdle lamellae, eyespots are absent, and their kinetosome roots include a large rhizoplast.

## Schizocladia

Schizocladia form branched filaments during the vegetative phase. Their walls contain alginates but lack cellulose and plasmodesmata, and their anterior flagellum carries tripartite mastigonemes.

## Rhizaria

Rhizaria consist of amoebae and amoeboflagellates with thin filopods, often reinforced by microtubules and often living inside tests. Their filose pseudopods are commonly used for prey capture and food selection.

## Cercozoa

Cercozoa are a diverse clade with tubular mitochondrial cristae, common cysts, microbodies, and extrusomes. Their kinetosomes connect to the nucleus through the cytoskeleton.

## Haplosporidia

Haplosporidia are parasites of aquatic animals and possess distinctive spores. Their placement among rhizarian-related groups reflects modern reliance on cell and molecular evidence.

## Foraminifera

Foraminifera have reticulate pseudopods with granular cytoplasm and bidirectional streaming. They form chambered tests, usually made of calcium carbonate, and are important fossil-forming protists.

## Gromia

Gromia has nongranular cytoplasm, an organic test, and filopodia that are not reticulate. These traits distinguish it from foraminiferans.

## Radiolaria

Radiolaria make skeletons of amorphous silica, or in acantharians, strontium sulfate. Their filopods are reinforced by microtubules, linking them to rhizarian cell architecture.

## Amoebozoa

Amoebozoa are amoeboid organisms with lobose pseudopods that are not supported by internal microtubules. Both naked and testate forms exist, and the group includes several amoeboid lineages.

## Tubulinea

Tubulinea are naked or testate amoebae that can produce tubular subcylindrical pseudopodia. They lack centrosomes and flagellated stages.

## Flabellinea

Flabellinea are flat amoebae that lack subcylindrical pseudopodia, centrosomes, and flagellated stages. Their flattened form separates them from tubuline amoebae.

## Stereomyxida

Stereomyxida form branched or reticulate networks and have trilaminate centrosomes. Their network-like organization is distinctive among amoebozoans.

## Acanthamoebidae

Acanthamoebidae are uninucleate cells with thin glycocalyx, prominent subpseudopodia, nonadhesive uroids, and double-walled cysts. These traits support their recognition as a separate amoebozoan group.

## Entamoebida

Entamoebida lack flagella, centrioles, mitochondria, hydrogenosomes, and peroxisomes. They have closed mitosis and reduced Golgi dictyosomes, showing strong cellular reduction.

## Mastigamoebidae

Mastigamoebidae have several pseudopodia and a single anterior flagellum, although some life stages lack flagella. Some taxa are multinucleate and lack mitochondria.

## Pelomyxa

Pelomyxa is anaerobic and lacks mitochondria, peroxisomes, and hydrogenosomes. Eumycetozoan slime molds produce fruiting bodies and have life stages that may be amoeboflagellate, nonflagellate amoeboid, or multinucleate.

## Opisthokonta

Opisthokonta includes animals and fungi together with protistan relatives. Its presence in protozoan classification shows that single-celled lineages can sit beside major multicellular kingdoms within modern eukaryotic supergroups.

## Mesomycetozoa

Mesomycetozoa are opisthokont-related protists that help bridge discussions of animal, fungal, and protistan relationships. Their inclusion reinforces the modern view that protozoan-like organisms are distributed across eukaryotic evolution.

## Key Events & Developments

- 1990: Carl Woese and colleagues introduced the three-domain system, reshaping protozoan classification around rRNA evidence.
- 2005: Paulinella chromatophora was recognized as an amoebozoan with primary plastids, complicating older ideas about plastid origins.
- June to October: Helicostomella subulata shows seasonal excystment, population growth, and encystment in temperate waters.

## Key People

- Carl Woese ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carl_Woese)): Led the three-domain shift that changed how protozoans and all life are classified.
- Robert H. Whittaker: Developed the older five-kingdom system based strongly on morphology and nutrition.
- Paulinella chromatophora: Important organism showing relatively recent primary endosymbiosis in an amoeboid lineage.

## Interesting Facts

- Protozoan ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Protozoa)) is no longer a formal taxonomic group, but it remains useful for nonfilamentous heterotrophic protists.
- Hydrogenosomes ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrogenosome)), mitosomes, and glycosomes show how protozoans modified or replaced standard mitochondrial energy systems.
- Mixotrophy lets some protozoans photosynthesize, feed, or borrow plastids from prey depending on conditions.
- Cysts allow many protozoans to survive harsh conditions and disperse through wind, sediment, or seasonal habitats.
