# Luther

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> Martin Luther was the German theologian whose critique of church authority and doctrine catalyzed the Protestant Reformation. His teaching and controversy divided Western Christianity, stimulated new Protestant traditions, and reshaped European religious, political, and cultural life.

## Introduction

Martin Luther, born in Eisleben on Nov. 10, 1483 and dead there on Feb. 18, 1546, was the German theologian and reformer whose words and actions catalyzed the 16th-century Protestant Reformation. The movement divided Western Christendom between Roman Catholicism and new Protestant traditions, especially Lutheranism, Calvinism, Anglicanism, Anabaptism, and Antitrinitarianism.

Luther's early life moved from Eisleben to Mansfeld, where his father Hans Luther prospered in copper refining and became a town councillor in 1492. Luther studied Latin, prayers, commandments, and creed at Mansfeld, attended a Brethren of the Common Life school at Magdeburg in 1497, and entered the University of Erfurt in 1501.

At Erfurt, Luther completed liberal arts training, earned a baccalaureate in 1502 and master's degree three years later, and encountered Scholasticism. His father expected law, but a thunderstorm near Stotternheim in 1505 became the catalyst for a vow to become a monk.

## From Monk to Reformer

On July 17, 1505, Luther left legal study and entered the Erfurt monastery of the Order of the Hermits of St. Augustine. He made general confession on Sept. 15, 1505, became a novice, and entered a strict monastic life of work, worship, canonical hours, poverty, and preparation for priesthood.

Luther was ordained and sent into study and teaching. A mission to [[Rome]] for observant Augustinian houses failed and left him with a negative impression of spirituality at the centre of Western Christendom. After returning, he transferred to Wittenberg, earned a doctorate in 1512, and became professor of biblical studies.

At Wittenberg, Luther lectured on Psalms, Romans, Galatians, Hebrews, and other biblical texts while taking on administrative duties. His early theological development is hard to trace precisely, but his teaching, spiritual anxiety, and study of Paul prepared the ground for later conflict.

The indulgences controversy ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ninety-five_Theses)) began in 1517, when Luther objected to Johann Tetzel's preaching on indulgences. His Ninety-five Theses questioned the sale and theology of indulgences and made his name widely known in Germany.

By the end of 1518, Luther had reached a new understanding of salvation. Against traditions that joined divine grace with human contribution, he argued that humans contribute nothing to salvation; reconciliation with God is wholly a work of divine grace ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Divine_grace)), received through faith.

Luther linked this insight to meditation on Paul's Letter to the Romans. The phrase about the righteousness of God became, for him, an illumination in which righteousness was not punishment but God's gift to believers.

## Confrontation with Rome

By the summer of 1518, the causa Lutheri required Luther to answer charges. Frederick III of Saxony intervened so that Luther would be examined in Augsburg instead of Rome, partly to ensure fair treatment and partly amid wider imperial and papal politics.

At Augsburg, Cardinal Cajetan pressed Luther on indulgences and justification. Cajetan defended the church's authority to distribute indulgences from the treasury of merits, while Luther denied that authority and insisted that faith was indispensable for justification. After three days in October 1518, Luther refused to recant and fled.

Pope Leo X's bull Cum postquam declared Luther's views in conflict with church teaching. Luther temporarily withdrew from public debate, but others continued reform arguments, and Johann Eck later pushed Luther into admitting that even general councils could err on matters not concerning the faith.

Formal proceedings resumed in 1519 and 1520. After several examinations, Leo X issued Exsurge Domine on June 15, 1520, charging that 41 sentences in Luther's writings were heretical, scandalous, or offensive and giving him time to recant.

Luther responded defiantly. On Dec. 10, 1520, he canceled classes and burned the papal bull outside a Wittenberg gate. The excommunication bull Decet Romanum pontificem followed on Jan. 3, 1521, formally declaring him a heretic.

Because secular enforcement was politically complicated, Charles V summoned Luther before the Diet of Worms. On April 17, 1521, Luther acknowledged his books and asked for time; the next day he refused to retract the substance of his writings, though the famous “Here I stand” formula is traditional rather than secure.

After the Diet, Luther was taken to the Wartburg, where he began one of his major achievements: translating the New Testament into German. His vernacular Bible supported his claim that Scripture alone is Christian truth's authority and influenced the development of written German.

Reform after Worms became more legal and political than purely theological. Luther returned to Wittenberg in 1522 to restrain riots and rapid change, preferring to “make haste slowly.” Other reformers, including Thomas Muntzer, Huldrych Zwingli, and Martin Bucer, developed their own positions.

Luther's later controversies included disputes with [[Erasmus]] over free will and grace and with Zwingli over the Eucharist. Luther defended the real bodily presence of Christ in communion, while Zwingli treated the words of institution symbolically, dividing the reform movement.

Luther married Katherine of Bora in 1525. Their household, remembered through Table Talks, included children and a strong domestic partnership; his grief at the death of his daughter Magdalene in 1542 shows the warmth of his family life.

In later years, Luther could not attend the 1530 Diet of Augsburg, so Philipp Melanchthon represented the Protestants and produced the Augsburg Confession. Luther increasingly served as theologian, adviser, and facilitator rather than direct political actor.

Luther's later career also included harsh and troubling writings, poor health, kidney stones, a coronary condition, lectures on Genesis from 1535 to 1545, preaching, and involvement in difficult political and pastoral matters such as Philip of Hesse's secret second marriage.

## Final Years and Death

In a June 1543 letter, Luther wrote that he desired “a good little hour” to move onward to God, saying he was tired and asking for prayer that the Lord would take his soul in peace. The words show how frequently death entered his late correspondence.

In February 1546, Luther travelled despite failing health to Eisleben to mediate a quarrel between the Mansfeld counts Albrecht and Gebhard. He succeeded, wrote his final letter to his wife, died on February 18, and was buried in the Castle Church in Wittenberg.

## Significance

Martin Luther was one of the most influential figures in Western civilization during the last millennium. He catalyzed the division of Western Christendom, emphasized vernacular language, and left theological writings that filled more than 100 folio volumes in the Weimar Edition.

Luther was a polemical theologian rather than a systematic one. Writings such as Bondage of the Will against Erasmus and his attacks on Zwingli emerged from controversy and often made forceful claims that are difficult to reconcile with other statements.

Key Protestant themes shaped by Luther include the Bible ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bible)) as the only source of religious authority, salvation by grace through faith alone, and the church as a community of believers rather than a hierarchy sharply divided between clergy and laity.

These reform ideas had earlier antecedents, but Luther gave them new force and centrality. His insistence that Scripture should reach commoners led him to translate the Bible, compose hymns, and advocate schools in cities.

Later interpreters have stressed Luther's fear of death, his struggle against the Devil, and his spiritual temptations, but his spiritual centre was trust in God's forgiveness and acceptance. He called this Christian freedom.

## Key Events & Developments

- 1483: Martin Luther was born in Eisleben.

- 1512: Luther received his doctorate and became professor of biblical studies at Wittenberg.
