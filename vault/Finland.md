# Finland

## Introduction

Finland ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Finland)) is a northern European country with a severe climate, vast forests, and a strong sense of national identity shaped by geography and history. Nearly two-thirds of the country is covered by woodland, making Finland the most densely forested country in Europe. It lies between western and eastern Europe, with Russia to the east and Sweden to the west, and its location has strongly influenced both its politics and its culture.

The modern state emerged after Finland had been part of [[Sweden]] for centuries and then became a Russian grand duchy in 1809. It declared independence on December 6, 1917, after the Russian Revolution. Finland later survived the Soviet period with a carefully balanced foreign policy and entered the European Union in 1995.

## Finnish environment

Finland is a land of forests, lakes, marshes, and thousands of islands. The country contains about 56,000 lakes, and its landscape is often described as a blue-and-green jigsaw puzzle. Much of the land is low-lying, though the northeast and northwest contain higher ground and some mountain areas.

The environment is shaped by ancient bedrock, glacial deposits, and long winters. The retreating ice sheet left ridges, moraines, and lakes across the country. Nature is deeply important to Finnish life, and this connection appears in literature, folklore, and everyday identity.

## Relief

The relief of Finland is generally gentle, with few dramatic mountain ranges except in the far northwest. The country’s surface is heavily influenced by glaciation, which created eskers, ridges, and scattered deposits across the land. The coastline is highly indented and includes many islands, especially in the southwest and around the Aland archipelago.

## Climate

Finland has long, cold winters and short, mild summers. North of the Arctic Circle, winter is extreme and the polar night can last for many weeks, while the midnight sun appears in summer. The southern parts of the country are somewhat warmer because of maritime influence, but winter remains the dominant season throughout the country.

## People

The population is concentrated in the south, especially around Helsinki, which is the largest city and the northernmost capital in continental Europe. The country’s peoples include Finns, Swedes, and the indigenous Sami, whose homeland lies in the far north. Finland has a long history of settlement, migration, and cultural exchange, and the population has gradually become more urban.

## Languages

Finland is officially bilingual, with Finnish and Swedish as the national languages. Finnish is spoken by the vast majority of the population, while Swedish is strongest in coastal areas and the Aland Islands. The Sami languages are also spoken in the north, and Finland has strong protections for its minority language communities.

## Religion

Christianity reached Finland from both the west and the east by the 13th century. Today, most Finns belong to the Evangelical Lutheran Church, while a smaller number belong to the Orthodox Church. Finland is one of the most church-affiliated countries in Scandinavia, though regular attendance is lower than formal membership suggests.

## Economy

Finland’s economy is based on private enterprise, but the state also plays a strong role in key sectors. After World War II, the country moved from agriculture and forestry toward industry, and later toward services and information technology. The economy expanded rapidly in the 1980s, but suffered a major recession in the early 1990s after the collapse of the Soviet Union and the general slowdown in Europe.

The recovery that followed focused on retooling industry and strengthening trade with western Europe. Finland joined the European Free Trade Association in 1961, became a full member in 1986, and entered the European Union in 1995.

## Manufacturing

Manufacturing became important as Finland industrialized, especially after the 1860s. Forest products have long been central, including timber, pulp, paper, furniture, and building materials. Heavy industry, engineering, chemicals, ships, and electronics also became major sectors. In the late 20th century, information technology and telecommunications grew especially quickly, with firms such as Nokia becoming internationally important.

## Finance

The Finnish financial system moved toward market orientation from 1980 onward. The Bank of Finland, established in 1811, serves as the central bank. In 2002, Finland adopted the euro, replacing the markka. The country has a well-developed banking system and a stock exchange in Helsinki.

## Trade

Because Finland has a small domestic market and limited energy resources, foreign trade is vital. The Soviet Union was once Finland’s main trading partner, but after its collapse trade shifted toward the European Union. Major exports include paper products, machinery, and manufactured goods; imports include fuel, raw materials, and consumer goods.

## Services

The service sector is a major part of the economy, though manufacturing has remained important. Government services, business services, and information technology all play large roles. Finland also invests heavily in research and development and uses grants, loans, and training programs to support strategic sectors.

## Justice and political process

Finland has an independent judiciary and a democratic system based on universal suffrage for adults. The president is directly elected, while parliament is selected by proportional representation. This system has produced a large number of political parties, including the Social Democrats, the Centre Party, the National Coalition Party, the Left Alliance, and the True Finn Party.

Women have played an important role in Finnish politics since they gained the right to vote and stand for parliament in 1906. In 2003, Finland became the first European country to have a woman president and a woman prime minister in office at the same time.

## Security

Finland has maintained a strong defense tradition and a policy of neutrality, although its security environment changed after the end of the Cold War. The country has compulsory military service for men, and its police system is organized under the Ministry of the Interior. Finland’s security concerns are shaped by its position near Russia and by developments in the Baltic region.

## Health and welfare

Finland provides broad health care and social security. Local health centres offer treatment, and the state reimburses much of the cost of medicines. The country has high life expectancy and a strong welfare system built around pensions, disability support, unemployment benefits, and family assistance.

## Housing and education

Housing policy in Finland emphasizes affordability and public support, especially for low-income households. Education is also a major public priority. School attendance is compulsory from age seven, and Finland has a strong system of comprehensive schools, vocational schools, and universities and polytechnics.

## Cultural life

Finnish culture is both deeply traditional and internationally open. The country has preserved its language and identity while absorbing influences from Russia, Scandinavia, and the wider European world. Cultural life is supported by museums, libraries, grants for artists, and local clubs and festivals.

## Literature and the arts

Finnish literature has produced major works in both Finnish and Swedish. The national epic Kalevala is central to Finnish cultural memory, and writers such as Aleksis Kivi, Mika Waltari, and Tove Jansson have gained international recognition. Finnish design, architecture, and art are also widely respected, with figures such as Alvar Aalto and Eliel Saarinen playing major roles in modern European design.

## History

![[attachments/finland_001.webp|420]]

*Flag of Finland*

The history of Finland is shaped by migration, Swedish rule, Russian rule, and modern independence. Early settlement came from several groups, including ancestors of the Sami and later Finno-Ugric populations. During the 12th century, Finland became a battleground between Sweden and Russia, and the struggle over trade, religion, and territory shaped its later development.

The Winter War of 1939–1940 and the Continuation War of 1941–1944 were major turning points in Finnish history. The postwar period brought a more stable foreign policy under Juho Kusti Paasikivi and Urho Kekkonen, and Finland gradually moved toward closer ties with the West while preserving good relations with the Soviet Union.

## Key Events & Developments

- It declared independence on December 6, 1917.
- The Winter War and Continuation War shaped modern Finnish national memory.
- Finland adopted the euro in 2002 and joined the European Union in 1995.
- The country developed a strong modern economy based on industry, technology, and services.
