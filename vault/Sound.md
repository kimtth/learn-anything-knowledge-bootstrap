# Sound

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> Sound is a mechanical wave that propagates through elastic matter as pressure variation and is perceived through hearing across a wider frequency range than human audition alone. The source develops wave models, frequency, amplitude, intensity, speed, spreading, attenuation, superposition, and interactions such as diffraction, refraction, and reflection.

## Introduction

![[attachments/sound_001.gif|420]]

*Animated diagram of sound waves from a speaker*

Sound ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sound)) is a mechanical disturbance that moves through an elastic material medium from a state of equilibrium. It can be defined subjectively as what the ear perceives, but that is too narrow because sound also includes frequencies humans cannot hear, such as dog whistles and sonar signals.

Sound travels as a longitudinal wave ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Longitudinal_wave)). In air, layers of molecules push and pull one another, creating alternating compressions and rarefactions, or high- and low-pressure regions. This differs from transverse waves, such as waves on a rope or electromagnetic waves, where the disturbance is perpendicular to the direction of travel.

## Plane Waves

A plane wave ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plane_wave)) is a simplified model in which a sound wave travels as a plane rather than as an expanding sphere. A single-frequency plane wave would be heard as a pure tone, like a gently struck tuning fork. This model helps explain basic wave properties.

The wavelength ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wavelength)) is the distance over which the pressure pattern repeats. The period is the time for one wavelength to pass a point, and frequency is the number of wavelengths passing per second, measured in hertz. Frequency and period are inversely related. Human hearing usually spans about 20 hertz to 20 kilohertz, and higher frequency is perceived as higher pitch.

The amplitude of a sound wave is the size of the pressure fluctuation above and below atmospheric pressure. Intensity is the average energy transmitted per unit area and is perceived as loudness. The human ear can sense pressure amplitudes as small as about $10^{-5}$ pascal, while pain begins around 10 pascals.

For ordinary sounds, waves behave nearly linearly: they travel with little distortion. At very high amplitudes, around 100 pascals, nonlinear effects appear because compressions and rarefactions no longer balance symmetrically. This can change wave shape and propagation.

## Speed of Sound

![[attachments/sound_002.png|420]]

*Mach cone diagram*

The speed of sound depends on the elasticity and density of the medium. In gases, it depends mainly on absolute temperature rather than pressure, as long as the gas behaves ideally. This explains why the pitch of wind instruments at the same temperature is not changed simply by altitude.

In liquids, the speed depends on the bulk modulus and density, with small temperature corrections. In solids, different elastic moduli matter depending on the shape and type of wave. Sound generally travels faster in stiffer media and slower in media that compress more easily.

## Circular and Spherical Waves

Real sound sources usually produce spherical waves that expand outward rather than perfect plane waves. Huygens' principle, associated with Christiaan Huygens, says every point on a wavefront can act as a source of smaller wavelets. These wavelets add together to form the next wavefront.

The principle of superposition says waves can occupy the same space at the same time without destroying one another. Two voices can cross in air and remain separately intelligible. This principle underlies interference, diffraction, and the analysis of complex sounds into simpler components.

## Attenuation

Attenuation is the weakening of sound as it travels. A plane wave is idealized as propagating without geometric loss, but a spherical wave spreads its energy over a growing surface area. This produces the inverse square law: intensity falls with the square of distance from the origin point.

For a spherical wave, each doubling of distance reduces intensity by a factor of four, or about six decibels. Additional loss can occur through absorption by surfaces, air, or materials. This is why distance, room surfaces, and atmospheric conditions strongly affect heard loudness.

## Diffraction, Refraction, and Reflection

Diffraction is the bending or spreading of sound around obstacles or through openings. Because sound wavelengths are often similar to everyday object sizes, sound bends around corners much more than light does. Low voice frequencies diffract more strongly than high ones, making voices around corners sound muffled.

Refraction is bending caused by changes in sound speed. In the atmosphere, temperature gradients bend sound. During the day, warm air near the ground can make sound refract upward and disappear into a shadow region. At night or under temperature inversion, sound bends back downward and can travel farther.

Reflection occurs when sound meets a boundary where propagation changes. Echoes and auditorium acoustics depend on reflection. Parabolic reflectors can focus weak distant sounds, while elliptical domes may create whispering chambers. Concert halls avoid shapes that focus sound too strongly and create bad echoes.

## Impedance and Acoustic Filtration

Acoustic impedance measures how easily a sound wave propagates through a medium. When sound meets a boundary between media with different impedances, some energy reflects and some transmits. The bell-in-vacuum experiment shows that poor coupling between vibrating matter and thin air prevents efficient sound transmission.

[[Musical Instruments|Musical instruments]] reduce impedance mismatch. A tuning fork held in air is quiet, but when it touches a tabletop, the table couples vibration to the air more efficiently. Violins and pianos use bodies or sounding boards in the same way.

Acoustic filters use impedance changes to remove unwanted frequencies, especially in air ducts and mufflers. Wider or narrower duct sections can reflect high frequencies, openings can remove low frequencies, and Helmholtz-type cavities can absorb narrow frequency bands.

## Interference, Beats, and Doppler Effect

Interference occurs when sound waves combine. Waves in phase reinforce one another constructively; waves out of phase cancel destructively. Speaker boxes prevent sound from the back of a speaker from diffracting around and cancelling sound from the front, especially at low frequencies.

Beats occur when two tones of nearly equal frequency mix. The heard tone rises and falls in loudness at a rate equal to the difference between the two frequencies. Musicians use beats to tune instruments: faster beats mean the pitches are farther apart.

The Doppler effect is a frequency shift caused by relative motion between source and listener. When source and observer move closer, the heard frequency rises; when they move apart, it falls. A passing train whistle is the classic example.

## Standing Waves and Resonance

Standing waves ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Standing_wave)) form when waves of the same frequency move in opposite directions and combine. A stretched string fixed at both ends has nodes at the ends and antinodes where motion is greatest. Its lowest frequency is the fundamental, and higher standing waves form harmonics.

Overtones are higher resonances above the fundamental. When overtone frequencies are exact multiples of the fundamental, they are harmonics. The overtone series shapes musical tone quality and explains why tuning systems such as just intonation can produce beatless chords.

Marin Mersenne's laws describe stretched strings. The fundamental frequency is inversely proportional to string length, directly proportional to the square root of tension, and inversely related to mass per unit length. These laws explain why lower guitar, violin, and piano strings are heavier and why higher strings are shorter or thinner.

Air columns also support standing waves, as in wind instruments. Open and closed tubes have different node and antinode patterns. Helmholtz resonators, such as bottles, jug-band jugs, and instrument cavities, resonate at particular frequencies determined by cavity volume and neck size.

## Complex Waves, Noise, and Hearing

![[attachments/sound_003.png|420]]

*Anatomy of the human ear diagram*

Complex sounds can be analyzed by Fourier spectrum, which breaks a wave into frequency components. A sound spectrograph shows how these components change over time and can represent speech or bird calls. Musical timbre depends partly on the relative amplitudes of spectral components.

Instrument bore shape affects tone. Cylindrical and conical bores can produce harmonic resonances, while faster flaring bores create nonharmonic overtones and rougher tones. Bells reduce impedance mismatch at instrument openings, helping instruments radiate sound.

Noise has no clear periodicity and therefore no fixed musical pitch. White noise contains equal intensity across the audio spectrum, while pink noise decreases by about three decibels per octave. Natural and musical sounds often decline in intensity at high frequencies in a pink-noise-like way.

The human ear has a huge dynamic range. It hears roughly 20 hertz to 20 kilohertz. The threshold of hearing is about $10^{-12}$ watt per square metre, while the threshold of pain is about a trillion times more intense. Equal-loudness curves, or Fletcher-Munson curves, show that perceived loudness depends strongly on frequency.

## Key Events & Developments

- 17th century: Marin Mersenne's string laws helped explain musical pitch.
- 17th century: Christiaan Huygens' wavelet principle explained wavefront propagation.
- 19th century: Lord Rayleigh's work helped define acoustic impedance units and classical acoustics.
- Mid-19th century: Helmholtz resonators were used to analyze musical tones before electronic analyzers.
- 20th century: Harvey Fletcher and W.A. Munson measured equal-loudness curves.

## Key People

- Christiaan Huygens ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christiaan_Huygens)): Explained propagation through wavelets and superposition.
- Marin Mersenne ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marin_Mersenne)): Formulated laws for vibrating strings.
- Lord Rayleigh ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lord_Rayleigh)): Associated with the rayl unit and foundational acoustics.
- Harvey Fletcher ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harvey_Fletcher)) and W.A. Munson: Measured equal-loudness curves for hearing.

## Interesting Facts

- Sound waves in air are pressure waves made of compressions and rarefactions.
- Low frequencies bend around corners better than high frequencies.
- Speaker boxes prevent destructive interference between front and back waves.
- Beats are useful because mistuned notes pulse faster when farther apart.
- Pink noise resembles many natural and musical spectra more closely than white noise.
