## Introduction

Religious symbolism ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Religious_symbol)) and iconography are the artistic forms, gestures, images, sounds, and movements used to express religious ideas and events. All world religions use symbols. Since the 20th century, many scholars have treated symbolic expression as one of the main features of religion, sometimes more central than rational explanation.

Iconography ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iconography)) gives religious ideas visible, audible, and kinetic form. Symbols and images preserve relationships between human beings and the sacred or holy. They are not only decorations; they can act as mediators, presences, and intelligible representations of transcendent reality.

Scholars of comparative religion ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Comparative_religion)), ethnology, psychology, theology, and liturgy have shown that symbols are structured systems. They work through ordered relations among form, content, and intention. A symbol can abbreviate meaning while also opening access to deeper religious reality.

## The Nature of Religious Symbols and Symbolization

The word symbol comes from the Greek symbolon, meaning token, contract, insignia, or means of identification. A religious symbol points beyond itself while also making something present. It may conceal and reveal at the same time, inviting interpretation rather than simple literal reading.

Symbols have both epistemological and ontological aspects. They help people know religious truth, but they may also be understood as participating in or mediating sacred reality. In ritual, a symbol can become an effective way of apprehending and entering the holy.

Christian debates over the Lord's Supper show different levels of symbolic understanding. Roman Catholic transubstantiation treats bread and wine as changed in substance into Christ's body and blood. Martin Luther taught the Real Presence without explaining how. Huldrych Zwingli stressed the sign or memorial character of the rite.

## Orthodoxy, Roman Catholicism, and the Protestant Reformers

Christian symbolism shows how one ritual can carry many interpretations. Orthodoxy, Roman Catholicism, and Protestant reformers all used symbol, word, gesture, and sacrament to connect visible action with divine meaning. Their differences concerned how closely the symbol is joined to what it represents.

The sacrament ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sacrament)) of the Lord's Supper includes signs, pictures, ritual actions, words, and music. It can be interpreted as remembrance, presence, mystery, or transformation. This range shows why religious symbols are not merely signs but complex links between worship, doctrine, and experience.

## Pictorial Symbolism

Pictorial symbolism develops nonrepresentational and ideographic symbolism into images of nature, human beings, gods, events, and sacred histories. A picture may show a part for a whole, such as a hand or eye for a complete figure, or it may show the whole sacred figure directly.

Sacred [[The Art of Sculpture (Sculpture)|sculpture]] ranges from Stone Age idols to Mesopotamian and Egyptian images, Greco-Roman gods and heroes, Hindu gods and demons, Chinese and Japanese pantheons, and Mahayana Buddhist bodhisattvas, saints, and spirits. Reliefs on temples can decorate, narrate myth, and teach sacred history.

Symbolic sculpture may simplify figures into abstract or geometrical forms or imitate nature realistically. Body parts can carry independent meaning: a raised hand can bless, a foot can symbolize Shiva, and votive limbs can be offered for healing. Sculpted objects can also indicate divine power or ritual function.

## Sacred Architecture and Holy Places

Religious architecture turns buildings into symbolic worlds. A Buddhist temple or stupa can represent the presence of the Buddha. The form of a holy place, its portals, paths, altars, relics, ceilings, towers, and offering tables may follow symbolic and iconological rules.

Ceilings and vaults often represent heaven. The Christian bell tower, or campanile, is popularly understood as the finger of God. Ancient Christian basilicas were seen as images of the heavenly Jerusalem. Hindu and Buddhist temples, Greek pediments, Egyptian facades, and Christian portals use figures and reliefs to narrate myth, ritual, salvation, and judgment.

Inside holy places, iconography continues on walls, capitals, apses, vaults, sanctuaries, and altars. Images may show divinity, sacred events, saints, founders, or cosmic order. The whole building becomes a readable religious environment.

## Human, Animal, and Plant Forms

Human figures in religious iconography can represent divinity, sanctity, salvation, or cosmic order. The hand of God may symbolize Christ, creative power, covenant, fidelity, or truth. Mary and the saints in Christian iconography show humanity as receiver of salvation and bearer of divine presence.

Theriomorphic and zoomorphic symbols represent the divine, demonic, cosmic, or allegorical through animal forms. Ancient Egyptian gods, the lamb of Christ, Hindu animal forms, Buddhist motifs, and totemic traditions all use animals to express sacred power. Christian evangelists can appear as lion, ox, eagle, and human forms.

Animal parts such as horns, wings, skulls, and feet can symbolize divine or diabolical powers. Plant forms also become sacred signs through trees, flowers, fruits, and leaves. These motifs connect fertility, creation, life, death, renewal, and cosmic order.

## Cultural and Conceptual Influences

Culture ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Culture)) shapes religious symbolism through work, leisure, war, peace, social rank, tools, occupations, and political institutions. Hunters, farmers, shepherds, warriors, artisans, and merchants all appear in religious images and metaphors. Missionary religions may speak of spiritual combat, discipline, and victory.

Political and social models also become religious symbols. Judaism, Christianity, and ancient Roman religion could describe the relation between God and humanity through a peace treaty model. Germanic and Indian religions could stress military virtues such as loyalty, duty, and comradeship.

Abstract ideas enter symbolism through number, geometry, polarity, and philosophical structure. Unity can express the oneness of divinity. Duality can appear as body and soul, divine pairs, syzygies, God and Devil, good and evil, or light and darkness. Mathematical and conceptual schemes organize gods, spirits, demons, humanity, myth, and theology.

## Religious Figures, Ritual Objects, and Community Symbols

Religious figures form a vast symbolic complex: gods, saviours, redeemers, heroes, avatars, Ishvaras, founders, prophets, lawgivers, saints, reformers, theologians, mystics, priests, dancers, and musicians. Christian Doctors and Fathers often have standard attributes, such as St. Augustine with the heart and St. Jerome with the lion.

Ritual objects also carry iconographic meaning. The offering, altar, instruments of sacrifice, fire, sacred meal, communion rites, and ritual liquids symbolize submission, gift, service, brotherhood, and even the giving of life for religion. The place and tools of ritual become part of the symbolic system.

Religious communities recognize themselves through emblems. Examples include the yin-yang within the taiji circle in Chinese universalism, the swastika in Hinduism and Jainism, the wheel of the law in Buddhism, the khandha in Sikhism, the star of David and menorah in Judaism, and the cross in Christianity.

## Conclusion

The future of religious symbolism remained open in the modern world. During much of the 20th century, Christian communities revived liturgical traditions and ritual symbolism, and theologians such as Paul Tillich based systems on the symbol. At the same time, some theologians rejected symbols, myths, pictures, and anthropomorphic ideas because of moral, social, or demythologizing concerns.

In non-Christian religions, the rejection of symbols seemed less acute. In secular, skeptical, and agnostic societies, symbols could appear dispensable, yet younger generations showed renewed interest in Eastern and Western symbolic traditions. By the late 20th century and early 21st century, a renewed appreciation of symbolism and iconography was visible despite opposite trends.

## Key Events & Developments

- The 20th century: Scholars increasingly emphasized the symbolic character of religion.
- The 1960s: Some theology showed indifference or hostility toward symbols because of moral, social, and demythologizing concerns.
- Late 20th century and early 21st century: New interest in religious symbols appeared among people encountering Eastern and Western traditions.
