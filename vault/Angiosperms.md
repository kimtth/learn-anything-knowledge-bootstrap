# Angiosperms

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> Angiosperms are flowering plants whose enclosed ovules develop into seeds and fruits. Their vascular tissues, flowers, roots, stems, leaves, and reproductive diversity make them central to terrestrial ecosystems, food systems, materials, and medicine.

## Introduction

Angiosperms ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flowering_plant)) are flowering plants, the largest and most diverse group of living green plants. They are vascular seed plants in which the ovule is enclosed within an ovary, which usually sits inside a flower, and in which the mature reproductive structures become fruits. This enclosed seed habit distinguishes them from [[Gymnosperms|gymnosperms]], whose seeds are normally exposed rather than enclosed.
Angiosperms are of great practical importance. They dominate much of the Earth's vegetation, especially on land, and form the main ultimate source of food for birds, mammals, and humans. Beyond food, they provide timber, fibre, medicines, ornamentals, oils, and countless other products. Their success is therefore both biological and economic.

Concrete medical examples show this value. The cancer drug vincristine comes from the Madagascar periwinkle, Catharanthus roseus, and the heart medicine digitalis comes from the foxglove, Digitalis purpurea. Muscle relaxants derived from curare are used during open-heart surgery. A significant loss of angiosperms would reduce both food sources and the oxygen supply of a habitat and would alter the world's precipitation.

## General Organization of the Plant Body

The angiosperm body is divided into a root system and a shoot system. Roots anchor the plant, absorb water and minerals, and often store food. Shoots include stems, leaves, and later flowers and fruits. Flowering plants became highly successful because they evolved specialized tissues and organs instead of relying on unspecialized cells for all life functions.

Unlike nonvascular plants, angiosperms possess extensive vascular tissues that move water, nutrients, and products of photosynthesis through the plant body. They also have localized regions of growth called meristems and cambia, which allow elongation and thickening. This organization supports large size, structural variety, and adaptation to many environments.

## Roots

Roots show two main patterns. In a primary root system, a dominant taproot grows downward and produces lateral branches. In an adventitious root system, the original primary root is replaced by many roots that arise from the stem. These patterns are associated broadly with dicotyledons and monocotyledons, though many modifications occur.

Roots may also be altered for special tasks such as storage, support, climbing, or life in unusual habitats, with structures such as tuberous roots and adaptations to different soils. Root hairs greatly increase absorptive surface, while internal tissues such as the endodermis and Casparian strip regulate the movement of water and minerals into the vascular system.

The growing root tip is highly organized. A protective root cap of living parenchyma shields the apex as it pushes through the soil, and worn cells are replaced from within by a special layer called the calyptrogen. Behind the tip lie five zones: cell division, cell elongation, primary tissue maturation, mature primary tissues, and (in woody dicotyledons) secondary growth. Lateral roots arise deep inside, from the pericycle, so they have an internal, or endogenous, origin. The endodermis carries water-impermeable strips of suberin in its walls, which force water and minerals to pass through living cells and let the plant control what enters the central cylinder.

## Stems

Stems support the plant, bear leaves and flowers, and conduct materials between roots and leaves. Their organization depends on nodes, internodes, buds, and branching patterns, with different modes of branching and many unusual transformations of the stem.

Stem modifications include tendrils for climbing, thorns for defense, cladodes that take over photosynthesis, and underground storage organs such as rhizomes, tubers, and corms. These examples show how the same basic shoot system can be reshaped to solve very different ecological problems. In stems the vascular tissue is split into many separate vascular bundles, unlike the single central cylinder of roots. Uneven growth of the inner pith produces the flattened pads of prickly-pear cacti (Opuntia), while uneven growth of the outer cortex produces the leaflike cladodes of epiphytic cacti such as the night-blooming cereus (Selenicereus).

## Leaves

Leaves are the main organs of [[Photosynthesis|photosynthesis]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Photosynthesis)) in most angiosperms. A typical leaf may include a base, stipules, petiole, and blade, but many leaves lack one or more of these parts. Leaves vary enormously in form, margin, venation, arrangement, and degree of complexity.

Simple and compound leaves, alternate and opposite arrangements, and many specialized leaf forms are all treated as products of development from the shoot apex. Leaves may also be modified for storage, defense, insect capture, or water conservation. Mesomorphic leaves suit places with plenty of water, xeromorphic leaves of dry habitats, hydromorphic leaves of aquatic habitats, and the contrasting patterns of monocot and dicot leaves illustrate how leaf structure reflects environmental pressure.

Development explains many of these differences. A dicot leaf grows from marginal meristems along the sides of the young primordium, which gives the netlike venation and the lobes of simple and compound leaves; growth is determinate, so the tip matures first and then maturation moves toward the base. Monocot leaves instead grow from an adaxial meristem, producing long flat blades in one plane with the stem, as in the iris (Iris), or shorter blades with cylindrical tips, as in the snake plant (Sansevieria trifasciata). Because of this pattern, monocot leaves usually lack a midvein, have parallel veins, carry stomata in rows between the veins, and look uniform in texture.

## Importance in the Food Chain and Human Use

Angiosperms are ecologically central. Because they capture solar energy and convert it into organic compounds, they are the main base of terrestrial food chains. Vegetative parts feed herbivores, while flowers, pollen, fruits, and seeds support insects, birds, mammals, and many other organisms.

Human dependence is even more obvious. Angiosperms provide cereals such as wheat, rice, and corn; vegetables, fruits, beans, nuts, oils, spices, beverages, fibres, and timber. Their medical value is also great, since many pharmaceuticals either come directly from flowering plants or were first discovered in them. A major reduction of angiosperm diversity would therefore threaten food supply, medicine, habitat, and climate patterns.

## Development and Meristems

Angiosperms grow from a fertilized egg into an embryo and then into the mature plant body through the activity of meristematic tissues. Apical meristems lengthen roots and shoots, while lateral meristems such as the vascular cambium and cork cambium increase thickness.

Three primary meristematic regions produce the main tissue systems. The protoderm forms the epidermis, the ground meristem forms the cortex and pith, and the procambium forms vascular tissues. This developmental organization underlies the great diversity of outward plant form.

## Tissue Systems

The vegetative body is composed of three integrated tissue systems: ground tissue, vascular tissue, and dermal tissue. Ground tissue includes parenchyma, collenchyma, and sclerenchyma, each with different roles in storage, metabolism, support, and protection. Living parenchyma remains especially important because it can continue metabolic work and even differentiate again under some conditions.

Vascular tissue consists of xylem and phloem. The water-conducting cells of xylem are tracheids and vessel elements, both dead and hollow at maturity; tracheids exchange water through paired bordered pits, while vessel elements have open perforation plates in their end walls. Their side walls show a developmental sequence of thickening patterns, from annular and helical in the first-formed protoxylem to scalariform and bordered pitting in the mature metaxylem. Sclerenchyma adds strength through sclereids, common in seed coats and nutshells, and elongated fibres. Living parenchyma fills the pith, cortex, fruit pulp, and seed endosperm and can divide again to form new buds, roots, or cell types after wounding.

Xylem transports water and minerals and also contributes mechanical support, while phloem moves sugars and other products of photosynthesis. In woody plants, secondary growth produced by the vascular cambium forms annual rings, heartwood, sapwood, and the broader architecture of trunks and branches. The cambium itself contains two cell types: tall fusiform initials that build the vertical conducting system and small ray initials that build the horizontal rays used for storage and short-distance transport. Because the cambium makes more cells inward than outward, far more xylem than phloem accumulates; the dark central heartwood is the oldest, dead xylem, while the lighter living sapwood still conducts water, often along a spiral grain that spreads water from any root to the whole crown.

## Dermal Tissue

The epidermis is the outer protective covering of the primary plant body, and its role is to limit water loss and control gas exchange. A cuticle of waxy substances reduces evaporation, while stomata with guard cells regulate the movement of water vapour, carbon dioxide, and oxygen.

Epidermal outgrowths, or trichomes, perform many functions. They can reduce overheating, absorb water, secrete chemicals, trap insects, deter herbivores, or help plants live in salty or dry conditions. Named cases include epiphytic bromeliads such as Spanish moss (Tillandsia usneoides), which absorb water through foliar trichomes; saltbush (Atriplex vesicaria) secretes excess salt through special trichomes; and the stinging nettle (Urtica dioica) uses stinging hairs for defense, while the prickles of roses deter herbivores. In woody plants, later protective tissues, formed by a cork cambium, replace the original epidermis as secondary growth progresses.

## Root, Stem, and Leaf Anatomy
Root anatomy is organized around a central vascular cylinder surrounded by cortex, endodermis, and epidermis. Lateral roots arise internally from the pericycle, which shows how new organs can form deep within existing tissues. In woody roots, secondary tissues add girth in the same general way as in stems.

Stem anatomy is organized through vascular bundles, cortex, pith, and epidermis, with significant differences among major plant groups and habitats. Leaf anatomy is closely related to function, especially water relations and light capture. Mesomorphic, xeromorphic, and hydromorphic leaves illustrate how structure serves environment, while monocot leaves show developmental and venation patterns distinct from those of dicots.

## General Features of Reproductive Structures

![[attachments/angiosperms_001.webp|420]]

*Diagram of flower anatomy*

The reproductive organs of angiosperms are the flowers, which are modified shoots with determinate growth. A complete flower typically includes sepals, petals, stamens, and carpels. Carpels and the fruits they become are distinctive hallmarks of angiosperms.

Flowers vary enormously in symmetry, fusion of parts, sex, and arrangement, yet they follow the same underlying plan, ranging from regular to irregular flowers, inferior to superior ovaries, and separate to fused petals, with many ways floral organs can be modified for pollination and fruit development.

## The Receptacle, Corolla, and Gynoecium

The receptacle is the floral axis to which all flower parts attach, and it may itself become important in the mature fruit, as in the strawberry. Petals form the corolla, whose colour, symmetry, and degree of fusion influence pollinator behaviour. Many floral differences are therefore linked directly to reproductive strategy.

At the centre lies the gynoecium, made of one or more carpels. Each carpel encloses ovules, bears a stigma for receiving pollen, and often a style through which pollen tubes grow. Especially important are the syncarpous and apocarpous arrangements and the positional relations that distinguish hypogynous, perigynous, and epigynous flowers.

## Fruits and Seeds

After fertilization, ovules become seeds and the ovary becomes a fruit. Fruits may be simple, aggregate, multiple, or accessory, and the main fleshy kinds include berries, drupes, and pomes. A key point is that many foods commonly called vegetables, such as tomatoes and squashes, are botanically fruits because they derive from floral structures.

Multiple fruits, such as the fig and pineapple, form from the ovaries of a whole inflorescence, while accessory fruits build in extra flower parts, as the floral tube (hypanthium) does in the pear. A fruit wall, or pericarp, is divided into an inner endocarp, a middle mesocarp, and an outer exocarp, any of which may be fleshy or dry. Fruit can even form without fertilization, a process called parthenocarpy.

Seeds contain the embryo and nutritive tissue for the developing seedling. Their coatings, appendages, and associated tissues can vary greatly, and these differences are closely tied to survival and dispersal. Some seeds carry an extra outgrowth, the aril; the red aril of Myristica fragrans, for example, yields the spice mace. Placentation patterns also vary, including axile placentation on a central column with locules, free-central placentation on a central column without partitions, basal placentation at the ovary base, and parietal placentation on the ovary wall.

## Inflorescences

Flowers may be solitary or grouped into inflorescences. Inflorescences are divided into indeterminate forms, in which the youngest flowers are at the top or centre, and determinate or cymose forms, in which the terminal flower develops first and ends further axis growth.

Racemes, spikes, catkins, corymbs, umbels, heads, panicles, and cymes are treated as recurring structural patterns. These arrangements matter because they affect how flowers are displayed, how pollinators move, and how efficiently reproduction occurs.

## Reproduction and the Life Cycle

The angiosperm life cycle alternates between a dominant sporophyte phase and highly reduced gametophyte phases. Pollen grains are the male microgametophytes, and the embryo sac within the ovule is the female megagametophyte. Meiosis produces spores that develop into structures capable of fertilization.

Within the anther, microsporocytes undergo meiosis to form pollen, whose outer wall patterns often relate to pollination method. Each pollen grain has two walls: an inner intine of cellulose and pectins and an outer exine made of decay-resistant sporopollenin, sculptured with the pores through which the pollen tube grows. Smooth pollen tends to mark wind pollination, as in oaks and corn (Zea mays), while spiny or sticky pollen marks animal pollination. Within the ovule, a single megasporocyte gives rise to an eight-nucleate embryo sac, usually through the Polygonum type of development, which occurs in about 70 percent of charted angiosperms.

A defining event then follows. The pollen tube delivers two sperm: one fuses with the egg to form the zygote, and the other fuses with two polar nuclei to form a triple-fusion nucleus that becomes the nutritive endosperm. This double fertilization is one of the most characteristic single features of angiosperms and lies behind the visible success of flowering plants.

## Pollination

![[attachments/angiosperms_002.webp|420]]

*Honey bee pollinating a flower*

The transfer of pollen to a receptive stigma is treated as one of the most varied and evolutionarily important processes in angiosperms. Wind pollination, water pollination, and animal pollination all occur, but animal interactions are especially rich and varied. Beetles, bees, butterflies, birds, bats, and other animals each correspond to distinctive floral adaptations.

Flowers use colour, fragrance, shape, nectar, and symmetry to guide pollinators. Bees pollinate more flowers than any other animal group and favour zygomorphic, blue or yellow corollas with a lower lip that acts as a landing platform. Bird-pollinated flowers, such as those of Fuchsia, are often bright red with much nectar but little odour, because birds smell poorly; bat-pollinated flowers open at night with strong scents; and some eucalypts (Eucalyptus) are pollinated by small marsupials such as honey possums. Many angiosperms also possess self-incompatibility systems, either sporophytic or gametophytic, that prevent self-fertilization, force outcrossing, and maintain genetic diversity. Successful pollination leads to pollen tube growth, fertilization, seed formation, and eventually fruit development.

## Seed Dispersal and Seedlings

Once fruits and seeds are mature, they must be dispersed. Dispersal occurs by wind, water, and [[Animals|animals]], with many structural adaptations such as wings, hairs, buoyant tissues, sticky surfaces, and fleshy edible coverings. Wind carries the tiny seeds of orchids, the plumed seeds of milkweed, and the feathery fruits of the dandelion, while explosive capsules fling out the seeds of touch-me-not (Impatiens). Buoyant fruits let coconuts (Cocos nucifera) float across oceans to neighbouring islands. These adaptations connect plant reproduction to ecological systems.
Seedling establishment completes the cycle. The embryo uses stored food in the seed, emerges through germination, and develops roots and shoots that recreate the adult body plan. Germination follows two patterns: in hypogeal germination the cotyledons stay in the seed underground, as in the garden pea, while in epigeal germination the cotyledons are lifted above ground and may turn green, as in the garden bean (Phaseolus) and castor bean. Seed lifespan varies enormously, from only a few days in the sugar maple (Acer saccharum) to over a thousand years in the sacred lotus (Nelumbo nucifera). This final stage is the continuation of the same integrated developmental system that began with flower and seed formation.

## Conclusion
angiosperms is the most successful and influential group of land plants because they combine structural specialization, flexible development, efficient transport tissues, and highly varied reproductive strategies. Their flowers, enclosed seeds, and fruits are central innovations, but their success also depends on roots, stems, leaves, tissues, and meristems working as an integrated whole.

At the same time, angiosperms are indispensable to life on Earth as humans know it. They dominate terrestrial ecosystems, support food webs, sustain agriculture, supply medicines and materials, and shape habitats and climate. Their diversity is therefore both a botanical achievement and a fundamental condition of human civilization. The fossil record places the earliest widely accepted angiosperms in the Early Cretaceous, after which families such as the magnolias, laurels, and roses diversified; modern molecular work has since recognized the eudicots, with their three-furrowed pollen, as a single great lineage.

## Key Events & Developments

- Flowering plants diversify into the largest and most successful group of green plants.
- The evolution of enclosed ovules, flowers, and fruits distinguishes angiosperms from gymnosperms.
- Meristems, vascular tissues, and specialized organs support their ecological dominance.
- Animal pollination and seed dispersal systems evolve in many lineages.
- Modern classification increasingly relies on DNA-based comparative data.
