# Islamic World

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> The Islamic world is the historical and cultural sphere shaped by Islam from Arabia through the caliphates, regional dynasties, trade networks, legal traditions, and later empires. It traces the formation of sects, shariah, and diverse Muslim societies across the Middle East, Africa, Asia, and Europe.

## Introduction

The Islamic world ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islamic_world)) is the historical and global community of Muslims, stretching from the Atlantic to the Pacific and from northern Africa through Central Asia to the northern Indian subcontinent. Muslims predominate in roughly 30 to 40 countries, but Arabs make up fewer than one-fifth of all Muslims, and more than half of the world's Muslims live east of Karachi.

Islamic history ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_Islam)) requires more than a list of dynasties. Across about 14 centuries, Muslim communities incorporated new peoples, languages, regions, and political forms while maintaining a sense of religious connection. The Islamic era begins with the Hijrah, [[Muhammad and the Religion of Islam (Muhammad)|Muhammad]]'s emigration to Medina, corresponding to July 16, 622 CE.

Islamdom describes the wider social and cultural world shaped by Muslim rule and Muslim communities, not only religious belief. It included non-Muslims, converts, merchants, scholars, soldiers, rulers, Sufis, jurists, and local societies that adapted Islamic institutions to many environments.

## Prehistory (c. 3000 BCE500 CE)

The prehistory of Islamdom lies in central Afro-Eurasia, from ancient Mesopotamia, Egypt, Persia, Hebrew religion, Greece, India, and Christianity to late antique Arabia. In a Muslim view, the same long history can be read through prophets from Adam, Noah, Abraham, Moses, and Jesus to Muhammad.

Empire building became possible because western Asia had already developed agrarian city societies, trade networks, ruling institutions, law, and religious traditions. Sumer, Babylon, Persia, Hellenistic kingdoms, Byzantium, and the Sasanians all supplied models or territories that later Muslim rulers inherited, challenged, or reorganized.

Axial Age religions helped prepare the setting. Abrahamic traditions centred on Abraham, Mazdean traditions centred on Ahura Mazda, and Christianity refined ideas of revelation, community, sacred law, and universal history. Islam later entered a world already used to scriptural religion, empire, trade, and long-distance cultural exchange.

## The Arabian Peninsula

[[Arabia]] contained a large arid centre with oases, wells, seasonal streams, and better-watered southern coastal and mountain lands. To the north were the irrigated states of Syria and Iraq. In the southwest, Yemen had settled kingdoms by the early 1st millennium BCE, with trade across East Africa, the Red Sea, India, the Indian Ocean, Iraq, Syria, and the Mediterranean.

Arabs were Semitic-speaking settled, semi-settled, and migratory tribal peoples, including camel-herding Bedouins. Their lives mixed raiding, agriculture, animal husbandry, caravan service, and participation in trade. Before Islam they usually formed temporary tribal confederations rather than lasting large states.

Arabia's entry into international politics strengthened tribal Arabs while weakening direct control by Byzantine and Sasanian powers. Client kingdoms such as the Ghassanids, Lakhmids, and Kindah drew Arabs into wider politics, languages, religion, administration, and poetry. Prosperity and imperial rivalry in the 5th and 6th centuries helped connect interior Arabia to broader communication networks.

[[Mecca and Medina (Mecca)|Mecca]] became important because it stood at major north-south and west-east routes while remaining outside direct control of the great powers. The Quraysh built a trading and pilgrimage city around the Kabah, a shrine that housed many deities and centred on Allah, a supra-tribal creator and covenant deity.

The Kabah linked shrine and market. Pilgrimage and trade fairs supported a wider social order in which local tribal religion was already moving toward more inclusive religious forms. The Meccan setting combined trade, neutrality, Arabic language, tribal negotiation, and religious symbolism in ways that prepared a new supra-tribal community.

## The Prophet Muhammad

![[attachments/islamic_world_003.webp|420]]

*The Muslim World circa 700-1300*

[[Muhammad and the Religion of Islam (Muhammad)|Muhammad]] belonged to the Quraysh but to a less powerful clan. Orphaned young and protected by his uncle, he gained commercial stability through marriage to the wealthy widow Khadijah. His solitary retreats in the hills outside Mecca reflected the spiritual habits of hanifs, seekers of monotheistic truth outside ordinary tribal cults.

Revelation began when Muhammad was about 40, when the angel Gabriel commanded him to recite. Khadijah and a small circle recognized him as God's messenger, but many Meccans resisted because his message challenged their religious customs, social order, and economic interests tied to the shrine.

The ummah formed after the Hijrah to Medina in 622. Muhammad became prophet, community leader, judge, military organizer, and model for believers. His mission created a new form of membership that could supersede tribe while still using existing Arab social habits.

The early conquests followed Muhammad's death and the caliphate of Abu Bakr, Umar, and Uthman ibn Affan. Arab Muslim forces established garrison cities such as Al-Fustat, Damascus, Kufah, and Basra. These amsar housed fighters, mosques, Friday prayer, sermons, Quran recitation, and administrative authority.

Expansion linked western Asia and the Mediterranean within 25 years. Muslim forces defeated Byzantine armies in Syria, advanced against the Sasanians after Al-Qadisiyyah, and built a system in which capitulation was usually preferred to destructive conquest. Within another century, Muslim rule stretched from the Iberian Peninsula to Central Asia.

## Early Tensions and the Formation of Sects

Conquest created tensions between Arab Muslims, local non-Arabs, Arab Christians, non-Muslim protected communities, and new converts. Non-Arab converts often became clients, or mawali, of Arab tribes. Tax systems, social separation, and reliance on local administrators all shaped early Islamic identity.

Intra-Muslim conflict began with Uthman's assassination in 656 and continued through civil wars known as fitnah. Leadership was religious, political, social, and economic at once because the ummah under one leader was understood as an earthly sign of God's favour.

Sunni and Shiite identities formed from disputes over leadership and memory. Sunnis emphasized communal solidarity and consensus, while Shiites argued that Muhammad's family, especially Ali and his descendants, had been wrongfully denied leadership. The Abbasid rejection of Alid claims helped make Shiism a permanent opposition.

Jafar al-Sadiq, who died in 765, gave early Shiism a powerful framework. Shiites understood the imamate as a chain of divinely guided leaders from Ali through designated successors. Sunni and Shiite labels simplified a complex history but helped consolidate lasting social and religious identities.

## The Abbasids

The Abbasids overthrew the Umayyads but inherited a fragile problem of legitimacy. They alienated Shiites, destroyed most of the Umayyad family, relied on Khorasanian military support, and founded Baghdad near the Sasanian capital to claim imperial inheritance. Abd al-Rahman I escaped to create an Umayyad state in Andalusia.

[[Baghdad]] became a vast courtly, bureaucratic, scholarly, and commercial centre. By the reign of Harun al-Rashid, from 786 to 809, its splendour surpassed anything in Europe. Yet court complexity, succession disputes, slave households, regional ambitions, and military dependence weakened centralized Abbasid power.

## Shariah

Shariah ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sharia)) developed from Quran, Hadith, local custom, reasoning, consensus, and analogy. Abu Abd Allah al-Shafii, who died in 820, organized legal sources into a hierarchy: Quran, Hadith traceable to Muhammad or companions, ijma, and qiyas. His system limited uncontrolled personal reasoning while making law more coherent.

Legal schools coalesced among Sunnis and Shiites. The four main Sunni madhhabs became Shafiiyyah, Malikiyyah, Hanafiyyah, and Hanabilah. The idea that the gate of ijtihad closed in the 9th century became firm only later, but al-Shafii's approach encouraged collecting and testing Hadith and helped restrain legal divergence.

Social life in Islamdom cannot be reduced to caliphs and dynasties. Households, markets, mosques, madrasahs, Sufi lodges, courts, guilds, caravan networks, and village communities carried Islamic norms into daily life. Women participated as family members, patrons, transmitters of learning, mystics, workers, and rulers in some settings, even where legal and social structures limited public authority. Because Islamic societies stretched from Spain and West Africa to India and Southeast Asia, gender roles, education, property, slavery, marriage, and household practice varied widely while still being argued through shared languages of law, piety, and custom.

## Iraq

[[Iraq]] remained culturally fertile even as Abbasid political control declined under Turkic military power. The generation around 870 produced major figures such as al-Tabari, al-Ashari, and al-Hallaj.

Al-Tabari wrote a vast Quran commentary and universal history, arranging multiple reports to preserve the ummah's collective memory and accepted disagreement. Al-Ashari used rational methods to defend popular Sunni beliefs. Al-Hallaj, an ecstatic mystic, represented the risky power of Sufi expression.

## Iran, Afghanistan, and India

Eastern Islamdom saw a Sunni revival and the rise of New Persian as a second high language of Islamic culture. Iranian dynasties such as the Samanids and Ghaznavids linked Sunni rule, Persian language, Turkic military power, and expansion toward India.

## The Samanids

The Samanids ruled from Bukhara and Samarkand from 819 to 999. They contained Turkic pastoralists, converted some tribes to Islam, fostered New Persian, and patronized scholars such as al-Razi, Avicenna, and Ferdowsi.

Ferdowsi's Shah-nameh used New Persian to bring ancient Iranian heroic lore, including Rustam, into Islamic culture. This Persianizing of Iran gave self-conscious Iranian Muslims a language and memory distinct from Arabic but still Islamicate.

## The Ghaznavids

The Ghaznavids began with Sebuktigin, a Turkic slave governor in Ghazna, and expanded under Mahmud. Mahmud presented himself as a Sunni frontier warrior, raided northwestern India, established power in Punjab, and sought prestige through ruling Iran and receiving Abbasid recognition.

Ghaznavid culture joined Turkic identity with Persian language. Mahmud brought writers and scholars to Ghazna, including al-Biruni, whose works covered astronomy, mathematics, physics, gems, drugs, and Indian culture. The title sultan gained importance as a practical alternative to caliphal rule.

## Turks

Turkic migrations into eastern Islamdom lasted about 400 years and included Seljuqs, Mongols, and neo-Mongols. Turkic slave soldiers were already important, but tribally organized warrior Turks widened the distance between rulers and ruled while creating durable imperial traditions.

## Seljuq Turks

The Seljuqs were Oghuz Turks converted to Sunni Islam, probably through Sufi influence. After defeating the Ghaznavids in 1040, they took Khorasan, western Iran, and Baghdad, where Toghril Beg entered in 1055. Alp-Arslan defeated Byzantium at Manzikert in 1071, opening Anatolia to Turkish settlement.

Nizam al-Mulk, minister to Alp-Arslan and Malik-Shah, wrote the Seyasat-nameh and urged centralized rule based on pre-Islamic and Marwanid-Abbasid models. The Seljuqs legitimized themselves through caliphal investiture and defense of Sunni unity, but tribal politics and reliance on slave soldiers limited centralization.

## Franks

The Crusades began after Pope Urban II appealed at Clermont in 1095 for aid against the Seljuqs. Crusaders captured Jerusalem in 1099, but their direct impact on Islamdom remained mainly Syrian. Local Muslim factionalism allowed coastal Crusader states to survive for a time.

Muslim recovery culminated in the retaking of Jerusalem in 1187. By 1291, western European Crusaders had failed to establish a permanent Holy Land presence. In Iberia, however, the Christian Reconquista succeeded over centuries, from Toledo in 1085 to Granada in 1492.

## Mongols

The Mongols were pagan horse-riding tribes who formed a vast confederation under [[Genghis Khan]]. Unlike earlier Arabs, Berbers, and Turks, they entered Islamdom without conversion and with extreme hostility toward sedentary civilization. Their attacks from 1220 destroyed many cities in Transoxania and Khorasan.

Hulegu destroyed the Ismaili centre at Alamut and captured Baghdad in 1258, ending the Abbasid caliphate there. Mongol violence was devastating, yet later Mongol and Timurid regimes helped create new political, artistic, and social forms across Iran, Central Asia, and India.

## Mamluks, Sufism, and Later Medieval Islamdom

The Mamluk state in Egypt and Syria resisted Mongol expansion. Its sultans came from freed slave military elites, creating instability but also cultural brilliance in Cairo. Scholars such as Ibn al-Nafis, al-Qalqashandi, and Ibn Khallikan contributed to medicine, administration, and biography.

Ibn Taymiyyah of Damascus argued that Shariah was complete and adaptable through disciplined analogy serving human advantage. He attacked Shiite ideas, saint veneration, and Sufi-influenced practices. His ideas later resurfaced powerfully in the Wahhabi movement of the 18th century.

Sufism linked regions and communities. In India, the Chishti order, especially under Nizam al-Din Awliya, brought Hindus and Muslims into shared spiritual relationships. At Konya, Jalal al-Din Rumi's poetry, shaped by his connection with Shams al-Din, became one of Persian literature's greatest achievements.

## Arabs and Imazighen

The Maghrib was Arabized more deeply by the Hilalian invasion of the 11th century, when the Banu Hilal and Banu Sulaym migrated west from Egypt. Earlier Arab conquest had converted many Imazighen, but this later migration spread Arabic language far more broadly.

Imazighen, or Berbers, were indigenous Afro-Asiatic-speaking peoples from Morocco to Algeria and the Sahara. Arab conquerors converted and enlisted them, and their participation made the conquest of Andalusia possible. The Sanhajah confederation later produced an Amazigh-led Islamic movement in the western Sahara through pilgrimage, reform, trade, and jihad.

Trans-Saharan routes connected Cairo, Timbuktu, Tripoli, Bornu, Lake Chad, Tunis, Fes, Tafilalt, Ghana, and Mali. Mansa Musa's pilgrimage showed Islam's influence among West African elites, while Ibn Battutah's travels from 1325 displayed Islamdom's unity within diversity from Andalusia and Mali to India and possibly China.

## Ottomans

The Ottomans rebuilt after defeat by Timur and fused frontier warfare, Shariah legitimacy, dynastic law, devsirme recruitment, millet organization, and Byzantine inheritance. Mehmed II conquered Constantinople in 1453, ended Byzantium, and transformed the city into Istanbul.

Ottoman legitimacy grew through defense and expansion of frontiers, rule according to Shariah in principle, and control of Mamluk Egypt, Syria, and the holy cities in 1516-1517. The state trained and paid ulema, incorporated religious office into government, and asserted dynastic law beside Shariah.

## Reign of Suleyman I

Suleyman I, the Lawgiver, ruled from 1520 to 1566 and brought the classical Ottoman system to maturity. His empire reached toward Vienna, the Maghrib, Iraq, and Yemen, and even sent help to Aceh against Portuguese expansion.

The Ottoman system rested on palace household, bureaucracy, armed forces, and religious establishment. Devsirme recruits filled elite army and bureaucratic roles, while ulema trained in medreses held religious offices. Ottoman language joined Turkic syntax with Arabic and Persian vocabulary, and Sinan reshaped Istanbul's skyline with mosques and schools.

## Safavids

The Safavid state began as a Sufi tariqah at Ardabil under Shaykh Safi al-Din. By the 15th century, its leaders transformed Alid loyalty into Imami Shiism and drew support from Turkic Kizilbash disciples, eastern Anatolia, Syria, the Caucasus, and Transoxania.

Ismail I defeated the Ak Koyunlu in 1501, took the pre-Islamic title shah, and made Iran a Shiite state. Safavid claims challenged Ottoman Sunni legitimacy and created a lasting Iranian-Shiite political identity.

## Shah Abbas I

Abbas I, ruling from 1588 to 1629, shifted Safavid legitimacy from charismatic Sufi-imam leadership toward absolute monarchy. He reduced dependence on Kizilbash tribes by using converted Circassian, Georgian, and Armenian captives in a paid army.

Safavid church-state relations differed from Ottoman integration. Shiite ulama retained more independence through waqf administration, and later mujtahids claimed authority as spokesmen for the hidden imam. Abbas moved the capital to Esfahan around 1597-1598, making it a symbolically Persian centre.

## Indo-Timurids (Mughals)

Babur, descended from Timur and Genghis Khan, founded the Mughal Empire after losing Central Asian hopes and turning from Kabul toward India. At Panipat in 1526, he defeated the Lodi sultanate with artillery and laid the foundation of an Indo-Timurid empire.

Akbar faced religious plurality on a scale greater than the Ottomans. He dealt with Hindus, Jains, Zoroastrians, Jesuits, Jews, Shiites, Ismailis, Sufis, and others by banning intolerance and the special tax on non-Muslims. With Abu al-Fadl, he encouraged religious discussion and a broad monotheistic vision called tawhid-i ilahi.

## Trans-Saharan Islam

Morocco resisted Ottoman incorporation and formed new states in response to Portuguese expansion. Sharifian legitimacy, based on descent from Muhammad, and Sufi holy men known as marabouts supported the Sadi dynasty, which expelled the Portuguese and ruled from 1511 to 1603.

Songhai expanded northward, controlling trade routes into Morocco until Moroccan invasion in 1591. Later the Alawite sharifs, especially Ismail, ruling from 1672 to 1727, reunited Morocco through tribal support, black slave armies, Sufi recognition, and barakah from prophetic descent.

## Indian Ocean Islam

Indian Ocean Islam connected South and Southeast Asian Muslims with East Africa, southern Arabia, India, and Arabia. Trade routes transmitted scholars, texts, reform, Sufism, and local adaptations. Muslims in this region faced Portuguese pressure and strong Hindu and Buddhist traditions.

Aceh became the first major Muslim state in Southeast Asia around 1524. Under Iskandar Muda, from 1607 to 1637, Aceh prospered in Indian Ocean trade and learning. Abd al-Rauf of Singkel studied in Arabia and made the first Malay translation of the Quran.

Shah Wali Allah of Delhi, from 1702 to 1762, responded to the collapse of Muslim political power in India by calling for unity around Shariah rather than Akbar-style Sufi inclusiveness. Like Ibn Taymiyyah, he emphasized Quran, Sunnah, Hadith, and disciplined reconstruction.

## Empire and Colonialism

Western colonialism transformed Islamdom from the 19th century. British hegemony in India was complete by 1818, and colonies and mandates multiplied after World War I. Even regions not formally colonized experienced technological, political, cultural, economic, or psychological dependency.

Reform and resistance took many forms. The Ottoman Tanzimat from 1839 to 1876 tried to imitate European law and administration while giving equal legal standing to subjects. Young Ottomans linked liberalism and nationhood with Islam. In Iran, Qajar rulers borrowed military techniques while Shiite mujtahids claimed independence from the shah, and constitutionalism gained religious support.

Islamic protest often took the form of jihad against Europeans in Southeast Asia, Africa, and elsewhere, while secular ethnic nationalism became the most common mode of protest by the early 20th century. The Young Turk Revolution of 1908 showed how European, Ottoman, Muslim, and national identities could combine uneasily.

## Reform and Revival in the Colonial Period

![[attachments/islamic_world_002.webp|420]]

*Islamic World*

Modern reform focused on the tension between Islamic identity and national identity. Reformist intellectuals tried to recover the authentic message of the first Muslim community while fighting colonial rule or adjusting to modern institutions.

Rashid Rida gave one Salafi interpretation a political direction. He opposed saint veneration, wanted Islam renewed through early scripture, and argued that Islamic precepts should shape society and politics. He saw the abolition of the Ottoman caliphate in 1924 as traumatic and admired Abd al-Aziz ibn Saud's capture of Mecca and the later founding of Saudi Arabia in 1932.

Hasan al-Banna founded the Muslim Brotherhood in Egypt in 1928, influenced by Rida's vision of a wholly Islamic society. In contrast, Ali Abd al-Raziq argued that Islam could not serve as the basis of a political system, separating religious revelation from state structure.

## Unity, Diversity, and Historical Change

The Islamic world is a historical and cultural term for societies connected by Islam, Muslim populations, Arabic and other Islamic languages, trade, scholarship, law, and political institutions. It does not describe one race, nation, or political system. Muslims live across the Middle East, Africa, Europe, Central Asia, South Asia, Southeast Asia, and the wider diaspora, and each region developed its own local cultures, legal traditions, social structures, and relations with non-Muslim neighbours.

After the death of the Prophet Muhammad in 632, Muslim rule expanded rapidly beyond Arabia. The early caliphates built administrations across former Byzantine and Sasanian territories, while Arabic became a major language of religion, scholarship, and governance. Islamic civilization also drew on Greek, Persian, Indian, Syriac, African, and local knowledge. Translation, debate, trade, and travel supported work in mathematics, astronomy, medicine, philosophy, literature, agriculture, law, and architecture.

Sharia refers broadly to the moral and legal path derived from the Qurʾan, prophetic practice, legal reasoning, and scholarly tradition. It is not a single unchanging legal code applied in the same way everywhere. Different schools of jurisprudence, local custom, rulers, judges, and modern states have interpreted law differently. Sunni and Shiʿi communities share core Islamic beliefs and practices but differ over early leadership and later religious authority, with further diversity inside each tradition.

Political unity also changed repeatedly. The Umayyad and Abbasid caliphates were followed by regional dynasties and empires such as the Ottomans, Safavids, and Mughals. Trade networks linked the Mediterranean, Sahara, Indian Ocean, Silk Roads, and Southeast Asian seas, spreading goods, people, languages, and religious ideas. Cities such as Baghdad, Cairo, Córdoba, Istanbul, Timbuktu, Samarkand, Isfahan, Delhi, and Malacca became important centres at different times.

Colonial intervention, nationalism, oil wealth, migration, reform movements, global media, and war transformed the modern Islamic world. Many societies have debated how to relate religious authority to constitutional government, education, gender roles, economic development, minority rights, and global power. Understanding the Islamic world requires attention to this diversity and to the difference between religious ideals, historical institutions, and the policies of particular governments.
