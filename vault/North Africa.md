# North Africa

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> North Africa encompasses the Maghreb, Libya, and adjacent Saharan and Mediterranean zones whose mountains, deserts, routes, and coasts shaped contact without sustained political unity. The source follows Phoenician, Greek, Roman, Arab, and later regional histories through the geography that conditioned trade, conquest, and settlement.

## Introduction

![[attachments/north_africa_003.webp|420]]

North Africa ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_Africa)) here means Morocco, Algeria, Tunisia, and Libya, excluding Egypt. Other definitions extend from Morocco to the Suez Canal or limit the region to the Maghrib. Greek, Roman, Arab, and European terms overlapped: Libya once named land north of the Sahara and sometimes all Africa; Africa began as a Roman province in Tunisia; Ifriqiyyah later referred to Tunisia and eastern Algeria; and Berber, increasingly replaced by Amazigh for self-reference, was used for non-Arabic-speaking peoples.

The region's [[Geography|geography]] made cultural contact easier than political unity. The Atlas Lands of Morocco, Algeria, and Tunisia face the Mediterranean but also include Saharan zones, while Tripolitania and Cyrenaica in Libya are separated by the Surt Desert. Mountains, narrow settlement corridors, poor harbours, and the Sahara shaped trade, conquest, local independence, and the repeated difficulty of imposing one centre of rule.

## Ancient North Africa

Human occupation in the Maghrib is very ancient. Ain el-Hanech in Algeria, Ternifine, and Sidi Abd el-Rahmane preserve early hominin evidence, including Homo erectus and hand axes. Middle Paleolithic industries such as Levalloisian, Mousterian, and Aterian tools reached high development, and the Ibero-Maurusian or Oranian blade industry spread along coastal Maghrib and Cyrenaica between about 15,000 and 10,000 BC.

Aterian toolmakers were skilled desert hunters, and North African Middle Paleolithic flake techniques reached unusual variety and workmanship. Neanderthal remains differ between the western Maghrib and Cyrenaica, while later blade industries mark the spread of modern humans along coastal zones.

Later Capsian and Neolithic cultures show gradual adoption of new techniques rather than simple population replacement. Between about 9000 and 5000 BC, Capsian influence spread northward and eastward, while the 6th and 5th millennia BC brought animal domestication and a pastoral economy centred on cattle. Rock carvings, stone tombs, and evidence of animals now absent from the desert show how climate, hunting, and early food production changed together. The Bronze Age remained weak because metallic ores were scarce.

## Carthage and Phoenician Expansion

Carthage grew from Phoenician trading activity in the western Mediterranean. Phoenicians from Tyre and Sidon sought anchorages on routes to Spain for silver and tin. Carthage, traditionally founded in 814 BC, became the largest Phoenician colony and an imperial power. By the 4th century BC, it controlled fertile hinterlands, coastal trading posts, and much of commerce from the Gulf of Sidra to Atlantic Morocco.

Phoenician settlers preferred offshore islands and defensible promontories with sheltered beaches where ships could be drawn up. Early western posts included Utica, Hadrumetum, Tipasa, Siga, Lixus, and Mogador, but most were small markets, anchorages, and watering points rather than large colonies.

Carthaginian wealth came from trade monopoly, western metals, textiles, foodstuffs, slaves, manufactures, and trans-Saharan routes through places such as Leptis Magna. Hanno and Himilco led famous Atlantic voyages in the 5th century BC. Carthage used tribute, troops, ports, settlements, and naval power to maintain its position, but its expansion into Sicily brought repeated conflict with Greek cities and rulers of Syracuse.

Its richest goods were often perishable, which helps explain why archaeology preserves less wealth than ancient reports imply. Carthage enforced monopoly by sinking intruders' ships, extracting recognition from rivals, and maintaining mercenary armies funded by western trade and gold coinage.

## Carthage and Rome

The Punic Wars with Rome transformed North Africa. The First Punic War ended in 241 BC with Carthage losing Sicily; Sardinia and Corsica followed in 238. Hamilcar Barca and the Barcid family then built a Spanish empire, and Hannibal invaded Italy, defeating Romans at Lake Trasimene and Cannae. Rome endured, Scipio Africanus invaded Africa, and Carthage ultimately lost its independence and power.

The First Punic War was costly at sea and on land: Rome unexpectedly defeated Carthaginian fleets several times, while a Roman invasion of Tunisia failed. Carthage then used Spain's minerals and manpower to rebuild, making the Barcid project both economic recovery and preparation for renewed war.

## Carthaginian Culture and Religion

Carthaginian religion ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Religion)) emphasized human weakness before powerful and unpredictable gods. Many personal names were theophoric, such as Hannibal, Favoured by Baal, and Hamilcar, Favoured by Melqart. Carthage's art and architecture were relatively limited compared with its power, though Phoenician, Egyptian, and Greek influences appeared. Its greatest North African legacy was urbanization, agricultural advance, and linking the region to Mediterranean civilization.

Mago's agricultural treatise was the one Carthaginian written work later remembered, and it fit the city's practical strengths. Limited artistic remains contrast with durable changes in farming, settled life, and Mediterranean connections.

## The Greeks in Cyrenaica

Cyrenaica looked north toward Crete and the Aegean. Greeks from Thera founded Cyrene about 630 BC, taking advantage of fertile land in the Akhdar Mountains. Cyrene prospered through grain, fruit, horses, and silphium. It established or influenced Barce, Taucheira, Euhesperides, Apollonia, Ptolemais, and Berenice, forming the Pentapolis. It later passed under Persian, Ptolemaic, and Roman authority while retaining a strongly Greek character.

## Roman North Africa

Rome acquired [[Africa]] in 146 BC and later reorganized administration under Augustus and later emperors. Carthage became a Roman colony and proconsular capital. Numidia, Mauretania Caesariensis, Mauretania Tingitana, Tripolitania, Byzacena, and other provinces developed through roads, forts, limes frontiers, towns, local elites, colonists, and military zones that managed settled and nomadic populations.

## Roman Frontier and Urban Society

![[attachments/north_africa_002.webp|420]]

*Roman ruins at Timgad in Algeria*

Roman control used frontier systems, roads, forts, walls, ditches, and agreements with chieftains. Urban society depended on immigrants and local clan or tribal leaders who became municipal elites. Estates, tenant farmers, sharecroppers, and local landowners supported towns. Africans entered imperial administration, and by the early 3rd century many provincials in Senate and equestrian posts came from Africa.

## Economy

Roman North Africa's economy ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economy)) was agricultural. By the 1st century AD, African grain supplied two-thirds of Rome's needs, and annual production may have exceeded a million tons. Olive oil became almost as important by the 2nd century and was exported empire-wide by the 4th. Livestock, horses, wild animals for Roman spectacles, fishing, timber, marble, ports, and a dense road system supported regional prosperity.

Roads made this agricultural system work. Roman Africa may have had about 12,500 miles of roads, many military in origin but open to trade. Carthage handled the largest overseas traffic, though many older Phoenician and Carthaginian ports expanded because bulky farm products moved most cheaply by sea.

## Later Roman Empire

North Africa suffered less than many provinces during the 3rd-century crisis, though landowner revolt, pillage, tribal revolts, inflation, and reduced building activity occurred. Diocletian and Constantine reorganized provinces and military command, creating Tripolitania, Byzacena, Africa, and Sitifis province and establishing a comes Africae. Some western Roman areas were abandoned, and fortified farms became common in insecure zones.

## Roman Cyrenaica

Roman Cyrenaica remained culturally Greek. Augustus regulated relations between Roman and non-Roman communities, and Jews formed important minorities. A major Jewish rebellion in 115 devastated Cyrene and spread to Egypt. Later reorganization divided Cyrenaica into Libya Superior and Libya Inferior. Desert raids, fortified farms, and Christian institutions shaped late antique life; Synesius of Cyrene became its best-known bishop.

## Arab Conquest to 1830

After conquering Egypt in 642, Arab armies moved west into the Maghrib. By 705 the region became part of the Umayyad empire. Arab Muslim rule had a more durable cultural effect than earlier conquests: by the 11th century, Berbers had become Islamized and partly Arabized. Islam appealed partly because it gave Berbers an ideology for resisting caliphal authority and supporting alternative rulers.

The conquest proceeded unevenly. Arab raids first concentrated on Cyrenaica, while Tunisia was raided before firm rule began. Uqbah ibn Nafi occupied Tunisia in 670 and founded Kairouan, which became the first major Arab administrative and religious centre in the region.

## The Banu Midrar of Sijilmassah

The Banu Midrar principality arose after the 740s when Miknasah Berbers migrated to Tafilalt. Abu al-Qasim ibn Wasul, called Midrar, founded Sijilmassah in 757. Under Yasa ibn Midrar, it controlled the Draa region and became the leading western Maghrib centre of trans-Saharan trade, though it played little wider political role before Fatimid conquest in 909.

## The Idrisids of Fez

The Idrisid state began when Idris ibn Abd Allah, a descendant of Muhammad, was invited by Awraba leaders to northern Morocco. Idris I was killed in 791, but Idris II founded Fez and built a distinct Sunni state with Arab refugee support. During Idris II's reign, much of present-day Morocco was included, and Fez became a major centre of Islamic urban culture.

## The Aghlabids

The Aghlabids took power in 800 in Ifriqiyyah and ruled amid tension between Arab landowners, troops, urban communities, peasants, and Maliki scholars. Maliki jurists defended common people against taxation and arbitrary rule, while Aghlabids tried to placate scholars and avoid troop rebellion. Their conquest of Sicily began in 827, framed as a religious enterprise.

## The Fatimids and Zirids

The Fatimids arose from Ismaili Shiite missionary activity among the Kutama Berbers. Abu Abd Allah al-Shii organized revolt, defeated Aghlabid forces, and conquered Tunisia by 909. Ubayd Allah Said founded a Shiite state named for Fatimah and built Al-Mahdiyyah. Fatimids ended Idrisid rule but struggled to dominate the western Maghrib, later relying on Zirid deputies.

## Berber Dynasties and Islamic Culture

After Almohad collapse, the Maghrib split among Hafsids, Marinids, and Zayyanids. Earlier Almoravid legalism and Almohad religious ideology failed as durable bases for rule. Later rulers legitimized power through cooperation with religious scholars, madrasahs, mosques, and Maliki law, while Sufism spread widely. In rural areas, Sufi holy men became major custodians of Islamic norms.

## North Africa After 1830

European colonialism began decisively with the French capture of Algiers in 1830 and Ottoman reoccupation of Tripoli in 1835. Tunisia and Morocco tried reforms under European pressure. Tunisia's Ahd al-Aman of 1856 and constitution of 1860 promised legal equality and consultative government, while Morocco faced European free-trade demands, military defeats, and internal tribal resistance.
