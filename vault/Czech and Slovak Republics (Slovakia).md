## Introduction

Slovakia ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slovakia)) is a landlocked central European country whose modern statehood came only in 1993, but whose history reaches back to Great Moravia and centuries under Hungarian rule. The Slovaks' long struggle for autonomy and the importance of national identity have shaped the republic.

## Relief and Climate

![[attachments/czech_and_slovak_republics_slovakia_003.webp|420]]

*Bratislava, Slovak republic*

Slovakia is dominated by the Carpathian mountain system, with the Danubian lowlands in the southwest and the Eastern Slovakian Lowland in the east. Its climate is more continental than that of the Czech Republic, with cold mountain zones, milder southern plains, and abundant forest and alpine landscapes.

The Western Carpathians ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_Carpathians)) run in three east-west belts, and the Central Western Carpathians hold the country's highest ranges: the High Tatra Mountains, where Gerlachovsky Peak reaches 8,711 feet (2,655 metres), and the Low Tatra to their south. Rivers such as the Vah, Hron, and Hornad drain southward into the Danube system, while the Poprad flows north. Mean annual temperatures fall to about 25 F in the High Tatras and rise above 50 F in the Danubian lowlands, and roughly two-fifths of the land remains forested, sheltered in parks such as Tatry National Park.

## People and Religion

Ethnic Slovaks form the majority, while Hungarians are the largest minority and Roma, Ruthenians, Czechs, Germans, and Poles also live in the country. The population is mainly Roman Catholic, with important Protestant, Greek Catholic, and Orthodox communities and a significant secular element.

## Economy

Slovakia was the less diversified and less secure successor republic after the split from Czechoslovakia. Heavy industry, armaments, privatization, foreign investment, and the shift to a market economy all shaped the early years of independence.

Under central planning Slovakia had become a major arms producer, accounting for as much as two-thirds of the federation's armament output, but this industry declined sharply after 1989. Privatization moved more slowly than in the Czech lands, especially after Vladimir Meciar's government suspended the voucher program in 1995. The koruna replaced the federal currency in 1993 and gave way to the euro in 2009, while tax incentives from 1998 and a flat tax in 2005 helped attract growing foreign investment.

## Agriculture, Industry, and Trade

Agriculture remains important in the lowlands, while forestry, mining, metallurgy, manufacturing, and energy production are central to the wider economy. The country depends heavily on foreign trade, especially with EU partners, and its service sector and tourism expanded markedly after the transition.

## Government and Society

Slovakia is a parliamentary republic with a strong constitutional court, a multi-party political system, and local institutions that changed after independence. Labor unions, taxation, housing, health care, education, and the effort to balance democratic rules with social transition all shaped the new state.

## Cultural Life

Slovak culture developed through long contact with Czech, Hungarian, Latin, German, and Slavic traditions. Its literature, drama, music, painting, film, museums, and libraries show how modern Slovak culture emerged while preserving a distinct national voice.

## History

The historical account runs from early settlement and Magyar domination through the rise of Slovak nationalism, the Czechoslovak period, World War II, communism, the 1968 Prague Spring, the Velvet Revolution, and the 1993 independence settlement. It closes with the Meciar era, later reform politics, and Slovakia's move toward the euro and European integration.

The Slovak Republic came into being on January 1, 1993, with Vladimir Meciar as prime minister and Michal Kovac as president. Meciar's populist nationalism strained relations with the Hungarian minority and the West, and disputes over privatization and minority rights marked the 1990s. Later governments turned firmly toward Europe, and Slovakia joined both NATO ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NATO)) and the European Union ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_Union)) in 2004.
