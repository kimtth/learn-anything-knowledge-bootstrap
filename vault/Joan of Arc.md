# Joan of Arc

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> Joan of Arc was a French peasant visionary whose leadership during the Hundred Years' War helped restore French confidence and support Charles VII. Her trial and execution, later rehabilitation, and canonization made her a lasting figure in French political, religious, and cultural memory.

## Introduction

Joan of Arc, called the Maid of Orleans, was born about 1412 at Domremy and died at Rouen on May 30, 1431. A tenant farmer's daughter, she became a French national heroine after leading forces loyal to the Valois dauphin Charles to the relief of Orleans.

Her mission joined intense piety with unusual courage and practical judgment. Joan believed she was guided by the voices of St. Michael, St. Catherine, and St. Margaret, yet she also showed a military and political nerve that made her presence decisive.

## Mission To Charles

France's crown was disputed between the dauphin Charles, son of Charles VI, and the Lancastrian English king Henry VI. English forces, allied with Philip the Good of Burgundy, controlled much of northern France, while Reims, the coronation city, lay in enemy-held territory.

Joan first went to Vaucouleurs in May 1428, asking Robert de Baudricourt to send her to the dauphin. He dismissed her, but when she returned in January 1429, her firmness and piety won support. Dressed in men's clothing and escorted through hostile territory, she reached Chinon after eleven days and identified Charles even though he had hidden among courtiers.

Charles had Joan examined by churchmen at Chinon and Poitiers because fears of heresy made such claims dangerous. Joan answered that her proof would come at Orleans. On March 22, 1429, she dictated letters of defiance to the English, and the theologians advised Charles to use her.

## Orleans And Reims

At Tours, Joan received a military household, a standard showing Christ in Judgment, and a banner bearing the name of Jesus. French troops left Blois on April 27, 1429, and Joan entered Orleans with supplies on April 29. The city had been under English siege since October 12, 1428.

The relief of Orleans came quickly. On May 4, Joan's arrival helped rouse the French to take an English fort. On May 6, she and La Hire attacked another position. On May 7, she was wounded at Les Tourelles but returned to encourage the assault until the English capitulated. The next day the English withdrew.

Joan then pressed Charles to go to Reims for coronation. First, French forces cleared towns along the Loire. With Jean, duc d'Alencon, and after accepting the help of Constable de Richemont, Joan saw Beaugency surrender. At Patay on June 18, 1429, the French routed the English and broke their reputation for invincibility.

The march to Reims began on June 29, 1429. Joan urged submission from towns along the way, and after pressure at Troyes, the royal army advanced through Chalons to Reims. On July 17, 1429, Charles was crowned as Charles VII, with Joan standing near the altar beside her banner and then kneeling before him as king.

## Paris, Capture, And Trial

After Reims, Joan wanted to attack Paris, but Charles and his advisers hesitated. A truce with Burgundy and a royal retreat toward the Loire frustrated her. On September 8, 1429, she took part in an attack on Paris between the gates of Saint-Honore and Saint-Denis, was wounded, and continued encouraging soldiers until ordered to withdraw.

Her later campaigns were smaller and less successful. She helped take Saint-Pierre-le-Moutier in October 1429, failed before La Charite-sur-Loire for lack of supplies, and was ennobled with her family in December 1429. When Burgundy threatened Compiegne, she left court with a small force.

Joan was captured on May 23, 1430, after leading a sortie from Compiegne. She twice repelled Burgundians but was outflanked by English reinforcements, unhorsed, and taken with her brother Pierre and squire Jean d'Aulon. Charles, then pursuing a truce with Burgundy, made no attempt to rescue her.

Pierre Cauchon, bishop of Beauvais, obtained Joan for trial in return for payment, and by January 3, 1431, she was in his hands at Rouen. The English-backed church court treated her as a heretic partly because her voices challenged ecclesiastical hierarchy and partly because condemning her would discredit Charles's coronation.

The trial began with interrogations in February 1431. Joan swore to tell the truth but refused to reveal private words spoken to Charles. She was chained, guarded in her cell, denied Mass, and repeatedly questioned about divine revelation, prophecy, men's clothing, salvation, and submission to the church. When threatened with torture, she said forced answers would not bind her.

## Death And Rehabilitation

On May 24, 1431, Joan faced sentencing at Saint-Ouen and, hearing that she would be abandoned to secular power, signed an abjuration on condition that it pleased God. She was returned to prison, changed back into men's clothes a few days later, and said the voices of Catherine and Margaret had reproached her. The judges called this relapse.

On May 30, 1431, Joan confessed and received Communion, an unusual concession for someone condemned as a relapsed heretic. She was taken to the Place du Vieux-Marche, handed over to the English and their French collaborators, and burned. Witnesses later agreed that she died maintaining that her voices came from God.

Rehabilitation came later. In 1450, Charles VII ordered an inquiry into the trial, and in 1455-1456, proceedings initiated under Pope Calixtus III revoked and annulled the sentence of 1431. Pope Benedict XV canonized Joan on May 16, 1920, and France established a national festival in her honour that same year.

## Character And Importance

Joan's importance lies partly in military history and partly in moral symbolism. Orleans helped secure loyalty to Charles VII in northern France, but the Hundred Years' War continued long after her death, and Burgundy's later defection was also crucial.

Her lasting power comes from courage under unjust procedure, faith in her mission, and the way French civil conflict made her a victim as much as foreign war did. Later generations often reshaped her meaning for political or religious purposes, yet she became a symbol of national consciousness with whom French people of many views could identify.

## Key Events & Developments

- c. 1412 Joan of Arc: Joan was born at Domremy on the frontier of Valois and Anglo-Burgundian France.
- May 1428 Vaucouleurs: Joan first asked Robert de Baudricourt for help reaching the dauphin.
- February 1429 Chinon journey: Joan crossed enemy-held territory to meet Charles.
- April 29-May 8, 1429 Orleans: Joan helped relieve the English siege.
- June 18, 1429 Patay: French forces routed the English.
- July 17, 1429 Charles VII: Charles was crowned at Reims with Joan present.
- May 23, 1430 Compiegne: Joan was captured after covering a retreat.
- May 30, 1431 Rouen: Joan was executed by burning.
- 1455-1456 Rehabilitation: The sentence against Joan was revoked and annulled.
- May 16, 1920 Canonization: Pope Benedict XV canonized Joan.

## Key People

## Interesting Facts

- La Pucelle means the Maid, the title by which Joan became famous in France.
- Joan's standard bore an image of Christ in Judgment and helped identify her public mission.
- The sword she requested was said to have been found at Sainte-Catherine-de-Fierbois.
- Charles VII did not try to rescue Joan after her capture while he sought a truce with Burgundy.
- The Rouen trial denied Joan normal protections and made obedience to her hostile judges a test of faith.
- Her canonization came almost five centuries after her execution.
