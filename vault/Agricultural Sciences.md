# Agricultural Sciences

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> Agricultural sciences apply natural science, engineering, and economics to food and fibre production. They include soils, crops, livestock, water management, machinery, processing, and the extension of research into farming practice.

## Introduction

Agricultural sciences ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agricultural_science)) are the sciences dealing with food and fibre production and processing, covering soil cultivation, crop growing and harvesting, animal production, and the processing of plant and animal products. Because food is the most basic human need, the domestication of plants and animals about 10,000 years ago aimed to secure it, and as [[India]]'s Jawaharlal Nehru observed, "most things except agriculture can wait." Scientific methods then revolutionized output: under prescientific farming six people could barely feed themselves and four others, while one farmer in the [[United States of America (United States)|United States]] can now feed more than 100.

## History

![[attachments/agricultural_sciences_001.webp|420]]

*Justus von Liebig, founder of scientific agricultural chemistry*

Early agricultural knowledge was practical experience passed verbally from farmer to farmer, some preserved in religious commandments but rarely treated as a science. A movement to educate farmers in special academies began in central Europe, the earliest founded at Keszthely, Hungary, in 1796, though it still taught only inherited experience. The scientific approach was inaugurated in 1840 by Justus von Liebig of Darmstadt, whose Organic Chemistry in Its Applications to Agriculture and Physiology launched the systematic development of the field. Under his influence, agricultural schools spread across [[Europe]] and [[North America]] and academic agriculture came to centre on the natural sciences.

## U.S. Agricultural Education and Research

Agricultural colleges appeared in the [[United States of America (United States)|United States]] during the second half of the 19th century. In 1862, President Abraham Lincoln signed the Morrill Act ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Morrill_Land-Grant_Acts)), under which Congress granted each state 30,000 acres of land for every representative and senator to endow at least one college teaching subjects related to agriculture and the mechanic arts. This established the highly successful land-grant system of agricultural education and research.

## Major Divisions

The agricultural sciences can be divided into six major groups. Across all of them, progress follows a common pattern: research to define the requirements to be met, design and development of products and processes to serve them, and extension of the resulting knowledge to bring improved technologies into the agricultural industries. This three-step approach has proved successful around the world.

## Soil and Water Sciences

![[attachments/agricultural_sciences_002.webp|420]]

*Center-pivot irrigation system in agricultural fields*

Soil and water sciences deal with the geological generation of soil, the physics and chemistry of soil and water, and the factors governing soil fertility. Soil science began with the theory of humus in 1809, after which Liebig added experimental method and a theory of mineral-nutrient supply. A general theory of soil fertility developed in the 20th century, covering cultivation, enrichment with nutrients, drainage, and irrigation, while improved tillage and terracing conserve moisture and control erosion.

## Animal Sciences

In modern societies people rely heavily on meat, milk, and eggs, supplied by sheep, goats, cattle, water buffalo, swine, and poultry raised across the world. The animal sciences combine applied physiology, nutrition, breeding and genetics, ecology and ethology, and livestock and poultry management, while veterinary scientists study the diseases of food animals. Animal nutrition research, well established by the turn of the 20th century, has clarified animal metabolism, nutrient needs, and the value of hundreds of feedstuffs, with findings applied widely since 1950.

## Animal Breeding and Genetics

Long before modern genetics, the idea that like begets like guided the selective breeding of agricultural animals. Scientific genetics made the work more precise through systematic selection and planned matings. The most important advances came with dairy cattle and the proved sire system, in which bulls are ranked by the performance of their offspring; combined with artificial insemination, this let farmers spread the influence of superior bulls across whole herds. Performance testing and early prediction of productivity allowed earlier culling and herds of higher genetic merit.

## Reproduction, Ecology, and Ethology

Animals are renewable resources because they reproduce, and reproduction has been studied intensively since the 1930s, first in the [[United Kingdom]] and then in the [[United States of America (United States)|United States]]. Artificial insemination spread proved sires in the 1950s, and hormones later allowed control of the estrous cycle and the transplantation of embryos from superior donors. Animal ecology and ethology are younger fields: from the mid-20th century physiologists studied how temperature, light, and diet affect animals, with attention since the 1970s to behaviour and the effect of stress on immune health.

## Agricultural Engineering

Four primary branches developed within agricultural engineering. Farm power and machinery engineering covers tractors, field machinery, and mechanization; farm structures engineering designs shelter for animals and people, crop storage, and special-purpose buildings; soil and water control engineering handles drainage, irrigation, conservation, hydrology, and flood control; and electric power and processing engineering distributes electricity on the farm and applies it to uses such as controlled lighting for plants and animals.

## Agricultural Economics

Agricultural economics covers agricultural finance, policy, marketing, farm and agribusiness management, rural sociology, and agricultural law. The idea of the individual farm as an economic unit arose in the 19th century and was refined through theories of production factors, accounting, and mathematical planning. Agricultural policy examines the relations among agriculture, economics, and society, with capitalist countries stressing prices and markets and planned economies stressing managed structures.

## Other Agricultural Sciences

Other branches address problems the main divisions leave open. Agricultural work science arose from the rural social problems of [[Germany]] during the [[Great Depression]] and applies ergonomics to work procedures, labour use, human work capacity, and the fit between mechanization and labour. Agricultural meteorology studies how weather and its variations affect crops and animals, using data on solar radiation, temperature, vapour pressure, and precipitation for risk analysis and for forecasting crop yields and animal performance.

## Emerging Agricultural Sciences

The agricultural sciences are entering a new era shaped by technologies such as monoclonal antibodies and gene splicing. Molecular biology is expected to transform plant genetics: engineers aim to raise photosynthetic efficiency and nutritional quality and to transfer traits such as nitrogen fixation, disease resistance, and tolerance of herbicides and stress. Animal scientists use embryo micromanipulation to produce clones, while recombinant DNA technology engineers microbes to make specific proteins.

## Core Equations

Agricultural science often quantifies yield, nutrient input, and resource efficiency:

$$Y=\frac{\text{crop mass}}{\text{area}}$$

Yield measures production per unit area, making it useful for comparing fields, crops, or farming methods.

$$\text{water-use efficiency}=\frac{\text{yield}}{\text{water used}}$$

This ratio turns the preceding idea into a measurable comparison, with the numerator as the quantity of interest and the denominator as the scale used for comparison.

## Key Events & Developments

- 1796: The first agricultural academy is founded at Keszthely, Hungary.
- 1809: The theory of humus launches modern soil science.
- 1840: Justus von Liebig begins the scientific approach to agriculture.
- 1862: The Morrill Act ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Morrill_Land-Grant_Acts)) creates the U.S. land-grant college system.
- 1950s: Artificial insemination ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artificial_insemination)) spreads the use of proved sires.

## Key People

## Interesting Facts

- One farmer in an advanced system can feed more than 100 people, against six feeding ten before science.
- Between 1940 and 1980, U.S. corn yields tripled and farm output per hour rose almost tenfold.
- The proved sire system and artificial insemination together transformed livestock breeding.
