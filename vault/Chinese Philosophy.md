## Introduction

Chinese philosophy is a set of long-lived traditions concerned with moral cultivation, social order, political legitimacy, knowledge, nature, and the relation between human life and the cosmos. It is often called humanistic because it gives sustained attention to family, education, ritual, government, and ethical conduct. Yet this does not mean it ignores nature or transcendence: many traditions connect human flourishing to Heaven, the Dao, qi, ancestors, spirits, and the wider pattern of the universe.

The concept of Heaven, or tian, is especially important. In early political thought, the Mandate of Heaven explained why rulers could govern and why they could lose legitimacy. A ruler who protected people and governed with virtue could be said to possess the mandate; famine, disorder, cruelty, and defeat could indicate its loss. This idea gave moral language to political criticism while still supporting the value of order and responsible rule.

## Classical Schools

[[Confucius and Confucianism (Confucianism)|Confucianism]], associated with Confucius and later thinkers such as Mencius and Xunzi, emphasizes moral learning, ritual propriety, humane concern, family relationships, and responsible government. Confucian ethics does not treat a person as isolated. It asks how one becomes trustworthy as a child, parent, friend, official, and citizen. Education and self-cultivation are central because good institutions require people capable of judgment and empathy.

[[Taoism (Daoism)|Daoism]], associated with the Daodejing and Zhuangzi, questions rigid social striving and encourages alignment with the Dao, the way or underlying course of things. Daoist writing values spontaneity, flexibility, simplicity, and awareness of how artificial distinctions can trap thought. The idea of wu wei, often translated “non-action,” does not mean doing nothing; it means acting without forcing a situation against its nature.

Mohism emphasized impartial concern, practical benefit, opposition to aggressive war, and the importance of standards for judging claims. Legalist thinkers emphasized clear laws, rewards, punishment, and state power as tools for order. These schools disagreed sharply about human nature and government, but together they show that ancient Chinese philosophy included debate over ethics, logic, war, administration, and social equality—not only harmony or tradition.

## Buddhism and Neo-Confucianism

[[Buddha and Buddhism (Buddhism)|Buddhism]] entered China from India and was translated, debated, and adapted over centuries. It introduced new vocabulary for suffering, impermanence, karma, meditation, emptiness, and liberation. Chinese Buddhist schools developed distinctive approaches, including Chan, later known in Japan as Zen. Buddhism also challenged Confucian assumptions about family duty and social life, leading to criticism as well as creative synthesis.

Neo-Confucianism responded to Buddhist and Daoist influence by developing more systematic accounts of principle, material force, mind, and moral cultivation. Thinkers such as Zhu Xi and Wang Yangming argued in different ways about whether moral knowledge is discovered through studying things, inward reflection, or both. Neo-Confucian learning shaped civil-service examinations, education, family ethics, and political culture across China, Korea, Japan, and Vietnam.

## Modern Transformation

The nineteenth and twentieth centuries brought military defeat, colonial pressure, revolution, science, nationalism, and new political ideologies. Chinese thinkers translated and debated Western philosophy, including liberalism, pragmatism, socialism, Marxism, evolution, and science. Some saw Confucianism as an obstacle to modernity; others tried to reinterpret it as a resource for democracy, ethics, or cultural renewal.

After 1949, Marxism became the official philosophical framework of the People’s Republic of China, but older traditions continued to influence education, family values, popular religion, and public debate. Contemporary Chinese philosophy therefore includes classical texts, Buddhist and Daoist thought, Marxist theory, science, nationalism, feminism, environmental ethics, and global intellectual exchange. Its continuing question is how moral self-cultivation, social responsibility, and political power should relate in a changing world.

## Key Events & Developments

- Late Zhou period: Confucian, Daoist, Mohist, and Legalist schools debated ethics and government.
- First millennium CE: Buddhism entered China and generated distinctive Chinese philosophical traditions.
- Song and Ming periods: Neo-Confucian thinkers renewed debates about principle, mind, and moral cultivation.
- 20th century: science, nationalism, Marxism, and Western philosophy transformed Chinese intellectual life.
