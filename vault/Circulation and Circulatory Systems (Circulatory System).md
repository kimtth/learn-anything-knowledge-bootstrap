# Circulation and Circulatory Systems (Circulatory System)

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> The circulatory system transports blood, gases, nutrients, hormones, and wastes through the body. Its heart, vessels, and regulatory mechanisms sustain tissue function and connect closely with respiration and immunity.

## Introduction

Circulation is the transport of nutrients, gases, and wastes through living organisms. Open systems, common among many invertebrates, contrast with the closed circulatory systems of vertebrates, and blood, lymph, vessels, and pumps work together to integrate the body.

The discussion moves from general principles to detailed comparisons across animal phyla, then to the vertebrate plan and embryological development.

Circulation becomes necessary when diffusion alone cannot supply all cells. In small or simple organisms, direct exchange with the environment may be enough. In larger animals, internal fluids must carry oxygen, nutrients, hormones, metabolites, and wastes while helping maintain a stable internal environment ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internal_environment)).

## Blood and hearts

Blood is a fluid medium of transport and internal stability, while the heart is the pump that drives flow. Peristaltic hearts, tubular hearts, chambered hearts, valves, and varied arrangements appear in annelids, mollusks, arthropods, echinoderms, and other groups.

Blood composition varies widely. In many invertebrates, respiratory pigments are dissolved in plasma rather than carried in cells. Blood may transport gases, nutrients, excretory products, hormones, and even provide mechanical force, as in arthropod molting or bivalve burrowing.

Simple hearts may be only contractile vessels that move fluid by peristalsis. In the earthworm the main dorsal vessel contracts from posterior to anterior 15 to 20 times per minute, pumping blood toward the head, while five paired segmental lateral vessels pulse with their own rhythms. More elaborate systems add chambers, valves, ostia, accessory pumps, and muscular walls; in arthropods the tubular heart hangs in a pericardial sinus and is expanded by alary muscles ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_entomology_terms)) so blood enters through paired ostia. Unusually, the heart of a tunicate can pause and then reverse, pumping blood the opposite way.

An open system and a closed system solve transport problems differently. In an open circulatory system ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Circulatory_system)), fluid leaves vessels and bathes tissues in sinuses or hemocoels. In a closed circulatory system ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Circulatory_system)), blood remains inside vessels, allowing more controlled pressure, direction, and distribution; in cephalopods the blood makes up about 6 percent of body weight and stays distinct from the interstitial fluid. This difference is crucial for comparing invertebrates with vertebrates.

## Major animal groups

Each major phylum modifies circulation for its own mode of life. In annelids the system is usually closed; in mollusks it is often open except in cephalopods; arthropods generally have open systems; echinoderms use multiple fluid systems; and hemichordates and chordates show increasingly elaborate vascular organization.

In [[Annelids|annelids]], a dorsal vessel often moves blood forward, while segmental vessels connect it to a ventral vessel. Leeches and polychaetes modify this plan in different ways. In [[Mollusks|mollusks]], most blood enters tissue sinuses directly, but cephalopods have closed systems that support their active predatory life.

In [[Arthropods|arthropods]], the open hemocoel system is adapted to different respiratory strategies. Crustaceans often have gills and accessory circulation, while insects rely mainly on tracheae for oxygen transport, reducing the blood’s respiratory role. Echinoderms instead combine coelomic, hemal, and water vascular systems.

## Vertebrate circulation

All vertebrates share a common circulatory plan, though the details vary with life in water or on land. The heart develops from an embryonic tube into a chambered pump, with specializations in fishes, amphibians, reptiles, birds, and mammals.

Fishes generally have a single circulation: the heart pumps deoxygenated blood forward to the gills, then oxygenated blood moves to the body. In the embryo six pairs of arterial arches form, but most adult fishes keep only four of the six, and the first gill slit becomes the spiracle. Lungfishes show partial separation that anticipates land vertebrate patterns, with oxygenated blood from the pulmonary vein entering a partly divided atrium. Amphibians combine lungs, skin, and a three-part heart with a divided atrium but single ventricle, whose spiral valve helps keep oxygenated and deoxygenated blood apart.

Birds and mammals independently evolved complete double circulation. Their hearts have fully separated right and left sides, and the left ventricle has a thicker muscular wall to drive high-pressure systemic flow while the thinner right ventricle pumps blood to the lungs at low pressure. Among reptiles only crocodiles approach this plan, having a complete ventricular septum that produces two equal chambers, though a connection between the systemic arches still lets blood be shunted during diving. This supports the high metabolic activity seen especially in birds and mammals.

This double circulation reflects the great transition from water to land. Fishes oxygenate blood in gills before it moves through the body, but land animals must send blood to lungs and then back to the body. Amphibians and reptiles show intermediate arrangements, while birds and mammals complete the separation of oxygenated and deoxygenated blood.

This comparison shows why circulation is an evolutionary record. The same basic heart tube and arterial arches are remodeled in different lineages to meet different respiratory demands. Circulation therefore connects anatomy, embryology, and adaptation.

## Embryology

![[attachments/circulation_and_circulatory_systems_circulatory_system_001.webp|420]]

*Simplified diagram of the human circulatory system*

Circulatory function begins early in development because diffusion alone soon becomes insufficient. The heart and blood vessels form from mesoderm, and their embryology helps explain both the unity and diversity of the vertebrate system.

The embryonic heart tube bends into an S shape and partitions into chambers, so that deoxygenated blood passes in sequence through the sinus venosus, atrium, ventricle, and conus arteriosus before reaching the arteries. Paired embryonic vessels are remodeled into adult arteries and veins, and the heart forms in its own section of body cavity, the pericardial coelom. Gill arches appear in vertebrate embryos, including humans, and their transformation helps explain the deep structural relationship between fish circulation and the circulation of land vertebrates.

## Conclusion

Circulation is one of the key integrative systems of animal life, and a major example of how structure, evolution, and function are intertwined.

## Key Events & Developments

- Early animal evolution: circulatory systems diversify across phyla.
- Vertebrate lineage: a closed, chambered circulatory plan develops.
- Embryonic development: the heart forms early from mesoderm.

## Interesting Facts

- Insects rely on tracheae more than blood for oxygen transport.
- Crocodiles have a fully divided ventricle.
- The vertebrate heart develops from a simple tube.
- Tunicates can reverse the direction of blood flow.
