# Indian Philosophy

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> Indian philosophy encompasses the subcontinent's systems of cosmology, metaphysics, epistemology, logic, ethics, language, religion, and liberation. Its classical debates distinguish Veda-accepting schools from Buddhist, Jain, materialist, deterministic, skeptical, and other traditions that reject that authority.

## Introduction

Indian philosophy ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_philosophy)) comprises the systems of thought developed by civilizations of the Indian subcontinent. Its central concerns include cosmology, metaphysics, epistemology, logic, ethics, language, religion, and liberation. The classical division distinguishes orthodox astika schools, which accept Vedic authority or a transcendent order, from unorthodox nastika ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C4%80stika_and_n%C4%81stika)) schools, which reject that authority.

Orthodox systems include Nyaya ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nyaya)), Vaisheshika ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vaisheshika)), Samkhya ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samkhya)), Yoga ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yoga)), Purva-Mimamsa ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/M%C4%ABm%C4%81%E1%B9%83s%C4%81)), and Vedanta ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vedanta)). Unorthodox systems include Buddhism, Jainism, Charvaka or Lokayata materialism, Ajivika determinism, and related skeptical or naturalistic movements. The boundary between philosophy and religion is often porous, but philosophical debate produced exact arguments about knowledge, language, causation, the self, moral action, and ultimate reality.

Comparison with Western philosophy reveals both affinities and contrasts. Indian thinkers investigated truth, the origin and apprehension of validity, cognition, inference, universals, and the relation between reason and revelation. They usually gave less attention to some Western distinctions, such as analytic and synthetic judgments or necessary and contingent truths. This different emphasis makes Indian philosophy a separate analytic tradition, not merely a religious appendix.

## General Characteristics

![[attachments/indian_philosophy_001.webp|420]]

*TRV Murti*

Atman ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C4%80tman_%28Hinduism%29)), karma, and moksha are among the recurring concerns of Indian thought, though not every school accepts them in the same way. Brahmanical systems often link the self, moral causation, and liberation; Buddhism rejects a permanent self while reworking karma and liberation; Jainism treats souls as real and morally burdened; Charvaka rejects afterlife, soul, and supersensible entities.

Pramana theory, the study of valid means of knowledge, is central. Indian logic is often a logic of cognitions rather than a purely formal calculus of propositions. Perception, inference, comparison, verbal testimony, postulation, nonapprehension, and other pramanas are admitted or rejected differently by different schools. Arguments about truth, error, memory, testimony, and doubt became highly technical.

Philosophical literature developed through layered genres. A school often began with compact sutras, received explanatory bhashyas, then produced subcommentaries, critical glosses, independent treatises, and polemics. Originality often appeared through interpretation, classification, objections, replies, and refashioning inherited terms rather than through abandonment of tradition.

Scripture and reason were held in different balances. Orthodox schools recognized shruti, especially the Vedas and Upanishads, yet also used independent reasoning in logic, metaphysics, hermeneutics, grammar, and debate. Theistic and non-theistic positions both occurred inside the orthodox fold, so astika does not simply mean theist, and nastika ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C4%80stika_and_n%C4%81stika)) does not simply mean atheist.

## Historical Background

Indian philosophical history is often divided into prelogical, logical, and ultralogical periods. The prelogical period reaches to the beginning of the Christian era and includes Vedic speculation, Upanishadic monism, early ascetic movements, Buddhism, Jainism, epics, law, statecraft, and the beginnings of formal schools. The logical period, from the early centuries CE to about the 11th century, saw rigorous Buddhist, Jain, Nyaya, Vaisheshika, Mimamsa, and Vedanta debate.

The ultralogical period, from about the 11th to 18th centuries, developed increasingly technical analysis, especially in Navya-Nyaya, later Mimamsa, Vedanta, and sectarian theology. Muslim rule, the decline of Buddhism as a major Indian institution, Jain persistence, Vaishnava and Shaiva devotion, and Sufi-Hindu encounter reshaped the intellectual setting without ending Sanskrit philosophical continuity.

Vedic foundations include hymns addressed to deities such as Agni, Indra, Vayu, Varuna, Mitra, Aditi, and Vishvakarman. The idea of rita joined natural order, moral law, cosmic truth, and ritual correctness. Later hymns and the Upanishads pushed toward monism, asking whether many divine names point to one reality and whether the inner self is identical with brahman.

Upanishadic thought interiorized sacrifice and made knowledge a path to liberation. Brahman became ultimate reality, atman became the deepest self, and liberation required insight into their relation. Ideas of transmigration, karma, and release matured from earlier afterlife speculation into a framework for later Hindu, Buddhist, Jain, and philosophical debate.

## Early Non-Vedic Challenges

![[attachments/indian_philosophy_003.webp|420]]

*Indian Engagement ceremony 05*

Sixth-century BCE India contained many rival teachers and movements. Purana Kassapa denied moral efficacy, Makkhali Gosala taught Ajivika determinism, Ajita Keshakambalin expressed materialist views, Sanjaya Belathiputta represented skepticism, Mahavira shaped Jainism, and Gautama Buddha taught a new path against both ritualism and extreme asceticism.

[[Buddha and Buddhism (Buddhism)|Buddhism]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buddhism)) taught the Four Noble Truths: dukkha, the arising of dukkha through craving, cessation, and the Eightfold Path. Dukkha includes pain, impermanence, and the unsatisfactory character of the five aggregates: form, feeling, conception, disposition, and consciousness. Buddhism rejected a permanent atman while retaining moral causation and liberation as transformed concepts.

Dependent origination explains conditioned existence through links such as ignorance, contact, sensation, craving, grasping, becoming, birth, decay, and death. The Middle Path avoids sensual indulgence and self-torture. The Eightfold Path organizes right view, resolve, speech, conduct, livelihood, effort, mindfulness, and concentration under morality, concentration, and wisdom.

[[Jainism]], associated with Mahavira, rejects a creator God but emphasizes the perfectibility of souls through discipline. Its core ethic is ahimsa, noninjury to living beings. Jain metaphysics distinguishes living soul, or jiva, from nonliving substance, or ajiva, and later develops nonabsolutism, perspectival judgment, and a many-sided logic of truth.

Ajivika thought under Makkhali Gosala denied freedom and treated events as governed by destiny, environment, and intrinsic nature. Charvaka or Lokayata materialism accepted perception as the only valid knowledge and rejected inference, scripture, afterlife, destiny, and soul because supersensible entities cannot be verified.

## Epics, Gita, and Statecraft

The Mahabharata ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mahabharata)) preserves proto-systematic thought. The Mokshadharma section contains early Samkhya ideas about matter, many souls, and lists of principles. It also records Pancharatra, Pashupata, Vedic, Vedantic, and theistic elements. The epic links philosophy with kingship, duty, asceticism, devotion, liberation, and social order.

The Bhagavadgita synthesizes Samkhya metaphysics, Upanishadic monism, and Krishna-Vasudeva devotion. It recognizes paths of action, devotion, and knowledge, but gives special importance to performing one's duty without attachment to results. Its teaching joins karma-yoga, bhakti, jnana, the gunas of prakriti, the imperishable self, and surrender to the supreme person.

Kautilya's Arthashastra, associated with the Mauryan period and Chandragupta Maurya, presents political philosophy centered on artha, material power and welfare. It describes seven elements of the state: king, minister, territory, fort, treasury, army, and ally. It also treats taxation, punishment, espionage, public welfare, agriculture, industry, diplomacy, and six foreign policies.

## Classical Sutra Systems

Purva-Mimamsa ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/M%C4%ABm%C4%81%E1%B9%83s%C4%81)), associated with Jaimini, investigates Vedic injunctions and dharma. Dharma cannot be known through perception or ordinary reasoning because it concerns what ought to be done. For Mimamsa, the Vedas are apaurusheya, authorless and free from human defect. The school therefore developed powerful hermeneutics, semantics, and theories of intrinsic validity.

Vedanta-sutras, attributed to Badarayana, shift emphasis from ritual injunctions to the Upanishadic inquiry into brahman. Later interpreters debated whether Vedanta completes Mimamsa or stands apart from it. Vedanta discusses the relation of brahman, world, self, action, meditation, knowledge, and liberation, while also arguing against rival theories such as atomism, momentariness, and radical pluralism.

Samkhya ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samkhya)), classically represented by Ishvarakrishna's Samkhya-karika, is a dualism of purusha and prakriti. Many selves are pure, passive witness consciousnesses, while prakriti evolves into intellect, ego, mind, senses, subtle elements, and gross elements through the three gunas: sattva, rajas, and tamas. Liberation comes through discriminating knowledge that separates self from nature.

Yoga ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yoga)), formulated in Patanjali's Yoga-sutras, is closely related to Samkhya but adds Ishvara as a special purusha. It emphasizes disciplined practice, restraint, meditation, and the arresting of mental modifications. Where Samkhya is intellectualist, Yoga is voluntarist: liberation requires training the body, senses, mind, and attention until the self stands apart in kaivalya.

Vaisheshika ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vaisheshika)), attributed to Kanada, offers pluralistic ontology and atomistic cosmology. Its categories include substance, quality, action, universality, particularity, inherence, and later negation. Nine substances are earth, water, fire, air, ether, space, time, self, and mind. Adrishta, an unseen force, explains phenomena that atomism and ordinary causation cannot fully explain.

Nyaya ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nyaya)), attributed to Gautama or Akshapada, seeks salvation through knowledge of 16 categories, including means of knowledge, objects of knowledge, doubt, inference, debate, fallacy, and defeat. Nyaya accepts perception, inference, comparison, and verbal testimony. Its famous five-member syllogism includes proposition, reason, example, application, and conclusion.

## Buddhist and Jain Philosophical Systems

Mahayana Buddhism grew from earlier divisions such as the Mahasangika separation from the Elders around 400 BCE. Sarvastivadins held that past, present, and future dharmas exist, while Sautrantikas accepted only present dharmas and moved toward representational epistemology. These debates sharpened Buddhist analysis of momentariness, causation, perception, and nirvana.

Nagarjuna gave Madhyamika its philosophical force. His doctrine of shunyata means that persons and dharmas lack fixed essence because all things arise dependently. He criticized causality, relation, substance, self, universals, and pramanas when treated as ultimately independent. Madhyamika distinguishes conventional truth from ultimate truth while refusing rigid dualities between samsara and nirvana.

Yogachara, associated with Asanga and Vasubandhu, developed a consciousness-centered analysis of experience. Dignaga and Dharmakirti transformed Buddhist logic and epistemology, pressing Brahmanical schools to refine theories of perception, inference, language, and debate. Buddhist arguments became one of the main engines of classical Indian philosophical precision.

Jain philosophy developed logic and epistemology through figures such as Kundakunda, Umasvati, Siddhasena, Akalanka, Hemchandra, and Yasovijaya. Its doctrines of anekantavada and naya hold that reality has many aspects and can be judged from partial standpoints. Syadvada expresses conditional predication through seven possible truth forms.

## Later Mimamsa, Grammar, and Logic

Later Mimamsa divided into Bhatta and Prabhakara schools. Kumarila remained closer to Jaimini and Shabara, treating injunctions as connected with desired results and defending Vedic authority against Buddhists. Prabhakara emphasized duty for its own sake and treated the sentence as the primary unit of meaning. Both schools were realist but differed over ontology, negation, universals, and apurva.

Grammatical philosophy reached a high point with Bhartrihari. His sphota theory treats meaning as grasped through an indivisible word or sentence principle rather than through isolated sounds. His shabdadvaita, or word monism, holds that distinctions among objects are bound to distinctions in language. Mandana-Mishra later integrated linguistic monism into debates about nondualism.

Nyaya-Vaisheshika synthesis developed through Prashastapada, Vatsayana, Uddyotakara, Vachaspati Mishra, Udayana, and later authors. The combined school defended realism, pluralism, theism, atomism, universals, external relations, correspondence truth, and the reality of negation against Buddhist and other critics.

Navya-Nyaya, founded especially by Gangesha's Tattvachintamani, created a technical analytic language. It analyzed cognition through qualifier, qualificandum, relation, and avacchedakata, or limiterness. Mithila and Navadvipa scholars such as Raghunatha Shiromani, Jagadisha, and Gadadhara refined logic, epistemology, and semantics with extraordinary precision.

## Vedanta Traditions

Vedanta ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vedanta)) became the most influential family of later Hindu philosophical systems. The three main textual foundations are the Upanishads, the Vedanta-sutras, and the Bhagavadgita. Its shared questions concern brahman, the world, the finite self, bondage, knowledge, devotion, grace, and liberation, but its schools differ sharply over whether reality is nondual, qualifiedly nondual, dual, or both different and nondifferent.

Gaudapada taught ajativada, the doctrine of no real origination, in the Mandukya-karika. Shankara developed Advaita Vedanta, holding that pure self-luminous consciousness alone is ultimately real, while the world and finite persons have empirical reality but are ultimately false appearances produced by ignorance. Reason may clarify and refute, but scripture reveals supersensible reality.

Ramanuja rejected a qualityless brahman and taught Vishishtadvaita, qualified nondualism, in which brahman is personal and possesses real attributes. Madhva taught Dvaita, a dualism of God, souls, and matter, and defended five real differences. Both gave devotion and divine grace a more central religious role than Shankara's abstract nondualism.

Nimbarka taught Bhedabheda, difference-with-nondifference among God, souls, and matter. Vallabha taught pure nondualism centered on Shri Krishna, treating the world as real and liberation as loving service through grace. Chaitanya and later Bengal Vaishnava thinkers such as Rupa Gosvamin and Jiva Gosvamin developed ecstatic Krishna devotion and the unthinkable relation between God and divine powers.

## Shaiva, Mughal, and Modern Thought

Shaiva-siddhanta teaches three categories: pati, Shiva as Lord; pashu, the soul; and pasha, the bonds. Its 36 tattvas range from pure principles to material nature. The world and souls are real, bonds include karma, maya, and ignorance, and liberation requires Shiva's grace while preserving the soul's individuality.

Kashmiri Shaivism, associated with Vasugupta, Utpala, Abhinavagupta, and Kshemaraja, is monist and idealist. Shiva is the sole reality, both material and efficient cause of the universe. Divine power appears as consciousness, bliss, desire, knowledge, and action. Liberation occurs through recognition that the individual self is identical with Shiva.

Mughal-era philosophy included Sufi-Vedanta comparison, Shattari yogic practice, and reforming devotional voices such as Ramananda, Kabir, and Guru Nanak. Akbar's 1581 Din-e Ilahi tried to make reason, ethics, and anti-priestcraft into an imperial religious synthesis. Dara Shikoh translated Hindu scriptures into Persian and compared Sufi and Upanishadic cosmology.

Modern Indian philosophy emerged from reform movements and Western university education. Ram Mohun Roy and the Brahmo Samaj joined reform with monotheistic and Vedantic reinterpretation. Ramakrishna and Vivekananda made plurality of spiritual paths central. Modern figures such as Sri Aurobindo, Rabindranath Tagore, Mahatma Gandhi, Muhammad Iqbal, S. Radhakrishnan, and K.C. Bhattacharyya recast evolution, nonviolence, intuition, selfhood, creativity, and value.

## Key Events & Developments

- Vedic speculation: Ideas of rita, sacrifice, gods, and cosmic order prepared later metaphysical inquiry.
- Upanishadic monism: Brahman, atman, knowledge, and liberation became central philosophical themes.
- Sixth-century BCE ferment: Buddhism, Jainism, Ajivika determinism, materialism, and skepticism challenged Brahmanical orthodoxy.
- Mauryan statecraft: Kautilya's political theory linked artha, monarchy, welfare, punishment, and diplomacy.
- Sutra systematization: Mimamsa, Vedanta, Nyaya, Vaisheshika, Yoga, and other schools codified doctrines in compact aphoristic form.
- Mahayana philosophy: Nagarjuna's Madhyamika and later Yogachara reshaped Buddhist metaphysics and epistemology.
- Navya-Nyaya analysis: Gangesha and later scholars created a precise technical language for cognition, logic, and relation.
- Vedanta plurality: Shankara, Ramanuja, Madhva, Nimbarka, Vallabha, and Chaitanya produced competing Hindu interpretations of ultimate reality.
- Mughal synthesis: Akbar and Dara Shikoh encouraged Hindu-Muslim philosophical comparison, though orthodox reactions persisted.
- Modern reform: Western education, social reform, nationalism, and revived Vedanta shaped 19th- and 20th-century philosophy.
