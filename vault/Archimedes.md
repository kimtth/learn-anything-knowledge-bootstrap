# Archimedes

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> Archimedes was an ancient Greek mathematician, physicist, and engineer whose geometric proofs, work on pi and volumes, hydrostatic principle, and water-raising screw united rigorous theory with practical mechanics. His defence of Syracuse and correspondence with Hellenistic scholars also place his work within the scientific culture of the ancient Mediterranean.

## Introduction

Archimedes (c. 290/280-212/211 BCE) was the best-known mathematician and inventor of ancient Greece. Three achievements stand out: the relation between sphere and circumscribed cylinder, the hydrostatic principle ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Archimedes%27_principle)) later called Archimedes' principle, and the Archimedes screw ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Archimedes%27_screw)) for raising water. He lived mainly in Syracuse, likely spent time in [[Egypt]], and corresponded with major scholars such as Conon and Eratosthenes. During the Roman siege beginning 213 BCE, his war machines helped defend Syracuse until the city fell in 212/211 BCE, when he was killed.
Biography and legend intertwined around his fame. Stories include testing a crown's metal mix by water displacement and creating mechanical celestial spheres taken to Rome by Marcellus. Whether or not every anecdote is exact, they show how strongly his mechanical and mathematical skill impressed antiquity.

In mathematics, Measurement of the Circle ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Measurement_of_a_Circle)) bounded pi by inscribed and circumscribed polygons, a method dominant for centuries. Other works treated conoids, spheroids, spirals, centers of gravity, and lever balance, often solving area and volume problems that modern readers describe as proto-integration. Parts of the transmitted texts may be inauthentic, yet his role in theoretical [[Mechanics - Energy, Forces, and Their Effects|mechanics]] and rigorous geometric method remains decisive.

One of Archimedes' proudest results was the relation between a sphere and its circumscribing cylinder. By tradition he wanted a sphere set in a cylinder carved on his tomb, and Cicero reportedly found that tomb long afterward.

## Key Events & Developments

- c. 290/280 BCE: Archimedes is born in Syracuse.
- 213-212/211 BCE: He engineers defenses during the Roman siege.
- In Measurement of the Circle ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Measurement_of_a_Circle)), he establishes polygonal bounds for pi.
- In works on balances and fluids, he shapes classical [[Mechanics - Energy, Forces, and Their Effects|mechanics]] and hydrostatics.

## Key People

- Hieron II ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hieron_II)): Ruler of Syracuse and close associate of Archimedes.
- Conon of Samos ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conon_of_Samos)) and Eratosthenes of Cyrene ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eratosthenes_of_Cyrene)): Major scholarly correspondents.
- Marcus Claudius Marcellus ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marcus_Claudius_Marcellus)): Roman commander at Syracuse.
- Cicero ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cicero)): Later source linked to rediscovery of Archimedes' tomb.

## Interesting Facts

- The Archimedes screw ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Archimedes%27_screw)) stayed useful for water lifting long after antiquity.
- His polygon method for pi guided computation until much later series methods.
- His legacy combines strict proof, physical reasoning, and engineering imagination.
