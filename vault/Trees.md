## Introduction

Trees ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tree)) are perennial woody plants that regularly renew growth. Most have a single self-supporting trunk with woody tissues and branches.

Trees include some of Earth's largest, oldest, and most ecologically important organisms, such as oaks, sequoias, and many forest species.

They store carbon dioxide ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carbon_dioxide)), move water, release oxygen, form soils through decay, recycle nutrients, stabilize landscapes, and support much of terrestrial life.

## Classification

Ancient Greek classification grouped plants by form as trees, shrubs, undershrubs, and vines. Modern classification uses genetics, cell structure, ecology, behaviour, morphology, and evolutionary relationships.

Trees occur in major groups of vascular plants ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vascular_plant)): pteridophytes such as tree ferns, gymnosperms such as cycads, ginkgoes, and conifers, and angiosperms, or flowering plants.

Tree ferns are remnants of ancient floras and can reach notable heights in humid tropical, subtropical, and warm temperate forests.

Gymnosperm trees include cycads, the single living ginkgo lineage, and conifers such as pines, spruces, firs, cedars, cypresses, redwoods, hemlocks, junipers, larches, and yews.

Angiosperms dominate modern floras and include most tree species. Monocotyledonous trees include palms, agaves, aloes, dracaenas, screw pines, and yuccas, while many tree species are dicotyledons.

## Popular Groupings

Popular tree categories include softwood and hardwood, broadleaf and conifer, evergreen and deciduous. These labels are useful but imprecise.

Some hardwoods have softer wood than some softwoods, and some broadleaf trees have narrower leaves than some conifers. Evergreen status can also depend on climate.

Deciduous trees lose leaves for part of the year or in response to dry seasons. Some conifers, including larches, dawn redwoods, swamp cypresses, and related genera, are deciduous.

Some plants called trees in everyday language are not botanical trees. The banana has no true woody trunk; its apparent trunk is made of tightly rolled leafstalks.

## Ecological Importance

Forests are vital for soil stabilization, erosion control, water conservation, flood prevention, nutrient cycling, carbon storage, and oxygen production.

Even small groups of trees or single trees can prevent washouts, hold stream banks, shade soil, provide habitat, and influence local water and temperature conditions.

Tree litter, including leaves, twigs, branches, roots, and fallen trunks, becomes organic matter. This matter helps create soil and recycle nitrogen, carbon, oxygen, and other nutrients.

## Economic Importance

Wood is the most important product from trees. It has long been used for houses, rafts, canoes, weapons, fuel, furniture, tools, paper, and many manufactured goods.

Carbonized and fossilized wood forms coal, while other fossilized or hardened tree products include amber and kauri gum.

Trees also provide food, fibres, resins, oils, medicines, shade, ornamental value, shelterbelts, landscape beauty, and recreational environments.

## Unusual Tree Forms

Some tropical ficuses grow unusual support systems. Banyans produce prop roots that become secondary trunks and support enormous spreading crowns.

Strangler figs may begin high in another tree after animals deposit seeds. Their roots grow downward, thicken around the host, and may eventually kill it.

Other distinctive trees include the water-storing ombu, the traveler's tree of Madagascar, the talipot palm, baobabs, buttressed tropical trees, mangroves, and desert tree forms such as yuccas.

## Tree Structure

As vascular plants, trees have three major organs: roots, stems, and leaves. Leaves carry out photosynthesis and exchange materials through a continuous vascular system.

The stem includes nodes where leaves are or were attached, internodes between nodes, buds, bark, cambium, xylem, phloem, and wood-producing tissues.

Roots anchor the tree, absorb water and minerals, store food, and interact with soil organisms. Root hairs greatly increase absorbing surface at low carbon cost.

Many roots form mycorrhizae, associations with fungi that help absorb phosphorus and other nutrients. These partnerships can affect competition among tree species.

## Specialized Roots

Buttress roots are planklike extensions from trunks, common in some tropical trees. They can provide support, resist tension, and help protect nutrients near the base.

Pneumatophores grow above water or wet soil to help aerate roots in hydrophytic trees such as mangroves, bald cypresses, and tupelo gums.

Red mangroves have stilt roots that help with support and aeration. Some flooded trees develop adventitious water roots and tissues adapted to low oxygen.

## Growth

![[attachments/trees_001.webp|420]]

*Trees during the sunset in Gulf Islands National Park Reserve, Sidney Island, BC, Canada*

Tree height growth depends on the number of growth units produced and the elongation of internodes. Water, soil, climate, season, and hormones influence this process.

Because leaves make the carbohydrates used for wood and bark, the previous year's climate can also affect current diameter growth.

Preformed leaves develop inside winter buds, while neoformed leaves arise during the current season, so seedlings and mature trees may grow by different rhythms.

Some trees are preformers, forming most leaves in the previous year's buds; others are neoformers, forming leaves during the current growing season.

Apical buds often dominate lateral buds and maintain monopodial growth. When terminal buds abort or lateral branches take over, sympodial growth occurs.

Trees may produce adventitious buds, epicormic shoots, root suckers, and multiple flushes of growth in response to light, damage, season, or environmental conditions.

## Adaptation and Competition

Tree species adapt to [[Climate and Weather (Climate)|climate]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Climate)), soil, topography, and living neighbours. Temperature, water, altitude, latitude, and competition strongly affect where trees survive.

Some species tolerate extreme habitats but compete poorly in favourable places. Black spruce, for example, can survive bogs and mountaintops but may lose to red spruce on better sites.

Competition occurs aboveground for [[Light|light]], space, and pollinators, and belowground for water, nutrients, space, fungi, and nitrogen-fixing partners.

In harsh environments, physical limits dominate survival; in moderate environments, competition among plants becomes more important.

## Core Equations

Biological systems are often summarized with growth, heredity, and energy-transfer relations:

$$\frac{dN}{dt}=rN\left(1-\frac{N}{K}\right)$$

The logistic-growth equation models population growth that slows as population size N approaches carrying capacity K.

$$p^2+2pq+q^2=1$$

Hardy-Weinberg proportions describe expected genotype frequencies when allele frequencies are p and q under idealized assumptions.

$$\Delta G=\Delta G^\circ+RT\ln Q$$

This equation adjusts standard free-energy change for actual concentrations or pressures through the reaction quotient.

## Key Events & Developments

- c. 300 BCE: ancient Greeks classified plants by general form, including trees and shrubs.
- Carboniferous Period: tree ferns and other ancient tree forms were far more widespread.
- Modern botany: trees are classified by evolutionary relationships rather than outward form alone.

## Interesting Facts

- Sequoias are among the most massive and longest-living organisms on Earth.
- Banyan trees can spread through prop roots that become new trunks.
- Root hairs greatly increase nutrient uptake without requiring large woody roots.
- Trees ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tree)) are not one single taxonomic group; tree form evolved in many plant lineages.
