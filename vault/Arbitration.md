# Arbitration

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> Arbitration is a binding form of dispute resolution in which parties submit a controversy to a neutral decision-maker. It is used in commercial, labour, and international settings as an agreed alternative to court litigation or political negotiation.

## Introduction

Arbitration ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arbitration)) is a nonjudicial method of resolving disputes by referring them to a neutral third party whose decision, or award, is binding. It is sharply distinct from mediation and conciliation. In those other processes, the third party may recommend or encourage settlement, but the outcome has no binding force unless the disputants agree. In arbitration, by contrast, the parties accept in advance that the decision will be final.

## Commercial Arbitration

![[attachments/arbitration_002.webp|420]]

*Diagram of the binding arbitration process*

Commercial arbitration is described as one of the most common modern uses of the process. Parties to a business dispute submit evidence and arguments to an arbitrator or tribunal chosen by agreement, and they accept the resulting award as final and binding. The practice traces back to medieval merchants who needed efficient ways to settle disputes in fairs, ports, and trading centres.

## Procedure and Selection of Arbitrators
The fairness and competence of the arbitrator are central to the value of the process. Arbitrators may be chosen directly by the parties or appointed through organizations such as chambers of commerce, trade associations, or other agencies that maintain panels of experts. A tribunal often consists of three persons, with each side selecting one and those two selecting a chair.

## Labour Arbitration

Arbitration also has an important role in labour relations, especially in disputes arising between management and unions. Here it must again be distinguished from mediation and conciliation, which often aim at compromise without imposing a binding result.

## International Arbitration

![[attachments/arbitration_001.webp|420]]

*Meeting room of the Permanent Court of Arbitration*

In the international sphere, arbitration is a peaceful legal technique for settling disputes between states. Modern international arbitration is linked particularly to the Hague Conventions of 1899 and 1907, which described it as the settlement of disputes by judges chosen by the parties and based on respect for law.

## Treaties and Institutional Frameworks

Several treaties provide for arbitration in international disputes, including the Geneva General Act for the Settlement of Disputes, the General Treaty of Inter-American Arbitration, and the European Convention for the Peaceful Settlement of Disputes. The United Nations Charter also recognizes arbitration as a proper means of peaceful dispute resolution.

## The Compromis and Applicable Law

An international arbitration agreement often does not specify every procedural detail. Many questions—such as the exact composition of the tribunal, the governing law, the place of hearings, the subject matter to be decided, and the timing of the award—may instead be addressed in a separate compromis, or submission agreement.

## Enforcement and Limits

In domestic commercial arbitration, enforcement often depends on the willingness of courts to recognize arbitration agreements and awards. In international arbitration between states, compliance is usually voluntary in the sense that states generally do not agree to arbitrate unless they are prepared to live with an adverse result.

## Conclusion

Arbitration ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arbitration)) is a flexible but binding alternative to ordinary judicial or political dispute settlement. Whether used in commerce, labour, or international law, it depends on the parties' prior agreement to accept a neutral decision-maker and abide by the resulting award.

## Key Events & Developments

- 1889: The English Arbitration Act helps make commercial arbitration legally enforceable.
- 1925: The United States Federal Arbitration Act gives similar support in American law.
- 1899: The Permanent Court of Arbitration is established at The Hague.
- 1907: The Hague conventions refine international arbitration rules.
- 1965: The investment-disputes convention expands arbitration beyond state-to-state disputes.
