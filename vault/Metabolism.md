# Metabolism

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> Metabolism is the total network of chemical reactions in living cells that extracts and stores energy, builds and renews molecules, and supports growth, maintenance, movement, and reproduction. Enzyme-controlled catabolic and anabolic pathways reveal the biochemical unity of organisms and their adaptations.

## Introduction

![[attachments/metabolism_001_mw.png|420]]

*Simplified diagram of cellular metabolism showing catabolism, ATP, and anabolism*

Metabolism ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metabolism)) is the total set of chemical reactions inside living cells. These reactions extract energy from nutrients or sunlight, convert that energy into usable forms, and build the molecules needed for growth, maintenance, movement, development, and reproduction. Although organisms differ greatly, the main chemical processes of cells are strikingly similar in animals, plants, fungi, and bacteria.

Metabolic reactions are controlled by enzymes, which allow chemical change to occur quickly and specifically under mild cellular conditions. Proteins, carbohydrates, lipids, nucleic acids, and other cell materials are all made and remade through coordinated pathways. Metabolism therefore explains both the unity of life and the special adaptations of different organisms.

Proteins illustrate this unity. About 20 amino acids can be assembled into many different proteins, much as letters form words, and those proteins become catalysts, structural materials, transporters, storage molecules, and movement systems. Variant processes usually modify shared biochemical themes rather than replacing them.

## Biological Energy Carriers

ATP ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adenosine_triphosphate)), or adenosine triphosphate, is the major carrier of biologically usable energy. Cells use ATP by breaking phosphate bonds, and they remake ATP from ADP and inorganic phosphate. This links energy-releasing reactions with energy-requiring work.

ATP works as an intermediate rather than as a permanent store. Compounds with higher phosphate-transfer potential can form ATP from ADP, and ATP can then donate phosphate energy to reactions such as glucose phosphorylation, protein synthesis through GTP, transport, motion, and biosynthesis.

Other energy carriers include NAD, NADP, FAD, FMN, ubiquinone, iron-sulfur proteins, and cytochromes. These carriers move hydrogen atoms or electrons between reactions. In photosynthesis, light-driven electron flow creates a proton gradient across chloroplast membranes, and this gradient helps form ATP, much as respiratory membranes do in mitochondria and bacteria.

Not all organisms can tolerate oxygen. Many bacteria and protozoa make ATP through substrate-level phosphorylation without a respiratory membrane gradient, while bacteria that do respire can place electron-transfer systems in the plasma membrane instead of mitochondria.

## Catabolism

Catabolism breaks large food molecules into smaller molecules and releases energy in stages. In phase I, proteins become amino acids, carbohydrates become sugars, and fats become glycerol and fatty acids. This stage releases little usable energy and mainly prepares food for deeper oxidation.

Phase I releases only a tiny share of useful energy, largely as heat: roughly 0.6 percent for proteins and carbohydrates and about 0.1 percent for fats. Its biological purpose is preparation, not ATP yield.

In phase II, small molecules are incompletely oxidized into a few central products, especially acetyl coenzyme A, oxaloacetate, and alpha-oxoglutarate. In phase III, the TCA cycle and respiratory chain complete oxidation to carbon dioxide and water in aerobic organisms. This convergence lets many nutrients feed a limited set of central pathways.

## Anabolism

Anabolism builds macromolecules from central metabolic intermediates. Catabolic pathways begin with many foods and converge on shared intermediates, while anabolic pathways begin from these diffuse intermediates and lead to specific products such as amino acids, nucleotides, lipids, carbohydrates, and cell structures.

Catabolism and biosynthesis are linked by ATP, ADP, AMP, NADH, NADPH, and other carriers, but the pathways are usually not exact reverses. Different enzymes and bypass reactions allow the cell to control breakdown and synthesis separately, preventing futile cycling and matching metabolism to need.

## Glycolysis and Carbohydrate Pathways

Glycolysis is a central route for glucose breakdown. Glucose is first phosphorylated to glucose 6-phosphate, trapping it inside the cell and preparing it for further reactions. Hexokinase works in most cells at low glucose levels, while liver glucokinase responds when blood glucose is high.

Through glycolysis, glucose is converted through phosphorylated intermediates into pyruvate, with ATP investment early and ATP formation later. Some reactions are reversible, while others are strongly irreversible and require special bypasses during glucose synthesis.

The phosphogluconate pathway, or pentose phosphate pathway, rearranges sugars and generates NADPH. NADPH supplies reducing power for biosynthesis, especially fatty-acid and steroid synthesis, while pentose phosphates provide material for nucleic acids. Some microorganisms also use alternative glucose pathways that pass through 6-phosphogluconate and KDPG.

## Lipid Catabolism

Glycerol from fats enters metabolism after phosphorylation to glycerol 1-phosphate and oxidation to dihydroxyacetone phosphate, a glycolytic intermediate. This links fat breakdown with carbohydrate metabolism.

Fatty acids must first be activated by attachment to coenzyme A. In higher organisms, fatty acyl groups are moved into mitochondria with help from carnitine systems; defects in these systems are inborn errors of metabolism. Once activated, fatty acyl coenzyme A molecules undergo beta-oxidation.

In beta-oxidation, each cycle removes two carbons as acetyl coenzyme A and produces reduced FAD and NADH. Palmitic acid, with 16 carbons, passes through seven cycles and yields eight acetyl coenzyme A molecules. Odd-chain fatty acids end with propionyl coenzyme A, which can be converted to TCA-cycle intermediates in many organisms.

## Amino-Acid Catabolism and Nitrogen Disposal

Amino acids are used mainly for protein synthesis, but excess amino acids can be broken down. Their catabolism usually begins with removal or transfer of the amino group to keto acids such as pyruvate, oxaloacetate, or alpha-oxoglutarate.

Nitrogen can be released as ammonia, but many animals convert it to safer products. The urea cycle uses carbamoyl phosphate, ornithine, citrulline, argininosuccinate, arginine, and urea to remove nitrogen. Carbon skeletons from amino acids enter central metabolism as pyruvate, acetyl coenzyme A, alpha-oxoglutarate, succinyl coenzyme A, fumarate, or oxaloacetate.

## The TCA Cycle and Complete Oxidation

The TCA cycle, also called the Krebs cycle, oxidizes acetyl coenzyme A in controlled steps. Acetyl coenzyme A condenses with oxaloacetate to form citrate, and later reactions regenerate oxaloacetate while releasing carbon dioxide and reducing equivalents.

The cycle solves a chemical problem: a two-carbon unit is difficult to dismantle directly, so it is joined to a four-carbon acceptor and oxidized as a six-carbon compound. Reactions involving isocitrate, alpha-oxoglutarate, succinate, fumarate, malate, and oxaloacetate generate NADH, FADH2, and ATP or GTP. Succinate dehydrogenase links the TCA cycle directly with the respiratory chain.

Each turn of the cycle produces one substrate-level ATP or GTP and several reduced carriers. When their electrons pass to oxygen, the yield is far greater than fermentation: complete aerobic oxidation of glucose through pyruvate and the TCA cycle can supply many times the ATP gained from anaerobic glycolysis alone.

## ATP Synthesis and the Respiratory Chain

ATP synthesis occurs by substrate-level phosphorylation and oxidative phosphorylation. Substrate-level phosphorylation transfers phosphate directly to ADP and does not require oxygen. It is important in fermentation and oxygen-poor tissues.

Oxidative phosphorylation uses the respiratory chain. Electrons move through carriers such as NAD, flavoproteins, ubiquinone, iron-sulfur proteins, and cytochromes to oxygen. The energy released creates a proton gradient and electrical potential across mitochondrial or bacterial membranes.

ATP synthetase uses the return flow of protons to form ATP from ADP and inorganic phosphate. This converts electrical or proton-gradient energy into chemical bond energy. Cyanide and carbon monoxide inhibit the terminal cytochrome oxidase step, showing how essential electron transport is to aerobic life.

Respiration therefore converts chemical energy into electrical or proton-gradient energy and then back into chemical bond energy. The supply of ADP can limit ATP synthetase activity, and similar proton gradients can also power bacterial movement or membrane transport.

## Anaplerotic Routes and the Glyoxylate Cycle

Anaplerotic reactions refill TCA-cycle intermediates removed for biosynthesis. Pyruvate or phosphoenolpyruvate can accept carbon dioxide to form oxaloacetate, allowing carbohydrate-derived carbon to replenish the cycle.

The glyoxylate cycle lets bacteria, fungi, and plants grow on acetate or fat-derived two-carbon units. It bypasses the carbon dioxide-releasing steps of the TCA cycle, splitting isocitrate into succinate and glyoxylate and condensing glyoxylate with acetyl coenzyme A to form malate. Higher animals lack this route and cannot make net glucose from acetyl coenzyme A alone.

## Gluconeogenesis and Carbohydrate Biosynthesis

Gluconeogenesis forms sugars from noncarbohydrate precursors such as lactate, glycerol, and glucogenic amino acids. Plants and photosynthetic microorganisms also fix carbon dioxide into sugar intermediates.

Many steps of glucose biosynthesis reverse glycolysis, but three irreversible glycolytic reactions require bypasses. Pyruvate is converted to phosphoenolpyruvate using energy, fructose 1,6-diphosphate is hydrolyzed to fructose 6-phosphate, and glucose 6-phosphate is hydrolyzed to glucose. These bypasses make glucose synthesis directionally possible.

## Lipid Biosynthesis

Fatty-acid synthesis uses acetyl coenzyme A but first converts it to malonyl coenzyme A through acetyl CoA carboxylase, a biotin-containing enzyme that consumes ATP. Malonyl groups and acetyl groups are then handled by acyl-carrier protein or fatty acid synthetase.

Phospholipids and lipoproteins combine fatty acids with precursors such as glycerol 1-phosphate, serine, and inositol. These precursors arise from central pathways, showing that membrane synthesis depends on carbohydrate and amino-acid metabolism as well as lipid metabolism.

## Amino-Acid and Nucleotide Biosynthesis

Amino-acid biosynthesis differs by organism. Humans synthesize about 10 of the 20 common amino acids and must obtain the rest from food. Plants and many microorganisms can synthesize a broader range, and some bacteria or legumes with nitrogen-fixing bacteria can use atmospheric nitrogen.

Microorganisms vary especially widely. Some strains of Escherichia coli can make all amino acids from a single carbon source and ammonium salts, while other bacteria may require many amino acids from their surroundings. Pathway complexity reflects the chemistry of each final amino acid.

Mononucleotides are needed for RNA, DNA, and energy exchange. Purine nucleotides such as AMP and GMP are built from ribose 5-phosphate through a long sequence leading to inosinic acid. Pyrimidine synthesis is simpler, beginning with aspartate and carbamoyl phosphate and leading toward uridine and cytidine nucleotides.

Deoxyribonucleotides are formed by reducing ribonucleoside diphosphates through thioredoxin and thioredoxin reductase. DNA also uses thymine instead of uracil, so dTMP is produced from dUMP with folic acid as a coenzyme.

## Metabolic Regulation

Metabolic regulation keeps catabolism, anabolism, ATP production, and building-block supply balanced. Feedback inhibition lets final products slow early pathway enzymes, as in amino-acid and nucleotide synthesis.

Allosteric regulation allows molecules to change enzyme activity without being substrates. Aspartate carbamoyltransferase is inhibited by CTP and stimulated by ATP, helping balance purine and pyrimidine synthesis. Phosphofructokinase is inhibited by ATP and citrate and stimulated by AMP, linking glycolysis to the cell’s energy state.

Cells also use coarse control by changing enzyme synthesis. In bacteria such as Escherichia coli, available nutrients can repress unneeded biosynthetic enzymes. Together, pathway structure, feedback, allostery, energy charge, and enzyme production allow metabolism to respond to changing conditions.

## Key Events & Developments

- Cellular life: Metabolism ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metabolism)) developed linked pathways for energy conservation and biosynthesis.
- Aerobic conditions: Oxidative phosphorylation became a major ATP-producing system in nonphotosynthetic cells.
- Modern biochemistry: Studies of enzymes such as aspartate carbamoyltransferase clarified catalytic and regulatory enzyme sites.
