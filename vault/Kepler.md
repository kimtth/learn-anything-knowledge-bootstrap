# Kepler

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> Johannes Kepler was a German astronomer who established the three laws of planetary motion, replacing circular orbital assumptions with ellipses and quantitative relations between distance and period. His work made heliocentric astronomy dynamic and later enabled Newton's theory of gravitation.

## Introduction

![[attachments/kepler_001.webp|420]]

*Portrait of Johannes Kepler*

Johannes Kepler, born in 1571 and dead in 1630, was a German astronomer who discovered the three major laws of planetary motion. He showed that planets move in ellipses with the Sun at one focus, that their orbital motion follows an area rule, and that their periods relate mathematically to orbital distance.

Kepler's laws later became central to physics after Isaac Newton explained them through universal gravitation. Kepler himself saw them as celestial harmonies revealing God's design. His work transformed Nicolaus Copernicus's Sun-centred system into a dynamic astronomy in which the Sun physically acted on planets moving in noncircular paths.

Kepler's achievement was broader than orbital theory. He explained vision, improved the theory of telescope optics, discovered semiregular polyhedrons, and tried to reform astrology by grounding it in physical and harmonic principles. His science joined theology, astrology, mathematics, and physical explanation.

## Social World And Education

Kepler grew up in a modest family in Weil der Stadt. German education in the late 16th century was controlled by churches and rulers, and scholarships trained poor boys for service as teachers, ministers, or officials. A ducal scholarship sent Kepler to the Lutheran Stift at Tubingen in 1589.

Copernican astronomy reached Kepler through Michael Maestlin, his mathematics professor. Maestlin privately supported Copernicus and lent Kepler an annotated copy of De revolutionibus. Kepler planned to become a theologian, but he came to believe that astronomy was also a religious vocation.

## Astronomical Work

Mysterium cosmographicum ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mysterium_Cosmographicum)), published in 1596, set Kepler's lifelong direction. He proposed that the spacing of the six Copernican planets could be explained by nesting the five regular polyhedrons between their orbits. The fit was imperfect, but close enough to convince him that mathematical harmony structured the universe.

Kepler's physical astronomy broke with older ideas of uniform circular motion. He imagined that a force from the Sun controlled planetary motion and explained why distant planets moved more slowly. William Gilbert's work on magnetism later helped Kepler imagine a physical solar force rather than merely geometrical order.

Tycho Brahe's observations gave Kepler the data he needed. At [[Prague]], after Tycho's death, Kepler gained access to highly accurate observations of Mars. A small error of about eight minutes of arc convinced him that circular orbits had to be abandoned, leading to the elliptical orbit of Mars and the first planetary law.

Astronomia Nova, published in 1609, presented the results of Kepler's struggle with Mars, including the ellipse and the area law. These discoveries also belonged to his wider effort to reform astronomy, astrology, and celestial physics through accurate observation and harmonic mathematics.

## Astrology, Optics, And Telescopes

Kepler's astrology was not simple fortune telling. He tried to restrict astrological claims and make them more reliable by connecting them with physical and harmonic principles. At the imperial court, astrology remained useful, but Kepler rejected many unsupported claims about stellar influence.

[[Principles of Optics (Optics)|Optics]] became another major field. In 1604, Kepler explained vision by showing that light from points in the visual field enters the pupil, is refracted by the eye, and forms an upside-down image on the retina. This gave a better account of why eyeglasses worked.

[[Galileo]]'s telescope discoveries in 1610 won Kepler's strong support. Kepler wrote replies defending the new Jovian moons, developed a theory of telescope optics in Dioptrice, and described a telescope using two convex lenses. His support mattered because Galileo had few confident allies at first.

## Linz, Harmonies, And Tables

Kepler's life worsened after 1611. His wife became ill, a son died of smallpox, Emperor Rudolf abdicated, and Kepler failed to gain a post at Tubingen because of theological suspicion. He moved to Linz, married Susanna Reuttinger in 1613, and continued working despite family losses and religious pressure.

Stereometria Doliorum Vinariorum grew from a practical problem about measuring wine barrels. Kepler objected to rough merchant methods and expanded mathematical treatment of volumes generated by conic sections. The work shows his habit of turning practical problems into theoretical mathematics.

Harmonice Mundi, published in 1619, gathered more than two decades of work on geometrical, musical, metaphysical, astrological, astronomical, and psychological harmony. In it Kepler stated the harmonic law, relating the squares of planetary periods to the cubes of their orbital distances.

Epitome Astronomiae Copernicanae, published from 1618 to 1621, became the most important theoretical resource for Copernican astronomy in the 17th century. Tabulae Rudolphinae, published in 1627, completed astronomical tables begun from Tycho's work and strengthened heliocentric astronomy against opponents.

## Final Years And Legacy

Kepler's final decade was marked by hardship. His mother was accused of witchcraft, and he spent years helping defend her until her exoneration in 1621. He later received support from Albrecht von Wallenstein, who expected horoscopes in return.

Kepler died in Regensburg in 1630 while trying to collect money owed to him. His epitaph contrasted measuring the heavens with measuring earthly shadows. His laws survived even when later science ignored much of their original theological and harmonic framework.

## Core Equations

Physical models often begin by relating force, energy, waves, and conserved quantities:

$$\vec{F}=m\vec{a}$$

This is Newton's second law: force is the product of mass and acceleration, so a larger mass or a faster change in motion requires a larger net force.

$$E=h\nu$$

This relation says that photon energy is proportional to frequency; higher-frequency radiation carries more energy per photon.

$$E=mc^2$$

This expresses mass-energy equivalence: mass is a form of energy, with the speed of light squared as the conversion factor.
