# Communication

## Introduction

Communication is the exchange of meanings through a common system of symbols. Communication is a human process involving messages, signals, signs, symbols, icons, gestures, vocal expression, feedback, and social context.

Communication theory ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Communication_theory)) treats communication as more than simple transmission. It shows that meaning depends on shared codes, social expectations, and the setting in which a message is sent and received.

Scholars have studied communication since ancient Greece, but it became a distinct modern subject only gradually. I.A. Richards defined communication as one mind acting on its environment so that another mind has a related experience. Later scholars found the field much broader, covering dozens of approaches from psychology and anthropology to politics, architecture, mass media, and social behaviour.

## Core concepts

Communication theory ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Communication_theory)) distinguishes between signals and signs, then places symbols and icons at a deeper level of cultural meaning. It also covers nonverbal communication, proxemics, kinesics, laughter, and the role of common social understanding.

Signals ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Signal)) may carry information without shared cultural depth, but symbols depend on convention and learned interpretation. That distinction explains why body language, space, tone, and gesture matter as much as spoken words in ordinary life.

Signs differ from signals because signs carry more inherent and concrete meaning. A badge, traffic sign, uniform, or barber pole can quickly tell people what action or interpretation is expected. Symbols go further because they organize abstractions, values, identities, and cultural realities that may be difficult to state directly.

Communication theory ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Communication_theory)) is linked to information theory, cybernetics, and feedback models associated with Shannon, Weaver, Wiener, and later theorists. Telegraphy, telephony, broadcasting, and mass media reshaped the scale and speed of communication.

Feedback ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Feedback)) is central because communication is two-way in practice, even when one side is not speaking loudly or directly. Modern media show how technical systems extend communication while also making it more complex and more impersonal.

Technological change also expanded the field. The telegraph, telephone, wireless radio, popular newspapers, periodicals, motion pictures, broadcasting, and television all made it possible for a few senders to reach large populations quickly. These media helped create mass communication as a cultural and institutional force.

## Conclusion

Communication ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Communication)) is a multidisciplinary field that reaches from biology and psychology to media systems and culture.

Communication ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Communication)) therefore becomes a bridge between individual behavior and collective life. Its scope includes language, signal systems, social interaction, and the technologies that amplify messages.

A final implication is that communication must be studied across many levels at once. A message may be a signal, a sign, a symbol, a gesture, a laugh, a spatial relation, a broadcast, or an institutional pattern. Its meaning depends on code, context, feedback, and the shared habits of a culture.
