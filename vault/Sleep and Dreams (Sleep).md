## Introduction

![[attachments/sleep_and_dreams_sleep_001.webp|420]]

*Hypnogram showing sleep stages across a night*

Sleep ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sleep)) is a normal, reversible, recurring state in which responsiveness to outside stimulation is reduced. It includes predictable physiological changes: internally generated brain activity, hormone fluctuations, muscle relaxation, and changes across the whole body. No single purpose fully explains sleep because it affects all physiology rather than one organ alone.

Sleep contrasts with wakefulness, when sensitivity and response to the environment are stronger. The alternation of sleeping and waking is a major example of biological periodicity in higher vertebrates. Sleep has no single perfect definition, so scientists use several behavioural, sensory, motor, subjective, and physiological signs together.

## The Nature of Sleep

Sleep posture usually involves relaxed skeletal muscles and the absence of purposeful waking behaviour. In humans and many animals, its usual posture is horizontal rest. Closed eyelids, reduced response to sound or light, and the seeking of quiet or monotonous surroundings also support sleep.

Three criteria help separate sleep from coma or hibernation: reversibility, recurrence, and spontaneity. A sleeper can usually be awakened more easily than a person in coma. Sleep also returns in partly predictable ways, influenced by prior wakefulness and internal timing.

Some cases blur the definition. Sleepwalking raises the question of whether the brain can be partly asleep and partly awake. Marine mammals may sleep with one half of the brain remaining responsive so they can surface for air. Such examples show why sleep must be defined by converging evidence rather than one sign.

Physiological study uses the electroencephalogram, or EEG, to identify brain-wave patterns associated with sleep. Yet EEG signs are not perfect by themselves. Subjective reports can also conflict with behaviour or physiology, so scientists still ask whether sleep should be measured by objective signs, inner experience, or both.

## Sleep in Animals and Development

Among [[Mammals|mammals]] and many birds, recurring, spontaneous, reversible inactivity with reduced response is generally accepted as sleep. In reptiles, fish, insects, and some amphibians, the evidence is harder to interpret. Bullfrogs may not meet sensory-threshold criteria, while tree frogs show clearer reduced sensitivity during rest.

Sleep development changes strongly across human development. Newborns may spend about half their sleep in a rapid-eye-movement stage, while adults spend about 25 percent and elderly people less than 20 percent. Deep NREM stage 3 sleep also declines with age and may disappear in some older people.

The timing of sleep also changes. Infants have polyphasic sleep, with many sleep and waking periods across the day. Children gradually move toward one main night sleep. In older age, daytime napping and shorter night sleep may return, partly because circadian influence weakens and sleep disorders become more common.

Sleep patterning depends on homeostatic sleep pressure and circadian rhythm. The longer a person stays awake, the stronger the pressure for sleep becomes. The circadian system supports wakefulness at some times, especially early evening, and when that support falls late in the day, sleepiness increases.

## NREM Sleep

NREM sleep ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-rapid_eye_movement_sleep)), or non-rapid-eye-movement sleep, is divided into stages 1, 2, and 3. Stage 1 is a drowsy transition into sleep. Stage 2 is often treated as the first stable stage of sleep and is marked by sleep spindles and other EEG patterns. Stage 3 is slow-wave sleep, the deepest NREM stage.

NREM sleep ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-rapid_eye_movement_sleep)) usually looks restful. Autonomic functions often slow and become less variable, and brain activity is lower on average, especially in the thalamus and frontal cortex. NREM stage 3 tends to appear early in the night and is given high priority during recovery after sleep deprivation.

NREM sleep is not simply passive. Brain imaging shows that memory-related regions such as the hippocampus may reactivate during NREM sleep after learning. Spindles and slow waves may help reinforce neural connections and support later daytime cognitive function.

## REM Sleep

![[attachments/sleep_and_dreams_sleep_002.webp|420]]

*EEG recording associated with REM sleep*

REM sleep, or rapid-eye-movement sleep, is a more activated state. Its EEG resembles lighter sleep or wakefulness more than deep NREM sleep. It includes rapid eye movements, high brain oxygen use, increased blood flow, higher brain temperature, and variable heart and breathing rates.

REM sleep also includes strongly reduced muscle tone, except in muscles needed for breathing. This motor inhibition helps prevent the sleeper from acting out dream movements. Animal studies point to brainstem structures such as the locus coeruleus as important in this inhibition.

REM activity is uneven across the brain. Brainstem, thalamic, and limbic areas may be active, while some frontal and parietal regions are less active. This pattern may help explain dreams: limbic activation fits emotional intensity, while reduced frontal control may relate to dream bizarreness, weak insight, and distorted time and space.

## NREM and REM Cycles

Adult sleep usually begins with about 70 to 90 minutes of NREM sleep before the first REM period, which may last 5 to 15 minutes. NREM and REM then alternate in ultradian cycles through the night. REM periods tend to lengthen later, while stage 3 NREM becomes less prominent.

This cycling shows that sleep architecture is organized rather than uniform. Different stages may serve different functions, including physical recovery, neural regulation, memory processing, emotional activity, and dream production. The exact purposes remain debated, but sleep is clearly active and structured.

## Insomnia

Insomnia is not one disorder but a group of disorders. It involves inability to begin or maintain sleep, and it is not simply caused by another known medical or psychiatric condition or by medication. People who describe themselves as poor sleepers often sleep more than they think, but physiological signs may show real disturbance.

Insomnia can include frequent body movement, stronger autonomic activity, less REM sleep, and waking rhythms intruding into sleep stages. Chronic insomnia may be linked to psychological disturbance. Long-term treatment with cognitive and behavioural methods, such as relaxation and temporary sleep restriction followed by gradual reinstatement, can be more effective than drugs.

## Sleep-Related Breathing Disorders

![[attachments/sleep_and_dreams_sleep_003.webp|420]]

*Person using a CPAP machine for sleep apnea*

Obstructive sleep apnea is a common modern sleep problem. During sleep, the upper airway behind the tongue repeatedly blocks airflow. This can happen many times per hour, lowering blood oxygen and raising carbon dioxide.

The repeated breathing interruptions fragment sleep and can lead to chronic sleep deprivation if untreated. Less common breathing problems during sleep may come from other causes, but apnea is especially important because its effects combine disordered breathing with disturbed sleep architecture.

## Parasomnias

Parasomnias include events such as sleep talking, sleepwalking, bed-wetting, teeth grinding, snoring, nightmares, and sleep terrors. Sleep talking is often only inarticulate mumbling and is common enough that it is not automatically pathological. Sleepwalking is common in children and can continue into adulthood.

Enuresis, or bed-wetting, may be secondary to organic conditions or a primary disorder, usually in childhood. Bruxism, or teeth grinding, is not tied consistently to one sleep stage and usually does not greatly change the overall sleep pattern.

Frightening sleep experiences need distinctions. Sleep terrors usually occur in early childhood when NREM sleep is suddenly interrupted. A child may scream, sit up, seem terrified, and later remember little or nothing. Anxiety dreams are more often linked to arousal from REM sleep and include remembered dream content matching the disturbed awakening.

## Key Events & Developments

- 1953: Eugene Aserinsky and Nathaniel Kleitman reported REM sleep, changing sleep science.
- 20th century: EEG studies made it possible to classify sleep stages by brain activity.
- Later sleep research: Brain imaging showed that both NREM and REM sleep contain complex, localized brain activity.
- Contemporary research: Sleep disorders such as insomnia and sleep apnea are studied with behavioural, physiological, and medical approaches.

## Key People

- Eugene Aserinsky ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eugene_Aserinsky)): Co-discoverer of REM sleep.
- Nathaniel Kleitman ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nathaniel_Kleitman)): Co-discoverer of REM sleep and major figure in modern sleep research.

## Interesting Facts

- Sleep ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sleep)) is reversible, unlike coma, and recurring, unlike many abnormal unconscious states.
- Newborns spend much more sleep time in REM-like sleep than adults do.
- Marine mammals may sleep with part of the brain still responsive so they can surface to breathe.
- REM muscle inhibition may protect sleepers from acting out dreams.
