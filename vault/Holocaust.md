# Holocaust

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> The Holocaust was the systematic, state-sponsored murder of six million Jewish people and millions of other victims by Nazi Germany and its collaborators during World War II. It grew from racial antisemitism, persecution, exclusion, deportation, and the Nazi Final Solution, making it central to the history of genocide and human rights.

## Introduction

The Holocaust ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Holocaust)), called Shoah in Hebrew and Hurban in Yiddish and Hebrew, was the systematic, state-sponsored murder of six million Jewish men, women, and children, along with millions of other victims, by Nazi Germany and its collaborators during [[World Wars (World War II)|World War II]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_War_II)). The Germans called this program the Final Solution to the Jewish question. The word Holocaust comes from a Greek translation of a Hebrew term for a sacrifice burned whole, a meaning later connected to the crematoria and open fires of extermination camps.

Nazi antisemitism existed before the Nazis took power in 1933. Adolf Hitler had already written in 1919 that antisemitism should lead to legal opposition and finally to the removal of Jews altogether. In Mein Kampf, written in 1925-27, he described Jews as an evil race seeking world domination. Nazi ideology joined older religious and political antisemitism to racial antisemitism, treating Jews not as a faith group that could convert or a political group that could be expelled, but as a racial enemy to be annihilated.

After [[Hitler]] became chancellor on January 30, 1933, the first goal was to consolidate power and destroy political opposition, but the attack on Jews began almost immediately. A boycott of Jewish businesses started on April 1, 1933; Jews were dismissed from the civil service a week later; Jewish participation in schools was restricted by quota; and Nazi students burned books on May 10. Persecution moved through law, propaganda, exclusion, and public humiliation before becoming mass murder.

The Nuremberg Laws ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuremberg_Laws)) of 1935 made racial antisemitism state law by defining Jews by ancestry and stripping them of citizenship and rights. Later steps excluded Jews from ordinary economic and social life. The violence of Kristallnacht ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kristallnacht)) in November 1938, when synagogues were burned, Jewish businesses destroyed, and many Jews arrested, showed that legal persecution could turn openly violent. Emigration became harder as Nazi control expanded and other countries admitted only limited numbers of refugees.

As [[Germany]] conquered territory, Nazi policy shifted from exclusion inside Germany to control over large Jewish populations in eastern Europe. Jews were forced into ghettos, especially in occupied Poland, where overcrowding, hunger, disease, forced labor, and terror weakened communities before deportation. Ghettos also made registration and transport easier for German authorities, who used Jewish councils under impossible pressure to carry out orders while trying to keep people alive.

## The Einsatzgruppen

The invasion of the Soviet Union in 1941 brought mobile killing to the center of Nazi policy. About 3,000 men of the Einsatzgruppen ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Einsatzgruppen)), or deployment groups, moved behind the Wehrmacht into conquered Soviet territory. Their task was to murder Jews, Soviet commissars, and Roma, often with help from local police, antisemitic civilians, and Axis troops.

The Einsatzgruppen ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Einsatzgruppen)) carried out mass shootings by entering towns, rounding up victims, taking them to the outskirts, and killing them in family groups. At Baby Yar near Kiev, 33,771 Jews were killed on September 28-29, 1941. In the Rumbula Forest near Riga, 25,000 to 28,000 Jews were killed on November 30 and December 8-9. More than 70,000 Jews were killed at Ponary near Vilna, and 9,000 Jews, half of them children, were slaughtered at the Ninth Fort near Kovno on October 28.

These shootings became known as a form of Holocaust by bullets. When Soviet counteroffensives threatened to reveal the crimes, special units returned to dig up bodies and burn them to destroy evidence. The Einsatzgruppen killed more than one million people, mostly Jews. Historians such as Christopher Browning emphasize conformity, peer pressure, careerism, obedience, and group solidarity, while Daniel Goldhagen emphasizes willing genocidal antisemitism; both note that men could ask to be excused and almost all chose to kill.

Mass shooting was soon joined by more centralized killing methods. Nazi Germany developed extermination camps and gas vans to make murder more efficient and to reduce the strain on killers. Camps such as Belzec, Sobibor, Treblinka, and Auschwitz-Birkenau became sites where deported Jews and other victims were killed soon after arrival, while some prisoners were temporarily spared for forced labor or camp tasks.

Deportations linked ghettos, occupied cities, rail systems, and camps into one killing process. The Nazi system used deception, bureaucracy, guards, police, collaborators, and forced Jewish administrative structures to move victims toward death. The bodies of victims were burned in crematoria or open fires, and prisoners such as the Sonderkommando were forced to work near the gas chambers before often being killed themselves.

Jewish and non-Jewish resistance occurred under extreme conditions. In the Warsaw Ghetto, 24-year-old Mordecai Anielewicz helped lead the Warsaw Ghetto Uprising. In Vilna, Abba Kovner recognized the full intent of Nazi policy in December 1941 and organized armed resistance that fought the Germans in September 1943. In [[Amsterdam]], a group led by Willem Arondeus, a homosexual artist and author, bombed a population registry in March 1943 to destroy records used to find Jews and others.

Revolts also broke out inside the killing system itself. Uprisings at Treblinka and Sobibor came as those camps were being dismantled and remaining prisoners were about to be killed. At Auschwitz in 1944, the Sonderkommando destroyed a crematorium as the killing process neared its end. These actions could not stop the Holocaust, but they showed that victims and allies resisted even when survival was unlikely.

By the winter of 1944-45, Allied armies were closing in, and desperate SS officials tried to evacuate camps and hide evidence. More than 50 death marches moved prisoners westward from concentration and extermination camps, sometimes for hundreds of miles, with little food, water, rest, or care. Those who stopped or fell behind were shot. In January 1945, just before the Red Army reached Auschwitz, about 60,000 prisoners were marched to Wodzisaw and put on freight trains to camps such as Bergen-Belsen, Gross-Rosen, Buchenwald, Dachau, and Mauthausen; nearly one in four died en route.

In April and May 1945, American and British forces entered western concentration camps while moving toward military objectives. These camps were not the most deadly parts of the Nazi system, but the starving survivors, bodies, and evidence shocked even battle-weary soldiers. Liberation revealed the scale of Nazi crimes to the wider world, while many victims had already been murdered in eastern killing sites and extermination camps.

## Conclusion

The Holocaust ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Holocaust)) is viewed as an emblematic manifestation of absolute evil. It revealed how deeply human cruelty could be organized through modern government, law, medicine, military power, bureaucracy, technology, and social obedience. For that reason, it became an essential subject in ethical discussion across law, medicine, religion, government, and the military.

## Remembrance

Survivors carried the plea to remember those who were killed and not let the world forget. Their own message became Never again: never for the Jewish people and never for any people. Remembrance is meant not only to honor the dead but also to help prevent recurrence.

Interest in the Holocaust ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Holocaust)) has grown rather than faded over time. Holocaust Remembrance days are observed in many countries, and institutions, memorials, museums, films, and educational programs continue to document and teach the history to later generations. Memory becomes a moral obligation because forgetting would weaken the warning left by victims and survivors.

## Key Events & Developments

- 1919: Adolf Hitler wrote that antisemitism should lead to legal opposition and the removal of Jews.
- 1933: Nazi Germany began boycotts, dismissals, school restrictions, and book burnings after Hitler came to power.
- 1935: The Nuremberg Laws ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuremberg_Laws)) turned racial antisemitism into state law.
- 1938: Kristallnacht ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kristallnacht)) showed the shift from legal exclusion to open violence.
- 1941: The Einsatzgruppen ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Einsatzgruppen)) began mass shootings behind the eastern front, including Baby Yar and other killing sites.
- 1942-44: Deportations, extermination camps, forced labor, and resistance shaped the central killing period.
- 1944-45: Death marches ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Death_march)), camp evacuations, liberation, and the revelation of evidence marked the collapse of Nazi control.
