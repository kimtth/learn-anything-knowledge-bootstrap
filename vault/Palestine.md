# Palestine

## Introduction

![[attachments/palestine_001.png|420]]

*Map of Mandatory Palestine around 1947*

Palestine ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Palestine)) is an eastern Mediterranean region comprising parts of modern Israel and the Palestinian territories of the Gaza Strip and West Bank. Its boundaries and political status have changed across three millennia, and the name has been used in different geographic and political senses. The region is also called the Holy Land ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holy_Land)) and is sacred to Jews, Christians, and Muslims.

The name Palestine ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Palestine)) derives from Philistia, the land of the Philistines, and was revived by Rome as Syria Palaestina in the 2nd century AD. It entered Arabic usage in the early Islamic era and regained official status after World War I under the British mandate. Since the 20th century, Palestine has been claimed by Jewish and Arab national movements, producing long conflict and repeated wars.

Palestine's strategic position made it important far beyond its size. Roads from Egypt to Syria and from the Mediterranean coast to the hills beyond the Jordan crossed the region, while settlement always depended on limited water. Rainfall generally decreases from north to south and from the coast inland, perennial rivers are few, and limestone terrain worsens the shortage.

The coastal route, the Megiddo pass, the Plain of Esdraelon, and the Jordan crossings made the region a corridor for armies, trade, and pilgrimage. Its small scale therefore never meant isolation; local towns were repeatedly drawn into larger Egyptian, Syrian, Mesopotamian, Roman, Byzantine, Islamic, and European systems.

## Land

Palestine includes Mediterranean coastal lowlands, interior hill country, valleys, and the Jordan system. The Plain of Akko, Plain of Sharon, Philistia, and Plain of Esdraelon provide fertile corridors and routes. Galilee is better watered and more wooded than Samaria or Judaea. The Jordan Valley, Dead Sea region, and adjoining uplands create sharp contrasts in climate, settlement, and agriculture.

The Plain of Akko narrows near Carmel, Sharon was once marshy before reclamation, and Philistia became known for orchards and grain. Esdraelon's basaltic soils and highway position made it both agriculturally rich and strategically exposed.

## People

The modern population geography west of the Jordan has been transformed by wars, migration, nationalism, and state formation. Israeli Jews constitute roughly half the population west of the Jordan, while Arabs, including Muslims, Christians, Druze, and smaller minorities, make up much of the rest. Zionists understand Judaism as religious and national identity; Palestinian nationalists stress Arab identity across religious diversity.

## Stone, Copper, and Bronze Ages

![[attachments/palestine_003.webp|420]]

*Hisham's Palace archaeological remains in Jericho*

Palestine's prehistory includes Paleolithic cave sites studied by Dorothy Garrod on Mount Carmel, Natufian Mesolithic settlements, Neolithic animal domestication and crop cultivation, and early urban sites such as Jericho. During the Copper Age, Ghassulian settlements developed agriculture and copper use. Early Bronze Age towns such as Megiddo, Jericho, Bet Shean, Khirbat al-Karak, and Ai emerged as Canaanite city-states.

## Middle and Late Bronze Ages

The Middle Bronze Age brought the urban Canaanite culture later encountered by Israelites. Egyptian documents, pottery, weapons, and burial forms show strong connections with Egypt and coastal Syria. In the Late Bronze Age, Egypt conquered and governed Palestine as part of Syria-Palestine, with districts, governors, and correspondence preserved in the Amarna letters. Weakening Egyptian control made Israelite settlement easier.

## Israelites and Philistines

Israelite tribes became established in the hill country by the early 12th century BC, joined by seminomadic Hebrews and some Canaanites. The Sea Peoples, including the Philistines, settled the southern coastal plain and reshaped the balance of power. Israelite monotheism, covenant traditions, the Tabernacle, and ethical law set the tribes apart from neighbouring Edomites, Moabites, Ammonites, Canaanites, and Philistines.

Constant pressure from neighbours pushed the tribes toward political unity. Saul became king around 1020 BC, but David defeated the Philistines, conquered neighbouring Hebrew states east of the Jordan, and organized administration. Solomon then used trade with Tyre, Arabia, and distant Ophir to support large building projects, including the Jerusalem Temple, palaces, and fortified towns.

## Hellenistic Palestine

[[Alexander the Great]] treated Palestine as a corridor to Egypt after the Battle of Issus in 333 BC, leaving Jewish religion and customs largely intact. After his death, Palestine passed between the Ptolemies and Seleucids. Ptolemaic rule preserved much local authority, while Seleucid needs for money and the policies of Antiochus IV Epiphanes helped provoke the Maccabean revolt.

## Roman Palestine

After Roman suppression of Jewish revolts, [[Jerusalem]] was destroyed, Legio X Fretensis was stationed there, Caesarea became central to administration, and Jamnia became a rabbinic centre. Hadrian's plan to build Aelia Capitolina and restrictions on Jewish practice helped trigger the Bar Kokhba revolt in 132. After its defeat, Judaea was renamed Syria Palaestina, and Jewish life shifted strongly toward Galilee.

## Arab Conquest and Islamic Rule

![[attachments/palestine_002.webp|420]]

*Dome of the Rock on the Temple Mount in Jerusalem*

The rise of [[Muhammad and the Religion of Islam (Islam)|Islam]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islam)) transformed Palestine. Abu Bakr launched campaigns into Syria, and Umar I's forces won decisive battles at Ajnadayn and Yarmuk. Jerusalem surrendered in 638, Caesarea in 640, and Palestine became part of Muslim Syria. Umar divided the region into administrative districts, including Jordan and Palestine.

## Crusades and Mamluks

The Crusaders captured Jerusalem and founded the Latin kingdom in 1100. Muslim resurgence under Zangi, Nur al-Din, and especially Saladin culminated in the Battle of Hattin in 1187, after which most of Palestine returned to Muslim rule. Frederick II briefly negotiated Christian control of Jerusalem, but al-Salih Ayyub restored it to Islam in 1244. Baybars I later advanced Mamluk power against Crusader remnants.

## Ottoman Palestine and Early Zionism

In the late Ottoman period, Palestinian Arabs participated in Ottoman politics, newspapers, and Arab cultural revival. Opposition to Zionism and Jewish immigration appeared before World War I. Zionist colonies grew from 19 in 1900 to 47 in 1918, many supported by Edmond, baron de Rothschild. In 1914, Palestine's population was mostly Muslim, with Christian Arabs and Jews forming smaller communities.

## World War I and the British Mandate

During World War I, Britain made conflicting commitments through the Husayn-McMahon correspondence, the Sykes-Picot Agreement, and the Balfour Declaration of 1917, which supported a Jewish national home in Palestine. After the Ottoman defeat, Britain received the mandate. Tensions deepened as Arabs feared Jewish immigration and land purchases, while Zionists saw restrictions as threats to the national home.

The mandate's contradictions sharpened after violence in 1929. The Shaw inquiry emphasized Arab fear of Jewish immigration and future domination, while the Hope Simpson report argued that land for new agricultural settlement was extremely limited. The Passfield White Paper briefly prioritized Arab land and labour concerns before being weakened by London politics.

The mandate also separated Transjordan administratively from western Palestine after Britain received authority over the territory. This helped make the Jordan River a sharper political boundary, even though older uses of the name Palestine had sometimes extended farther east.

## Arab Revolt and World War II

The Arab Revolt of 1936-1939 mobilized Palestinian Arabs through strikes, tax resistance, attacks, and rural rebellion. The Peel Commission called the mandate unworkable and proposed partition. During World War II, Zionists opposed British immigration limits amid the Holocaust. Jewish organizations strengthened militarily and industrially, while Irgun and LEHI attacked British targets; the Biltmore Program demanded unrestricted immigration and a Jewish state.

The revolt began after local violence and grew into a general strike led by the Arab Higher Committee under Amin al-Husayni. Rebels attacked British installations and Jewish settlements, Britain sent more than 20,000 troops, and Zionist forces expanded their own armed organization during the same crisis.

## 1948 and Refugees

The creation of [[Israel]] in 1948 caused massive Palestinian displacement. More than 400 Arab villages disappeared, Jaffa and Haifa's Arab life collapsed, and the centre of Palestinian life shifted to the West Bank, Gaza, Israel's Arab minority, and refugee communities in Lebanon, Jordan, Syria, Egypt, and Iraq. Estimates of displaced Palestinians range widely, but more than 600,000 and likely over 700,000 lost homes.

This displacement became central to Palestinian politics because refugees were divided among different states, camps, citizenship systems, and military authorities while continuing to claim connection to lost towns and villages.

The refugee population was distributed unevenly: many moved to the West Bank and Gaza, while others went to Lebanon, Jordan, Syria, Egypt, and Iraq. Wealthier urban families often fled to neighbouring capitals or towns, while many peasants entered refugee camps, making class as well as geography part of the new Palestinian condition.

## Israeli Arabs, West Bank, and Gaza

About 150,000 Arabs remained in Israel, becoming citizens but living under military jurisdiction until 1966. Jordan annexed or integrated the West Bank, granted citizenship to many Palestinians, and competed to speak for them. Gaza, under Egyptian control from 1948 to 1967, remained stateless, crowded, and politically constrained, though some Palestinians accessed Egyptian education and local institutions.

Israeli Arabs were concentrated largely in villages such as those of western Galilee and lost much agricultural land, pushing many into wage labour. Jordan reserved West Bank parliamentary representation, but differences between East Bank Jordanians, urban Palestinians, and camp refugees kept political integration tense.

## PLO and Guerrilla Movements

The Palestine Liberation Organization was formed in 1964 as an umbrella body claiming to represent Palestinians. Its charter called for liberation and an independent state while rejecting Israel. Fatah, led by Yasir Arafat, and groups such as the PFLP, DFLP, PFLP-GC, and al-Saiqah joined the PLO. Arafat became chairman in 1969, making the PLO the central symbol of Palestinian nationalism.

The Palestine National Council served as the PLO's parliament, while the executive committee managed its activities. Guerrilla organizations entered the council after 1968, bringing divergent ideologies and tactics under one umbrella even as Fatah became the dominant force.

## PLO, Camp David, and Lebanon

After 1967, Palestinian politics were transformed by Israeli occupation, Arab defeat, and the PLO's armed struggle. The Camp David Accords of 1978 and Egyptian-Israeli treaty of 1979 proposed Palestinian autonomy but did not include the PLO directly. Israel invaded Lebanon in 1982, defeated PLO and Syrian forces, besieged West Beirut, and forced thousands of Palestinian fighters to disperse.

## Oslo, Palestinian Authority, and Assassination

The peace process led to the Declaration of Principles in 1993 and the creation of the Palestinian Authority. In 1995, Arafat, Yitzhak Rabin, and Shimon Peres signed an agreement expanding Palestinian self-rule in the West Bank. Rabin was assassinated by a Jewish extremist in November 1995. Arafat won the PA presidency in 1996, while Hamas rejected the process and continued violent opposition.

The 1995 agreement assigned Palestinian control over major West Bank towns including Janin, Nabulus, Tulkarm, Qalqilyah, Ram Allah, Bethlehem, and most of Hebron, with gradual redeployment from hundreds of villages. Permanent-status talks were supposed to settle Jerusalem, refugees, settlements, sovereignty, and borders by 1999.

## Second Intifada and Gaza Split

Negotiations failed over Jerusalem, refugees, settlements, sovereignty, and borders. Ariel Sharon's 2000 visit to Al-Haram al-Sharif helped ignite the second intifada. Israel reoccupied West Bank cities in 2002. After Arafat's death in 2004, Mahmoud Abbas became PLO chairman and PA president. Israel withdrew from Gaza in 2005. Hamas won 2006 elections and seized Gaza in 2007, leaving Palestinian politics split between Hamas in Gaza and Fatah-led authority in the West Bank.

## Gaza Conflict and Blockade

After Hamas took Gaza, Israel declared the territory a hostile entity and imposed a blockade. Rocket attacks and Israeli strikes became recurring features. A six-month cease-fire brokered by Egypt in 2008 collapsed, leading to a 22-day war with heavy Gaza casualties. In 2010, Israeli commandos raided a civilian ship challenging the blockade, killing nine people and further damaging relations.
