# Marx and Marxism (Marxism)

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> Marxism is a family of philosophical, historical, economic, and political doctrines developed chiefly from Marx and Engels. Its analyses of historical materialism, class, labor, value, and revolution inspired divergent socialist, Soviet, Trotskyist, Maoist, and Western interpretations.

## Introduction

![[attachments/marxism_001.webp|420]]

*Portrait of Karl Marx*

Marxism ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marxism)) is the doctrine developed mainly by Karl Marx and, to a lesser extent, Friedrich Engels in the mid-19th century. It began as a philosophical anthropology, a theory of history, and an economic-political program, then became a set of competing socialist, Soviet, Trotskyist, Maoist, developing-world, and Western interpretations.

[[Marx and Marxism (Marx)|Marx]]'s work was not a closed philosophical system. It criticized [[Hegel and Hegelianism (Hegel)|Hegel]], post-Hegelians, earlier economists, and inherited concepts such as alienation, praxis, labour, value, and appropriation, but it treated them dynamically in relation to historical, social, political, and economic realities.

## A Concrete Historical Method

The distinctiveness of [[Marx and Marxism (Marx)|Marx]]'s thought lies in refusing abstract answers about human nature, matter, knowledge, or society. Each problem is examined through its relation to other problems and, above all, through concrete historical reality rather than pure philosophy.

## Historical Materialism

![[attachments/marxism_002.webp|420]]

*Historical materialism diagram*

In 1859, [[Marx and Marxism (Marx)|Marx]] summarized what later became known as historical materialism ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Historical_materialism)). People enter relations of production independent of their will; these relations correspond to productive forces and form society's economic structure, on which law, politics, and forms of consciousness arise.

For [[Marx and Marxism (Marx)|Marx]], social existence determines consciousness, not the reverse. The mode of production shapes social, political, and intellectual life. He used this hypothesis in The Communist Manifesto, Das Kapital, The German Ideology, and other writings, though he never fixed every term with exact precision.

## The Economic Structure

The economic structure is the real foundation of society. It includes labour, means of production, and relations of production: the social and political arrangements that regulate production and distribution. Marx did not fully explain the correspondence between productive forces and relations, leaving later followers room for disagreement.

Above the foundation stands a superstructure of legal, political, and ideological forms. Individuals become aware of conflicts between productive forces and property relations through these forms. Law, politics, morality, religion, and metaphysics are tied to material production and change with real social life.

[[Marx and Marxism (Marx)|Marx]]'s anthropology begins with natural human need. People produce means to satisfy needs, create new needs, struggle with nature, and become human through labour. In creative labour, they humanize nature and themselves, making history the process of human self-creation.

Capitalism blocks this fulfillment through alienation. Workers produce objects they do not own, create values that devalue them, and depend on things external to them. Alienation becomes sharper through market production, division of labour, class antagonism, surplus value, and commodity fetishism.

Political and ideological alienation accompany economic alienation. In bourgeois society the person is split into political citizen and economic actor, while the state serves the propertied class. Religion is treated as a product of human consciousness and as an opium for people, disappearing only when society changes.

[[Marx and Marxism (Marx)|Marx]]'s economic analysis in Das Kapital studies capitalism as a whole, especially mid-19th-century England. Borrowing categories from [[Adam Smith]] and David Ricardo, he argues that all economic value comes from labour and that capital arises when money advances through production and returns enlarged.

## Surplus Value

Surplus value comes from labour power. The capitalist buys labour power for wages, but the worker produces more value during the day than the value needed to reproduce those wages. The extra value belongs to the capitalist, making exploitation central to Marx's account of capital.

Capitalism develops through contradictions. Machinery may lower costs for one capitalist, but general adoption raises machinery outlay faster than wages; since labour alone creates surplus value, the profit rate tends to fall. Unemployment, crises, a reserve army of workers, and exhaustion of both earth and worker point toward systemic breakdown.

[[Marx and Marxism (Marx)|Marx]] inherited ideas of class struggle from utopian socialism, Henri de Saint-Simon, and French historians of the Revolution, but made class conflict the central fact of social evolution. Under capitalism, bourgeois owners of production and proletarian workers become the basic opposing classes.

## The Proletarian Revolution

The Communist Manifesto presents the bourgeoisie as producing its own grave-diggers. Capitalist production creates the material conditions for resolving its own contradictions; revolution would end bourgeois society and open an association where the free development of each is the condition for the free development of all.

[[Marx and Marxism (Marx)|Marx]] used two ideas of revolution: a final violent rupture when class opposition reaches its extreme, and permanent revolution through a provisional coalition between proletariat and petty bourgeoisie. He never fully reconciled these models, nor did he finish a systematic analysis of classes in Das Kapital.

## The Role of Engels

Friedrich Engels became a communist in 1842 and learned about English industrial workers while managing his father's Manchester factory. His Outline of a Critique of Political Economy anticipated Marx's later critique, and The Condition of the Working Class in England analyzed industrial capitalism's social consequences.

Engels collaborated with [[Marx and Marxism (Marx)|Marx]] on The Holy Family, The German Ideology, and The Communist Manifesto, supplied economic and technical data for Das Kapital, criticized drafts, and after Marx's death edited volumes 2 and 3. His role made Marxism both a partnership and a posthumous editorial project.

## Anti-Duhring

Engels's Anti-Duhring linked Marx's critique to later Marxism. It treated philosophy, political economy, and socialism, argued that reality and science were dialectical, restated Marx's ideas on value, labour, capital, and surplus value, and described socialism as planned production without capitalist value relations.

Anti-Duhring also helped turn Marxism ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marxism)) from a critique of daily life into an integrated doctrine joining philosophy, history, and science. Engels rejected absolute moral foundations for socialism, insisting that values and political possibilities depend on historical, economic, and social conditions.

## German Marxism after Engels

After Engels, theoretical leadership passed to Karl Kautsky, who presented Marxism mainly as economic theory and gave orthodox German Marxism an evolutionist form. He expected capitalist concentration and worker pauperization, opposed compromise with the bourgeois state, but thought peaceful acquisition of power was possible.

Eduard Bernstein challenged orthodoxy from 1896 onward. He argued that Marxism needed revision, that the labour theory of value was outdated, that class struggle was less intense, and that cartels and syndicates softened capitalist crises. Kautsky and Rosa Luxemburg replied that capitalist contradictions were still deepening.

Radicals such as Karl Liebknecht and Rosa Luxemburg opposed [[World Wars (World War I)|World War I]] after Social Democratic deputies funded the war. Luxemburg's Accumulation of Capital argued that capitalism needed noncapitalist markets among peasants, artisans, colonies, and underdeveloped countries, and would collapse only when this outside sphere was exhausted.

## Austrian Marxism

The Austrian Marxist school developed through Marx-Studien from 1904 and Der Kampf from 1907. Its figures included Max Adler, Karl Renner, Rudolf Hilferding, Gustav Eckstein, Friedrich Adler, and Otto Bauer.

## Nationalities and Finance Capital

Otto Bauer analyzed nationalities through the experience of the Austro-Hungarian Empire, supported self-determination, and emphasized cultural nationhood. Rudolf Hilferding's Finance Capital argued that banks and monopolies controlled capitalism and influenced [[Lenin]]'s theory of imperialism.

## Russian and Soviet Marxism

Russian Marxism began after Das Kapital was translated into Russian in 1872. Georgy Plekhanov introduced Marxism into Russia, but Vladimir Ilich Lenin adapted it to Russian conditions, peasant society, party organization, and revolutionary strategy.

## Lenin and the Bolsheviks

[[Lenin]], born in 1870, became active in St. Petersburg revolutionary circles and argued in What Is to Be Done? that a Marxist party needed professional revolutionaries, discipline, correct theory, and leadership over the masses. At the 1903 party congress, his Bolsheviks split from L. Martov's Mensheviks.

In One Step Forward, Two Steps Back, [[Lenin]] defended Bolshevik organization against the Mensheviks. He later drew lessons from the failed 1905 Revolution, attacked Kantian influence in Materialism and Empirio-criticism, formed an independent Bolshevik party in 1912, and opposed World War I through the Zimmerwald and Kiental conferences.

[[Lenin]] treated Marxism ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marxism)) as an unfinished science requiring development. He applied Marx's models to Russia in The Development of Capitalism in Russia, stressed dialectic as praxis, and made the party the motive force that could bring workers to awareness and establish the dictatorship of the proletariat.

For [[Lenin]], the peasantry was crucial. The proletariat led the revolution, but peasant discontent had to be directed through land seizure. In 1917 he encouraged peasants to take land before formal agrarian reform, creating the formula of dictatorship of the proletariat and peasantry. His 1921 ban on factions later helped [[Stalin]] suppress opponents.

## Stalinism

Joseph Stalin codified Marxism-Leninism as the official doctrine of Soviet and eastern European communist parties after Lenin's death in 1924. He simplified Marxism into a doctrine of power, activism, dialectical development, economic determination, class policy, and sudden revolutionary leaps.

Stalin's dialectic stressed unified nature, perpetual movement, contradictions, qualitative leaps, and the economic root of social change. It differed from [[Marx and Marxism (Marx)|Marx]]'s emphasis on class consciousness by producing revolution from above: forced industrialization, collectivized agriculture, repression, and strong central power.

## Trotskyism

Leon Trotsky, active in the Russian Revolutions of 1905 and 1917, opposed Stalin after Lenin's death. He argued that revolution in backward Russia had to be proletarian, socialist, agrarian, international, and permanent, because isolated revolution would be threatened by capitalist countries and needed developed industrial techniques.

Trotskyism emphasized revolutionary situations, education of the working class, a party open to revolutionary tendencies, resistance to bureaucracy, and carefully planned insurrection. It opposed Stalin's claim that socialism could be built in one country.

## Maoism

[[Mao Zedong]]'s Chinese Marxism grew from China's revolutionary reality after 1930 and from Chinese culture, peasant life, military struggle, and Marxism-Leninism adapted to Chinese needs. Chinese communists took power in 1948 and brought this variant into government.

Maoism centered on contradictions. Antagonistic contradictions between people and enemies required revolution, while nonantagonistic contradictions within socialist society required criticism and self-criticism. Mao also stressed permanent revolution, mass mobilization, struggle against bureaucracy, and campaigns such as the Great Leap Forward in 1958 and Cultural Revolution in 1966.

Maoism became a peasant Marxism with rural and military outlook. It used Marxist analysis but also drew on Chinese ideas of nature and a Promethean vision of humanity struggling against nature, seeking to mobilize the whole population against internal and external dangers.

In Cuba, Fidel Castro's Marxism began as rejection of political, economic, and social injustice and gradually identified with Marxism-Leninism. It rejected dogmatism, bureaucracy, and sectarianism, elevated guerrilla revolution over party politics, and tried to join Marxist ideas with those of Simon Bolivar.

Marxism in the developing world was shaped by colonial history and weak industry. Many movements followed the Maoist model of agrarian revolution against feudalism and imperialism, or bypassed capitalism by depending on existing communist countries for support against imperialism.

Western Marxism divided between traditional communist parties and the New Left. Eurocommunism in the 1970s sought democratic socialism, alliances, civil liberties, and independence from Soviet authority, but it faded by the 1980s. Western Marxists such as Gyorgy Lukacs, Antonio Gramsci, the Frankfurt School, Jean-Paul Sartre, and others emphasized culture, philosophy, humanist Marx, and critique of modern capitalist society.

## Marxism as Many Traditions

Marxism ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marxism)) ultimately became many traditions rather than one stable doctrine. Its later history includes Soviet orthodoxy, revolutionary heresies, peasant adaptations, anti-imperial variants, Eurocommunism, and Western philosophical reinterpretations.

## Key Events & Developments

- 1848: [[Marx and Marxism (Marx)|Marx]] and Engels published the Communist Manifesto.
- 1867: Volume 1 of Das Kapital presented Marx's mature economic critique.
- 1917: The Russian Revolution made Marxism-Leninism a state doctrine.
- 1938: [[Stalin]]'s Short History codified Soviet dialectical materialism.
- 1966: Mao's Cultural Revolution gave permanent revolution a mass Chinese form.

## Key People

- Karl Marx ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karl_Marx)): Founder of the central doctrines of historical materialism, alienation, class struggle, and critique of capital.
- Friedrich Engels ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Friedrich_Engels)): Collaborator, systematizer, editor, and major transmitter of Marxism.
- Vladimir Ilich Lenin ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vladimir_Ilich_Lenin)): Adapted Marxism to Russian revolution, party organization, and proletarian-peasant strategy.
- Joseph Stalin ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph_Stalin)): Codified Marxism-Leninism into Soviet official doctrine.
- Leon Trotsky ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leon_Trotsky)): Developed permanent international revolution against Stalin's socialism in one country.
- [[Mao Zedong]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mao_Zedong)): Created a Chinese, peasant, mass-mobilizing form of Marxism-Leninism.

## Interesting Facts
