# Diplomacy

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> Diplomacy is the conduct of relations among political communities through negotiation, representation, treaties, and communication. It seeks to manage conflict, cooperation, trade, and international order.

## Introduction

Diplomacy is the established way of shaping the decisions and behaviour of foreign governments and peoples through dialogue, negotiation, and other means short of war or violence. In modern history, it grew out of the post-Renaissance European state system and first meant the official relations between sovereign states. Over time, however, it widened into a much larger field. By the 20th century, diplomacy included summit meetings, international conferences, parliamentary diplomacy, the international activity of supranational and subnational bodies, unofficial contact by nongovernmental groups, and the work of international civil servants. The word itself comes from the Greek idea of a folded document, which once gave the bearer a privilege or permit to travel. Later it came to mean the formal papers issued by rulers and chancelleries, especially agreements between sovereigns. Only after that did the term become closely tied to international relations in general. This account follows that wider meaning and explains the nature of diplomacy, its historical growth, and the ways modern diplomatic work is organized through personnel, missions, and public institutions.

## Nature and purpose

Diplomacy is often confused with foreign policy, but they are not the same thing. Foreign policy is set by political leaders and gives the broad goals, strategies, and tactics of a state, while diplomacy is one of the main tools used to carry those goals forward. A government may rely on diplomacy, but it may also use intelligence work, subversion, pressure, or war. In that sense, diplomacy is a method of statecraft, not the whole policy itself. Its purpose is to create contact, reduce danger, explain positions, and seek outcomes that protect a state's interests without direct violence. It is also the field where states try to manage misunderstanding, preserve peace when possible, and turn conflict into controlled negotiation. Diplomacy is not only about friendly talks; it can also be a hard and careful form of competition in which each side tries to protect its position while keeping communication open.

## China

China developed one of the earliest and most disciplined diplomatic traditions. By the 8th century BC, the many Chinese states already used leagues, missions, and formal polite discourse, including resident envoys who could also serve as hostages for good conduct. The Chinese classics present diplomacy as a moral art in which truth, restraint, and trust matter greatly. Zhuangzi advised diplomats to speak honestly and never embellish what they report, because flattery or sharp distortion destroys confidence between states. After the Qin and Han unifications, this system changed. China became a large and powerful empire, and its foreign relations were usually shaped by defense, tribute, trade control, and the reception of outside envoys rather than by regular Chinese missions abroad. With rare exceptions, such as Buddhist missions to India and Zheng He’s voyages, Chinese rulers expected foreigners to come to China and show respect. This tributary order remained strong until European colonial power forced new ideas of sovereignty and equality into East Asia. China’s history therefore shows a different model of diplomacy, one built less on constant balance among equal states and more on moral authority, hierarchy, and controlled contact.

## India

Ancient India also had a highly developed diplomatic tradition, but it was based on a more openly realistic view of power. In the Artha-shastra, Kautilya described foreign relations as a matter of self-interest rather than moral ideals. He classified states, measured power through several factors, and gave rulers a set of tools that included conciliation, seduction, subversion, and coercion. He also listed six forms of policy, such as peace, war, nonalignment, alliances, displays of force, and double-dealing. To carry out these policies, India used several kinds of diplomats, including plenipotentiaries, special envoys, royal messengers, consular agents, and spies. The system included rules for immunity, the start and end of missions, and the duties of envoys. An envoy had to report to the king, protect the ruler’s honour, keep treaty terms, win allies, and weaken enemies by indirect means. This was a practical and often ruthless diplomacy, and it shows that early international contact in Asia was not simple or naive. It was a structured craft of intelligence, negotiation, and calculated pressure.

## Greece

Ancient Greece gave post-Renaissance Europe many of the habits that later became modern diplomacy. The earliest signs appear in literature, especially Homer, and in the rules around the Olympic Games and the city-state system. Greek states created leagues and assemblies, and they used heralds and envoys to manage conflict and alliance. Heralds were the first diplomats in a formal sense and were protected by divine immunity through Hermes, the god of messages. Because heralds could move safely even in war, they were the natural channel for warnings and truce messages. Envoys usually traveled in small groups, were older and politically important, and were chosen for their speaking skills because they had to persuade public assemblies. Greek diplomacy, however, was usually episodic rather than continuous. Missions were temporary, not permanent, and commercial or private contact between states followed different rules. This Greek pattern helped define later European practice: public speech, legal protection for messengers, and the idea that diplomacy is linked to persuasion between communities that remain formally separate.

## Rome

Rome inherited Greek practice and turned it toward imperial administration. As Rome expanded, it negotiated with conquered peoples and sometimes granted them limited self-government through treaty. During the Republic, the Senate led foreign policy, and under the Empire the emperor became the final authority. Roman envoys were received with ceremony, carried written instructions, and were protected by immunity. Rome also used different kinds of missions, from brief messengers to larger embassies made up of several leading citizens. At the same time, Roman diplomacy depended heavily on archives and record keeping. Trained archivists preserved documents, identified them through paleographic methods, and helped shape diplomatic procedure. Roman law, with its strong respect for contracts, became the base for treaties, and Roman ideas about foreigners mixed with Greek natural law to form a broader law of nations. When the Western Empire collapsed, the Roman Catholic church preserved much of this legal and diplomatic inheritance. In that way, Rome gave Europe a durable framework for treaties, records, immunity, and the long memory of state practice.

## The Middle Ages

After the Western Empire broke apart in the 5th century, much of the older diplomatic system weakened, but it did not vanish completely. Monarchs still negotiated directly with nearby rulers or through envoys, and the papacy kept using legati. At the same time, the Eastern Roman Empire continued as Byzantium and became a major diplomatic center. Its court in Constantinople maintained foreign affairs offices and special channels for dealing with visiting envoys. Medieval diplomacy was therefore split between local rule, papal action, and Byzantine continuity. It was also strongly shaped by display. Rulers often used ceremony, rank, and controlled access to impress visitors and to suggest greater power than they truly had. This period did not yet have modern resident embassies, but it did preserve the core ideas of mission, privilege, protocol, and negotiation. Medieval diplomacy was less regular than later European diplomacy, yet it kept alive the habits that would later become standard in permanent state systems.

## Islam

Islam brought another important diplomatic tradition. The early Muslim community imagined a single human society under faith, with less need for independent states and permanent missions. In theory, diplomacy between separate rulers would only be temporary, because non-Muslim states would eventually accept Islam. In practice, however, diplomatic missions existed from Muhammad’s time onward, and Muslim rulers built clear rules to protect emissaries and manage exchange. As Islamic power expanded across Africa, Asia, and Europe, contact and conflict with Byzantium also shaped Muslim practice. The result was a diplomacy that blended religious purpose with careful statecraft. Muslim rulers had to deal with other Muslim states as well as with non-Muslim powers, and so they developed rules for safe conduct, mission exchange, and formal communication. The Islamic world therefore shows that religious unity did not remove diplomacy; instead, it gave diplomacy a different moral language and a new political setting.

## Byzantium

Byzantium produced the first professional diplomats in the strict sense. Its envoys received written instructions and were told to be polite, to entertain generously when possible, and to support trade by selling Byzantine goods at lower prices. From the 12th century onward, their most important task became intelligence gathering. As Byzantine power weakened, timely information from envoys helped emperors play foreign powers against one another and survive in a difficult environment. Byzantine diplomats worked as licensed spies as well as representatives, and this mixture of report, negotiation, and secret knowledge influenced neighboring peoples and later also Rome and the Italian city-states. When Byzantium collapsed, much of its diplomatic style survived in the Ottoman world and in Renaissance Italy. This legacy was very important because it linked formal mission work with political reporting, flexible tactics, and careful use of information. Byzantium showed that diplomacy can be a tool of survival even when a state lacks overwhelming military strength.

## Diplomacy of the Roman Catholic church

While Byzantium was declining, the Roman Catholic church kept a strong diplomatic presence, especially in Constantinople and in its struggle with the Holy Roman emperors. Popes acted as arbiters, and papal legates served as peacemakers. Church emissaries had great prestige, and in many courts they ranked ahead of secular envoys. The church also helped make diplomatic law more formal. Its lawyers developed rules for the status, privilege, and conduct of papal envoys, and these rules were later adapted for secular use. In addition, rules from medieval church councils helped shape modern international conferences. From the 6th century on, legates and lower-ranking nuncii carried letters of credence, which became a standard part of diplomatic procedure. The church therefore did more than send messengers. It helped build the habits of protocol, precedence, and legal form that later states would adopt. Its role shows how a religious institution could become a carrier of diplomatic order across a long historical transition.

## Venice

Venice became one of the most important bridges between medieval and modern diplomacy. At the end of the 12th century, the word ambassador began to appear, first in Italy, and by the late 15th century secular rulers commonly used it for their envoys. Venice’s contacts with Constantinople gave it early access to Byzantine methods, including written instructions and systematic archives. The Venetian archive, which recorded diplomatic documents from 883, became a model of organized state memory. Venice also emphasized reports about conditions in the host country. At first these reports were spoken on return, but from the 15th century they were written down. Other Italian states, then France and Spain, copied Venetian style and method. Venice helped make diplomacy more continuous, more recorded, and more professional. It also showed that information from abroad could be treated as a central part of government, not just as a side result of missions. This made Venetian diplomacy a key step toward the resident embassy and the modern foreign service.

## The Renaissance to 1815

*Delegates at the Congress of Vienna (1814-15)*

The period from the Renaissance to 1815 marks the real rise of modern diplomacy. Italian city-states began to send resident envoys to one another, to popes, and to emperors, even though many embassies were still temporary for a long time. Envoys increasingly lived abroad, reported regularly, and acted as stable representatives rather than occasional messengers. Across Europe, diplomatic practice became more formal, more regular, and more tied to state rivalry. By the Congress of Vienna in 1814-15, the major powers were trying to set clear boundaries, balance power, and establish rules that would prevent another French hegemony. Vienna also fixed classes of heads of mission and linked precedence to the date on which credentials were presented. The old struggle over rank and standing was reduced by formal rules. This long era also saw the growth of permanent foreign ministries and the steady replacement of short-term missions by resident representation. It was the age when diplomacy became part of a broader state system and a recognized profession.

### The development of Italian diplomacy

Italian diplomacy developed first because the peninsula was divided into many competing states, each of which needed reliable contact with the others. Venice, Milan, and Mantua were among the earliest to send resident envoys, and these missions could last months or years. Envoys often traveled without wives, because they were thought to be less discreet, and they maintained close contact with the ruling court. Italy also helped define the language and habits of modern diplomacy: the ambassador became the normal envoy of a sovereign ruler, and written instructions became standard. Since Italian states were always watching one another, diplomacy had to be constant, careful, and well informed. This produced a style that valued archives, reporting, intelligence, and negotiation over simple ceremonial contact. The Italian city-states therefore created the practical model that later spread across Europe. Their contribution was not only political but also administrative, because they taught rulers to see diplomacy as a permanent state function and a daily instrument of policy.

### The spread of European diplomatic norms

From the late 18th and early 19th centuries, European diplomatic norms spread beyond Europe and began to shape the wider world. European envoys to China faced the kowtow demand and rejected it as inconsistent with sovereign equality. At the same time, European power transformed Asia, Africa, and the Americas. Latin American states adopted the existing European system after independence, the British Empire unified India under colonial rule, and Japan modernized by adopting Western ideas of sovereignty and diplomatic practice. Across the world, royal courts gave way to cabinets, ministers replaced kings at conferences, and newspapers and public opinion began to influence foreign policy. New technologies and mass literacy made diplomacy less private and more visible. International organizations also appeared, such as the telegraph and postal unions and later the Hague peace conferences, which tried to codify war and encourage disarmament. European diplomatic forms became global, but they were adapted in different ways by different states, creating a world system that was shared yet uneven. This was the long phase in which diplomacy became a worldwide language of statecraft.

## Diplomacy since World War I

![[attachments/diplomacy_003.webp|420]]

*UN building, Geneva*

World War I changed diplomacy in deep ways. It made governments rely more on public arguments, conferences, propaganda, and open political language. The war also reduced trust in old aristocratic methods and encouraged broader public scrutiny. After the war, diplomacy became more connected to mass politics, international bodies, and the problem of managing many states at once. The growth of the League of Nations, the rise of conference diplomacy, and the spread of newly independent states all pushed diplomacy away from a closed club of great powers. In the 20th century, diplomacy also had to cope with ideological conflict, decolonization, and the widening role of international institutions. Modern diplomacy did not become simpler after the war; it became larger, more public, and more contested. It had to handle not only bilateral relations but also global coordination, public opinion, and new kinds of political pressure.

### The Soviet model

The Soviet Union created a sharp break with earlier European practice. After the Russian Revolution, the new regime rejected many Western diplomatic habits and used political language in new ways. It abolished diplomatic ranks, published secret treaties, and tried to expose the hidden deals of old ruling classes. Its foreign ministry built a press bureau and a propaganda bureau, and in negotiations it often used public agitation instead of private influence. The Communist International also worked outside normal embassy channels to influence politics in capitalist countries. The Soviet model was therefore not simply another diplomatic style; it was an attempt to replace elite secrecy with ideological struggle and direct political mobilization. It still used missions and negotiations, but it framed them differently and treated publicity as a weapon. This made Soviet diplomacy a highly political instrument that combined state action, party ideology, and international pressure.

### The League of Nations and conference diplomacy

Conference diplomacy revived during World War I and continued strongly afterward, especially in the 1920s. The Paris Peace Conference was heavily publicized, and journalists followed it closely. President Woodrow Wilson’s call for open covenants of peace showed the new ideal that agreements should be visible and openly reached, even though this ideal also created confusion between process and result. The League of Nations became the most important forum for this new diplomacy, and it supported a wider world of committees, specialized agencies, and multilateral meetings. Over time, conference diplomacy became a normal part of international life, especially for problems that no single state could solve alone. It worked alongside bilateral embassies rather than replacing them. Conference diplomacy also had a social and political side, because publicity, public statements, and media attention could shape outcomes. This made international negotiation more open, but also more complex and more vulnerable to pressure. It was a new diplomatic arena built for a crowded world.

## Credentials

The appointment of a new head of mission is a careful process meant to avoid embarrassment and preserve rank. The host government is first consulted informally, and if there is no objection, formal consent is sought. The new ambassador then receives a letter of credence from the sending head of state and presents it to the host head of state. In many major capitals, a copy is first given privately to the foreign minister, so that the ambassador can begin practical work before the formal ceremony. The ceremony itself can be highly traditional, including an open carriage ride, speeches, and the exchange of credentials and recall letters. The date of presentation sets precedence in the local diplomatic corps. At the United Nations, the process is simpler and there is no doyen in the same sense, because membership changes quickly. Consuls are appointed by notification rather than by the same full ceremony, and their work centers on visas, commerce, and help to nationals. Credentials therefore show that diplomacy is not only about content; it is also about form, rank, and recognized status. They are a visible sign of lawful representation.

## Personnel

Diplomatic personnel are usually chosen through careful selection and training. In most states, diplomacy is a professional career, although some governments still use ambassadorial appointments for political reasons or special honors. Even when the ambassador is not a career officer, the staff around him or her is usually professional. Candidates often face difficult written and oral examinations that test writing, analysis, summary skills, judgment, poise, and stability. States also try to diversify their diplomatic corps, so language ability is no longer always required at the point of entry, but language training remains important after selection. Foreign services teach economics, geography, international politics, law, and national history as well as etiquette and practical skills. A modern diplomatic service is also usually divided both geographically and functionally, with desks and offices that handle regions, finance, law, administration, archives, science, disarmament, narcotics, and cultural affairs. Personnel work is therefore not a minor side of diplomacy. It is one of the core systems that make diplomatic negotiation possible, because a state needs trained people to report, interpret, and act with a steady and informed judgment.

- ancient period: Diplomacy begins with envoys, tribute, and negotiated peace between rulers.
- modern period: Permanent embassies and professional foreign services become normal.
- 20th century: International organizations and treaty systems expand diplomatic work beyond bilateral talks.
