## Introduction

Reptiles ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reptile)) are air-breathing vertebrates with internal fertilization, amniotic development, and epidermal scales covering part or all of the body. Living reptiles include turtles, tuataras, lizards, snakes, and crocodiles. Birds share ancestry with crocodiles in Archosauria and are technically one reptile lineage, but they are usually treated separately.

Living reptiles number more than 8,700 species, but extinct forms were far more varied. They included marine plesiosaurs, pliosaurs, ichthyosaurs, and the giant herbivorous and carnivorous dinosaurs. Reptiles and Synapsida, the mammal-like line that led to mammals, diverged during the Middle Pennsylvanian, about 312 million to 307 million years ago.

Most modern reptiles are ectothermic, meaning body temperature depends strongly on external heat. Birds, some extinct dinosaurs, and mammals evolved endothermy, while the leatherback sea turtle is near-endothermic among living reptiles. No reptile, living or extinct, developed mammary glands for feeding young.

## General Features

The typical reptile has a dry external covering of epidermal scales. These scales contain beta keratin, and the skin between scales contains alpha keratin, a material shared with other vertebrates. Scales range from microscopic tubercles in dwarf geckos to large turtle scutes and crocodile plates.

Other reptilian traits include a single occipital condyle, cervical vertebrae with midventral keels, fusion of the second cervical intercentrum to the axis in adults, and a lower jaw made of several bones. The ear has one auditory bone, the stapes, which transmits vibrations to the inner ear.

Reproduction is usually sexual and internal. Sperm may be deposited by copulation or cloacal contact, and parthenogenesis occurs in some groups. Development may be internal, sometimes with placental attachment, but most species lay shelled eggs. In every case, the embryo is enclosed in amniotic membranes.

## Importance

Reptiles ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reptile)) have local economic and ecological value even though they are less important commercially than poultry and hoofed mammals. Lizards reduce insect pests, and snakes control rodents. When rodent-eating snakes are heavily harvested for leather, rodent populations can rise sharply.

Many turtles, crocodiles, snakes, and lizards are harvested for food, medicine, leather, and the pet trade. Commercial harvesting can exceed natural replacement, especially when large adults are removed. Since large individuals are often breeding males and females, their loss can cause rapid population decline.

Overharvesting of crocodiles in the 1950s and 1960s caused local extinctions and near-worldwide population drops. National and international regulation, conservation, and management later allowed many crocodilian populations to rebound. Regulated harvest can sometimes support both conservation and local economies.

The largest living crocodiles include Nile and saltwater crocodiles, which regularly exceed 6 metres. American crocodiles, Orinoco crocodiles, and gavials may also pass that length. The leatherback sea turtle reaches about 2.7 metres and 680 kg, while Galapagos tortoises can weigh about 255 kg.

The largest modern lizard is the Komodo dragon, about 3 metres long. Some monitor lizards and green iguanas approach large sizes, but living reptiles are generally smaller than their extinct relatives. The Cretaceous turtle Archelon reached about 3.6 metres, Phobosuchus may have reached 15 metres, and some dinosaurs reached 30 metres and immense weights.

## Geographic Diversity

Reptile diversity varies strongly by region. North America contains different eastern and western patterns: the east has more water snakes and some turtle groups, while the west has many iguanid lizards and garter snakes. The eastern United States has more snake species, while western areas have more lizard species.

In Central and South America, reptiles become more numerous near the Equator. Crocodilians, turtles, lizards, and snakes are highly diverse. Tropical iguanids such as black iguanas, basilisks, and anoles replace many northern forms, and more than 200 anole species occur in northern South America and the West Indies.

[[Asia]] has many temperate lizards in Agamidae and Lacertidae, as well as terrestrial and burrowing snakes. Tropical Asia is extremely rich, with aquatic snakes, softshell turtles, crocodiles, cobras, monitors, skinks, geckos, pythons, pit vipers, vine snakes, and specialized burrowing forms.

[[Australia]] is unusual because venomous snake species outnumber nonvenomous ones. Its fauna includes many elapid snakes, pythons, blind snakes, geckos, skinks, agamid lizards, side-necked turtles, and crocodiles. The same broad faunal pattern extends to New Guinea.

[[Africa]] has a North African component related to Eurasia and a larger sub-Saharan component. North African reptiles are often terrestrial or burrowing desert forms. Sub-Saharan Africa has cobras, skinks, geckos, true vipers, side-necked turtles, wall lizards, spiny-tailed lizards, chameleons, tortoises, and crocodiles. Madagascar has distinct genera because of long isolation.

## Defense

The most common reptile defense is avoidance. Snakes and lizards crawl or run into cover, while turtles and crocodiles plunge into water. If escape is too risky, reptiles use hissing, rattling, body inflation, posturing, biting, tail use, balling, odours, or venom.

Defensive displays include rattlesnakes vibrating a tail rattle and some nonrattling snakes vibrating their tails against dry leaves to imitate the sound. Cobras raise the front of the body and spread a hood. Vipers coil with the neck in an S-curve and the head ready to strike. Lizards may flatten, puff the throat, and turn broadside to look larger.

## Spitting, Tails, Balling, and Odours

Some Asian and African cobras and the ringhals spit venom defensively at large animals. Their specialized fangs direct a fine stream of venom forward. The venom has little effect on skin but can badly damage eyes and cause blindness if not washed out quickly.

Some lizards use the tail as a weapon, swinging thick spiny tails against snakes. Others shed the tail voluntarily, a process called autotomy. The detached tail wriggles and distracts the predator while the lizard escapes. A new tail grows, though it often differs from the original.

Balling protects the head by hiding it under body coils. Some pythons form a tight ball with the head in the centre. The African armadillo lizard rolls on its back and grips its tail in its mouth, presenting a ring of hard spines. Other reptiles release foul musk from cloacal or shell glands.

## Feeding Habits

Most modern reptiles eat animals, including insects, mollusks, frogs, birds, mammals, fishes, and other reptiles. Land tortoises are mainly vegetarian, and herbivory also occurs in green iguanas, chuckwallas, spiny-tailed agamids, and the marine iguana, which dives for seaweed.

Carnivorous reptiles usually have broad diets, and prey size often rises with predator size. Large pythons and green anacondas can eat mammals such as small pigs and deer. Large Nile, saltwater, and Orinoco crocodiles can attack and eat humans. Extinct carnivorous dinosaurs such as Allosaurus and Tyrannosaurus likely killed very large prey.

## Locomotion

Most reptiles have a sprawling gait, with limbs projecting sideways and bending downward. Though sometimes called inefficient, it works well for energy-conserving lifestyles. The trunk and tail often bend side to side during walking and running, increasing stride length.

Some reptile lines shifted toward vertical limb posture. Dinosaurs used erect limbs, probably with joint-locking mechanisms that reduced standing effort. Crocodiles are the only living reptiles that use a vertical limb posture in walking; they can also high-walk, gallop when young, and belly-slide into water.

Snakes move by pressing body curves against fixed points such as rocks or uneven ground. Heavy snakes can also use rectilinear movement, stretching and contracting belly sections to move smoothly in a straight line without obvious undulation.

## Swimming and Flying

Aquatic reptiles often swim by lateral undulation of the rear body and tail, like fish. Crocodiles, aquatic lizards, sea snakes, mesosaurs, and ichthyosaurs used or use this method. Sea snakes have flattened tails, while turtles paddle with webbed feet or flipperlike forelimbs.

Several reptile groups experimented with flight. One archosaur line became birds. Pterosaurs developed skin wings supported by the arm and an elongated fourth finger and probably used soaring, gliding, and flapping. Modern flying lizards of the genus Draco are gliders, with skin wings supported by elongated ribs.

## External Covering and Senses

Reptile skin is dry, keratinous, and has few or no glands. It differs from amphibian skin, which is moist and glandular, and from mammalian skin, which contains many specialized glands. Cycles of epidermal growth lead to shedding of outer layers.

Reptile vision varies widely. Turtles and many diurnal lizards and snakes have round pupils; crocodiles and nocturnal snakes often have vertical slits; some tree snakes have horizontal slits; and some geckos form pinhole-like pupils. Snakes lack movable eyelids and have a fixed transparent scale over the eye.

Hearing is well developed in crocodiles and many lizards but less so in snakes and turtles, which detect mainly low-frequency ground vibrations. The reptilian ear usually includes a tympanum, stapes, middle-ear cavity, inner ear, and eustachian tube. Lizards often hear best between 400 and 1,500 hertz.

Chemoreception is important in many reptiles. Smell cells line part of the nose, while Jacobson's organ, or the vomeronasal organ, is highly developed in lizards and snakes. Snakes flick the tongue to collect odor particles and transfer them to this organ. Pit vipers, boas, pythons, and some other snakes also have infrared heat receptors for detecting prey.

## Classification

Reptile classification ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taxonomy)) depends heavily on fossils because living reptiles are only a small fraction of the groups that once existed. Higher categories use skeletal characters, especially skull, vertebral, and limb-girdle traits. Classification changes often as new fossils, data, methods, and philosophies appear.

Class Reptilia includes air-breathing amniotic vertebrates usually covered in keratinous scales. Excluding birds, there are more than 8,700 living species. Reptiles share amniotic membranes with mammals, though fossil classification must infer such soft embryonic traits from living relatives and skeletal relationships.

Parareptiles or Anapsida include extinct groups such as mesosaurs, pareisaurs, and procolophonians, and are often associated with skulls lacking temporal openings. Mesosaurs were Lower Permian aquatic reptiles with long jaws, pointed teeth, flattened tails, and paddlelike hind feet.

Sauropterygia included aquatic reptiles from the Triassic to Cretaceous, such as nothosaurs, plesiosaurs, and placodonts. Plesiosaurs and related forms often had paddlelike limbs. Placodonts were seallike mollusk eaters, sometimes armoured and turtlelike.

Archosauria includes crocodiles, pterosaurs, dinosaurs, and birds. Pterosaurs were flying reptiles with hollow bones and wing membranes supported by an elongated finger. Dinosauria includes bird-hipped ornithischians and other dinosaur groups, and birds represent the living continuation of one dinosaur line.

## Key Events & Developments

- 312 million to 307 million years ago: Reptile and synapsid lines diverged from a common amniote ancestor.
- 1950s-1960s: Heavy crocodile harvesting caused widespread local extinctions and population declines.
- Late Cretaceous: Giant reptiles such as Archelon, mosasaurs, and large dinosaurs showed the extreme size range of extinct forms.

## Key People

- Charles Darwin ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_Darwin)): His theory of natural selection ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_selection)) provides the evolutionary background for reptile diversity and adaptation.
- Modern herpetologists: Classify living and fossil reptiles using anatomy, fossils, phylogenetic methods, and conservation data.
