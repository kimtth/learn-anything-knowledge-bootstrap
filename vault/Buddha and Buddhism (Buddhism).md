# Buddha and Buddhism (Buddhism)

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> Buddhism is a diverse religious and philosophical tradition that began in India with the Buddha’s diagnosis of suffering and path of ethical discipline, meditation, insight, and liberation. Its teachings on karma, rebirth, no-self, dependent origination, and nirvana developed through Theravada, Mahayana, and Vajrayana institutions, texts, rituals, and regional adaptations across Asia and the modern world.

## Introduction

Buddhism ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buddhism)) is a religion and philosophy that grew from the teachings of the Buddha in northern India and later spread across Asia and beyond. It is a living tradition shaped by scripture, meditation, monastic life, and regional adaptation.

Buddhism is both historical and practical. It is not only a system of ideas about suffering and liberation, but also a lived religious world with institutions, rituals, ethical discipline, and philosophical debate.

The founder's life gives the tradition its shape. Born near Lumbini into a life of luxury, the future Buddha was shielded by his father from old age, sickness, and death. He married the princess Yashodhara at age 16 and had a son, but at age 29 he saw human suffering on chariot rides outside the palace and resolved to renounce his wealth and family. For about six years he practiced meditation and extreme self-mortification with five companions, until he fainted from weakness while bathing in a river and concluded that mortification was not the path to liberation. Sitting in meditation under a tree, he received enlightenment, identified with understanding the Four Noble Truths.

For the next 45 years the Buddha spread his message throughout northeastern [[India]], established orders of monks and nuns, and received the patronage of kings and merchants. At the age of 80 he fell ill, gave his disciples a final teaching, and passed into nirvana; his body was cremated and his relics enshrined in stupas that became focal points of veneration.

## Foundations

Buddhism emerged amid social change, dissatisfaction with ritual, and a search for liberation from suffering. Its central ideas include karma, rebirth, dependent origination, the Four Noble Truths, the Eightfold Path, and nirvana.

These foundations form the core framework shared across Buddhist traditions. Buddhism begins with a diagnosis of suffering and proceeds toward a disciplined path of transformation rather than toward faith alone.

Karma is moral causation linking action, intention, rebirth, and future experience. It is not fate in a simple sense; it is a structure through which intentional acts shape the conditions of continued existence. This gives Buddhist ethics its urgency, because conduct affects both present character and future rebirth.

Dependent origination explains existence as a chain of conditioned processes rather than as the expression of a permanent self. Ignorance, craving, attachment, becoming, birth, aging, and death are linked in a cycle that can be broken through insight and discipline. This doctrine connects metaphysics, psychology, and practice.

The Four Noble Truths identify suffering, its cause, its cessation, and the path to cessation. The Eightfold Path then organizes right view, intention, speech, action, livelihood, effort, mindfulness, and concentration. Together they form a complete training of thought, ethics, and meditation.

The four truths are named in Pali as the truth of misery (dukkha), the truth that misery arises from craving (samudaya), the truth that craving can be eliminated (nirodha), and the truth that elimination follows a methodical path (magga). Beside them stands the law of dependent origination (paticca-samuppada), a chain of twelve links running from ignorance through consciousness, contact, feeling, craving, and grasping to birth, old age, and death.

The belief in rebirth, or samsara, was already linked with karma in pre-Buddhist India. Because Buddhism also teaches no-self, the continuity of an ever-changing identity across lives is illustrated by the image of a fire, which looks unchanged yet differs at every moment.

## Core Teachings

Human existence is marked by suffering, causation, and impermanence. Liberation comes through ethical conduct, mental discipline, meditation, and insight into the lack of a permanent self.

The teaching of non-self is especially important because it undercuts the ordinary assumption that the person is a fixed inner essence. That insight connects philosophy, ethics, and meditation into a single path.

The path is not described as passive resignation. It is a deliberate training of conduct, attention, and understanding that changes how one lives in the world.

Ethics stays close to practice. Compassion, restraint, generosity, nonviolence, truthful speech, and disciplined livelihood are not merely social virtues; they are part of weakening craving and ignorance. This is why Buddhist teaching joins moral action to meditation and insight.

The aim of practice is to remove the delusion of ego and so overcome the round of rebirths. The term nirvana means "passing away" or "dying out"—the dying out in the heart of the fires of lust, anger, and delusion. It is not extinction, and the craving for annihilation was expressly rejected by the Buddha; he even refused to say whether a fully purified saint continues or ceases to exist after death, because such speculation was not relevant to the path.

## Early Tradition

Theravada Buddhism preserved the Pali canon and a strong monastic tradition. It rests on the Tipitaka, commentaries, meditation practices, cosmology, and the analysis of personality into aggregates and other doctrinal categories.

The early tradition is presented through both scripture and institution. Monastic discipline, doctrinal classification, and meditative practice all help define what Buddhism looked like in its formative classical shape.

Early Buddhism was never simply one note. It already contained many layers of teaching, interpretation, and practice.

[[Cosmos (Cosmology)|Cosmology]] deserves special attention. Early Buddhist worlds include realms of desire, form, and formlessness, along with heavens, hells, human worlds, and other forms of rebirth. These realms are not eternal destinations; they are conditioned states within samsara.

The analysis of personality into five aggregates is another key early teaching. Form, feeling, perception, mental formations, and consciousness explain the person without requiring a permanent soul. This analysis supports meditation because it trains practitioners to see experience as changing processes.

Meditation receives sustained treatment. Calm, concentration, mindfulness, and insight are used to transform perception and weaken attachment. Meditation is not escape but disciplined investigation of body, feeling, mind, and phenomena.

Theravada, the "Way of the Elders," traces its lineage to the Sthaviravada school, which formed after the Council of Vaishali about a century after the Buddha's death, and was one of eighteen traditional Hinayana schools. It preserved its teaching in the Pali Tipitaka, or Three Baskets—the Vinaya of monastic discipline, the Sutta of discourses, and the Abhidhamma of scholastic analysis. Established in Sri Lanka under Asoka in the 3rd century BCE, the dominant Mahavihara line later reached Myanmar in the late 11th century, Thailand in the 13th and early 14th centuries, and Cambodia and Laos by the end of the 14th century.

The cosmos is described as having three planes: the realm of desire (kama-loka), the realm of material form (rupa-loka), and the realm of formlessness (arupa-loka). Five buddhas are destined to appear in the human cosmos, with Gotama as the fourth and Metteyya (Maitreya) as the fifth, the future buddha now believed to wait in the Tushita heaven.

Meditation in Theravada takes two forms. The first passes through four stages of jhana, moving from detachment and joy toward inner serenity, indifference, and finally pure consciousness. The second form, vipassana or insight meditation, uses intense concentration to see that all reality is impermanent, filled with suffering, and devoid of self.

## Mahayana and Vajrayana

Mahayana Buddhism broadened the ideal of salvation through the bodhisattva path and introduced new scriptures and doctrines. Madhyamika, Yogacara, Avatamsaka, Tiantai, Pure Land, Nichiren, Chan/Zen, and Esoteric or Vajrayana Buddhism developed as major movements within the broader tradition.

These traditions are presented as creative expansions rather than simple departures. Mahayana enlarged the field of Buddhist aspiration, while Vajrayana added ritual, mantra, visualization, and initiatory practices that intensified the path to awakening.

This range shows Buddhism as internally diverse but still linked by common goals and vocabulary.

Madhyamika, associated with Nagarjuna, teaches emptiness, arguing that things lack independent self-existence because they arise dependently. Yogacara, associated with Asanga and Vasubandhu, analyzes consciousness and the ways experience is constructed. These schools gave Mahayana deep philosophical systems.

Other Mahayana schools developed around specific scriptures and practices. Avatamsaka or Huayan emphasized interpenetrating reality; Tiantai organized the Buddha’s teachings around the Lotus Sutra; Pure Land focused devotion on Amitabha and rebirth in Sukhavati; Chan or Zen emphasized meditation and direct realization.

Pure Land was one of the most socially powerful forms of Buddhism because it opened salvation to ordinary believers through trust, recitation, devotion, and the vow of Amitabha. It was especially important in China and Japan, where teachers such as Honen and Shinran reworked devotion into mass religious movements.

Nichiren Buddhism centred practice on the Lotus Sutra and gave Japanese Buddhism a more militant scriptural focus. Chan and Zen emphasized meditation, lineage, discipline, and sudden or direct awakening, yet they also built institutions, monasteries, literary traditions, and arts of training.

Vajrayana used mantra, mandala, initiation, visualization, deity yoga, and esoteric ritual. In Tibet, schools such as Rnying-ma-pa, Bka'-gdams-pa, Dge-lugs-pa, and others organized tantric practice, monastic discipline, and scholastic learning into distinctive institutions.

The esoteric traditions also developed complex symbolic worlds. Buddhas, bodhisattvas, protective deities, mandalas, ritual gestures, sacred syllables, and visualization practices were used to transform ordinary perception into awakened perception. These practices were disciplined systems rather than decorative mythology.

Mahayana arose in India in the 1st century CE and viewed the Buddha as a supramundane being who multiplies himself, often as a pentad of celestial buddhas: Vairocana, Aksobhya, Ratnasambhava, Amitabha, and Amoghasiddhi. Madhyamika, founded by Nagarjuna (c. 150–250 CE) and his disciple Aryadeva, systematized the doctrine of emptiness (shunyata) and the two truths, relative and absolute, later dividing into the Prasangika school of Buddhapalita and a Svatantrika line. Yogacara, founded by Asanga and Vasubandhu in the 4th–5th century, taught that only consciousness is real and described the alaya-vijnana, or storehouse consciousness, that holds the seeds of past deeds.

In China the Huayan or Avatamsaka school, systematized by the patriarch Fazang, used the image of a golden lion to show how each part expresses the whole, while the Tiantai or Tendai school, founded by Zhiyi and carried to Japan by Saicho on Mount Hiei, centred on the Lotus Sutra. Pure Land grew from the vows of the monk Dharmakara, whose eighteenth vow promised rebirth in the Western Paradise of Amitabha to anyone calling his name at death; the recitation, called nembutsu in Japan, was spread by Honen and Shinran in the 13th century, an age believed to be the "latter days of the law."

The Nichiren school, named after its founder Nichiren (1222–82), held that the Lotus Sutra holds the supreme teaching. Chan or Zen, traced to the South Indian monk Bodhidharma (arrived c. 520 CE), split into the Linzi (Rinzai) branch with its koan and the Caodong (Soto) branch with its silent sitting, or zazen. Vajrayana united wisdom and compassion, symbolized by the yab-yum union; in Tibet the reforming Dge-lugs-pa or "Yellow Hats" of Tsong-kha-pa grew alongside the older Rnying-ma-pa of Padmasambhava, while esoteric practice took form in China as Zhenyan and in Japan as Shingon, founded by Kukai at Mount Koya.

## Spread Across Asia

![[attachments/buddhism_001.webp|420]]

*Map of the historical expansion of Buddhism across Asia*

Buddhism ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buddhism)) spread from India into Sri Lanka, Southeast Asia, Central Asia, China, Korea, Japan, Tibet, Mongolia, and the Himalayan kingdoms. In each region it adapted to local politics, languages, and religious culture while maintaining continuity with earlier teachings.

The spread of Buddhism is presented as a long process of translation and accommodation. Monasteries, royal patronage, merchants, and pilgrims all helped carry the tradition across borders.

Regional forms emerged because Buddhism had to fit different civilizations. This flexibility was one of the tradition’s great strengths.

In [[India]], Buddhism arose, flourished, and eventually declined as a major institutional force, though its ideas continued to influence South Asian religion. In [[Sri Lanka]], Theravada became closely tied to kingship, monastic reform, relics, and the Pali textual tradition. From there it strongly influenced mainland Southeast Asia.

In [[China]], Buddhism entered through translation, Central Asian contacts, and monastic activity. It had to explain itself in relation to Daoist and Confucian language, survive periods of persecution, and build schools that became deeply Chinese. Korea and Japan then adapted many Chinese forms while creating their own institutions and devotional movements.

In Tibet and [[Mongolia]], Buddhism became intertwined with monastic power, tantric ritual, scholasticism, and political authority. Buddhist history is therefore also the history of translation, kingship, pilgrimage, monastic networks, and regional reinvention.

The earliest clear spread outside India came under Asoka in the 3rd century BCE, whose son, the monk Mahinda, converted King Devanampiya Tissa of Sri Lanka and helped found the Mahavihara monastery; in the late 1st century BCE King Vattagamani sponsored a council at which the Pali oral tradition was first committed to writing. Buddhism entered Central Asia along the trade routes, encouraged by the Kushan king Kaniska, whose patronage supported the Gandhara school of art that blended Greek and Persian elements into Buddhist images.

In [[China]], tradition says Buddhism arrived after the Han emperor Mingdi dreamed of a flying golden deity. Early Chinese Buddhism mixed with Daoism and used Daoist vocabulary in its first translations. Korea received Buddhism from China in the 4th century CE, and scholars such as Wonhyo (617–686) taught an ecumenical Buddhism.

Japan received Buddhism from Korea in the 6th century, where it was first treated as a talisman protecting the state and promoted by Prince Shotoku, who became regent in 593; during the Nara period (710–784) it became the state religion, centred on the Great Buddha statue, or Daibutsu, at Nara. In Tibet Buddhism was firmly established with the help of the Indian master Atisa, who arrived in 1042, and among the Mongols the Altan Khan adopted the Dge-lugs-pa tradition in 1578.

## Monastic and Devotional Life

![[attachments/buddhism_002.webp|420]]

*Buddhist monks receiving offerings in Indonesia*

Monasteries, monks, nuns, pilgrimage, relics, ritual, recitation, meditation, and devotion are all central. The relation between lay supporters and the monastic community is a defining feature of Buddhist life.

Monastic life is shown as the institutional backbone of Buddhism. Monasteries preserve texts, train practitioners, and provide a visible model of discipline, while lay communities sustain them materially through gifts and support.

Devotion and meditation are not opposites. They are complementary ways through which Buddhists seek merit, insight, and liberation.

Recurring rites include Uposatha, festivals marking events in the Buddha’s life, the rainy-season retreat or Vassa, ordination ceremonies, and tantric initiations or abhiseka. These practices show how Buddhist time, community, and authority are organized ritually.

Ordination is especially important because it creates the disciplined community that preserves Buddhist teaching. Monks and nuns take vows, receive training, observe rules of conduct, and enter a lineage that links them to earlier communities. In many regions, temporary or lifelong ordination also shaped education and male religious identity.

Women's Buddhist roles have varied by region and period. Nuns, laywomen, donors, teachers, mothers, patrons, and devotional specialists all helped sustain Buddhist communities, even where full female ordination was restricted, interrupted, or debated. The question of the bhikkhuni lineage shows how institutional authority, gender, scripture, and modern reform intersect. Buddhism's social history is therefore not only a story of monks and monasteries but also of households, female patrons, and women seeking religious discipline and recognition.

Festivals and anniversaries mark the Buddha's birth, awakening, preaching, and death, while local celebrations honour relics, images, saints, and regional protectors. Buddhist ritual life is therefore both universal and local: shared themes are expressed through specific calendars, shrines, languages, and customs.

Lay people gain merit through gifts, worship, pilgrimage, recitation, support of monks and nuns, and ethical conduct. Monastics preserve learning and discipline, but lay devotion keeps Buddhist institutions socially rooted. This exchange is one of Buddhism’s most durable social structures.

The ritual calendar gives this life its rhythm. The four monthly holy days of uposatha, set by the new and full moons, bring laypeople and monks together for the recitation of precepts, offerings to the Buddha image, and a sermon, while the more pious observe eight precepts for the day. The Buddha's birth, enlightenment, and final nirvana are celebrated together at Vesak, the full moon of the sixth lunar month, in Theravada lands, but on separate days in Mahayana countries, where the birth may fall on April 8, the enlightenment on December 8, and the death on February 15.

The three-month rainy-season retreat, vassa, runs from July to October and is especially important in mainland Southeast Asia, where a layman may take temporary vows for its duration; its close is marked by the kathina, a robe-offering ceremony in which a whole village cooperates. Admission to the sangha proceeds in two steps, the lower ordination (pabbajja) and the higher ordination (upasampada), the latter given only from age 20 before at least ten monks. In Vajrayana the initiate also receives abhiseka, a ritual sprinkling of consecrated water.

## Historical Development

![[attachments/buddhism_003.webp|420]]

*Great Stupa at Sanchi, a major Buddhist monument in India*

Buddhism is a tradition that repeatedly transformed itself while preserving a central concern with liberation. Its growth runs through councils, commentaries, kingdoms, reform movements, and the continuing vitality of Buddhist practice into the modern world.

This historical narrative is one of continuity through adaptation. Buddhism survives not by staying unchanged, but by repeatedly reworking its institutions and doctrines for new settings.

That pattern helps explain why the tradition can be at once ancient and modern, textual and ritual, philosophical and devotional.

Buddhism declined in India while expanding elsewhere. Changes in royal patronage, competition with Hindu traditions, Muslim conquests, and shifts in monastic institutions all contributed to Buddhism's reduced presence in its homeland. Yet by then it had become deeply rooted in Sri Lanka, Southeast Asia, Tibet, China, Korea, and Japan.

In [[Sri Lanka]] and mainland Southeast Asia, Theravada became closely tied to kingship, monastic reform, Pali learning, relic devotion, and lay merit-making. In Myanmar, Thailand, Cambodia, and Laos, Buddhist institutions helped shape law, kingship, education, festivals, and village religious life.

In [[China]], Buddhism faced repeated challenges because it introduced monastic celibacy, foreign scriptures, and ideas that differed from Confucian family duty. Yet translation, Chinese monastic institutions, pilgrimage, Pure Land devotion, Chan practice, and scholastic synthesis made it a major part of Chinese civilization.

In [[Japan]], Buddhism moved through court patronage, monastic schools, esoteric ritual, Pure Land devotion, Zen discipline, Nichiren activism, and modern lay movements such as Soka-gakkai and Rissho-Kosei-kai. Japanese Buddhism was highly creative because imported forms were repeatedly reorganized for new social settings.

In Tibet, Buddhism became a major political and cultural structure. Monasteries, reincarnate lamas, scholastic debate, tantric initiation, ritual art, and state authority became closely linked. In Mongolia, Tibetan forms of Buddhism strongly influenced elite culture, monastic life, and political legitimacy.

## Conclusion

Buddhism is not a single fixed system but a family of traditions united by the Buddha’s teaching on suffering, causation, discipline, and awakening. Its history is one of both continuity and reinvention.

The conclusion makes Buddhism legible as a tradition of path and practice. What unites its many schools is less a fixed creed than a shared orientation toward liberation.

Buddhism ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buddhism)) is both one tradition and many traditions. It can be philosophical, monastic, devotional, ritual, meditative, royal, village-based, scholastic, esoteric, reformist, or modern lay-centred, depending on region and period. Its unity lies in the repeated attempt to transform suffering through discipline, insight, compassion, and awakened understanding.

That range makes Buddhism a civilizational tradition, not only a doctrine. It shaped art, kingship, education, pilgrimage, literature, temple economies, burial practice, reform politics, and everyday lay devotion across much of Asia.

It also shaped concepts of time, merit, death, rebirth, compassion, renunciation, monastic discipline, lay devotion, ritual memory, and sacred geography, giving Buddhist societies shared maps of moral action and cosmic order.

## Texts and Authority

The canon and the ways later traditions interpreted it carry major weight. The Pali Tipitaka anchors Theravada identity, while Mahayana and Vajrayana traditions recognize a broader body of sutras and tantras as authoritative Buddhist speech. Commentaries are treated as essential, not secondary, because they mediate doctrine for later communities.

This textual world matters because Buddhist schools often define themselves through what they accept as the Buddha's word and how they read it. Interpretation is therefore part of the tradition's structure.

Buddhist authority is not only textual. Relics, stupas, images, teachers, ordination lineages, monastic rules, ritual initiations, and meditation experience all serve as sources of religious legitimacy. Different schools balance this evidence differently.

The Sangha, or monastic community, is therefore more than a group of specialists. It preserves discipline, transmits teachings, performs rituals, teaches lay followers, and embodies the possibility of liberation. Its relation with lay society is one of the major continuities across Buddhist history.

Scriptural authority also changed as Buddhism moved between languages. Pali, Sanskrit, Chinese, Tibetan, Korean, Japanese, Mongolian, and vernacular traditions preserved different textual bodies. Translation did not merely copy doctrine; it created new vocabularies through which Buddhist ideas entered local cultures.

Commentarial traditions gave later teachers a way to organize huge bodies of material. They classified teachings, ranked scriptures, explained difficult doctrines, and linked practice to school identity. This is why scholasticism is part of lived Buddhism rather than a remote academic activity.

## Regional Development

In Sri Lanka, Theravada became closely tied to royal patronage and monastic continuity, while Southeast Asian Buddhism developed through local dynasties and transregional exchange. In China, Buddhism interacted with Daoism and gradually formed major schools such as Tiantai, Huayan, Pure Land, and Chan. Korea absorbed Chinese traditions and reshaped them through local scholarly and ritual life, and Japan developed distinctive forms such as Tendai, Shingon, and Pure Land devotion.

These regional histories show Buddhism adapting to different political and cultural settings without losing a recognizable core.

Modern Buddhism includes reform movements, nationalist uses of Buddhist identity, global meditation communities, lay associations, academic study, and new interpretations of ethics and psychology. Buddhism therefore does not end with decline or preservation alone. It keeps being translated, institutionalized, challenged, and renewed.

The modern period also changed the balance between monastic and lay authority. In some places, monks remained central teachers and ritual specialists; in others, lay meditation movements, social service groups, publishing networks, and international teachers carried Buddhist practice beyond older institutional boundaries.
