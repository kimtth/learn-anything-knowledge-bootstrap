# Pacific Ocean

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> The Pacific Ocean is Earth's largest and deepest ocean, extending between Asia, the Americas, Antarctica, and the Arctic. Its relief, island patterns, tectonic boundaries, winds, storms, circulation, tides, fisheries, trade, exploration, and environmental pressures make it a central physical and human oceanic system.

## Introduction

Pacific Ocean ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pacific_Ocean)) is the largest ocean, extending from the Antarctic region to the Arctic and lying between Asia and Australia on the west and the Americas on the east. Excluding adjacent seas, it covers about 63.8 million square miles, roughly one-third of Earth's surface. It has more than twice the area and water volume of the Atlantic Ocean and exceeds the total land area of the globe.

The Pacific stretches about 9,600 miles from Antarctica to the Bering Strait and about 12,000 miles from Colombia to the Malay Peninsula. Its mean depth is 14,040 feet, and its greatest known depth, 36,201 feet in the Mariana Trench, is the deepest point known in any ocean.

## Boundaries and Drainage

The Pacific meets the Arctic Ocean in the Bering Sea and mixes with the Atlantic in Drake Passage. Its boundary with the Indian Ocean is less distinct, generally running from Sumatra through Java, Timor, Australia, Tasmania, and Antarctica. Because major mountain systems redirect drainage, only about one-seventh of continental runoff enters the Pacific.

## Relief

The Pacific basin has eastern, western, and central physiographic regions. The eastern region runs along the American cordilleras, with narrow shelves, steep slopes, and trenches such as the Middle America and Peru-Chile trenches. The western region is more complex, bounded by trenches from the Aleutians to Tonga and Kermadec, with island arcs and deep continental seas. The central region is broad, stable, and low-relief.

## Islands and the Andesite Line

The Andesite Line ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andesite_line)) marks a major geologic boundary between continental, high islands and oceanic, low islands. Western islands such as the Aleutians, Kurils, Ryukyus, Taiwan, New Guinea, and New Zealand have continental character. East of the line, basaltic oceanic islands dominate. Micronesia, Melanesia, and Polynesia contain coral islands, volcanic islands, continental islands, and vast island chains such as Hawaii.

## Geology and Plate Tectonics

![[attachments/pacific_ocean_003.png|420]]

*Pacific Ring of Fire map*

[[Plate Tectonics|Plate tectonics]] explains the Pacific's major features. Western volcanic arcs and trenches are convergent zones where plates collide and one is subducted. The East Pacific Rise creates new crust. The northeastern margin includes strike-slip motion along the San Andreas Fault system. In the southeast, the Nazca Plate collides with the South American Plate, producing the Andes and the Peru-Chile Trench.

## Climate and Winds

![[attachments/pacific_ocean_004.png|420]]

*Pacific Ocean currents map*

The Pacific's [[Climate and Weather (Climate)|climate]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Climate)) reflects planetary wind and pressure systems. Trade winds dominate the eastern and equatorial circulation, while the doldrums mark equatorial calms. Westerlies and polar fronts create middle-latitude storms, especially the strong Southern Hemisphere winds called the roaring forties. The western Pacific also has monsoon circulation driven by seasonal heating and cooling of Asia.

## Tropical Storms and Temperature

The western Pacific between about 5 and 25 degrees north is a major typhoon region, especially in late summer and early autumn when surface waters are warm. Pacific waters are temperature-stratified: the surface zone is variable, while deep waters are cold and stable. The deep zone contains about 80 percent of Pacific volume and averages about 38.3 F.

## Salinity and Hydrology

Surface salinity depends on wind, precipitation, and evaporation. Equatorial waters are relatively fresh because rainfall is high and evaporation is low, while southeastern open-Pacific waters reach higher salinities. Trade winds drive the North and South Equatorial currents, the equatorial countercurrent, the Kuroshio, the Oya Current, the California Current, the East Australian Current, the South Pacific Current, and the Antarctic Circumpolar Current.

## Tides

Unlike the Atlantic, Pacific tides include many daily and mixed patterns. Diurnal tides occur in parts of Southeast Asia and around New Guinea seas, while mixed tides prevail along much of the Pacific coast. Most tidal ranges are small, but the Gulf of California, Korea Bay, and parts of Australia have much larger ranges.

## Resources, Fisheries, and Trade

The Pacific has highly varied biological resources, including kelp forests, plankton-rich upwelling zones, coral ecosystems, and major fisheries. Japan, Russia, China, the United States, Peru, Chile, South Korea, and Indonesia are major fishing countries. Tuna is especially important for island states because many stocks fall within exclusive economic zones. The Pacific also carries key trade routes linking East Asia, North America, Australia, New Zealand, and Southeast Asia.

## Environmental Impact

The Pacific Ocean ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pacific_Ocean)) can absorb and disperse large waste loads, but nearshore pollution has grown around ports and coastal cities. Crude oil transport, hazardous cargoes, sewage, heavy metals, fertilizers, and pesticides have polluted confined waters. In some areas, commercially important fish and crustaceans have been depleted or made unsafe.

## Exploration

Pacific settlement began with migrations from Southeast Asia into Melanesia, Micronesia, and Polynesia, including long-distance canoe voyages to Hawaii and New Zealand. European exploration began with Ferdinand Magellan, continued through Spanish, Dutch, British, and French voyages, and peaked with James Cook's three voyages. Later oceanographic work included the Challenger Expedition, Carnegie, Dana II, Albatross, Galathea, Vityaz, and the Trieste descent into the Mariana Trench in 1960.

## Key Events & Developments

- 1519-1522: Magellan's expedition crossed the Pacific.
- 1768-1779: James Cook's voyages mapped much of the ocean.
- 1870s: The Challenger Expedition advanced oceanographic study.
- 1960: Trieste descended to the Mariana Trench.
- Modern satellites: Remote sensing transformed ocean observation.

## Key People

- Ferdinand Magellan ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ferdinand_Magellan)): Led the first European crossing of the Pacific.
- Abel Janszoon Tasman ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abel_Janszoon_Tasman)): Explored New Zealand, Fiji, Tonga, and nearby regions.
- James Cook ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Cook)): Produced exceptionally accurate Pacific charts.
- Charles Darwin ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_Darwin)): Helped explain reefs and guyots through subsidence theory.
- Luis Vaez de Torres ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Luis_Vaez_de_Torres)): Charted the strait between New Guinea and Australia.
