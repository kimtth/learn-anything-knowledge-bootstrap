# Native American Peoples

> [!abstract] Reference navigation

## Introduction

Native American peoples ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indigenous_peoples_of_the_Americas)), also called American Indians, Amerindians, aboriginal Americans, or First Nations people, are the Indigenous peoples of the Western Hemisphere, though the term often refers especially to groups whose original territories lay in present-day Canada and the United States. Before European conquest, Indigenous Americans used fire, dogs, stone tools, atlatls, harpoons, bows, cordage, netting, basketry, and pottery. Some lived by hunting and gathering, while others farmed maize, beans, squash, potatoes, turkeys, llamas, alpacas, and other domesticated or semidomesticated species.

The pre-Columbian Americas ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pre-Columbian_era)) included communities from small hamlets to cities such as Cahokia, with perhaps 10,000 to 20,000 people, and Teotihuacan, with perhaps 125,000 to 200,000 residents. After Europeans arrived in the 16th century, Indigenous peoples were devastated by epidemic disease, conquest, enslavement, and discriminatory law, yet they also became active and successful in movements for political change, autonomy, education, land rights, religious freedom, legal recognition, and cultural revitalization.

## Native American Culture Areas

![[attachments/native_american_peoples_001.webp|420]]

*Native American culture areas map*

Anthropologists often use culture areas ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cultural_area)) to compare Native American societies. A culture area is a geographic region where certain traits tended to co-occur, such as salmon fishing, woodworking, large villages, and social hierarchy on the Northwest Coast. The common North American areas are the Arctic, Subarctic, Northeast, Southeast, Plains, Southwest, Great Basin, California, Northwest Coast, and Plateau, though some scholars combine or subdivide them.

Culture areas are comparative tools, not fixed ethnic borders. Each region contained many languages, political units, and local adaptations, and neighbouring regions exchanged foods, technologies, spouses, ceremonies, and trade goods. The approach is useful because environment and economy mattered, but it must be balanced with attention to individual nations, migration, alliance, and change over time.

## The Arctic

![[attachments/native_american_peoples_002.webp|420]]

*Inuit woman historical photograph*

The [[Arctic]] includes northern Alaska and Canada near and above the Arctic Circle. Its Indigenous peoples include Eskimo groups, Inuit, Yupik/Yupiit, and Aleut, with Eskimo-Aleut languages. They relied on hunting and gathering in a harsh climate, using sea mammals, fish, caribou, dog sleds, fur clothing, snow or earth-covered houses, small kin-based bands, storytelling, mythology, and folklore. Many Alaskan groups prefer Native Alaskan, while Canadian Arctic peoples generally prefer Inuit.

## The Subarctic

The Subarctic covers most of Alaska and Canada south of the Arctic, except the Maritime Provinces. It is dominated by taiga, cool climate, swamps, and coniferous forest. Innu, Cree, Ojibwa, Chipewyan, Beaver, Slave, Carrier, Gwich'in, Tanaina, and Deg Xinag peoples spoke Athabaskan and Algonquian languages, lived mostly in mobile kin-based bands, hunted moose, caribou, beaver, waterfowl, and fish, and used berries, roots, sap, snowshoes, toboggans, fur clothing, winter semisubterranean houses, and summer tents or lean-tos.

## The Northeast

![[attachments/native_american_peoples_003.webp|420]]

*Iroquois longhouse*

The Northeast stretches from Quebec, Ontario, and the Maritimes to the Ohio Valley and North Carolina coast. Algonquian and Iroquoian-speaking peoples included Algonquin, Iroquois, Huron, Wampanoag, Mohican, Mohegan, Ojibwa, Ho-chunk, Sauk, Fox, and Illinois. Many farmed maize, beans, squash, and seed crops; others used wild rice, salmon, or shellfish. Villages, hamlets, tribes, confederacies, wickiups, longhouses, dugouts, birchbark canoes, deerskin clothing, and medicine societies were characteristic.

## The Southeast

The Southeast extended from the southern edge of the Northeast to the Gulf of Mexico and west beyond the Mississippi Valley. Cherokee, Choctaw, Chickasaw, Creek, Seminole, Natchez, Caddo, Apalachee, Timucua, and Guale peoples lived in a warm, agriculturally rich region. Many societies formed chiefdoms with elites and commoners, hamlets along waterways, ceremonial towns, earthen mounds, maize, beans, squash, tobacco, hunting, fishing, shellfish, bird imagery, and the ritual black drink.

## The Plains

The Plains lie between the western mountains and the Mississippi Valley, from the Subarctic to the Rio Grande. Before horses, some peoples lived in earth-lodge villages and farmed maize, beans, squash, sunflowers, and tobacco along waterways. After Spanish horses became common by about 1750, bison hunting and equestrian nomadism expanded among Crow, Sioux, Blackfoot, Cheyenne, Comanche, Arapaho, and Kiowa peoples. Tepees, leather clothing, feathered regalia, sign language, drums, and the Sun Dance became widely recognized.

## The Southwest

The Southwest, mainly Arizona and New Mexico, contains deserts, basins, plateaus, and river oases. Pueblo peoples such as Zuni and Hopi farmed; Yumans, Pima, and Tohono O'odham combined farming and foraging; Navajo and Apache groups combined farming, foraging, and raiding. Maize, beans, squash, cotton, adobe and stone pueblos, hogans, kachina dances, pottery, textiles, jewelry, kivas, sand paintings, basketry, and water-control features such as check dams were important.

## The Great Basin

The Great Basin centres on intermontane deserts of Nevada and nearby states. Washoe and Numic-speaking Mono, Paiute, Bannock, Shoshone, Ute, and Gosiute peoples usually lived in mobile kin-based bands. Seeds, pinon nuts, small game, digging sticks, nets, basketry, grinding stones, rock art, wickiups, and brush shelters were common. Northern and eastern groups adopted horses, bison hunting, and Plains-style tepees where conditions allowed.

## California

California included extraordinary ecological and linguistic diversity, with about 20 language families and many microenvironments. Hupa, Yurok, Pomo, Yuki, Wintun, Maidu, Yana, and other peoples often organized into independent tribelets rather than centralized states. Outside the Colorado River, hunting and gathering dominated. Acorns were central because people learned to leach toxins and make flour; fishing, shellfish, hunting, ceremonial houses, basketry, and local political autonomy were important.

## The Northwest Coast

The Northwest Coast runs from southern Alaska to northern California along rainforests, intertidal zones, and ocean. Tlingit, Haida, Tsimshian, Kwakiutl, Bella Coola, Nuu-chah-nulth, Coast Salish, and Chinook peoples had abundant salmon, fish, sea mammals, shellfish, birds, and plants. Their rich resource base supported hereditary elites, commoners, slaves, corporate houses, ranked villages, plank houses, seaworthy watercraft, wood and stone carving, totem poles, basketry, and potlatches.

## The Plateau

The Plateau lies among the Subarctic, Plains, Great Basin, California, and Northwest Coast areas, drained by the Fraser and Columbia rivers. Salish, Flathead, Nez Perce, Yakama, Kutenai, Modoc, Klamath, Spokan, Kalispel, Pend d'Oreille, Coeur d'Alene, Walla Walla, and Umatilla peoples used fish, camas, game, trade, permanent riverside villages, upland foraging camps, A-frame dwellings, and conical structures. Horses encouraged some groups to adopt buffalo hunting and Plains forms.

## Prehistory

Native American prehistory was long distorted by racist theories such as the idea that mounds were built by a lost non-Indigenous civilization. In the 1890s, Cyrus Thomas showed that the great mounds of the Northeast and Southeast were built by Native Americans. Humans reached the Americas at least 13,000 years ago and perhaps earlier, likely through or along Beringia, the land bridge exposed between Asia and North America from about 28,000 to 10,000 BC.

The Beringia model includes both movement and long residence. Falling sea levels exposed a broad land bridge, while glaciers blocked easy entry into parts of North America until ice-free or coastal routes opened. Some groups may have followed the coast using boats, while others entered through interior corridors along the Mackenzie, Yukon, Liard, and Peace systems.

## Paleo-Indian

Paleo-Indians lived among Pleistocene megafauna such as mammoths, mastodons, giant bison, ground sloths, sabre-toothed cats, and short-faced bears. Clovis, Folsom, and related interior cultures used distinctive projectile points, but sites such as Gault and Jake Bluff show they were generalized hunter-gatherers using many plants and animals, not only big-game hunters. The Folsom and Clovis finds proved ancient coexistence of humans and extinct megafauna.

The classic Folsom and Clovis discoveries were important because they overturned scholarly skepticism about deep antiquity in the Americas. George McJunkin's report of giant bison remains near Folsom and later mammoth-associated finds near Clovis showed that ancient hunters lived with extinct animals, while later recovery methods broadened the picture beyond spectacular kill sites.

## Pacific Coast Archaic Cultures

Pacific Coast Archaic peoples used diverse microenvironments. Inland dry groups used flint tools, grinding stones, seeds, and small game; wetter areas supplied elk, deer, acorns, fish, and birds; coastal groups used shellfish, sea mammals, fish, or mixed marine foods. Many groups preferred microblades, while Old Cordilleran peoples used lanceolate points, long blades, and choppers.

## Hohokam

The Southwest farming complexes of the early Common Era included Ancestral Pueblo, Mogollon, and Hohokam. Ancestral Pueblo peoples used check dams, expanded between about 750 and 1150, and developed rituals and ceremonialism. Mogollon villages moved from pit houses toward Pueblo influence and reached artistic height in the Classic Mimbres period. Hohokam people built irrigation canals in the Gila basin and developed compact settlements with multiroom buildings and Mexican influences.

## Eastern Woodland Cultures

Eastern Woodland cultures refer to early agricultural peoples east of the Mississippi Valley, not simply the later Northeast and Southeast culture areas. Adena peoples built large burial and effigy mounds from about 500 BC to AD 100, and Hopewell peoples continued mound building and wide exchange. On the Plains, farming villages and later equestrian nomadism interacted, producing Mandan, Hidatsa, Crow, Wichita, Pawnee, Ponca, Apache, Comanche, Sioux, and Cheyenne adaptations.

Agriculture did not transform the Eastern Woodlands all at once. Local seed crops predated maize, and corn initially supplemented rather than replaced older economies. Mound building, exchange networks, ritual landscapes, village farming, and hunting developed in different combinations, showing that farming was one part of broader social and ceremonial change.

## Native American History

Native American history must use archaeology, oral tradition, art, folklore, and colonial documents because Indigenous written sources from the 15th to 19th centuries are often scarce. Experiences varied by society and colonizer: stratified farmers such as the Natchez faced Europeans differently from hunter-gatherers such as Apache groups, and Spanish, French, English, Dutch, and Swedish colonial systems differed greatly.

## North America and Europe circa 1492

In 1492, estimates for Indigenous population north of the Rio Grande range from about 900,000 to 18,000,000. Native North America was linguistically and politically diverse, with more than 50 language families and 300 to 500 languages, far more than western Europe. Most polities used consensus-based leadership, though Southeast and Northwest Coast societies often had hereditary hierarchy.

This diversity means that contact cannot be reduced to one Native response. Some peoples lived in dense farming towns, some in mobile bands, some in ranked houses or chiefdoms, and others in confederacies. Leaders often gained authority through persuasion, kinship, ritual status, or military need rather than through centralized state power comparable to European monarchies.

## Colonial Encounters

Colonial encounters involved disease, trade, missionization, military alliance, enslavement, land loss, and diplomacy. Spain imposed conquest, missions, and labour systems in the Southwest and Southeast; France emphasized trade and alliance in the Subarctic, Northeast, and Mississippi regions; England expanded settler colonies that displaced Indigenous peoples; the Netherlands and Sweden created smaller but influential colonial footholds.

Spanish colonization changed Southwestern and Southeastern societies through missions, presidios, tribute demands, livestock, metal goods, and epidemics. Pueblo peoples, mission Indians, and many other groups had to negotiate between coercive conversion, labour obligations, and survival. In French zones, trade in furs and military alliance created different pressures: Native nations often remained indispensable diplomatic partners, but dependence on trade goods and involvement in imperial wars reshaped politics.

English colonial expansion produced intense land pressure. Settlements such as Jamestown, Plymouth, Boston, St. Mary's City, New Amsterdam, and later inland colonies brought fields, livestock, fences, courts, and settlers into Indigenous territories. Some Native groups used alliance, trade, diplomacy, and warfare to preserve autonomy; others were drawn into cycles of debt, dependency, displacement, or conflict. Dutch and Swedish colonies also affected trade routes, diplomacy, and access to European goods.

Land agreements often failed because English and Indigenous legal assumptions differed. English settlers tended to treat land as alienable property held by permanent title, while many Native peoples understood agreements as use rights, alliance obligations, or continuing relationships. Annual gifts that colonizers saw as diplomacy could be understood by Native communities as rent or proof that obligations remained active.

## Wars, Treaties, and Dispossession

From the 17th to 19th centuries, Native peoples faced shifting imperial wars, the French and Indian War, Pontiac's War, the American Revolution, U.S. expansion, Canadian treaty-making, Plains warfare, reservation systems, and boarding schools. The Northwest Ordinances, U.S. treaties, Canadian Numbered Treaties, and later policies of reorganization, termination, and self-determination repeatedly redefined Indigenous sovereignty, land, citizenship, and cultural survival.

The Plains and western expansion period brought new military and political crises. Horse-mounted societies such as Lakota, Cheyenne, Comanche, Crow, Blackfoot, Kiowa, and others built powerful regional systems, but railroads, miners, settlers, bison destruction, forts, and U.S. military campaigns undermined them. Reservation confinement, rationing, allotment, and the transfer of children to boarding schools tried to replace Indigenous landholding, languages, religions, and family systems with Euro-American models.

In [[Canada]], the Numbered Treaties and the Second Riel Rebellion reflected similar struggles over land, food, sovereignty, and political recognition. Treaty texts, oral promises, government enforcement, and Indigenous expectations often diverged. Across the continent, Native communities continued adapting through treaty claims, religious renewal, intertribal movements, legal appeals, and selective adoption of new political tools.

United States law defined a lasting sovereignty paradox. The Marshall court treated Native nations as domestic dependent nations, denied them full foreign status, but also recognized that states could not simply impose their laws on Indigenous lands. Later disputes repeatedly turned on this tension between federal power, tribal authority, treaty rights, and state pressure.

Assimilation policies attacked land and family together. The Dawes allotment system divided communal holdings and opened so-called surplus land to non-Indigenous buyers, while boarding and residential schools separated children from language, kin, ceremony, and subsistence knowledge. Poverty on reservations was then blamed on Native culture rather than on marginal land, broken promises, corruption, and coercive policy.

## Developments in the Late 20th and Early 21st Centuries

Late-20th-century and early-21st-century Native activism emphasized sovereignty, treaty rights, sacred sites, repatriation, religious freedom, mascots, education, economic development, identity, and international Indigenous rights. U.S. laws such as the American Indian Religious Freedom Act and repatriation movements strengthened some claims, while the [[United Nations]] adopted the Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples in 2007 despite earlier opposition from some settler states.

Modern Native self-determination has included tribally controlled schools and colleges, language revitalization, repatriation of ancestors and sacred objects, cultural heritage programs, health initiatives, court cases over fishing and hunting rights, and economic development through gaming, energy, tourism, arts, and natural resources. Controversies over sacred places such as Devils Tower, over museum collections, and over sports mascots show that sovereignty and representation remain active political questions.

Urbanization created new Native identities as many people moved to cities while maintaining tribal citizenship, kinship, ceremonial ties, and pan-Indigenous organizations. Native veterans, women leaders, artists, writers, lawyers, activists, and community elders helped transform public understandings of Indigenous survival from a story of disappearance into one of continuity, adaptation, and political presence.

Language and education became major fields of cultural renewal. Boarding schools had punished Native languages and family practices, but later communities created immersion programs, tribal colleges, curriculum projects, dictionaries, archives, and youth camps. These efforts treat language not only as communication but also as a carrier of law, ceremony, humour, ecological knowledge, and kinship.

Native economies also diversified. Tribal governments pursued gaming, tourism, energy development, fisheries, forestry, arts markets, agriculture, and professional services, while also debating environmental risk, revenue distribution, and cultural responsibility. Economic sovereignty rarely removed poverty by itself, but it gave many communities tools for funding schools, health care, language programs, legal claims, and cultural centres.

Legal struggles continued around sovereignty, treaty interpretation, jurisdiction, water, fishing, hunting, child welfare, religious freedom, and repatriation. Courts, Congress, Canadian governments, tribal councils, and international forums all became arenas where Native peoples argued that treaties and Indigenous nationhood were living political realities, not historical relics.

Reform did not move in a straight line. The Indian Reorganization Act of 1934 encouraged written constitutions and ended allotment, but later termination policies tried to dissolve federal relationships with selected tribes. Canadian policy likewise shifted between suppressing status and restoring limited rights. Modern self-determination grew from resisting these reversals as much as from new legislation.

The larger story is one of survivance: Native communities endured conquest, epidemic disease, dispossession, removal, allotment, assimilation policy, and cultural suppression, yet continued to create new political, artistic, religious, and intellectual forms. Their histories remain regional and national, ancient and modern, local and global at the same time.

Contemporary Indigenous citizenship is therefore layered. A person may belong to a tribal nation, a local community, an urban Native network, a Canadian or U.S. legal category, and a wider Indigenous rights movement. These overlapping identities help explain why land, language, ancestry, residence, enrollment, ceremony, and political recognition can all matter at once.

## Key Events & Developments

- 28,000-10,000 BC: Beringia connected Asia and North America.
- At least 13,000 years ago: Humans were present in the Americas.
- 1492: European colonization began reshaping Native American history.
- 1890s: Cyrus Thomas demonstrated that Native Americans built major mounds.
- 1978: The American Indian Religious Freedom Act was passed.
- 2007: The United Nations adopted the Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples.

## Key People

- Cyrus Thomas ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cyrus_Thomas)): Archaeologist who refuted racist Mound Builder theories.
- George McJunkin ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_McJunkin)): Reported the Folsom bison remains that helped prove Paleo-Indian antiquity.
- Edward Sapir ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edward_Sapir)): Linguist who emphasized California's extraordinary language diversity.
- James Mooney ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Mooney)): Anthropologist who investigated Native population estimates.
- George Catlin ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Catlin)): Artist who documented Plains ceremonies and life.

## Interesting Facts

- Cahokia included Monks Mound, the largest human-made earthen structure in North America.
- Plains sign language allowed communication across many spoken-language groups.
- Potlatch ceremonies distributed wealth and confirmed status on the Northwest Coast.
- Acorn flour became a reliable California food because people learned to leach toxins from acorns.
