## Introduction

Propaganda ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Propaganda)) is the dissemination of information, arguments, rumours, half-truths, or lies to influence public opinion. It is a more or less systematic effort to manipulate beliefs, attitudes, or actions through symbols such as words, gestures, banners, monuments, music, clothing, insignia, hairstyles, coin designs, and postage stamps.

Deliberateness and manipulation distinguish propaganda from casual conversation or free exchange of ideas. The propagandist has a goal and selects facts, arguments, and symbols to achieve the greatest effect. He may omit relevant facts, distort them, or divert attention from information that weakens his message.

Propaganda differs from [[Education|education]], though the boundary is often disputed. An educator tries to present multiple sides of an issue and helps people learn to evaluate evidence. A propagandist may see himself as an educator and believe he is spreading truth, while a receptive audience may also regard propaganda as education.

## Related Terms

The term agitprop comes from communist practice and combines agitation and propaganda. In Leninist usage, propaganda could mean systematic instruction in doctrine, while agitation meant short, urgent slogans and arguments directed at mass action. Communist parties developed agitprop sections for this work.

Propaganda of the deed ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Propaganda_of_the_deed)) means performing an action partly for its symbolic effect rather than its direct practical result. Examples include staging an atomic test, publicly punishing a criminal, or giving foreign aid mainly to influence opinion rather than to build the recipient's economy.

Overt propaganda reveals its source, while covert propaganda hides or disguises it. Covert methods include unsigned political advertising, clandestine radio, bribed editors, or statements secretly backed by governments, parties, or businesses.

Psychological warfare, or psychwar, is propaganda used before or during war to confuse, demoralize, or deceive enemies and to support one's own side. Diplomatic negotiation, legal argument, collective bargaining, commercial advertising, and campaigns may all mix overt and covert propaganda with symbolic actions.

## Planning a Propaganda Campaign

A rational propagandist must answer many questions. What changes are desired, in whom, and by when? What are present and expected world conditions? What are conditions in nations, regions, interest groups, and other subsystems? Who should distribute the message?

Other questions concern symbols, media, target reactors, measurement of effects, countermeasures by opponents, and ways to measure and answer those countermeasures. Social science can answer such questions only with moderate confidence for major campaigns, even with substantial research money.

Planning is difficult because propaganda acts inside a changing global system. Population, trade, travel, education, technology, and political power shift constantly. A message may produce unintended effects far from its original target and may even boomerang against the propagandist.

## Goals

Goals are simple when a propagandist wants to sell a safe, useful product or service. They become complex when the goal is to convert large numbers of people to a religion, ideology, social order, war, or revolution.

Large campaigns require stages, phases, and intermediate goals. A propagandist may struggle to specify exactly what beliefs, values, or actions he wants in different groups and at different times. Value conflicts arise over victory, suffering, liberty, equality, economic ruin, war crimes, and national security.

Such campaigns therefore pose value dilemmas. Is military or revolutionary success worth its cost? Is speed worth suffering? Can liberty be increased without losing equality? What risks are acceptable, for what goals, and over what time periods?

## Political Propaganda and Image-Making

Modern political propaganda often uses polling, data processing, imagery, and money. Polls discover what voters already believe, data analysis interprets attitudes, image-makers shape a candidate to fit those attitudes, and mass advertising sells the image through media such as television.

A 1970 political statement argued that the candidate can become less important than management, image, and advertising. This is likely overstated, but it captures the flavour of modern campaign propaganda.

A manufactured candidate image can still be vulnerable to a real leader with political skill, genuine concern, spontaneous voice and gesture, and many face-to-face appearances. Leaders such as Franklin D. Roosevelt and Jawaharlal Nehru illustrate the power of authentic political performance against purely managed imagery.

## Symbols and Predispositions

Symbols alone do not determine behaviour. A reactor's response is shaped by predispositions, memories, prior associations, economic inducements, physical inducements, and social pressures. People may ignore messages, perceive them selectively, or rationalize them away.

Economic inducements include gifts, bribes, raises, and threats of job loss. Physical inducements include love, violence, and protection from violence. Social pressures from groups can encourage or block action even when a person is predisposed to agree with the propagandist.

A skilled propagandist selects symbols that match existing predispositions and pressures. Leaders and role models can become especially powerful symbols because they concentrate emotion, authority, trust, and identity.

## Leaders and Charisma

Charismatic leaders can magnify propaganda. They may function like parental, heroic, or sacred figures. Examples of symbolic figures include Uncle Sam, Uncle Ho Chi Minh, la belle France, Britannia, La Pasionaria, Kuan-yin, Karl Marx, Sun Yat-sen, Abraham Lincoln, Winston Churchill, Mao Tse-tung, Stalin, Joan of Arc, Mahatma Gandhi, Martin Luther King, and the Buddha.

Such figures work because reactors often respond to symbols through affection, admiration, fear, loyalty, or reverence. A well-symbolized leader or role model can give a message parental or even godlike force.

## Media of Propaganda

![[attachments/propaganda_001.webp|420]]

*Historical propaganda poster using a soldier figure and slogan text*

There are thousands of propaganda media. They include written, audiovisual, and organizational channels: family, schools, advertising firms, public relations agencies, trade unions, churches, temples, theatres, novels, poetry, special-interest groups, parties, front organizations, governments, coalitions, and the United Nations.

The propagandist must choose media to which the target audience is attentive and receptive. Leaders, role models, and organizations may be more important than channels alone because trust and identification often shape how messages are received.

The communications revolution created a massive proliferation of schools, news systems, publishing, broadcasting, meetings, and speeches. Modern individuals are bombarded by more symbols and messages than they can process, making attention itself a scarce resource.

## Key Events & Developments

- 1918: Vladimir Ilich Lenin was associated with communist agitprop practice.
- 1970: Zolton Ferency described modern political campaigns as driven by polls, data, imagery, and money.
- 20th century: Psychological warfare and mass communications became central propaganda tools.
- modern era: Television, polling, data processing, and public relations reshaped political persuasion.
