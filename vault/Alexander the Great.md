# Alexander the Great

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> Alexander the Great was the king of Macedonia whose campaigns destroyed the Persian Empire and created the political setting for the Hellenistic world. His conquests reshaped warfare, administration, trade, urban foundations, and cultural exchange from Greece to India.

## Introduction

Alexander the Great, also known as Alexander III or Alexander of Macedonia, died on June 13, 323 BCE, in Babylon. He was king of Macedonia from 336 to 323 BCE. During that short reign he overthrew the Persian empire, carried Macedonian armies as far as [[India]], and laid the foundations for the Hellenistic world ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hellenistic_period)) of territorial kingdoms. Already in his own lifetime he became the subject of extraordinary stories, and later tradition turned him into a hero only loosely connected to the historical man.

## Life

![[attachments/alexander_the_great_001.webp|420]]

*Alexander the Great in the Alexander Mosaic*

Alexander was born in 356 BCE at Pella in Macedonia, the son of Philip II and Olympias, daughter of King Neoptolemus of Epirus. From the ages of 13 to 16 he was taught by [[Aristotle and Aristotelianism|Aristotle]], who gave him an interest in philosophy, medicine, and scientific inquiry, though Alexander later moved beyond [[Aristotle and Aristotelianism|Aristotle's]] narrower view that non-Greeks should be treated as slaves. Even in youth, Alexander was involved in rule and war. In 340 BCE, while Philip attacked Byzantium, Alexander governed Macedonia and defeated the Maedi, a Thracian people. Two years later, at the Battle of Chaeronea ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Chaeronea_%28338_BC%29)), he commanded the left wing and showed striking courage against the Sacred Band of Thebes ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sacred_Band_of_Thebes)).

His succession was not entirely secure. After Philip divorced Olympias, Alexander quarreled with his father and temporarily fled, first with his mother to Epirus and then himself to Illyria. Father and son were later reconciled, but Alexander's position remained uncertain until Philip's assassination in 336 BCE. The army acclaimed Alexander king without opposition. He acted immediately and ruthlessly, executing rivals and suppressing factions opposed to him. He then marched south, secured Thessaly, and at Corinth was appointed generalissimo of the Greek League for the invasion of [[Asia]] already planned by his father.

## Securing Greece and Macedonia

After consulting Delphi, where the priestess reportedly called him invincible, Alexander turned first to securing his rear. In the spring of 335 BCE he advanced into Thrace, forced the Shipka Pass, crushed the Triballi, crossed the Danube to disperse the Getae, and then turned west to defeat a coalition of invading Illyrians. While he was away, rumours of his death sparked revolt in Thebes, and other Greek states, especially [[Athens]] under the influence of Demosthenes, showed sympathy. Alexander reacted with astonishing speed. In only fourteen days he marched south, took Thebes, and destroyed the city. That destruction was meant as a warning and secured his authority over [[Greece]] before the Asian campaign began.

## The Persian Expedition Begins

From the time of his accession, Alexander had fixed his mind on the invasion of the Persian empire. He needed Persian wealth to support the army inherited from Philip and to repay heavy debts. Greek military experience had already shown that Persian territory was vulnerable if struck by a disciplined army with strong cavalry. In the spring of 334 BCE, Alexander crossed the Dardanelles, leaving Antipater in [[Europe]] with more than 13,000 men. Alexander himself led about 30,000 infantry and more than 5,000 cavalry. The army was notable for its balanced combination of forces: archers, javelin men, cavalry, the phalanx, and the elite hypaspists. Parmenio, his second in command, was an experienced officer already established in Asia Minor.

After a symbolic visit to Ilium inspired by [[Homer]], Alexander faced his first Persian army at the Granicus River near the Sea of Marmara. The Persian plan almost succeeded, because crossing the river under attack was dangerous, but the Persian line eventually broke and Alexander won a major victory. This was more than a battlefield success. It opened Asia Minor to him and showed both allies and enemies that his campaign would be aggressive, rapid, and personally led.

## Expansion of the Campaign

Although not every stage of the campaign is recorded in full detail, Alexander's campaign clearly developed into an enormous enterprise rather than a limited punitive expedition. He appears to have imagined from the beginning an operation without fixed limits. His army was accompanied not only by soldiers but also by surveyors, engineers, architects, scientists, court officials, and historians. This detail suggests that conquest, administration, observation, and exploration were all part of the broader project.

The campaign continued with repeated victories, the destruction of Persian resistance, and movement farther east than any previous Macedonian force had gone. Alexander's achievement was not simply tactical. He kept the army functioning across immense distances, through unfamiliar landscapes, and against an empire far larger than Macedonia. His ability to combine cavalry strike power, infantry discipline, and relentless operational speed was central to his success.

## Evaluation

Alexander's long-term plans are hard to judge because reliable evidence is limited. Later reports about grand schemes to conquer the western Mediterranean and establish a universal monarchy may come from forgery or later invention. Even if some such plans were genuine, they were immediately dropped by his successors after his death. What seems more certain is that had he lived longer, he would likely have completed the subjugation of remaining independent regions in Asia Minor, such as Paphlagonia, Cappadocia, and Armenia. In his final years, however, his attention appears to have turned increasingly toward [[Exploration|exploration]], especially of [[Arabia]] and the Caspian region.

In administration, Alexander often improvised and adapted existing systems rather than replacing everything completely. One clear exception was finance. Though the details are incomplete, he appears to have established a central financial organization, perhaps with collectors partly independent from local satraps. This system later proved imperfect, partly because of the character and failures of his treasurer Harpalus. Yet Alexander did successfully introduce a new coinage based on the Athenian silver standard, replacing the older bimetallic systems of both Macedonia and Persia. Combined with the release of large quantities of bullion from Persian treasuries, this monetary reform stimulated trade and benefited the wider economy of the Mediterranean world.

## Cities and Hellenistic Expansion

One of Alexander's most important long-term achievements was the founding of many new cities. Plutarch speaks of more than 70. Some of these settlements may not have retained their first colonists, and intermarriage with local women could weaken purely Greek ways. Even so, Greek influence remained strong in many of these foundations. Alexander's successors, especially the Seleucids, continued the process. As a result, Greek language, thought, institutions, and customs spread across much of [[Asia]], reaching as far as Bactria and [[India]]. This spread of Hellenic culture was one of the most lasting effects of Alexander's conquests.

## Limits of His Imperial Vision

Some of Alexander's broader ambitions were less successful. His plans for racial fusion failed. Although he may have hoped to unite Macedonians and Iranians into a new imperial elite, the Macedonians themselves resisted this idea strongly. They rejected equal partnership with conquered peoples, and later Hellenistic states did not realize such a vision in the form Alexander may have imagined. The Iranian satraps often proved ineffective, and many were removed or executed, though the justice of such actions can no longer be clearly determined.

## Historical Significance
Alexander is one of history's most consequential conquerors. Militarily, he destroyed the Persian empire and demonstrated the reach of a disciplined, well-led Macedonian army. Politically, he left behind the framework of the Hellenistic kingdoms. Economically, his coinage reforms and release of treasure energized exchange across the eastern Mediterranean and much of western [[Asia]]. Culturally, his city foundations and the movements of soldiers, settlers, and administrators spread Greek influence deep into non-Greek lands.

At the same time, he was not a flawless architect of empire. Much of his administration was improvised, some social aims failed, and his plans remain partly obscure. Yet his impact was extraordinary. During only thirteen years as king, he transformed the political map from [[Greece]] to the edge of [[India]] and created conditions for centuries of cultural exchange.

## Legacy and Legend

Already during his lifetime, Alexander became the subject of fabulous stories, and after his death those stories grew into a full-scale legend. The legendary Alexander bore only partial resemblance to the historical king, but the growth of that legend itself is part of his importance. He was remembered not only as a conqueror but as an ideal of heroic ambition, courage, and world-changing power. He is therefore remembered through a double image: Alexander as a real ruler whose military, political, and economic acts reshaped a vast world, and Alexander as a legendary figure whose fame expanded even beyond his conquests.

## Key Events & Developments

- 356 BCE: Alexander is born at Pella.
- 336 BCE: He succeeds Philip II as king of Macedonia.

- 334 BCE: He crosses the Dardanelles and begins the Persian campaign.
- 323 BCE: He dies in Babylon before fully completing his eastern ambitions.
