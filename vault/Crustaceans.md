# Crustaceans

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> Crustaceans are chiefly aquatic arthropods, including crabs, lobsters, shrimp, and barnacles, recognized by segmented bodies, jointed appendages, and hard exoskeletons. They are ecologically and economically important in aquatic food webs.

## Introduction

Crustaceans ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crustacean)) are arthropod invertebrates in the subphylum Crustacea, with about 45,000 known species worldwide. Familiar examples include crabs, lobsters, shrimps, barnacles, copepods, water fleas, and wood lice. Most are aquatic, but some live on land or in damp habitats.

Crustaceans usually differ from other [[Arthropods|arthropods]] by having two pairs of sensory appendages before the mouth and paired mouth appendages that act as jaws. The group is extremely diverse, so no simple definition fits all species; parasitic forms may lose many adult features and be recognized as crustaceans mainly from their life histories.

## General features

Crustacean diversity ranges from microscopic plankton to large lobsters and spider crabs. The American lobster can reach 20 kilograms, while the giant Japanese spider crab has legs spanning up to 3.7 metres. At the other extreme, some water fleas are less than 0.25 millimetre long, and many copepods are under one millimetre.

Most crustaceans live in water. They occur in freshwater, seawater, inland brines, lakes, rivers, open oceans, sea bottoms, sand grains, seaweed, wooden structures, and deep ocean trenches. Some Arctic species complete development quickly during short summers, while deep-sea isopods and amphipods can live at depths near 10,000 metres.

## Ecology

![[attachments/crustaceans_003.webp|420]]

*Atyidae sp 5*

Crustaceans ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crustacean)) play many ecological roles. Planktonic copepods and krill graze on microscopic marine plants and become food for fishes, seabirds, and whales. Benthic crustaceans feed fishes and some whales, and crabs act as major predators.

The struggle between crabs and their prey has shaped evolution. Many marine mollusks developed heavy or ornamented shells, while crabs developed stronger pincers. Crustaceans can also be parasites: copepods parasitize animals from whales to sea anemones, and parasitic isopods may live in the gill chambers of prawns. Some freshwater crustaceans serve as intermediate hosts for the lung fluke Paragonimus.

## Form and function of external features

The basic crustacean body is made of segments, or somites. Some somites fuse into rigid regions, while others remain flexible. Each somite can potentially bear paired jointed appendages, though appendages are reduced, lost, or specialized in many groups.

At the front is the acron, an unsegmented region that fuses with several somites to form the head in most crustaceans. At the rear is the telson, which may bear rami forming a furca. The head, thorax, and abdomen vary greatly, and a carapace may cover part or all of the body.

## Appendages

Appendages are one of the main keys to crustacean classification. Many are derived from biramous or multiramous limbs. A typical biramous limb has a basal protopodite with two branches: an inner endopodite and an outer exopodite.

From front to back, crustaceans generally have antennules, antennae, mandibles, maxillulae, maxillae, and trunk limbs. Trunk limbs may be similar, or they may specialize as feeding limbs, swimming legs, walking legs, pincers, reproductive structures, or abdominal pleopods. Decapods have three pairs of maxillipeds and five pairs of thoracic limbs, some of which may bear chelae.

## Exoskeleton and molting

The exoskeleton, also called the cuticle or integument, protects the body and gives muscles attachment points. It may be thin and flexible in some parasitic copepods or thick and rigid in crabs. The procuticle contains chitin, proteins, and often calcium salts.

Growth requires molting. In proecdysis, calcium is withdrawn from the old cuticle and a new one begins forming. During ecdysis, the old exoskeleton splits and is shed. In metecdysis, the new soft cuticle hardens and calcifies. Intermolt is the period between molts, and some crustaceans eventually enter terminal anecdysis, when molting stops.

## Hormones

Crustacean hormones control molting, reproduction, water balance, blood glucose, heart function, and pigment movement. Much knowledge comes from decapods, isopods, and amphipods.

The X-organ-sinus-gland complex in the eyestalk releases hormones into the blood, including molt-inhibiting signals. The Y-organs produce ecdysteroids that promote molting. Brain and thoracic centres help regulate sex-organ development, and some male reproductive glands influence whether young males develop male or female traits.

## Evolution and paleontology

Crustacean evolution is studied through comparative anatomy and the fossil record. The group has ancient roots, with some fossil classes known from the Cambrian and later periods.

Fossils preserve carapaces, shells, appendages, and body forms, though soft-bodied details are often missing. Because many living and fossil groups differ greatly, classification has changed as new anatomical and paleontological evidence has appeared.

## Classification

![[attachments/crustaceans_001.webp|420]]

*Crustacea diversity*

Classification ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taxonomy)) of Crustacea uses many traits: carapace form, trunk segmentation, fusion of somites, appendage number and specialization, eyes, caudal furca, respiratory organs, genital openings, egg attachment, and larval stage.

The subphylum Crustacea includes several major classes. Cephalocarida are small primitive horseshoe shrimps. Branchiopoda include many freshwater leaf-limbed forms. Remipedia are elongated marine cave dwellers. Maxillopoda includes barnacles, copepods, fish lice, and related forms. Ostracods are seed shrimps with bivalved carapaces. Malacostraca includes many large and familiar forms such as crabs, lobsters, shrimps, amphipods, isopods, and mantis shrimps.

Within Maxillopoda, barnacles show how crustaceans can become sedentary as adults while retaining free-swimming larvae. Copepods are extremely abundant, often planktonic, and include free-living, parasitic, and semiterrestrial forms. Fish lice are ectoparasites, while rhizocephalans parasitize other crustaceans and become highly modified adults.

Malacostraca is one of the most important classes, with about 22,000 species. It normally has compound eyes, eight thoracic segments, and six abdominal segments. It includes peracarids with brood pouches, stomatopods with powerful grasping limbs, and decapods with familiar crabs, lobsters, prawns, and shrimps.

## Key Events & Developments

- Cambrian: Early fossil crustacean groups such as ostracod-related forms appeared.
- Devonian to Carboniferous: Several fossil groups show early diversification of crustacean body plans.
- Modern oceans: Planktonic copepods and krill became central links between microscopic plants and larger animals.
- Modern taxonomy: Classification changed as larval history, appendages, fossils, and parasitic adaptations were compared.

## Interesting Facts

- Crustaceans ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crustacean)) include both giant spider crabs and microscopic water fleas.
- Krill and copepods are vital food for fishes, seabirds, and whales.
- Barnacles are crustaceans even though adults look unlike crabs or shrimps.
- Molting is necessary because the hard exoskeleton cannot grow continuously.
- Parasitic crustaceans can be so modified as adults that their larvae reveal their identity.
