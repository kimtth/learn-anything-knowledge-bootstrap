# War

The source stresses that no single theory explains war and that modern destructive capacity makes its study urgent.

## Introduction

War ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/War)) is commonly understood as a conflict among political groups involving hostilities of considerable duration and magnitude. Social scientists often add that war is initiated and conducted according to socially recognized forms, making it an institution recognized in custom or law. Military writers usually reserve the term for hostilities between groups sufficiently equal in power that the outcome is uncertain for a time.

War has always been a major subject of analysis, but the 20th century, after two world wars and under the threat of nuclear, biological, and chemical destruction, made the study of war especially urgent. Theories of war matter because they shape expectations, political behaviour, and even self-fulfilling prophecies.

No single approach explains war fully. Philosophical, political, economic, technological, legal, sociological, psychological, and biological explanations each illuminate part of the phenomenon. Most serious theories combine several factors because war is a complex social institution.

Theories of war have changed with the international system. After the religious wars of the 17th century, European wars were often limited conflicts fought for sovereign interests. Strategy and maneuver dominated analysis. The French Revolution changed this pattern by creating large conscript armies and wars fought for mass political ideals.

After the Napoleonic Wars, the mainstream view returned to war as a rational, limited instrument of national policy. Carl von Clausewitz gave the classic statement in On War ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/On_War)), defining war as violence used to compel an opponent to fulfill one's will. This view treated war as an instrument of foreign policy.

[[World Wars (World War I)|World War I]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_War_I)) challenged that model because it mobilized entire populations and economies. Later theories treated modern total war as something that should occur only when a state's survival was at stake, or as an ideological catastrophe no longer rationally controllable. [[World Wars (World War II)|World War II]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_War_II)) and nuclear weapons deepened the urgency of understanding war as both a possible instrument of policy and a threat to humanity's survival.

Modern definitions usually describe war as armed conflict between political units on a large scale. They include civil wars but exclude smaller phenomena such as banditry, piracy, and many insurrections.

## Biological and Psychological Explanations

Some theories locate war in innate biological or psychological drives. Ethology compares human conflict with animal aggression, territoriality, rivalry, intrusion by strangers, and frustration. Such comparisons can suggest hypotheses, but they are limited because human behaviour changes rapidly with culture, institutions, geography, and historical environment.

Animal studies of overcrowding and territorial defense are suggestive but uncertain when applied to humans. An animal territory is not the same as a territorial state, and symbolic animal aggression is not equivalent to organized military violence.

Psychological theories focus on human nature, maladjustment, fear, insecurity, false images of foreign peoples and leaders, public opinion, and decision makers. Some theorists emphasize innate aggressiveness; others stress education, social adjustment, and better leadership selection. These approaches matter, but they cannot ignore the institutions that give people opportunities and incentives to act peacefully or violently.

## Social Theories and Nationalism

![[attachments/war_003.webp|420]]

*Damage in Gaza Strip during the October 2023 - 29*

Social theories explain war through states, classes, institutions, and international relations. Liberal theories traditionally argued that free trade, public opinion, representative government, and rational dispute settlement would reduce war. Classical liberals often blamed autocratic governments for wars and hoped democracy and commerce would make conflict irrational.

After [[World Wars (World War I)|World War I]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_War_I)), many liberals accepted that unregulated international society did not automatically produce peace. They increasingly supported international organization as a corrective to state rivalry.

Socialist theories, especially those influenced by Karl Marx, traced war to class structure and capitalism's search for raw materials, markets, and cheap labour. [[Lenin]] explained capitalist wars as products of imperial competition and argued that a revolutionary vanguard was needed because proletarian international solidarity had failed in 1914. Soviet theory later distinguished wars among capitalist states, wars between capitalist and socialist states, and colonial liberation wars, but events such as conflicts within the socialist camp exposed weaknesses in that framework.

Nationalism ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nationalism)) is another powerful explanation. The doctrine of national self-determination links nations to states and can legitimize both the creation and breakup of states. Because no state perfectly contains all members of one nation and no nation-state perfectly excludes minorities, nationalism creates recurring tensions over borders, assimilation, secession, and reunification. It also makes compromise and defeat harder to accept.

## Interest Groups and the State

Some theories focus on groups inside states rather than treating the state as a single actor. Many liberal and socialist accounts assume that ordinary people prefer peace while governments, elites, or special interests pull them into war.

The military is the most obvious interest group. In some societies, military glory and the spoils of victory encouraged war, and professional militaries can seek resources, status, and political influence. Yet officers may also be cautious because they understand war's risks more directly than civilians. Heavy military preparedness can indirectly increase tensions even when military leaders do not actively seek war.

Economic groups connected to arms, finance, and war industries also appear in theories of war. The idea of a military-industrial complex recognizes that parts of an economy can benefit from armaments. Still, it is difficult to prove that such groups alone decide questions of war and peace.

Modern science and technology form another special interest. Nuclear scientists, space researchers, chemists, engineers, biologists, and geneticists may receive resources through defense work, but many also oppose war because modern science makes mass destruction possible.

## Diplomacy and International Control

Many theorists see the international environment as the main cause of war. States seek survival and interests without a reliable world authority able to control all others. In that setting, fear, suspicion, arms races, trade conflicts, and competitive behaviour can escalate. Much theory therefore seeks restraints that make escalation less likely.

[[Diplomacy]] offers cooperative alternatives to dangerous competition. Better information, reduced misperception, recognition of costs, and bargaining can help states avoid war. The balance of power in 1815-1914 drew interest because it appeared to restrain great-power war through equilibrium, moderation, and shifting alliances.

[[Game Theory|Game theory]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Game_theory)) added tools for analyzing conflict. In zero-sum situations, one side's gain is another's loss, but rational bargaining may still avoid war. In non-zero-sum situations, cooperation can enlarge the total benefit. Diplomacy can sometimes transform conflicts by adding gains from cooperation and reducing the costs of preparing for war.

Regional integration and security communities also reduce war among members. The European Union and North Atlantic Treaty Organization are examples of structures that limit conflict within a region. Yet regional success may shift conflict to interregional levels, so the problem of war remains universal.

## International Law, the United Nations, and Disarmament

[[International Law|International law]] assumes an international society of states that accepts norms of behaviour. It differs from domestic law because no sovereign stands above states to enforce it. International law concerns both the legality of war and the regulation of war. In the 20th century, treaties such as the Covenant of the League of Nations, the Kellogg-Briand Pact of 1928, and the Charter of the United Nations expressed a consensus that armed force is illegal except in cases such as self-defense. But legal norms are weak without enforcement and political agreement among major powers.

The [[United Nations]] is charged with maintaining international peace and security. Its Charter combines three approaches: peaceful settlement of disputes, collective security against aggression, and disarmament. Peaceful settlement can slow disputes and give reason a chance, but states resist outside decisions on vital interests. Collective security has failed because states have not agreed reliably on aggression or on acting against aggressors regardless of identity.

Disarmament and arms limitation rest on the idea that arms races lead to war. The theory is partly flawed because arms races often result from political tensions rather than causing them by themselves. Reducing armaments does not necessarily reduce the tensions that made states arm in the first place, and disturbing a strategic balance can itself increase danger.

Because its original approaches have had limited success, the United Nations developed practices of preventive diplomacy, peacekeeping, and collective legitimization through the General Assembly. These tools can contain local conflicts, discourage escalation, and make war more politically costly. Some thinkers conclude that only world government could abolish war completely; others see that as utopian. Both sides agree that war's destructive potential requires continued study, restraint, prevention, and mitigation.

## Key Events & Developments

- 17th century: After religious wars, European conflicts became more limited and dynastic.
- 1789: The French Revolution began a shift toward mass conscript armies and ideological war.
- 1815-1914: The balance-of-power era in Europe inspired theories of diplomatic restraint.
- 1832-37: Carl von Clausewitz's On War ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/On_War)) was published posthumously.
- 1914: [[World Wars (World War I)|World War I]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_War_I)) exposed the limits of theories treating war as rational and limited.
- 1928: The Kellogg-Briand Pact renounced war as an instrument of national policy.
- 1945: The [[United Nations]] was founded with peace, security, and collective action as central aims.
- 1956 and 1968: Soviet interventions in [[Hungary]] and Czechoslovakia exposed tensions inside the socialist camp.
- 1979-89: The Afghan mujahideen fought the Soviet Union, challenging simple socialist theories of liberation war.

## Key People

- Carl von Clausewitz ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carl_von_Clausewitz)): Prussian theorist who defined war as an instrument of policy.
- Leo Tolstoy ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leo_Tolstoy)): Writer whose War and Peace treated war as calamity and social disaster.
- [[John Stuart Mill]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Stuart_Mill)): Liberal thinker associated with individual freedom and representative government.
- Thomas Paine ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Paine)): Early republican liberal who stressed public opinion and peace.
- Karl Marx ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karl_Marx)): Socialist theorist who linked war to class structure and capitalism.
- [[Lenin]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vladimir_Lenin)): Revolutionary who tied war to imperialism and capitalist rivalry.
- Joseph Frankel ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph_Frankel)): Scholar associated with analysis of war and international politics.

## Interesting Facts

- Not every armed conflict is usually called a war; scale, duration, and political status matter.
- Some theories treat war as rational policy, while others treat it as social catastrophe.
- National self-determination can both legitimize states and generate wars over borders and minorities.
- Arms races may be symptoms of political conflict rather than its root cause.
- Collective security requires states to act against aggressors even when doing so is inconvenient.
- Peacekeeping and preventive diplomacy grew because the United Nations' original enforcement mechanisms proved difficult to use.
