## Introduction

Eastern Orthodoxy ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eastern_Orthodox_Church)), officially the Orthodox Catholic Church, is one of the three major doctrinal and jurisdictional traditions of Christianity. It is marked by continuity with the apostolic church, ancient liturgy, territorial churches, and the faith defined by the first seven ecumenical councils.

Most Orthodox Christians have lived in the Balkans, the Middle East, and former Soviet lands, though modern diaspora communities exist in western Europe, the Americas, and elsewhere. The terms Eastern Orthodox and Greek Orthodox can be misleading because the tradition includes Russian, Slavic, Arab, Georgian, Romanian, Serbian, Bulgarian, Greek, and other churches.

## Nature and significance

The word orthodox means right-believing and was used in the Greek-speaking Christian world for communities that preserved the true faith against heresies. Eastern Orthodoxy is distinct from the Oriental Orthodox churches, such as the Armenian, Coptic, Syriac, Ethiopian, Eritrean, and Malankara churches, which separated after disputes around the Council of Chalcedon in 451.

The Schism of 1054 between East and West was the result of centuries of cultural, political, linguistic, and theological estrangement. Rome and Constantinople developed different ideas of church authority. The West emphasized Roman primacy, while the East emphasized the authority of ecumenical councils and the importance of major local churches.

## Organization and autocephalous churches

Eastern Orthodoxy ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eastern_Orthodox_Church)) is organized as a communion of self-governing, or autocephalous, churches. These include ancient patriarchates such as Constantinople, Alexandria, Antioch, and Jerusalem, and national churches such as Russia, Georgia, Serbia, Romania, Bulgaria, Cyprus, Greece, Albania, Poland, the Czech and Slovak republics, and America.

The ecumenical patriarch of Constantinople has primacy of honour, not universal jurisdiction like the pope in Roman Catholicism. This primacy is symbolic and cooperative, and Constantinople has often convened pan-Orthodox meetings. The territorial principle is the normal basis of organization, though modern diaspora communities have often produced overlapping ethnic jurisdictions.

## History

At the beginning of the 2nd millennium, the church of Constantinople stood at the height of influence within the Byzantine Empire. Byzantine emperors and patriarchs worked within an ideal of one Christian society, or oikoumene, led by empire and church together. The relationship is sometimes called caesaropapism, but Byzantine texts more often imagined emperor and patriarch as a dyarchy, like body and soul.

Byzantine monasticism was a major spiritual force. Mount Athos, protected by Nicephorus II Phocas and centred on the laura of St. Athanasius the Athonite, became a lasting monastic centre. St. Symeon the New Theologian shaped Orthodox mysticism. Byzantine missions also spread Orthodoxy to Bulgaria, Serbia, Russia, and the Caucasus.

Relations with the Latin West worsened over authority, theology, language, and politics. The Crusades deepened the rupture, especially after the sack of Constantinople in 1204 and the installation of a Latin patriarch in Hagia Sophia. Orthodox authority survived in exile at Nicaea, and new Slavic churches developed autocephalous or autonomous structures.

The Mongol invasion devastated Kievan Rus, but the Russian church survived as a unified social and spiritual institution. The metropolitan of Kiev and all Russia gained moral authority under Mongol rule. Later, Moscow claimed increasing independence, and in 1448 the Russian church became effectively autocephalous by electing its own metropolitan.

Under Ottoman rule, the patriarchate of Constantinople became the official institution for Orthodox Christians under the sultan. In the 19th century, Greek, Serbian, Romanian, and Bulgarian national movements created or restored autocephalous churches. The Bulgarian case caused a schism when phyletism, or organizing the church by ethnicity on the same territory, was condemned in 1872; reconciliation came in 1945.

After [[World Wars (World War I)|World War I]], Orthodoxy changed dramatically through the loss of Greek Asia Minor, Balkan realignments, the Russian Revolution of 1917, Soviet persecution, and Orthodox migration westward. Despite political violence and diaspora fragmentation, the church preserved unity in faith, worship, tradition, and sacramental life.

The ecumenical patriarchate was greatly reduced after the Greco-Turkish War and population transfers of 1922, leaving only a small Greek population in Istanbul and nearby areas by the early 21st century. It retained honorary primacy and jurisdiction over some diaspora dioceses and Greek islands.

Ancient sees such as [[Alexandria]], Antioch, and [[Jerusalem]] remained important. Alexandria became connected with emerging African communities; Antioch became the largest Arab Christian Orthodox body in Syria, Lebanon, and Iraq; and Jerusalem remained guardian of many Christian holy places.

The churches of Cyprus and Georgia have distinctive histories. Cyprus was autocephalous from 431 and survived Arab, Crusader, Venetian, Ottoman, and British rule. Georgia received autocephaly from Antioch in the 6th century, lost it after Russian annexation in 1801, and restored it in 1917.

In North America, Orthodoxy began through Russian missions in Alaska. Monks reached Kodiak Island in 1794, and St. Herman became famous as an ascetic and defender of Indigenous people. Later immigration created Greek, Russian, Serbian, Arab, Romanian, and other Orthodox jurisdictions.

## Doctrine

Orthodox doctrine claims continuity with the apostolic faith expressed in Scripture, liturgy, the Fathers, saints, and the seven ecumenical councils: Nicaea I, Constantinople I, Ephesus, Chalcedon, Constantinople II, Constantinople III, and Nicaea II. Later councils, such as those supporting Gregory Palamas in the 14th century, are also viewed as faithful expressions of tradition.

Orthodoxy treats truth as a living experience in the communion of the church, not only as external propositions. Councils, creeds, and theology are normal expressions of truth, but they must be received by the whole church. Definitions are often used to exclude error rather than to exhaust the mystery of faith.

## God, Christ, and the Holy Spirit

[[Jesus - The Christ and Christology (Christology)|Christology]] follows the councils of the first eight centuries. Orthodoxy rejects Arianism, Nestorianism, and monophysitism and confesses Christ as one person in two natures. The title Theotokos for the Virgin Mary protects the belief that the one born of her is truly God the Word incarnate.

The Holy Spirit is central to Orthodox life. Christians receive the Spirit through baptism and chrismation, and worship invokes the Spirit in prayer and sacraments. The eucharistic epiklesis asks the Spirit to descend on the congregation and the gifts.

The Trinity is understood in the East with strong emphasis on the Father, Son, and Holy Spirit as distinct divine persons sharing one essence. The Filioque dispute arose because the Western church added “and from the Son” to the creed’s statement about the Spirit’s procession. Orthodox theologians objected both to the unilateral change and to the theology it implied.

Orthodox apophatic theology stresses that God’s essence is unknowable and transcendent. God can be known through divine energies, grace, worship, and communion, but not grasped as an object. This view shaped Orthodox mysticism and later theology.

## The structure of the church

Church structure is based on communion, bishops, synods, territorial churches, and canonical tradition. Each autocephalous church governs itself through its bishops, and the head may be called patriarch, archbishop, or metropolitan.

Orthodoxy has often resisted clericalism. Lay participation in administration and sometimes episcopal elections survived or was restored in some places. Priests and deacons may be married if married before ordination, but bishops are chosen from the celibate clergy, usually monastics.

Monasticism ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monasticism)) began in the 3rd and 4th centuries and remained central to Orthodox spirituality. Solitary and communal forms both developed, especially in Egypt, Byzantium, Mount Athos, and Slavic lands. Hesychasm, the Jesus Prayer, and elders or startsy deeply influenced Orthodox spirituality and writers such as Fyodor Dostoyevsky, Nikolay Gogol, and Leo Tolstoy.

## Sacraments and worship

Orthodox worship is liturgical, sacramental, and strongly shaped by continuity with ancient practice. Baptism and chrismation initiate a person into the church, and the Eucharist is the centre of Christian life.

In the Eucharist, the East has not speculated as much as the West about the exact nature of the change in bread and wine. The liturgy includes Christ’s words of institution, but the culminating prayer is the invocation of the Holy Spirit. The change is called metabole, or sacramental change.

The orders of ministry are bishop, priest, and deacon, with lector and subdeacon as minor orders. Penance developed from public reconciliation into private confession and is viewed more as spiritual healing than as a legal tribunal. Anointing of the sick is a prayer for healing, and marriage is celebrated through crowning as an eternal union sacramentally projected into the kingdom of God.

## Architecture, icons, and mission

Orthodox architecture developed many forms, with Hagia Sophia as a central model: a great dome over a basilica symbolizing heaven descending to earth in the Eucharist. Icons became a visual confession of the Incarnation, especially after the Iconoclastic Controversy of 725-843.

Icons show that God became visible in the humanity of Jesus Christ and that saints reveal transfigured humanity. The East generally avoided three-dimensional statues because of associations with pagan idolatry and developed a strong theology of painted images within worship.

Orthodox mission differed from Western mission by accepting vernacular liturgy and Scripture translation. Cyril and Methodius used Slavic language in the 9th century, helping Christianity take root in local cultures. Later Russian missions carried this pattern through Siberia, Alaska, Japan, and other regions.

## Key Events & Developments

- 325-787: The seven ecumenical councils defined central Orthodox doctrine.
- 451: Chalcedon helped separate Eastern Orthodoxy from later Oriental Orthodox traditions.
- 1054: The schism between Rome and Constantinople symbolized the East-West break.
- 1204: Crusaders sacked Constantinople and deepened the separation from the Latin West.
- 1448: The Russian church elected its own metropolitan and became effectively autocephalous.
- 1917: The Russian Revolution began a new age of persecution and reform attempts.
- 1970: The Orthodox Church in America was granted autocephaly by Moscow.

## Interesting Facts
