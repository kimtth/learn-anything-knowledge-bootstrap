## Introduction

The Czech Republic ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Czech_Republic)) is a central European country made up of the historic lands of Bohemia, Moravia, and the southern tip of Silesia. It is landlocked, but Bohemia briefly had access to Baltic and Adriatic coasts in the Middle Ages. The country is strongly centred on [[Prague]], a capital famous for bridges, spires, architecture, and cultural memory.

[[Prague]], on the Vltava River, has been praised since the 18th century as one of Europe’s most beautiful cities. It was the birthplace of Franz Kafka and Rainer Maria Rilke. Moravia’s largest city, Brno, was the site of Gregor Mendel’s genetic experiments and the birthplace of Milan Kundera. Bohemia is associated with breweries and Pilsner beer from Plzen, while Moravia is known for vineyards, wine, skilled labour, textiles, and engineering.

The modern Czech state emerged from the breakup of Czechoslovakia on January 1, 1993. Its recent history includes Nazi occupation, communist rule, the Prague Spring of 1968, the Charter 77 dissident movement, the Velvet Revolution of 1989, membership in NATO ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NATO)) in 1999, and membership in the European Union ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_Union)) in 2004.

## Relief and Climate

![[attachments/czech_and_slovak_republics_czech_republic_002.webp|420]]

The Bohemian Massif ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bohemian_Massif)) covers most of the country. It forms a large elevated basin, the Bohemian Plateau, ringed by mountain systems. The Sumava Mountains lie in the southwest, the Ore Mountains form part of the German border, and the Giant Mountains contain Mount Snezka, the highest point at 5,256 feet, or 1,602 metres.

The drainage system places the Czech Republic in the headwaters of central Europe. The Elbe and Vltava drain Bohemia toward Germany, the Morava drains Moravia toward the Danube, and the Oder flows north toward Poland. Soils include chernozems, brown soils, podzols, mountain soils, alluvial soils, and heavy clays.

The [[Climate and Weather (Climate)|climate]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Climate)) mixes continental and oceanic influences. Temperatures and rainfall vary by elevation and region, with oceanic influence weakening from west to east. The growing season is about 200 days in the south but much shorter in mountains. Annual precipitation ranges from about 18 inches in central Bohemia to more than 60 inches on windward slopes of the Krkonose Mountains.

Plant and animal life also changes by elevation. Oak, beech, and spruce dominate forest zones, while high areas support taiga-like and tundra-like vegetation. Forests remain an important feature of the landscape, though large areas were cleared for farming and timber. Wildlife includes bears, wolves, lynx, wildcats, otters, martens, minks, and other animals.

## People and Religion

Czechs make up roughly nine-tenths of the population. Moravians often see themselves as a distinct group within the majority, and smaller minorities include Slovaks, Poles, Germans, and Roma. Czech is the official language and belongs to the West Slavic language group. Czech and Slovak are mutually intelligible and use the Latin alphabet.

Religious life was restricted under communism, when atheism was official policy and church property was nationalized by 1949. Religious freedom returned in 1989. By the early 21st century, Roman Catholics formed the largest religious group, while Orthodox congregations, Protestant churches, the Evangelical Church of Czech Brethren, and the Czechoslovak Hussite Church also existed. Almost one-third of the population claimed no religious affiliation.

Settlement patterns reflect industrialization, urbanization, and historical regions. Population density is high, and communities are usually close together. Some frontier areas are less dense because of the postwar expulsion of about three million Sudeten Germans. Rural settlements are compact in lowlands, while mountain villages often stretch along narrow valleys.

## Economy

The Czech economy entered a major transition after the Velvet Revolution of 1989. The postcommunist government introduced price liberalization, foreign trade and investment, currency convertibility, privatization of state firms, and tax reform.

Compared with Slovakia, the Czech lands had a more diversified and stable economy, skilled labour, better access to western Europe, and low foreign debt. After the split of Czechoslovakia, the two republics had to divide assets, military property, currency arrangements, and economic institutions. The Czech Republic performed relatively well in its early years as a separate state.

Agriculture ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agriculture)) is among the more advanced in eastern Europe, though land is less efficiently used than in western Europe. Important crops include wheat, sugar beets, barley, rye, oats, potatoes, hops, and grapes. Bohemia is known for hops used in brewing, while southern Moravia is the centre of wine production. Forests cover about one-third of the country, though acid rain damaged many forests before renewed reforestation.

The country produces coal, including bituminous, anthracite, and brown coal, though reserves are limited. Industry is broad, and engineering is the largest branch. Food processing, brewing, chemicals, rubber, cement, textiles, footwear, glass, iron, steel, and heavy manufacturing are also important.

## Government and Society

The [[Constitution and Constitutional Government (Constitution)|constitution]] adopted on December 16, 1992, established the Czech Republic as a parliamentary democracy. It reflects Western liberal ideas and incorporates principles from the Charter of Fundamental Rights and Freedoms.

The Parliament has a Chamber of Deputies elected by proportional representation and a Senate elected by district. Executive power is shared by the president and prime minister. The president is directly elected for a five-year term, appoints the prime minister, and serves as head of state.

Local government was reorganized after communism. The older 77 districts remained recognized, but in 2000 the country reestablished 13 regions and one capital city region. Local governments handle roads, utilities, public health, schools, and local taxation.

The judicial system includes the Constitutional Court, Supreme Administrative Court, Supreme Court, high courts, regional courts, and district courts. During the 1990s, legal reform aimed to meet standards of the Organization for Security and Co-operation in Europe. The political process uses universal direct suffrage and includes parties such as the Civic Democratic Party, Czech Social Democratic Party, Green Party, and Communist Party of Bohemia and Moravia.

Security and welfare changed after the Cold War. Soviet troops withdrew in 1991, the Warsaw Pact dissolved, and the Czech Republic joined NATO in 1999. By the end of 2004, the military had become all-volunteer. Health care was privatized and restructured after communism, housing improved after shortages in the late 1980s, and education remained built around compulsory schooling, secondary tracks, universities, and research institutions.

## Cultural Life

![[attachments/czech_and_slovak_republics_czech_republic_003.webp|420]]

*Znojmo Old Town Panorama from Castle*

Czech culture developed between German and Slavic lands and absorbed wider European influences. Italian Renaissance and Baroque architecture, German cultural mediation, Slavic identity, and national revival all shaped literature, music, theatre, film, visual arts, and daily customs.

Daily life includes public holidays such as January 1, Liberation Day, Cyril and Methodius Day, Jan Hus Day, Czech Statehood Day, Independence Day, and Students’ Fight for Freedom and Democracy. Many Czechs, including atheists, celebrate Easter and Christmas. Food traditions include potato and sauerkraut soups, chicken and pork dishes, dumplings, fruit-filled dumplings, apple strudel, honey cake, beer, and Moravian wine.

Czech literature reaches back to Old Church Slavonic texts connected with the mission of Cyril and Methodius to Moravia in 863 CE. Czech-language writing emerged in the late 13th century. The Kralice Bible, published between 1579 and 1593, helped preserve literary Czech during later suppression. The National Revival of the 19th century rebuilt language, history, literature, theatre, journalism, and national identity through figures such as Josef Dobrovsky and Frantisek Palacky.

In the 20th century, Czech and German-language writers from Bohemia and Moravia gained international importance. Franz Kafka, Franz Werfel, Rainer Maria Rilke, and Max Brod formed part of Prague’s German-Jewish literary world. Later writers such as Milan Kundera, Josef Skvorecky, Ludvik Vaculik, and Ivan Klima were shaped by communism, censorship, exile, and underground publication.

The [[The Art of the Theatre (Theatre)|theatre]] tradition is strongly tied to the Prague National Theatre, completed in 1881 with private donations. The liberated theatre movement of the 1930s developed political satire, and Vaclav Havel became internationally known for plays such as The Garden Party and The Memorandum.

[[The Art of Music (Music)|Music]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Music)) is a major Czech contribution. Johann Stamitz influenced Classical symphonic form, while Bedrich Smetana, Antonin Dvorak, Leos Janacek, and Bohuslav Martinu drew on folk sources and won international fame. The Czech Philharmonic became one of the world’s leading orchestras, and folk music and jazz also remained important.

Film was state-supported under communism and later declined in output after the withdrawal of state sponsorship. The Czech New Wave of 1962-1968 produced films by Milos Forman and Jiri Menzel, whose Closely Watched Trains won an Academy Award. Czech animation is also famous through artists such as Jiri Trnka and Jan Svankmajer.

## History

The Czech Republic ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Czech_Republic)) came into being on January 1, 1993, after the dissolution of the Czechoslovak federation. Assets were divided roughly two to one in favour of the Czechs, and special arrangements were made for the natural gas pipeline, diplomatic service, and armed forces. Vaclav Havel became president, and Vaclav Klaus became prime minister.

The early republic pursued political and economic reform, especially rapid privatization. The first general election after independence, held in 1996, weakened the governing coalition but did not immediately remove it. Political life was marked by centre-right and centre-left competition, debates over reform, and the legacy of the Velvet Revolution.

The country joined NATO ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NATO)) in 1999 and the European Union ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_Union)) on May 1, 2004. Vaclav Klaus became president in 2003 and was reelected in 2008. During the Czech EU presidency in 2009, a centre-right government collapsed after a no-confidence vote. The Lisbon Treaty caused controversy until the Czech Constitutional Court ruled that it did not threaten the constitution, after which Klaus signed it.

In 2010, Petr Necas became prime minister at the head of a centre-right coalition. His government adopted austerity measures after the euro-area financial crisis, but corruption scandals and leadership struggles limited its effectiveness.

## Key Events & Developments

- 863 CE: The mission of Cyril and Methodius helped begin the region’s early Slavic literary tradition.
- 1348: Charles University was founded in Prague.
- 1579-1593: The Kralice Bible helped preserve literary Czech.
- 1938-1939: The Munich Agreement and German invasion led to Nazi control of Bohemia and Moravia.
- 1948: A communist coup brought Czechoslovakia into the Soviet bloc.
- 1968: The Prague Spring was crushed by Soviet military invasion.
- 1989: The Velvet Revolution ended communist rule.
- 1993: The Czech Republic became independent after the Velvet Divorce.
- 1999 and 2004: The country joined NATO and then the European Union.
