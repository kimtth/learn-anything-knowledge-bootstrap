# Algae

## Introduction

Algae ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algae)) are a group of predominantly aquatic, photosynthetic organisms usually placed in the kingdom Protista. They range from microscopic forms such as Micromonas, about 1 micrometre in diameter, to giant kelps reaching 60 metres in length. They are crucial for life on Earth: algae produce much of the planet's oxygen, form the food base for almost all aquatic ecosystems, contribute to the origin of crude oil, and provide foods and pharmaceutical and industrial products for human use. They have many different life cycles, show a wider variety of photosynthetic pigments than land plants, and possess cellular features not found in plants or animals.

Algae are eukaryotic organisms that photosynthesize but lack the specialized multicellular reproductive structures characteristic of plants. They also lack true roots, stems, and leaves. The prokaryotic blue-green algae, or cyanobacteria, are not fully included here, though some scientists have classified them as algae because their oxygen-producing photosynthesis seemed as important as their cell structure. The taxonomy of algae changes rapidly because new information continually alters how relationships among groups are understood. The study of algae is called phycology, and a specialist is a phycologist.

Algae vary greatly in form. Some are parenchymatous, or tissue-like, such as giant kelps. Others are coenocytic, meaning that they are essentially single multinucleate cells not divided by cross walls, as in the green seaweed Codium. Some algae are flagellated and swim freely, from single cells like Ochromonas to large colonies like Volvox. Others form exact or variable colonies, such as the coccoid Scenedesmus or capsoid Chrysocapsa. This structural diversity is one of the central themes of algal biology: algae are not one simple form of life but a wide range of evolutionary solutions to life in water and other habitats.

## Distribution and Abundance

Algae are almost ubiquitous. They are most common in aquatic habitats, but they fall into many ecological types according to where they live. Planktonic algae float suspended in water, while neustonic algae live on the water surface. Cryophilic algae live in snow and ice, thermophilic algae in hot springs, and edaphic algae on or in soil. Epizoic algae grow on animals, epiphytic algae on fungi, plants, or other algae, and corticolous algae on bark. Some algae live on rocks, in porous stone, or in rock fissures, and many live inside other organisms as endosymbionts.

This ecological survey shows that algae are not restricted to open water. They occupy an enormous range of environments and often enter intimate associations with other living things. Their success comes from flexibility in structure, physiology, and nutrition.

## Toxicity

Although algae are essential to life, some are harmful to humans through the toxins produced by certain kinds, especially some dinoflagellates. Paralytic shellfish poisoning is caused by saxitoxin and related compounds, which are among the most powerful known toxins and interfere with neuromuscular function. Species such as Alexandrium tamarense and Gymnodinium catenatum are commonly associated with this poisoning.

Other toxic syndromes include diarrheic shellfish poisoning from okadaic acids, neurotoxic shellfish poisoning caused by toxins from Gymnodinium breve, and ciguatera, caused when humans eat tropical fish that have concentrated toxins from algae such as Gambierdiscus or Ostreopsis; and amnesic shellfish poisoning, due to domoic acid produced by some diatoms. Symptoms can progress from stomach distress to disorientation, memory loss, and death. These examples show that algae are not only ecologically fundamental but sometimes dangerous participants in food chains and coastal environments.

## Cell Structure

Eukaryotic algal cells contain three major double-membrane organelles: the nucleus, the chloroplast, and the mitochondrion. Most cells contain one nucleus, though some are multinucleate and some are siphonaceous, with many nuclei not separated by cell walls. The nucleus contains most of the cell's DNA, which usually appears as linear strands except during mitosis, though in Dinophyceae and Euglenophyceae the nuclear DNA remains permanently condensed into chromosomes.

Chloroplasts are the sites of [[Photosynthesis|photosynthesis]]. Their thylakoid membranes contain chlorophylls, carotenoids, and phycobiliproteins that harvest light. Mitochondria are the sites of cellular respiration, where food molecules are broken down and energy is released. The inner mitochondrial membrane may have flattened, tubular, or paddlelike cristae, and the form of these structures is useful in classification. These organelles reveal how algal cells combine energy capture and energy release in elaborate and varied ways.

## Flagella

Many algae possess flagella, complex structures built around an axoneme with nine outer pairs of microtubules surrounding two central microtubules. Dynein proteins and their arms convert the chemical energy of ATP ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adenosine_triphosphate)) into the mechanical energy needed for flagellar bending and movement, with opposite sets of dynein arms becoming active in sequence to produce the power stroke and recovery stroke that give many flagella their characteristic motion.

Flagellar membranes are themselves complex and may contain chemoreceptors, scales, hairs, and para-axonemal structures. These features help the alga respond to its environment, identify mating partners, and move effectively through water. Flagella thus illustrate the union of cell biology, behaviour, and ecology in algal life.

## Mitosis and Cellular Respiration
Mitosis in algae is highly diverse. In some groups the nuclear envelope breaks down during division, while in others it remains intact. Spindle microtubules may stay outside the nucleus, enter it through openings, or form entirely inside it. This diversity provides important clues for understanding both algae and the general evolution of cell division in eukaryotes.

Cellular respiration ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cellular_respiration)) in algae resembles that of other eukaryotes. Organic molecules such as sugars, fatty acids, and proteins are first broken down in the cytoplasm, and their final energy-releasing steps take place in the mitochondria. Most algae are aerobic, though a few euglenophytes can live anaerobically. Respiration and photosynthesis are complementary processes: the first releases energy by breaking down organic molecules, while the second stores energy by building them.

## Photosynthesis and Light-Absorbing Pigments

[[Photosynthesis]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Photosynthesis)) is the process by which light energy is converted into chemical energy, allowing carbon dioxide and water to become organic molecules. Much of what is known about photosynthesis was first learned from algae, especially the green alga Chlorella. The light reactions, which generate chemical energy, are distinguished from the dark reactions, or Calvin cycle, in which carbon dioxide is fixed into sugars.

The pigment systems of algae are more varied than those of land plants. All algae use chlorophyll a. Green algae and euglenophytes also use chlorophyll b. Other groups employ additional chlorophylls, chlorophyllides, carotenoids, and phycobiliproteins that absorb light at wavelengths not captured by chlorophyll a or b. Because pigment composition varies, different algae are adapted to different light environments. This diversity of pigments helps explain why algae occupy such a wide range of aquatic habitats.

## Alternative Nutrition

Not all algae are photosynthetic all the time. Some colourless forms obtain food and energy by oxidizing organic molecules absorbed from the environment or engulfed as particles. These are called heterotrophic algae because they depend on organic matter produced by other organisms. Many algae also make sugars and sugar alcohols, such as rhamnose, trehalose, and xylose, for use in metabolism. This flexibility shows that the boundary between plantlike and nonplantlike nutrition is not simple in algal life.

## Reproduction and Life Histories

Algae reproduce sexually, asexually, or in both ways. Asexual reproduction may occur through ordinary cell division, fragmentation, or spores. Some red algae produce water-borne monospores, while some green algae form aplanospores, which are nonmotile, or zoospores, which carry one or more flagella and can swim. Sexual reproduction involves meiosis, so offspring receive half their genetic information from each parent.
Algae display many different life cycles. This variety is another reason they have been difficult to classify in a single simple scheme. Their reproductive strategies show repeated evolutionary experimentation across different lineages.

## Evolution and Classification
Algal classification is a changing field shaped by morphology, pigments, cell structure, fossils, and molecular biology. Some groups appear ancient, while others seem more recent. Fossil evidence is especially good for the Dasycladales, calcified unicellular forms that date back at least to the Triassic Period. There is debate about the origins of red algae, green algae, and dinoflagellates, and similarities among some groups may result from endosymbiosis rather than simple direct descent.

It also surveys major groups such as the green algal classes Chlorophyceae, Charophyceae, Micromonadophyceae, Pleurastrophyceae, and Ulvophyceae; the chromophytes, including diatoms, brown algae, and golden algae; the Cryptophyceae; the dinoflagellates; the Euglenophyceae; and the red algae, or Rhodophyta. Each group is distinguished by combinations of pigments, storage products, mitochondrial form, flagellar features, and habitat. The classification itself illustrates the larger point that algae are not a single coherent branch but a broad set of lineages linked by photosynthesis and aquatic adaptation.

## Conclusion
Algae are among the most important and diverse organisms on Earth. They sustain aquatic food webs, contribute much of the planet's oxygen, offer many useful products, and illuminate basic biological processes such as photosynthesis, cell division, and evolution. At the same time, some species produce toxins that can harm ecosystems and human health. Their diversity of form, habitat, pigmentation, nutrition, reproduction, and evolutionary history makes algae a central subject for understanding both life in water and the broader complexity of eukaryotic evolution.

## Key Events & Developments

- 1830s: The study of algae begins to take recognizable scientific form.
- 1970s: Prokaryotic blue-green algae become a major point of taxonomic debate.
- 20th century: Fossil, cellular, and molecular evidence reshapes algal classification.
- Late 20th century: New research expands understanding of harmful algal toxins and biotechnology.
- 21st century: Molecular biology continues to refine relationships among major algal groups.

## Key People

- Phycologists ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phycologists)): Scientists who study algae and drive the field's classification and ecology.
- Taxonomists ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taxonomists)): Researchers who revise algal groupings as new evidence appears.
- Scientists studying Chlorella: Investigators whose work helped illuminate photosynthesis.
