# The History of Western Music (Music, Western)

## Introduction

![[attachments/western_music_001.webp|420]]

*Gregorian chant notation*

Western music developed from ancient Mediterranean, Near Eastern, Greek, Roman, Christian, and European traditions into a vast body of liturgical, secular, vocal, instrumental, theatrical, and concert music.

Music began in ancient societies as communication, labour aid, ritual support, and religious ceremony. In early cultures, music served function: magic, worship, military zeal, labour, drama, dance, and social gatherings, long before pure pleasure in sound became an end in itself.

The cradle of Western music lay in the Fertile Crescent ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fertile_Crescent)) and eastern Mediterranean. Mesopotamian, Egyptian, Hebrew, Greek, and Roman traditions combined to form the basis later carried into western Europe through Christianity.

## Ancient Middle East, Egypt, and Greece

Mesopotamian cultures had instruments of all main types: idiophones, aerophones, chordophones, and membranophones. A stone hymn from about 800 BCE gives evidence of early musical notation.

Egyptian music used similar instruments and functions, while Hebrew musical culture, known through the Old Testament and synagogue practice, became directly influential through Christian liturgy. The shofar and sung scripture remained important practices.

Greek music gave Western culture its most direct ancient theoretical legacy. The Greek cults of Apollo and Dionysus became prototypes for classical clarity and romantic emotional abandon.

The Greek doctrine of ethos, discussed by Plato and Aristotle, held that music affects the soul and conduct. Music was therefore connected to education, drama, politics, and moral formation.

Pythagoras linked musical intervals to numerical ratios, creating an acoustical basis for Western music theory. Greek modes, rhythms, notation, instruments, and ideas passed to Rome.

## Rome and Early Christian Chant

Roman music adapted Greek and eastern Mediterranean models. It favoured the diatonic scale and a metrical concept based on equal durations and accent.

After the Roman Empire declined, the Christian Church preserved and reshaped ancient musical inheritance. Local Western liturgical traditions included Ambrosian chant in Milan, Mozarabic chant in Spain, Gallican chant in France, and Roman chant.

Gregorian chant, traditionally linked to Pope Gregory I, organized a vast repertory of melodies and functions for the mass and divine offices. Church modes gradually systematized tonal materials.

From the 9th century, tropes expanded chant by adding text, melody, or both to existing liturgical music. These additions helped singers handle long melismas and encouraged creative expression.

## Ars Antiqua, Ars Nova, and Secular Song

From about 1150 to 1300, polyphony developed rapidly in the period later called Ars Antiqua. By the early 14th century, Philippe de Vitry's Ars Nova described new rhythmic and harmonic practices.

The Ars Nova used duple as well as triple divisions and gave greater importance to thirds and sixths, while older 13th-century music had emphasized triple rhythms and perfect consonances.

In 14th-century Italy, Florence became a centre for madrigals, ballatas, and caccias. Francesco Landini, Giovanni da Cascia, Jacopo da Bologna, and others developed new secular forms.

Secular monophonic song flourished among goliards, troubadours, trouveres, minnesingers, and other poet-musicians. Their songs treated love, war, famine, satire, chivalry, and daily concerns.

## Renaissance Music

The 15th century saw the rise of Burgundian and Franco-Flemish music. Guillaume Dufay, Gilles Binchois, and English composer John Dunstable shaped richer harmonies and new continental styles.

The mass became a unified polyphonic setting of the Kyrie, Gloria, Credo, Sanctus, and Agnus Dei. Composers used plainsong or secular melodies as cantus firmus, often in long tenor notes.

The motet became a Latin sacred polyphonic setting, no longer always tied to a plainsong tenor. Chansons and secular forms expanded in freedom and variety.

In 16th-century Italy, the madrigal developed from the frottola into a refined secular vocal form. Early madrigals were restrained and often homophonic; later ones became dramatic, chromatic, text-sensitive, and highly expressive.

English composers such as William Byrd, Thomas Morley, John Wilbye, Thomas Weelkes, Orlando Gibbons, John Dowland, and Thomas Campion developed madrigals, ayres, canzonets, and balletts.

French chanson continued medieval and Renaissance secular traditions with sectional structure, vivid programmatic writing, and attention to poetic accent. German lied remained more conservative and often used cantus firmus principles.

## Baroque Era

Around 1600, Western music entered a dramatic turning point. The Baroque era, lasting roughly to 1750, valued drama, energy, grandeur, expressive contrast, and expanded musical vocabulary.

Two styles coexisted: prima prattica, the older 16th-century polyphonic style, and seconda prattica, or modern style, which placed text and dramatic expression above strict counterpoint.

The Florentine Camerata tried to revive ancient drama and created monody: solo song, recitative, arioso, and aria with basso continuo. Giulio Caccini and Jacopo Peri composed early operas based on Orpheus and Eurydice.

Opera moved from Florence to [[Rome]] and then Venice, where the first public opera house opened in 1637. Monteverdi became a major figure in early opera, linking Renaissance madrigal technique with Baroque drama.

French opera, shaped by Jean-Baptiste Lully and later Rameau, merged Italian opera with ballet and developed the French overture. England blended masque and opera through Henry Purcell and John Blow.

Cantata and oratorio developed alongside opera. Carissimi standardized the cantata, and the oratorio emerged as sacred drama without full staging. In Germany, Lutheran church cantatas became central.

## Bach, Handel, and the Late Baroque

![[attachments/western_music_002.webp|420]]

*Portrait of Johann Sebastian Bach*

The Baroque reached its height in Johann Sebastian Bach and George Frideric Handel, both born in 1685 in Germany and both trained as organists.

Handel, shaped by Italy and England, became a dramatic composer of opera, oratorio, cantata, and orchestral music. Bach, shaped by church service and teaching, wrote Passions, cantatas, organ works, harpsichord music, and instructional pieces.

Their music consolidated the tonal system, modulation, figured bass, chromatic harmony, and a hybrid texture combining homophonic foundation with independent polyphonic lines.

## Classical Period

![[attachments/western_music_003.webp|420]]

*Portrait of Ludwig van Beethoven*

The Classical period brought clarity, balance, formal structure, regularity, and instrumental dominance. Rococo elegance, Enlightenment ideals, and later Sturm und Drang intensity all shaped the style.

Haydn, [[Mozart]], Gluck, C.P.E. Bach, and young [[Beethoven]] helped consolidate the style. Instrumental music became more important than vocal music for the first time.

The orchestra, string quartet, piano trio, sonata, symphony, concerto, divertimento, serenade, and chamber ensembles became standardized. Homophonic melody with accompaniment replaced much late-Baroque polyphonic density, though counterpoint remained important.

Classical sonata form and related structures organized contrast, development, return, and tonal movement. Opera seria remained powerful, but Gluck reformed opera to reduce artificiality and restore dramatic truth.

Comic opera developed through opera buffa, opera comique, English ballad opera, and German singspiel. Mozart brought these traditions to high achievement in The Marriage of Figaro, Don Giovanni, Cosi fan tutte, The Abduction from the Seraglio, and The Magic Flute.

## Romantic Music

Nineteenth-century Romantic music emphasized expression, nationalism, virtuosity, colour, subjective feeling, drama, expanded harmony, and new relations between music and literature.

Italian opera remained dominant through Rossini, Bellini, Donizetti, and especially Verdi. Late-century verismo by Leoncavallo, Mascagni, and Puccini pursued realism and topical subject matter.

German opera became deeply Romantic with Weber's Der Freischutz and reached a new ideal in Wagner's music drama, where drama, music, design, and performance were meant to fuse into total art.

Nationalism shaped music by Russian, Bohemian, Scandinavian, French, and other composers. Smetana, Dvorak, Grieg, Nielsen, Sibelius, Rimsky-Korsakov, and others used national materials and identities.

Piano music flourished through technical improvements and middle-class demand. Character pieces, waltzes, mazurkas, polonaises, etudes, sonatas, and variations were central to Chopin, Schumann, Liszt, and Brahms.

The German lied became a major art form through Schubert, Loewe, Mendelssohn, Schumann, Brahms, Hugo Wolf, and Richard Strauss, joining Romantic poetry to piano accompaniment.

## Sacred, Chamber, and Later Currents

![[attachments/western_music_004.webp|420]]

*Portrait of Igor Stravinsky*

Chamber music after Beethoven and Schubert became less central because Romantic composers often preferred orchestral colour, but Mendelssohn, Schumann, and Brahms preserved the tradition.

Sacred music became more conservative, especially after renewed study of Palestrina and Gregorian chant. Oratorio and choral societies allowed some fusion of sacred, secular, operatic, and civic musical idioms.

Later Western music moved toward modernism, nationalism, experimental harmony, new rhythm, orchestral colour, electronic media, and global influences, though this source emphasizes the long line from antiquity to Romantic and early modern developments.

## Key Events & Developments

- c. 800 BCE: Mesopotamian hymn inscription suggests early notation.
- 6th century BCE: Pythagoras recorded numerical interval ratios.
- Late 6th century: Gregorian chant organization was traditionally linked to Gregory I.
- Early 14th century: Philippe de Vitry's Ars Nova described new rhythmic practice.
- c. 1530: Renaissance Italian madrigal emerged.
- 1600: Opera and Baroque monody appeared around Florence.
- 1637: First public opera house opened in Venice.
- 1750: Bach's death often marks the end of the Baroque.
- 1821: Weber's Der Freischutz signaled German Romantic opera.
