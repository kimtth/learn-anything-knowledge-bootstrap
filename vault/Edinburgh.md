## Gaelic Dun Eideann

Edinburgh, called Dun Eideann ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edinburgh)) in Gaelic, is the capital city of Scotland. It stands in southeastern Scotland, near the southern shore of the Firth of Forth, where an arm of the North Sea reaches west into the Scottish Lowlands. The city and its nearby area form an independent council area. Most of this area, including the port of Leith, belongs historically to Midlothian, while the northwestern area around South Queensferry belongs historically to West Lothian. The council area covers 102 square miles, or 264 square km. The population was 431,393 in the city and 476,626 in the council area in 2001, and 459,366 in the city and 482,640 in the council area in 2011.

## Landscape

The city site of Edinburgh occupies about 7 miles, or 11 km, of north-facing slope between the Pentland Hills and the broad estuary of the Firth of Forth. At the coast it merges with the old seaport of Leith. The landscape is marked by upthrusts of lava. The most famous is Arthur's Seat ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arthur%27s_Seat)), the centre of the royal park, which rises 823 feet, or 251 metres, and dominates the southeastern side of the city. Valleys between these hills were cut deep by glacial action in the Pleistocene Epoch, so the city grew on and around difficult natural obstacles.

## Climate

Edinburgh has a mild maritime climate ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oceanic_climate)). The nearby sea reduces extremes of temperature. Winters are relatively warm, with average daily minimum temperatures staying above freezing, while summers are cool and seldom rise much above 70 F, or 21 C. Easterly winds are often cold but fairly dry. Warmer southwesterly winds from the North Atlantic Current often bring rain.

## The Old Town

Edinburgh Castle, 443 feet, or 135 metres, above sea level, dominates the city from Castle Rock. Archaeology shows that the rock was not first fortified only by the Gododdin in the 6th century, as once thought, but originated as an occupied site in the Bronze Age and has been used for about 3,000 years. Its first documented use as a royal castle belongs to the reign of Malcolm III Canmore, from 1058 to 1093. Later damage and rebuilding erased much earlier material, so little survives from before James IV, who reigned from 1488 to 1513.

## Holyrood

Holyrood stands at the lower end of the Royal Mile, away from the tight crowding of the Old Town. The Augustinian abbey was built on the first broad area of solid ground at the foot of the volcanic crag and tail. It was founded in 1128 and rebuilt around 1220. The surviving ruins of the nave are described as bold and imaginative work from that period.

## Princes Street Gardens

Princes Street Gardens ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Princes_Street_Gardens)) lie between the Old Town and the New Town in the drained bed of the North Loch. Their character is both recreational and ceremonial. Flower beds are changed several times a year, and the floral clock planted in 1903, described as the first in the world, contains about 24,000 plants around a 36-foot, or 11-metre, circumference. The gardens also include lawns, groves, recreational spaces, a bandstand, an outdoor dance floor, and many memorials.

## The New Town

The New Town was approved by the town council in 1767 as a planned suburban district for people of rank and fortune. The architect James Craig designed an orderly grid five streets deep and seven streets wide, with a broad central axis ending in grand squares. St. George's Church was planned for the west end and St. Andrew's for the east. Princes Street, the southernmost street, was built only on its north side so its houses could face the castle across the valley.

## Transportation

Edinburgh is served by First ScotRail, the regional rail carrier. It has frequent trains to London and major Scottish cities, plus regular service to other parts of Scotland and England. The city has two central railway stations: Waverley, described as the second largest in Britain, and Haymarket. Edinburgh's airport provides international service.

## Health

Edinburgh had a strong medical tradition before the formal foundation of the Edinburgh faculty of medicine in 1726. Healing was both practiced and taught in the city. The opening of the new Royal Infirmary in 1748 made Edinburgh one of the world's leading medical centres. This medical importance connects the city's university, hospitals, and professional culture.

## Education

The City of Edinburgh maintains state schools that provide free primary and secondary education. It also provides free nursery schools, schools for children with special needs, and community education for youth and adults. The city also has more fee-paying independent schools than any other Scottish city. their visible social style, including distinctive blazers and the habit of pupils rarely wearing overcoats, even in winter.

Edinburgh's early history began with the first settlers of Scotland after the glaciers. As early as 7000 BC, rivers and estuaries gave access to the interior, and the Forth was one of the most important routes. Stone Age explorers left evidence on the shoreline and mudflats. They did not yet need the protection of the steep hills, but by about 1500 BC, finds of swords and other metal objects suggest that people used the crags of Arthur's Seat, also called the Lion's Head, for defence.

## Key Events & Developments

- 7000 BC: Early settlers used the estuaries and rivers of Scotland, including the Forth, as routes into the interior.
- 1500 BC: Finds of weapons suggest that people were using Arthur's Seat ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arthur%27s_Seat)) and nearby crags for defence.
- 1000 BC: Archaeology shows occupation of Castle Rock, confirming the deep prehistory of the city site.
- 2nd century AD: The Romans used the Edinburgh plain strategically, with Cramond serving as a major garrison.
- AD 600: A Welsh poem about the Gododdin refers to Din Eidyn, the hill fort later associated with Edinburgh.
- 1127: By this period, David I had granted Edinburgh royal burgh status, strengthening trade and manufacture.
- 1329: Robert I confirmed Edinburgh's privileges as a royal burgh.
- 1507: Scotland's first printing press was established in the Cowgate under royal licence.
- 1513: The defeat at Flodden led Edinburgh to build the Flodden Wall for defence.
- 1532: James V established the Court of Session in Edinburgh, confirming the city's legal importance.
- 1582: James VI granted the town council a charter that led to the Toun's College, later the University of Edinburgh.
- 1603: James VI moved south to become James I of England, and Edinburgh lost direct court importance.
- 1707: The Act of Union ended Edinburgh's independent political life, though it remained a legal and administrative centre.
- 1767: The town council approved James Craig's plan for the New Town.
- 1772: The North Bridge opened, allowing large-scale expansion beyond the Old Town.
- 1947: The Edinburgh International Festival began, making the city a major world arts centre.
- 1995: The medieval Old Town and Neoclassical New Town became a UNESCO World Heritage site.
- 1999: A new Scottish Parliament and government restored Edinburgh's role as Scotland's political centre.
- 2004: The new Scottish Parliament complex opened at Holyrood, and Edinburgh became UNESCO's first City of Literature.
