# Artificial Intelligence

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> Artificial intelligence develops computer systems that perform aspects of intelligent behaviour, including learning, reasoning, problem solving, perception, language use, and adaptation. The field contrasts symbolic rule-based methods with connectionist neural networks and situated robotics while testing the limits of narrow expertise, generalization, and machine understanding.

## Introduction

Artificial intelligence ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artificial_intelligence)), or AI, is the ability of a digital computer or robot to perform tasks commonly associated with intelligent beings. The term applies especially to attempts to build systems that can reason, learn, generalize, discover meaning, and adapt to new situations.

Computers already perform some difficult tasks very well, such as proving theorems or playing chess, and expert-level AI exists in limited areas such as medical diagnosis, search engines, and speech or handwriting recognition. Yet these systems still do not match human flexibility across broad domains.

## What Intelligence Means
Intelligence cannot simply mean complex behaviour. Insect behaviour may be highly elaborate yet still be instinctive and rigid. Human intelligence, by contrast, includes the capacity to adapt when circumstances change.

This is why the digger wasp is a useful example: its behaviour is fixed and repetitive even when conditions are altered. True intelligence requires flexible adjustment rather than mere repetition.

## Learning

One important part of AI is learning. The simplest form is trial-and-error learning, where a program stores solutions it has already found. This is called rote learning ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rote_learning)) and is relatively easy to implement.

More difficult is generalization, in which a system uses past experience in new but similar cases. A program that has learned only specific verb forms by memory cannot handle a new verb, while one that has discovered a rule can extend what it knows. Generalization is a central challenge in AI.

## Reasoning

To reason is to draw inferences appropriate to a situation. Deductive reasoning, where the conclusion follows necessarily from the premises, differs from inductive reasoning, where evidence supports but does not guarantee a conclusion.

Computers have been fairly successful at drawing certain inferences, especially deductive ones. But real reasoning is harder than formal inference alone, because it must select the inferences that matter to solving the present problem.

## Problem Solving

AI problem solving is a search through possible actions in order to reach a goal. Some methods are special-purpose, designed for one narrow kind of problem. Others are general-purpose, such as means-end analysis, which tries to reduce the gap between current and goal states step by step.
Problem solving is one of the classic areas of AI. It shows that the difficulty is not just finding a possible path but doing so efficiently in very large spaces of alternatives.

## Perception

In [[Human Perception (Perception)|perception]], the environment is scanned by natural or artificial sensors and analyzed into objects and spatial relations. This is difficult because objects look different under different angles, lighting, and contrasts.

Still, artificial perception had already advanced enough for systems to identify people, let autonomous vehicles move at moderate speeds, and allow robots to navigate buildings and collect objects. Early projects such as FREDDY combined perception with action in limited settings.

## Language

[[Language]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Language)) is a system of signs whose meaning depends on convention. Human language is especially powerful because it is productive, allowing an unlimited variety of sentences. Some AI programs can appear to converse fluently in narrow settings.

However, fluency is not the same as genuine understanding. It raises the difficult question of what it would mean for a machine truly to understand language rather than merely manipulate symbols in convincing ways.

## Symbolic and Connectionist Approaches

AI research is said to follow two broad and partly competing methods. The symbolic, or top-down, approach tries to reproduce intelligence by working with symbols, rules, and explicit reasoning. The connectionist, or bottom-up, approach seeks intelligence through networks modeled loosely on neurons.
This division is one of the main organizing contrasts in AI. Each approach captures something important, but each also faces major limitations.

## Chess and the Turing Test

Alan Turing used [[Chess|chess]] as an early test case for machine intelligence because it provided difficult but clearly defined problems. In 1997, Deep Blue defeated world champion Garry Kasparov, fulfilling Turing's prediction that computers would eventually play excellent chess.

But this success did not necessarily explain human thinking. Deep Blue's power depended mainly on extraordinary speed and hardware. The Turing test shifts debate away from defining intelligence directly and toward whether a machine's performance can be indistinguishable from a human's in conversation.

## Microworld Programs and Expert Systems

![[attachments/artificial_intelligence_001.webp|420]]

*SRI Shakey robot at the Computer History Museum*

To reduce complexity, some AI researchers built programs for simplified artificial environments called microworlds. Famous examples include SHRDLU, which manipulated virtual blocks in response to English commands, and Shakey, a mobile robot in a specially prepared environment.
Other notable work involved expert systems such as DENDRAL for chemical analysis and MYCIN for diagnosing blood infections. These systems could perform impressively in restricted areas but lacked common sense and could fail absurdly outside their domain.

## CYC and Common Sense

The CYC project aimed to build an enormous knowledge base containing large amounts of everyday commonsense knowledge. The hope was that once enough facts and rules were encoded, the system could support more advanced intelligent behaviour.
This is one of the largest symbolic AI experiments. It also shows how difficult it is to supply machines with the everyday background knowledge that humans use constantly without noticing.

## Connectionism and Neural Networks

![[attachments/artificial_intelligence_002.webp|420]]

*Diagram of an artificial neural network*

Connectionism developed from attempts to understand the brain at the neural level. Artificial neural networks consist of simple processing units connected by weighted links. By adjusting those weights through training, a network can learn patterns.

This work traces from McCulloch and Pitts to later researchers such as Rosenblatt, whose perceptrons helped establish the field. It explains how learning happens mechanically through repeated correction of output, later generalized into back-propagation methods.

## Neural Network Applications

Neural networks were used for tasks such as visual recognition, handwritten text reading, speech processing, business forecasting, medical detection, and telecommunications control. Their value lies in pattern recognition and learning from data rather than explicit symbolic programming.
Some experiments had networks learn forms such as English verb conjugation. These examples suggested new ways of thinking about learning and representation in machines.

## Nouvelle AI and Situated Intelligence

Nouvelle AI is associated especially with Rodney Brooks. This approach rejects the idea that intelligence requires rich internal symbolic models of the world. Instead, it emphasizes embodied systems acting directly in real environments.

Robots such as Herbert, which searched offices for empty soda cans, showed how apparently intelligent behaviour can emerge from the interaction of a small set of simple behaviours. This led to the situated approach, where the world itself supplies much of the information the system needs.

## Conclusion

AI is a field divided among different methods but united by the attempt to recreate important aspects of intelligence in machines. Learning, reasoning, perception, language, and adaptive behaviour all remain central goals.

Progress in narrow tasks does not settle the deeper question of human-level intelligence. AI therefore appears both as a practical technological field and as a continuing philosophical inquiry into what intelligence really is.

## Core Equations

Engineering and technology topics often combine efficiency, signal flow, and computational cost:

$$\eta=\frac{\text{useful output}}{\text{input}}$$

Efficiency compares useful output with total input, so losses appear as the gap between the two.

$$SNR=10\log_{10}\left(\frac{P_s}{P_n}\right)$$

Signal-to-noise ratio compares signal power with noise power on a logarithmic decibel scale.

$$T(n)=O(n\log n)$$

Big-O notation describes how running time grows with input size, here at a rate typical of efficient divide-and-combine algorithms.

## Key Events & Developments

- AI develops around learning, reasoning, perception, language, and problem solving.
- The Turing test reframes debate about machine intelligence.
- Expert systems show strong performance in narrow domains.
- Neural networks revive interest in connectionist learning.
- Nouvelle AI and situated intelligence emphasize embodiment and real-world interaction.
