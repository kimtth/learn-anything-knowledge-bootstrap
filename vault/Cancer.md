# Cancer

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> Cancer comprises diseases in which genetically altered cells escape normal controls on growth and death, invade surrounding tissue, and may metastasize. Research in cell biology and molecular genetics supports prevention, screening, diagnosis, and treatments that combine surgery, radiation, drugs, and immune-based approaches.

## Introduction

Cancer is a group of more than 100 diseases in which abnormal cells grow in an uncontrolled way. Cancer remains one of the world's major causes of illness and death, but treatment has improved greatly since the middle of the 20th century through earlier diagnosis, surgery, radiation therapy, and chemotherapy.

The modern understanding of cancer comes from cell biology ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cell_biology)), [[Principles of Genetics and Heredity (Genetics)|genetics]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Genetics)), and biotechnology. These fields have made it possible to explain how normal cells become cancerous and how that knowledge can be used for prevention, diagnosis, and treatment.

## Presentation

![[attachments/cancer_001.webp|420]]

*Diagram showing cancer cells reproducing to form a tumour*

Tumours may be benign or malignant, but malignant tumours are distinguished by their ability to invade nearby tissues and later spread to distant parts of the body. A tumour is not made only of cancer cells; it also includes supporting host cells and immune cells, which together form the tumour stroma ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tumor_microenvironment)).

Cancer growth depends on repeated cell division, failure of cell death, and the ability to recruit blood vessels. A tumour can only expand beyond a very small size if it develops angiogenesis, the formation of new capillaries inside the mass. Once that happens, the tumour can sustain itself more independently through its own growth signals and through surrounding stromal cells.

A clinically detectable tumour may contain about one billion cells and weigh about one gram. To reach that size, a transformed cell must double its population many times. Many tumours therefore grow silently for years before producing symptoms, and some pass through a recognizable precancerous stage, such as colon polyps that may take years to become carcinoma.

## Angiogenesis and Invasion

Angiogenesis is a turning point in tumour progression. Cancer cells can stimulate blood-vessel growth by producing factors such as VEGF, while also shifting the balance away from inhibitors. Endothelial cells enter the tumour, and the tumour and vessel cells then stimulate one another.

Invasion begins when cells lose cohesion and detach from the original mass. They move through the extracellular matrix, cross basement membranes, and use enzymes to clear a path. This microinvasion creates access to blood and lymphatic vessels and makes later metastasis possible.

## Dissemination and Metastasis

![[attachments/cancer_002.webp|420]]

*Illustration of cancer metastasis through the bloodstream*

Once tumour cells enter the bloodstream, they can be carried throughout the body and lodge in distant organs. Only a tiny fraction of these cells form new tumours, but those that do create metastases, which are the chief threat to life in cancer.

Spread is not random. Many cancers show preferred routes of dissemination, such as the common spread of many tumours to the lungs or bone. Both blood flow and tissue affinity help explain why some cancers seed particular organs.

Metastasis also depends on immune escape. Some abnormal cells display mutant proteins that immune cells can recognize, but tumours that survive have often evaded immune surveillance. This helps explain why people with damaged or suppressed immune systems have higher risks of some cancers, especially lymphomas linked to viruses such as Epstein-Barr virus and human T-cell leukemia viruses.

## Diagnosis and Treatment

Cancer diagnosis depends heavily on biopsy, which can remove all or part of a tumour for microscopic examination. Excisional (removing the whole small tumour), incisional (removing a piece of a large mass), needle, fine-needle aspiration, and frozen-section biopsies all serve as ways of identifying tumour type, extent, and margin status. Fine-needle aspiration yields cells rather than tissue; it is relatively painless and can be interpreted within a day, while a tissue sample may take two days. Frozen sections taken during an operation can confirm within minutes whether a tumour has been completely excised.

Treatment is built around surgery, chemotherapy, radiation, and newer immune-based methods. Surgery is the oldest and still the principal curative therapy for many localized cancers, but its power is limited because about two-thirds of patients already have tumours that have spread beyond the primary site. Combining methods can transform outcomes: in childhood rhabdomyosarcoma, cure rates rose from 20 percent with radical surgery alone to 80 percent when radiation and chemotherapy were added.

Surgery may still cure localized disease, relieve pain or obstruction (palliative surgery), or prevent cancer in high-risk conditions such as undescended testes. Chemotherapy, by contrast, can circulate through the bloodstream and attack disseminated cells. The first agent was a nitrogen-mustard compound used in the 1940s against Hodgkin disease, and about 100 drugs are now in use; because cancer cells develop drug resistance, lasting responses usually require combinations rather than a single agent, an approach first proven in leukemia and lymphoma.

## Immunotherapy and Chemoprevention

Immunotherapy is a way of directing the body's own defenses toward cancer cells. Antibodies can be made to recognize a tumour antigen and linked to a drug or radioactive isotope, acting as a "magic bullet" that delivers the agent straight to the malignant cell. Tumour vaccines try to train T cells to recognize cancer cells, and T cells themselves can be engineered: in an experimental treatment for chronic lymphocytic leukemia, patient T cells were modified with a virus so they would bind and destroy malignant B cells, with some persisting as long-lived memory T cells.

Chemoprevention aims to interrupt carcinogenesis at an early stage before a tumour fully develops. Agents include naturally occurring compounds such as the dithiothiones found in broccoli and cauliflower, and drugs such as tamoxifen, which lowers breast-cancer incidence. These approaches connect to screening and early detection — mammograms, self-examination, and tests for cervical, prostate, colorectal, and skin cancers — showing that cancer medicine is increasingly concerned not only with cure but also with prevention.

## Causes of Cancer

![[attachments/cancer_003.webp|420]]

*Illustration of oncogene activation in cancer*

Cancer begins with genetic damage in DNA. The key players are proto-oncogenes, which promote growth, and tumour suppressor genes, which restrain it. When either class is altered, cell control is lost, especially if DNA-repair systems fail or apoptosis is blocked.

Oncogenes, discovered through retroviral research, show how viruses, inherited mutations, and faulty repair mechanisms can all contribute to tumour formation. The molecular picture that emerges is one of cells escaping the ordinary rules that govern division, repair, and death.

Among tumour suppressor genes, p53 has special importance. When p53 is disabled, damaged cells may continue dividing instead of stopping for repair or undergoing apoptosis; its inactivation accounts for about 70 percent of colon carcinomas, 30 to 50 percent of breast cancers, and 50 percent of lung cancers, and in some tumours p53 is blocked by antagonist proteins such as MDM2 or by human papillomavirus proteins. Other inherited or sporadic defects involve genes such as BRCA1, BRCA2, APC, WT1, VHL, and NF1/NF2; the gene MTS1, which codes for the p16 protein that holds the cell cycle at the G1-to-S transition, shows that hereditary cancer research also explains many nonhereditary tumours.

## Chemicals and Radiation

Chemical carcinogenesis often occurs in two stages: initiation and promotion. Initiators cause permanent genetic damage, while promoters stimulate cell division and help fix that damage in place; complete carcinogens can do both. Many initiators become reactive only after the body metabolizes them, and because most are mutagens they can be screened with the bacterial Ames test. Among the most potent human carcinogens are the polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons in tobacco smoke and broiled or smoked food; workers in the aniline dye and rubber industries have shown up to a 50-fold rise in cancer incidence. Promoters such as the phorbol ester TPA and hormones like diethylstilbestrol (DES), which causes vaginal cancer in exposed daughters, illustrate the second stage, and dietary saturated fat may promote colon cancer.

Radiation is another major cause. Ultraviolet light from the sun produces basal-cell, squamous-cell, and melanoma skin cancers by forming pyrimidine dimers in DNA; fair-skinned people with little protective melanin are most at risk, and UV-B rays are thought the most dangerous. Ionizing radiation is a powerful carcinogen that acts after long latency periods, as first seen around 1900 in the skin cancers of early X-ray and radium pioneers. Both chemical and physical agents work partly by damaging DNA and disturbing normal growth control.

## Conclusion

Cancer is both a biological disease and a major public-health challenge. The central point is that cancer arises when normal controls on cell growth, repair, and death break down, but that understanding those failures has made prevention and treatment steadily more effective.

## Key Events & Developments

- Ancient times: Cancer is recognized as a disease.
- Mid-20th century: Surgery, radiation, and chemotherapy greatly improve treatment.
- 1970: Retroviral research helps identify oncogenes.
- Late 20th century: Molecular biology reshapes cancer science.
- Early 21st century: Cancer remains a major global disease burden.

## Key People

- Peyton Rous ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peyton_Rous)): His sarcoma-virus research helped open the way to the discovery of oncogenes.
- Robert Weinberg: Author of a major modern account of cancer biology.
- Johns Hopkins researchers: Representative of the scientific community that advanced molecular cancer study.
