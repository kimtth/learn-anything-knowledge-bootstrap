## Introduction

*Biochemical Components of Organisms (Biochemistry)*

Biochemistry ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biochemistry)) is the study of the chemical substances and processes of living organisms. It is the chemistry of life, concerned with metabolism, enzymes, heredity, nutrition, disease, and the molecular basis of vital processes in plants, animals, and microorganisms.

The subject is a bridge between chemistry and biology. It explains life by chemical structure and reaction, and it depends heavily on the understanding that cells are governed by organized molecular systems. That bridge is the reason biochemistry became a central modern science.

## Historical Development

Biochemistry traces back to the gradual emergence of chemistry as a pure science and then to its application in physiology and medicine. Early work on combustion, gases, and organic matter laid the groundwork for later understanding of biological oxidation, digestion, and metabolism.

The 19th and 20th centuries brought decisive advances in the study of enzymes, hormones, vitamins, nucleic acids, and protein structure. By the mid-20th century biochemistry had become one of the central sciences of modern biology and medicine. This growth is cumulative: one discovery made the next possible.

Biochemistry gained force when chemical methods became precise enough to address living material directly. That change allowed biology to be studied at the level of molecules.

## Metabolism and Enzymes

Metabolism is defined as the total set of chemical changes by which organisms break down substances for energy and build complex molecules needed for life. These reactions depend on enzymes, which are organic catalysts produced by cells and themselves directed by genetic information.

This makes metabolism a highly integrated system rather than a set of isolated reactions. Enzyme action is one of the most important discoveries in modern biology because it explains how living matter can carry out specific, rapid, and controlled chemical transformations.
Metabolism cannot be separated from cellular organization. Reactions must occur in a controlled environment, or life would lose the order needed to persist.

## Nutrition and Photosynthesis

Animals require organic food, water, minerals, essential amino acids, essential fatty acids, and vitamins. Plants differ because they can manufacture their own organic material from simple substances through [[Photosynthesis|photosynthesis]].

Photosynthesis is treated as one of the central biochemical processes in nature. It allows green plants to build carbohydrate from carbon dioxide and water using sunlight, and it forms the basic energetic foundation for most life on Earth.

The nutritional section makes a broad comparative point: animals depend on preformed organic material, while plants can synthesize it. Despite that difference, the chemical patterns inside plant and animal cells are fundamentally similar.

## Digestion and Absorption

Digestion is the chemical breakdown of large food molecules into smaller building blocks suitable for absorption. Proteins become amino acids, polysaccharides become monosaccharides, and food must be liquefied and hydrolyzed before the body can use it.

Digestive enzymes such as pepsin and trypsin act on food, and nutrients move across intestinal walls into blood and lymph. These processes show how chemical change and physiological transport work together.
Digestion is not just a matter of breakdown. It is a preparatory stage that makes absorption, distribution, and later metabolism possible.

## Blood, Metabolism, and Hormones

Blood is treated as one of the most intensively studied tissues in biochemistry. Hemoglobin, plasma proteins, and antibodies all receive attention because they illustrate transport, immunity, and biochemical specificity in living systems.
Metabolism is also linked to hormonal regulation. Hormones help coordinate chemical changes across the body, showing that biochemistry is not only about molecules in isolation but about the regulation of whole organisms.

Blood, as a major example, shows how a circulating tissue can reflect the chemistry of the entire body. Hormones then connect local reactions to organism-wide control.

## Genes, Nucleic Acids, and Protein Synthesis

Heredity works through genes, which are composed of nucleic acids and located in chromosomes. DNA and RNA are the molecular basis of inheritance and the direct controllers of protein synthesis.

The relationship between genes and enzymes is a central theme. Experiments by Beadle and Tatum and later molecular genetics showed that genes determine which proteins a cell can make and therefore which metabolic pathways are possible.
This gene-enzyme connection is one of the key achievements of biochemical research. It shows that inheritance is expressed through chemical instructions, not through vague vital forces.

## Evolution and the Origin of Life

Biochemical study also links to the origin and evolution of life. Comparing proteins, nucleic acids, and metabolic systems across species made it possible to ask how life began and how it diversified chemically over time.

That broader question gives biochemistry its scope: it is not only a practical science of health and nutrition but also a fundamental way of explaining living systems as chemical systems.

Biochemistry therefore moves from practical medicine to planetary history. Chemistry becomes a way to understand both disease and the emergence of life itself.

## Conclusion

Biochemistry ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biochemistry)) is the science that explains life through chemistry: metabolism, enzymes, nutrients, blood, hormones, genes, and evolution all belong to one coherent molecular picture. Living organisms are chemically ordered systems whose functions can be understood only through biochemical analysis.

Read the left side as the reactants or starting materials and the right side as the products; the coefficients show the proportions needed to conserve atoms or charge.

## Key Events & Developments

- Chemistry becomes a distinct pure science before it is applied to life.
- Enzymes are recognized as central to biological reactions.
- Vitamins, hormones, nucleic acids, and proteins become major biochemical subjects.
- Molecular genetics links genes to protein synthesis.
- Biochemistry becomes essential to medicine and biology.
