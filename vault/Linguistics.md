# Linguistics

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> Linguistics is the scientific study of language, examining sound, word structure, syntax, meaning, change, acquisition, and social use. It emerged from philology while retaining historical questions and developed structural, generative, psychological, and sociolinguistic approaches to language systems.

## Introduction

Linguistics ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linguistics)) is the scientific study of language. The term came into use in the middle of the 19th century to mark a newer approach that differed from older philology. Philology emphasized written texts, literary culture, and historical development, while linguistics gave special attention to spoken language and to how language systems work at a given time.

Several major contrasts organize the field. Synchronic study describes a language at one time, while diachronic study follows change through time. Theoretical linguistics seeks general principles of language structure, while applied linguistics uses linguistic knowledge for practical tasks such as language teaching. Microlinguistics studies language systems more narrowly, while macrolinguistics connects language with society, psychology, culture, and use.

Early traditions of grammar reached high levels before modern linguistics. Panini's Sanskrit grammar, Greek analysis of parts of speech, and Latin school grammar all shaped later thinking. The history of linguistics therefore moves between inherited grammatical categories, new data from many languages, and changing ideas about what counts as scientific explanation.

## The European Middle Ages

Medieval grammar is difficult to judge because many treatises remained unavailable to modern scholars even by the middle of the 20th century. The period was often neglected because its work combined Latin learning with premodern philosophy, but medieval scholars used Latin deeply while also coming from varied linguistic backgrounds.

Some medieval grammars continued late-antique traditions based on Donatus and Priscian. Other works studied grammatical distinctions alongside logic and metaphysics. These speculative grammarians treated grammar as part of broader intellectual inquiry, not only as school instruction, and their ideas deserve analysis within the philosophical learning of the schools.

## The Renaissance

The Renaissance ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Renaissance)) brought two major bodies of new linguistic data. European vernaculars gained recognition and later support from academies and learned institutions, while voyages and missions brought knowledge of languages in Africa, East Asia, the Americas, Siberia, Central Asia, New Guinea, Oceania, the Arctic, and Australia.

Renaissance grammar did not produce a large theoretical revolution. It often rejected speculative medieval grammar and returned to late Roman models, especially Priscian. Still, serious grammars of European vernaculars began in the 15th century, and missionaries and traders gathered word lists and elementary grammars for many non-European languages.

## The 19th Century and the Comparative Method

The period's central achievement was a systematic way to compare sound systems, grammar, and vocabulary in order to show genealogical relationships among languages.

The comparative method ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Comparative_method)) showed that Latin, Greek, Sanskrit, Celtic, Germanic, Slavic, and many other languages descended from an earlier language now called Indo-European or Proto-Indo-European. Sir William Jones helped bring Sanskrit's resemblance to Greek and Latin to scholarly attention in 1786, and Jacob Grimm and later scholars developed regular sound correspondences into historical laws.

## Structuralism

Structuralism became a central slogan for several schools of linguistics. European structuralism is usually linked to Ferdinand de Saussure's posthumous Course in General Linguistics of 1916, though related ideas appeared earlier in Wilhelm von Humboldt and even in Saussure's own Indo-European vowel work.

Saussure distinguished langue, the language system, from parole, actual speech behaviour. He also separated form from substance. These ideas made language a system of relations, where units matter not as isolated sounds or meanings but through their place in a structured pattern.

American structuralism developed differently, especially through Franz Boas, Edward Sapir, and Leonard Bloomfield. It emphasized careful description, distribution, and field methods, especially for Indigenous American languages. Bloomfield's behaviourist and physicalist leanings later encouraged analysis of observable forms and discouraged mentalistic explanation.

## Phonology

Phonology grew from the realization that physical speech sounds vary even when speakers and hearers identify the same word. Absolute phonetic identity is an ideal, because two pronunciations of the same word can differ in measurable acoustic detail.

A phone is a speech sound considered phonetically, while a phoneme is a unit of contrast in a language system. English bit and pit differ because /b/ and /p/ contrast in initial position. Phonetic details such as voicing, aspiration, and muscular tension can vary, but phonemic identity is discrete: a sound belongs to one phoneme or another.

Different schools used different criteria for deciding whether phones represent the same phoneme. Post-Bloomfieldian linguists emphasized phonetic similarity and distribution. Phones occurring in complementary contexts could be grouped together when they were similar enough and did not create a meaning contrast.

The phonological question also shows the difference between surface variation and structural contrast. Minimal pairs, complementary distribution, free variation, and context all help determine whether sound differences matter linguistically or are predictable variants within one system.

## Morphology

Morphology describes the internal structure of words, while syntax describes how words combine into larger units. English unacceptability can be divided into elements such as un-, accept, -able or abil-, and -ity, showing that words often contain smaller grammatical units.

Bloomfield called minimal grammatical units morphemes and first defined them through recurring phonetic-semantic resemblance, such as un- in unacceptable, untrue, and ungracious. Later linguists modified this view because morphemes could not always be treated simply as concrete word segments with one fixed form.

Morphological analysis must handle roots, affixes, variants, word classes, and meaning. The uncertainty over whether accept should be further divided into ac- and -cept illustrates how analysis depends on patterns across related forms, not only on one word in isolation.

## Syntax

Syntax studies how forms combine into phrases, clauses, and sentences. Bloomfield treated syntax as the study of free forms made of free forms and placed special importance on form classes and constituent structure.

A form class is a set of forms that can substitute for one another in the same constructions. This idea resembles traditional parts of speech but does not match them exactly, because one form can belong to different classes depending on its distribution.

Immediate-constituent analysis explains how a phrase is built from smaller parts. In poor John, poor and John are immediate constituents; in lost his watch, the immediate constituents are lost and his watch, because his and watch first combine into the smaller construction his watch.

A sentence such as Poor John lost his watch is therefore not just a sequence of words. Its constituent structure is layered, with smaller forms grouped into larger phrases until the whole sentence is reached.

Tree diagrams can represent whole classes of sentences by placing form-class labels at nodes. If actual words are substituted for the labels, new sentences of the same structural type can be generated. This approach connects individual sentences with general patterns.

Constructions are endocentric when the whole construction belongs to the same class as one immediate constituent, as in poor John, where John is the head. They are exocentric when the whole does not match either immediate constituent in that way.

## Semantics

Semantics, the study of meaning, was treated by Bloomfield as the weak point of linguistic science. His behaviourist definition linked meaning to the situation in which an utterance is produced and the response it calls forth, but he thought a full scientific account of such situations was not yet possible.

Because of this view, many American structuralists neglected meaning for about thirty years after Bloomfield's Language of 1933. Exceptions included anthropological linguists and Bible translators, whose work required attention to meaning, culture, and lexical distinctions in the languages they described.

Later developments made meaning unavoidable again. Generative grammar ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Generative_grammar)) first emphasized syntax, but later versions included a semantic component, and other traditions connected meaning with fields, components, translation problems, and the relation between language and culture.

## Transformational-Generative Grammar

![[attachments/linguistics_004.webp|420]]

*Aspect's paradigm vs Generative Semantics' paradigm*

Phrase-structure rules show how a generative grammar can define well-formed sentences. A rule system begins with an initial symbol such as S for sentence and applies rules until no further rule can apply, producing a structural description.

A derivation of a sentence such as The man will hit the ball can be produced by applying phrase-structure rules in different orders. The important result is not the order of steps but the same tree diagram or phrase marker assigned to the sentence.

In generative grammar, to say that a grammar generates a sentence means that the sentence belongs to the class of well-formed sentences defined by the rules. Generation is static, like a mathematical formula defining many possible values, not a real-time act of producing one sentence after another.

Transformational grammar adds transformational rules to phrase-structure rules. These rules convert an underlying phrase marker into a derived phrase marker, allowing related sentence types to share a deeper structural base.

The passive transformation illustrates how Noam Chomsky's system differs from earlier sentence-to-sentence transformations. An underlying structure such as NP-Aux-V-NP can yield both an active sentence and a passive sentence through rules that permute and add elements.

In the passive rule, a structure underlying The man will hit the ball can become The ball will be hit by the man. The transformation treats noun phrases, auxiliary material, verb forms, and by-phrases as parts of a formal relation rather than as separate memorized sentences.

Derived phrase markers represent the changed structure of a transformed sentence. In a passive sentence, the ball becomes the grammatical subject even though the man remains the agent in meaning, showing how surface grammar and underlying relations can differ.

Chomsky's system changed substantially in 1965. The grammar came to include syntactic, semantic, and phonological components. The syntactic component assigned both deep structure and surface structure; semantic interpretation came mainly from deep structure, while phonetic realization came from surface structure.

The base component contained categorial rules and a lexicon, while transformations related underlying and derived structures. This model made syntax central, because it generated the structural forms that meaning and sound then interpreted.

Generative phonology also depended strongly on syntax. Instead of analyzing phonemes without grammatical information, generative grammarians argued that phonological rules often need syntactic structure, and that phonological representations may include abstract distinctive features rather than only surface sounds.

## Tagmemics

Tagmemics, developed by Kenneth L. Pike, aimed to analyze language and other patterned human behaviour. In language, it treated structure as organized across phonology, grammar, and lexicon rather than giving one mode absolute priority.

A tagmeme is a composite element of a construction, often described as slot-filler or function-class. A noun-as-subject tagmeme, for example, combines a class of possible fillers with the grammatical function they perform. This approach separated function from the particular forms that fill it.

## Borrowing

Borrowing occurs when languages take words from one another, often because a new object, institution, or practice lacks a local name. Renaissance financial vocabulary borrowed from Italian into other western European languages shows how cultural and economic importance can spread words.

In the 20th and 21st centuries, borrowing increased because inventions multiplied and international communication became rapid and important. Scientific and technological vocabulary became especially international.

The comparative method ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Comparative_method)) reconstructs earlier languages by comparing related words and expressions in daughter languages or dialects. It depends on regular sound change, the principle that the Neogrammarians defended strongly in the 1870s and that later became central to historical linguistics.

Historical language change involves both divergence and convergence. Communities that separate may develop different forms, while communities in contact may become more similar, especially when one is politically or culturally dominant.

Johannes Schmidt's wave theory of 1872 criticized the simple family-tree model. Innovations spread like waves from important centres along lines of communication, and different changes do not cover exactly the same territory, so neighbouring dialects may not divide sharply.

Internal reconstruction supplements comparison by using irregular patterns inside one language. Latin honos : honoris can suggest an earlier *honosis, because irregular alternations may preserve evidence of older regular patterns changed by sound laws.

## Psycholinguistics

Psycholinguistics was named in the 1940s and became more widely used after a 1954 survey edited by Charles E. Osgood and Thomas A. Sebeok. Its boundaries with linguistics and psychology are difficult to draw, but it focuses on language topics using both linguistic and psychological ideas.

Child language acquisition became a central psycholinguistic topic. Many researchers preferred acquisition to learning because children rapidly gain fluent control of native-language grammar in ways that ordinary learning theories seemed unable to explain.

From the 1960s, Chomsky's generative grammar shaped acquisition research. The key question was how children infer grammatical rules from limited speech and then produce utterances they have never heard. This led to the claim that children may be born with knowledge of formal principles common to human languages.

## Sociolinguistics

Sociolinguistics studies language in relation to society, but its boundary with sociology, anthropology, and psycholinguistics is not sharp. Some traditions, especially Chomskyan linguistics, connected language most closely with cognitive psychology, while other schools emphasized language's social and cultural setting.

The social and psychological views ultimately need connection. Language acquisition is part of socialization, and linguistic competence includes knowing how to use language appropriately in socially prescribed situations, not only how to produce grammatical sentences.

Sociolinguistics studies social variation, speech communities, language choice, multilingualism, standard languages, and the relation between dialects and social identity. It widens language study beyond isolated structure while still depending on linguistic description.

## Stylistics

Stylistics has both broad and narrow senses. Broadly, it studies synchronic variation other than regional dialect; narrowly, it applies linguistic analysis to literary texts, identifying features that give a work its individual character or produce aesthetic effects.

Applied linguistics most often means applying linguistic knowledge to language teaching. It also contributes to speech therapy, education for the deaf, and language planning, especially when governments must choose, standardize, or promote an official language or dialect.

## Dialectology

Dialectology, or disciplined dialect study, dates from the first half of the 19th century. Local dictionaries and grammars first described regional speech, and dialect maps later tried to divide language territory into areas.

Nationalism and Romantic interest in folklore gave dialectology strong momentum. Early fieldworkers often interviewed older, less-travelled, less-literate speakers, recording traits that differed from the standard language and later tracing the fate of old sounds and forms in local speech.

As evidence grew, scholars saw that dialects were not sharply bounded units. Each feature had its own isogloss, so linguistic atlases mapping individual traits became more accurate than maps pretending that whole dialect areas had clean borders.

The focus on dialect maps, isoglosses, and local speech variation shows how macrolinguistic study connects language history, geography, and social life.

## Key Events & Developments

- 1786: Sir William Jones argued that Sanskrit, Greek, and Latin came from a common source.
- 1822: Comparative work on Germanic sound correspondences helped advance historical linguistics.
- 1870s: The Neogrammarians promoted regular sound change as a principle of historical explanation.
- 1916: Ferdinand de Saussure's Course in General Linguistics helped define European structuralism.
- 1933: Leonard Bloomfield's Language shaped American structuralism and its caution about meaning.
- 1954: Charles E. Osgood and Thomas A. Sebeok's Psycholinguistics helped name a research field.
