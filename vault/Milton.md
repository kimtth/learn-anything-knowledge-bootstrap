# Milton

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> John Milton was an English poet and polemicist whose Paradise Lost, Paradise Regained, and Samson Agonistes made him a central epic poet. His prose interventions during the Civil Wars and Restoration defended conscience, toleration, and resistance to religious and political coercion.

## Introduction

![[attachments/milton_001_mw.webp|420]]

*Portrait of John Milton from Christ's College, Cambridge*

John Milton, born in [[London]] on December 9, 1608 and probably dying there on November 8, 1674, was an English poet, pamphleteer, historian, and civil servant. He is usually considered the most important English author after William Shakespeare.

Milton is best known for Paradise Lost ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paradise_Lost)), widely regarded as the greatest epic poem in English. With Paradise Regained ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paradise_Regained)) and Samson Agonistes ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samson_Agonistes)), it established him as one of the greatest English poets.

His prose defended liberty of conscience ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Freedom_of_thought)), religious toleration, Scripture as the highest guide in faith, and resistance to tyranny. From the Civil Wars through the Restoration, he opposed the Church of England, state-enforced religion, monarchy, and political oppression.

## Public Image

William Faithorne made an engraving of John Milton in 1670. Such portraits belong to Milton's later public image, when blindness, political danger, and poetic achievement had already made him a major figure.

## A Controversial Career

The National Portrait Gallery preserves visual memory of Milton as a public writer. His fame joined literary greatness with political controversy, because his career passed through civil war, regicide, republican service, and restored monarchy.

## Early Life and Education

Milton's family history was marked by religious conflict. His Roman Catholic grandfather expelled Milton's father for reading an English Protestant Bible. The elder John Milton became a London scrivener, moneylender, musician, and supporter of his son's education.

Milton studied at St. Paul's School, probably from 1620, and received private tutoring from Thomas Young, a Scottish Presbyterian. He learned Latin, Greek, Hebrew, Italian, and other languages, and formed an important friendship with Charles Diodati.

At Christ's College, Cambridge, Milton studied from 1625 to 1632. He earned degrees but did not enter the ministry, probably because he disliked church hierarchy, compulsory religious uniformity, and scholastic culture.

## Study and Travel

After Cambridge, Milton spent about six years in private study at Hammersmith and Horton, reading Greek and Latin authors while supported by his father. This period shaped his ideal of disciplined learning before public achievement.

In 1638, Milton traveled on the Continent, especially in [[Italy]]. He joined literary circles in [[Florence]] and [[Rome]], met admirers among Italian humanists, and visited the aged [[Galileo]], later the only contemporary named in Paradise Lost ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paradise_Lost)).

Milton returned to England early after hearing of Charles Diodati's death and the approach of civil war. His Latin elegy Epitaphium Damonis mourned Diodati and marked the emotional importance of that friendship.

## Early Translations and Poems

Milton showed early skill as a translator and poet. As a teenager he translated Psalm 114 from Hebrew into English heroic couplets, later translating and paraphrasing it into Greek.

His Latin elegies, verse letters, and poems from Cambridge show classical learning, religious argument, and early anti-Catholic polemic. In Quintum Novembris treats the Gunpowder Plot and already explores ideas later important in Paradise Lost ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paradise_Lost)): evil, providence, and God's ability to bring good from human sin.

On the Morning of Christ's Nativity presents Christ's birth as the defeat of pagan darkness through divine humility. On Shakespeare, Milton's first published English poem, argues that Shakespeare needs no stone monument because readers themselves become living monuments to his genius.

The companion poems L'Allegro and Il Penseroso contrast mirthful daytime activity with reflective darkness and melancholy. Together they suggest a balanced personality that joins pleasure, thought, imagination, and studious retirement.

## Comus and Lycidas

Comus, performed in 1634 at Ludlow Castle, is a masque written for the installation of John Egerton as lord president of Wales. Its plot follows three children through a forest, where the Lady is separated, deceived by the seducer Comus, and tested by temptation.

The debate between the Lady and Comus turns the masque into a moral drama. Comus defends appetite and sensual license, while the Lady defends right reason, self-control, chastity, and freedom of mind. Her rescue by her brothers and an attendant spirit represents the soul's victory over deception and temptation.

Lycidas, written in 1637, mourns Edward King, a Cambridge student drowned in the Irish Sea. It is a pastoral elegy that transforms King into the shepherd Lycidas and joins personal grief with anxiety about vocation, poetry, ministry, and divine justice.

The poem also attacks corrupt bishops and ministers of the Church of England. Its satire of the episcopacy anticipates Milton's later prose, while its final image of King rising heavenward turns grief into resurrection and judgment.

## Antiprelatical and Divorce Tracts

After returning from abroad, Milton turned from poetry to controversial prose. In 1641 to 1642, he wrote five antiprelatical tracts attacking the Church of England's bishops, its resemblance to Roman Catholic hierarchy, and the connection between church hierarchy and monarchy.

Milton first seemed to favour Presbyterianism as an alternative, but he later rejected rigid church systems more broadly. He increasingly grounded religious authority in Scripture and the conscience of each believer rather than in any established institution.

Milton married Mary Powell in 1642, but she soon returned to her Royalist family. During their separation he wrote four divorce tracts, arguing that marriage depends on mutual compatibility, love, and sympathy. A marriage without those qualities had already failed in substance, so forced continuation violated personal liberty.

## Of Education and Areopagitica

Of Education ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Of_Education)), published in 1644, presents a curriculum for the Renaissance gentleman. Milton emphasizes Greek and Latin as ways to reach classical wisdom directly and to prepare a man for public and private duties in peace and war.

Areopagitica, also published in 1644, is Milton's most famous prose defense of free expression. It opposes licensing and censorship, arguing that books carry living reason and that truth must be tested through open inquiry, experience, and debate.

## Antimonarchical Tracts and Commonwealth Service

After the execution of Charles I in 1649, Milton defended republican government. The Tenure of Kings and Magistrates argues that tyrannical kings may justly be deposed and executed, drawing on Scripture, classical sources, church fathers, political theory, and Reformation theology.

Milton became Latin secretary for the Council of State, translating foreign correspondence and defending the Commonwealth against attacks abroad. In Eikonoklastes, he answered Eikon Basilike, a work that portrayed Charles I as a pious martyr.

Milton also battled continental polemicists. His Latin Defense of the English People answered Claudius Salmasius, while The Second Defense answered attacks on the regicides and on Milton himself. By 1652, he was totally blind, probably from glaucoma, but he interpreted blindness as a trial accompanied by inner illumination.

## History, Logic, and Theology

Milton's late prose shows wide learning. History of Britain, published in 1670, grew from long research into early British history, including Anglo-Saxon materials, Bede, Geoffrey of Monmouth, Raphael Holinshed, and William Camden. It remained incomplete and ended with the Norman Conquest.

Artis Logicae, published in 1672, is a Latin textbook based on the logic of Petrus Ramus. De Doctrina Christiana, probably written between 1655 and 1660, is an unfinished systematic theology discovered in 1823. It draws heavily on Scripture and Protestant theology while showing Milton's tendency to subordinate the Son to the Father.

## Paradise Lost

After the Restoration, Milton published his three major late poems. Paradise Lost ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paradise_Lost)) appeared in 1667 in ten books and in 1674 in twelve books. It abandoned his earlier idea of an Arthurian epic and turned instead to biblical history, fall, redemption, and Christian heroism.

The poem adapts classical epic conventions but revises their values. It includes war, heroic conflict, elevated style, a muse, in medias res structure, epic similes, and a descent-like view of hell, but it rejects pride, revenge, and martial glory as true heroism.

The Son is heroic because he accepts humility, incarnation, suffering, and self-sacrifice for fallen humanity. Satan has energy, defiance, and classical grandeur, yet the poem uses him to expose the destructive pride behind pagan and martial heroism.

## Invocation and Style

At the opening of Paradise Lost ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paradise_Lost)), Milton invokes the Heavenly Muse connected with Horeb, Sinai, Sion, and Siloa. This replaces the pagan muse with the Judaeo-Christian Godhead and asks for inspiration like that given to [[Moses]].

The poem begins after the war in heaven, with the fallen angels in hell. Later books explain their rebellion and then move toward Satan's temptation of Eve. The arrangement makes disobedience, pride, and the consequences of sin central to the poem's structure.

Milton's blank verse rejects rhyme and uses enjambment, sonorous rhythm, and long movement. His epic similes compare Satan's shield to the moon seen through [[Galileo]]'s telescope, his spear to a mast, and fallen angels to leaves in Vallombrosa.

## Christian Heroism in Paradise Lost

Paradise Lost ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paradise_Lost)) seeks to "justify the ways of God to man" by showing that divine justice and mercy operate through fall and redemption. Though Satan succeeds in tempting Adam and Eve, the Son's future self-sacrifice gives humanity hope of regeneration.

The poem contrasts false heroism with Christian heroism. Satan resembles classical warriors in courage and ambition, but the Son's meekness, patience, obedience, and love define the higher heroic model.

## Paradise Regained

Paradise Regained ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paradise_Regained)), published with Samson Agonistes ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samson_Agonistes)) in 1671, is a brief four-book epic about Jesus' temptation in the wilderness. A tradition links its origin to Thomas Ellwood, who supposedly asked Milton what he would say about paradise found after writing about paradise lost.

The poem draws on Job and on the temptation narratives in Matthew and Luke. Jesus defeats Satan not by battle but through patience, fortitude, faith, reason, and refusal of ease, pleasure, worldly power, and false argument.

## Debates in Paradise Regained

Paradise Regained ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paradise_Regained)) unfolds as a series of debates. Satan offers worldly goods and persuasive sophistry, while Jesus answers with right reason grounded in faith. The poem is narrower than Paradise Lost ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paradise_Lost)), but it studies the mind of the Christian hero with psychological precision.

## Samson Agonistes

Samson Agonistes ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samson_Agonistes)) is a dramatic poem focused on the last day of Samson. Unlike the biblical account in Judges, it begins after Samson has revealed his secret to Dalila, been blinded, and become a captive of the Philistines.

Samson's conflict is inward. He moves through depression, self-pity, anger, suicidal thought, shame over pride, and fear that God has rejected him. When Dalila offers care, he rejects her and begins to recover moral clarity.

## Regeneration in Samson Agonistes

Samson debates Manoa, Dalila, and Harapha, and each encounter marks progress toward atonement and regeneration. He recognizes that pride in his strength made him vulnerable to temptation.

At the temple of Dagon, Samson again becomes God's instrument and destroys the Philistines. Milton presents him not simply as a muscular warrior but as a fallen person who, through suffering and repentance, regains divine favour.

## Later Years and Death

After the Restoration of Charles II, Milton was arrested and faced possible execution for his republican service, but supporters such as Christopher Milton, Andrew Marvell, and William Davenant may have helped secure clemency.

Milton continued to defend worship freedom and republican ideas. He likely died in [[London]] in 1674 from gout complications and was buried in St. Giles Cripplegate Church.

## Fame and Reputation

Paradise Lost ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paradise_Lost)) first won praise from a small circle, including Andrew Marvell. John Dryden adapted it into heroic couplets as The State of Innocence and Fall of Man, and by the late 17th century the poem had appeared in several editions, including Latin versions.

In the 18th century, Joseph Addison, Alexander Pope, [[Voltaire]], and Joseph Warton raised Milton's status, though Samuel Johnson later criticized his style. Romantic writers such as William Blake, Percy Bysshe Shelley, and Lord Byron admired Satan as a rebel.

In the 20th century, T.S. Eliot reduced Milton's reputation by attacking his verse as stiff. Later scholarship restored Milton's importance by reading his work through the religious, political, gender, and intellectual conflicts of 17th-century England.

## Interesting Facts

- Milton could compose in several languages, including Latin ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin)), Greek, Hebrew, and Italian.
- Paradise Lost ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paradise_Lost)) uses blank verse rather than rhyme because Milton considered rhyme a limiting ornament.
- [[Galileo]] is the only contemporary person named in Paradise Lost ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paradise_Lost)).
- Milton's blindness became part of his self-understanding as inward illumination rather than only punishment or loss.
- Areopagitica remains one of the classic English defenses of freedom of speech and publication.
