## Introduction

![[attachments/nutrition_001.webp|420]]

*MyPlate nutrition diagram*

Nutrition ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nutrition)) is the assimilation of food materials by living organisms so they can grow, maintain themselves, and reproduce. Food supplies energy for nutrient absorption, transport, synthesis, movement, excretion, and other activities. It also provides materials from which structural and catalytic parts of cells are built. Organisms differ in what they need, how they obtain or synthesize food substances, and how those substances function, but broad nutritional patterns recur across life.

## Nutritional Patterns

![[attachments/nutrition_002.webp|420]]

*Balanced meal plate*

Organisms may be classified by nutritional pattern. Autotrophs such as green plants and some bacteria need only inorganic compounds for growth, while heterotrophs, including animals, fungi, and many bacteria, require organic and inorganic compounds. Phototrophs use light, chemoautotrophs use chemical compounds, lithotrophs use inorganic electron donors, and organotrophs use organic electron donors. Higher plants are photolithotrophs, while animals are chemoorganotrophs.

## Herbivores

![[attachments/nutrition_003.webp|420]]

*Animal diet artifacts*

Herbivores face the problem that plant cell walls are mainly cellulose, which higher animals cannot digest directly. Ruminants such as cattle and sheep solve this through the rumen, where microorganisms ferment leaves, release cell contents, produce acetic acid and other short-chain fatty acids, and synthesize some water-soluble vitamins. Ruminal fermentation also hydrogenates dietary fats, making beef fat and butterfat relatively saturated.

## Carnivores

Carnivores form a small portion of the animal kingdom because each predator needs many prey animals over its lifetime. They have teeth, claws, and digestive enzymes for breaking muscle protein into amino acids, and they can use animal fat and bones. Obligate carnivores such as cats cannot obtain all nutrients from plants or bacteria and must get preformed vitamin A and certain very-long-chain unsaturated fatty acids from prey.

## Omnivores

Omnivores have teeth and digestive systems suited to concentrated diets, without large fermentation chambers. Humans, dogs, rodents, most monkeys, and chickens are examples. Omnivores can digest meat but do not always require it, except when no other practical source of vitamin B12 exists. Their intestinal bacteria produce short-chain fatty acids, and some animals obtain vitamins through coprophagy.

## Nutrients and Minerals

Nutrients include inorganic materials and organic compounds that organisms cannot synthesize sufficiently. Nonessential nutrients may still be used directly when present in food, saving energy. Essential minerals vary by organism: boron is important for higher plants, fluorine supports tooth formation, iodine is needed for thyroxine, silicon is required by diatoms, and calcium is needed in large amounts by animals for bone and eggshells.

## Carbohydrates

Carbohydrates, especially those synthesized by plants, supply much of the energy used by animals. Sugars in fruits attract animals that disperse seeds, while sucrose accumulates in sugarcane and sugar beet. Complex carbohydrates must be broken into simple sugars before use. Sucrose requires sucrase, lactose requires lactase, and starch digestion depends on molecular structure and cooking, which makes starch granules swell and become more digestible.

## Proteins

Proteins are major working materials in plant and animal tissues, built from about 20 amino acids. Animals digest protein into free amino acids, while plants synthesize amino acids from nitrogenous compounds and sulfur. Some amino acids are essential in animal diets. Growth increases protein demand, which is reflected in milk composition; fast-growing piglets receive protein-rich milk, while human infants grow more slowly and require less protein energy proportionally.

## Vitamins

Vitamins are organic substances needed in small amounts, usually as enzyme-related catalysts, and deficiencies can cause disease, growth failure, or death. Vitamin requirements are organism-specific. Vitamin A can be formed from carotene by most animals but not cats. Vitamin D is produced through sunlight in many species and functions hormonally in calcium and phosphate regulation. Vitamin C is essential in tissues, but many species synthesize it themselves.

## Syntrophism

Syntrophism occurs when different organisms support one another nutritionally. In a balanced aquarium, plants use light and animal waste products to make cell material and oxygen, while animals use plant-derived materials. Among microorganisms, one organism's metabolic products may supply another's needs. Some yeasts and fungi survive together in thiamin-free media because each supplies the part of thiamin the other lacks. Such relationships can become mutualism.

## Key Events & Developments

- Autotrophy and heterotrophy: Basic categories describe how organisms obtain food materials.
- Rumen fermentation: Microorganisms allow ruminants to use cellulose-rich plants.
- Vitamin research: Organism-specific vitamin requirements explained many deficiency diseases.
- MyPlate: A revised U.S. dietary-guideline system emphasized human nutrition patterns.

## Key People

- U.S. Department of Agriculture ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/U.S._Department_of_Agriculture)): Introduced MyPlate dietary guidance.
- Ruminant microorganisms: Bacterial communities that ferment cellulose and synthesize nutrients.
- Intestinal bacterial flora: Microbial populations that synthesize vitamins and support host nutrition.

## Interesting Facts

- Cellulose is abundant but unusable to many animals without microbial help.
- Cats are obligate carnivores and need preformed vitamin A.
- Vitamin D is not a true dietary vitamin for many species because sunlight can generate it.
- Coprophagy lets some animals recover vitamins synthesized by gut bacteria.
