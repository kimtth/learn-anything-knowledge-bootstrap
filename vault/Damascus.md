# Damascus

## Introduction

Damascus, the capital of [[Syria]], is one of the most celebrated cities of the Middle East. It lies in the southwestern part of the country and has long been praised for beauty, age, and fertility. The geographer al-Maqdisi ranked it among the earthly paradises, and Mark Twain described it in 1867 as a city that seemed to measure time by empires rather than by years.

The Arabic name Dimashq may come from a pre-Semitic word, suggesting very ancient origins. The city is also called al-Sham, the common name for Syria as a whole, and al-Fayha, or the Fragrant, perhaps because of its orchards and gardens. Damascus has been conqueror and conquered, capital and provincial city, but its fame has endured through commerce, learning, religion, and migration.

## Landscape

Water and geography explain the location of Damascus. The Barada River rises in the Anti-Lebanon Mountains, waters the fertile oasis of al-Ghutah, and then disappears into the desert. This oasis made large settlement possible in an otherwise dry region.

The city stands about 2,250 feet, or 690 metres, above sea level, south of Mount Qasiyun and overlooking the river. The earliest settlement probably lay in the eastern part of the walled Old City. Over time, city and oasis grew together, and Damascus came to dominate nearby rural settlements.

Irrigation was crucial. From the 2nd millennium BCE onward, successive rulers developed branches from the Barada that expanded al-Ghutah, especially east and west. The city also benefited from its position at the desert edge and at the eastern end of an easy route through the Anti-Lebanon range. This made it a caravan centre and, after Islam, the starting point of the northern pilgrimage road to Mecca and Medina.

## Climate

Damascus has a dry inland climate. It is about 50 miles, or 80 kilometres, from the sea, but two mountain ranges block much maritime moisture. The city receives only about 7 inches, or 178 millimetres, of rain each year, mostly from November to February.

The Anti-Lebanon Mountains receive more rain and winter snow, and this replenishes the water table that feeds the Barada River and smaller springs. Winters are fairly cold because of the city’s elevation, with average temperatures around 40 to 45 °F, or 5 to 7 °C. Spring is short, followed by six or seven months of hot dry summer, when midseason averages are near 80 °F, or 27 °C, and sometimes reach 100 °F, or 38 °C.

## People

The population of Damascus grew more than tenfold during the second half of the 20th century. Rural migrants came for work and education, making the city grow faster than Syria as a whole and lowering the average age of Damascenes.

The city’s majority is Arab Sunni Muslim, but Damascus includes many minorities. Alawites from the coastal mountains are especially important in the army and intelligence services. Druze, Kurds, Circassians, Twelver Shiites, Ismailis, Palestinians, and several Christian communities maintain distinct identities. The Jewish community, once flourishing, declined sharply because many Jews emigrated in the late 20th century after travel and employment restrictions were eased.

## Economy

[[The Forms Of Government Their Historical Development (Government)|Government]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Government)) is the most important economic activity in Damascus. National administration, politics, the military, and secret services are centred in the capital. This gives the city a command role that reaches far beyond municipal affairs.

Damascus has long been known for manufactured goods, especially textiles. Since the mid-20th century, new industries have included textiles, chemicals, cement, and food processing, mainly south, east, and northeast of the city. Major factories and strategic industries are state-run, while private firms became more active in small service businesses from the 1990s onward.

Traditional artisan crafts remain important in the Old City, including copper engraving, mother-of-pearl inlaid woodwork, and brocade. The city’s older role as a desert port changed as modern imports shifted to Syrian ports such as Latakia, Tartus, and Baniyas. Damascus still distributes goods within Syria and to the Arabian Peninsula, hosts an international trade exposition, and has potential for cultural tourism. Financial liberalization under Bashar al-Assad in the early 2000s was limited, though a stock exchange eventually opened after delays.

## Transportation

Damascus is connected by highways to [[Beirut]], Amman, Aleppo, and [[Baghdad]]. A rail line north to Homs links the city with the national railway system, and trucking carries imported products into the capital.

Damascus International Airport, about 20 miles, or 32 kilometres, east of the city, serves regional and international flights. Within the city, buses, taxis, microbuses, and shared-route cars carry most passengers. Animal-drawn carts have mostly disappeared except in poorer districts. Since the early 1980s, private car growth without matching street expansion has created congestion, especially in commercial and administrative areas. Belt roads and satellite projects such as Dummar and Qura al-Assad were intended to reduce pressure.

## Government

![[attachments/damascus_003.webp|420]]

*Damascus, Syria, Panoramic view of Damascus*

Damascus is administered as a muhafazah, or governorate. The president of Syria appoints the governor, who runs the city with a council of elected and appointed members. The city is divided into quarters, each with a mayor appointed by the governor.

The post of governor of Damascus has national importance because Syria is highly centralized and political activity is national rather than municipal. The surrounding oasis and rural district form a separate governorate called Rif Dimashq, or Rural Damascus, though Damascus city remains its capital.

## Education

[[Education]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Education)) in Damascus benefits from Syria’s relatively high literacy rates by regional standards. Primary education is mandatory, and public schools provide primary and secondary education for most children. Private schools supplement the public system, and the [[United Nations]] runs separate schools for Palestinian refugee children.

The University of Damascus was founded in 1923 by joining four older institutions. It became a pioneer in the Arab world by using Arabic as the sole language of instruction and research. It is Syria’s oldest and largest university, and the city also has technical and advanced research institutes.

## Monuments and Heritage

![[attachments/damascus_001.webp|420]]

*Umayyad Mosque, Damascus*

Damascus is Syria’s cultural and political centre. Its major monuments include the Great Mosque of the Umayyad period and the tomb of Saladin. The Old City preserves layers of Hellenistic, Roman, Islamic, Ottoman, and later urban history.

The cultural institutions of Damascus include the Arabic Language Academy, founded in 1919, which works to preserve and modernize Arabic. The National Museum, established in 1936, displays artifacts from six millennia of Syrian civilization. Other institutions include a military museum in the Ottoman takiyyah of Suleyman I, a Museum of Arabic Calligraphy in a Mamluk madrasah, and the Museum of Arts and Popular Traditions in al-Azm Palace.

The modern arts have also grown in Damascus. Institutes support music, theatre, and folklore, and the Dar al-Assad for Culture and Arts opened in 2004. Private galleries expanded from the late 20th century, though publishing, journalism, and artistic expression remained limited by state control and bureaucratic restriction.

## History

Damascus is ancient, but its exact first settlement remains uncertain. Excavations in 1950 showed an urban centre from the 4th millennium BCE at Tall al-Salhiyyah, southeast of the city. Pottery from the 3rd millennium BCE was found in the Old City, and Damaski appears in an Ebla clay tablet from the same period.

The first certain written reference to Damascus ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Damascus)) appears in Egyptian records from Thutmose III in 1490 BCE. In the 1st millennium BCE, it became the capital of an Aramaean principality. The Aramaeans left traces in canal systems, place-names, and the use of Aramaic, which remained a wider Levantine language until Islam.

Before the Common Era, Damascus passed under Assyrian, Babylonian, Persian, Greek, and Roman rule. [[Alexander the Great]] brought it into the Hellenistic world in 333 BCE. Under Rome, Damascus gained the status of a metropolis under Hadrian and a colonia under Severus Alexander. Its Old City kept a Hellenistic and Roman plan, with a main east-west street and major gates.

In the Islamic period, Damascus ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Damascus)) reached one of its greatest moments as the capital of the Umayyad empire at the beginning of the 7th century. Later it remained an important city under successive Muslim dynasties and empires, including periods linked with rulers such as Nur al-Din and Saladin. Through all these changes, the city kept its role as a commercial, religious, and intellectual centre of the Levant.
