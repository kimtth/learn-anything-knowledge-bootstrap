## Introduction

Time ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Time)) is a measured or measurable period and a continuum without spatial dimensions. It is central to philosophy, mathematics, science, calendars, clocks, history, religious thought, and daily social life.

Time seems more puzzling than space because it appears to flow or pass. Yet asking how fast time flows leads to absurdity, because a rate of flow would require another time scale outside time itself.

In a spacetime ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spacetime)) picture, ordinary change can be represented as world lines, but the moving present itself is difficult to locate inside that picture.

Philosophers often divide between process views and manifold views. Process thinkers treat temporal flow or becoming as real, while manifold philosophers often treat temporal flow as an illusion within a spacetime structure.

## Experience and Prescientific Ideas

Human awareness of time is shaped by [[Death|death]], aging, memory, expectation, and the irreversibility of life. People know that growth leads to decay and that life can end at any moment.

Religions have interpreted time through afterlife, resurrection, rebirth, cycles, salvation, judgment, and release. Zoroastrianism, Judaism, Christianity, and Islam often connect death with future life, while Buddhism, Orphism, Pythagoreanism, and Plato include forms of rebirth.

Prescientific life was dominated by cycles: day and night, lunar phases, seasons, planting, harvest, birth, decay, and return. These repeating patterns encouraged cyclical views of time in many cultures.

Industrial society weakened some natural rhythms by making work possible under artificial light, controlled temperature, and clock time. Modern life increasingly treats time as schedule, resource, and measurable unit.

## Philosophical Time

Henri Bergson argued that scientific time as a dimension misrepresents lived duration. For him, real time is experienced as flowing, qualitative, and grasped by intuition rather than by measurement alone.

Alfred North Whitehead and other process philosophers treated becoming as metaphysically important. They resisted views that reduce reality to a static manifold of events.

Manifold philosophers argue that spacetime ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spacetime)) already contains events, so the idea of the moving present or advancing consciousness is hard to explain. Future events do not need to “pop into existence” if they are part of the four-dimensional structure.

## Newton, Kant, and Science

Isaac Newton distinguished absolute time from relative or apparent time measured by stars and clocks. Absolute time was an ideal scale that made mechanics simpler.

Newton's view encouraged the idea of absolute space and time as metaphysical entities. Immanuel Kant later treated space and time as phenomenally real forms of experience but not properties of things in themselves.

Kant argued through antinomies about beginnings and limits, though later logic and mathematics led many philosophers to find fallacies in those arguments.

Nineteenth-century science brought new interest in systems that develop through time, especially through [[Theory of Evolution (Evolution)|evolution]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Evolution)), thermodynamics, history, geology, and biology.

This shift made time central not only to mechanics but also to irreversible processes, natural history, and explanations of development.

Although defining time is difficult, measuring it is highly successful. Time measurement assigns numbers to epochs, which are moments of events, or intervals, which are durations.

Any regular phenomenon can serve as a clock. Astronomy, physics, chemistry, geology, and biology all use changing processes to measure or infer time.

A time scale assigns numbers either to epochs, meaning moments of events, or to intervals, meaning durations between events.

Two fundamental time scales are dynamical time, based on celestial motions governed by gravitation, and atomic time, based on electromagnetic radiation from atomic quantum transitions.

The Earth's rotation provides a familiar time scale but not a perfectly uniform one. Tidal friction, core motions, and seasonal effects change the rotation rate.

## Atomic Clocks and Units

The atomic clock ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atomic_clock)) provides the most precise time scale. It supports highly accurate measurements of time, distance, radar, lasers, spacecraft, radio astronomy, pulsars, celestial mechanics, relativity, and astrophysics.

Because atomic time is so stable, one synchronized atomic clock ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atomic_clock)) can provide the SI second, TAI, UTC, and terrestrial dynamical time with high accuracy.

Modern scientific and legal time uses the SI second, defined by atomic phenomena. A minute is 60 seconds, an hour is 3,600 seconds, and an astronomical day is 86,400 seconds.

The traditional division of the day into 24 hours, the hour into 60 minutes, and the minute into 60 seconds goes back to ancient Egypt.

## Calendars

Calendars organize longer periods. The Christian year-numbering system was devised by Dionysius Exiguus about 525, using AD and BC without a year zero.

The Julian calendar, introduced by Julius Caesar, made every fourth year a leap year. The Gregorian calendar, introduced in 1582, improved the rule by making most centurial years common unless divisible by 400.

Calendar days can differ from strict astronomical or atomic measures because civil time must remain linked to Earth's rotation, social life, and legal convention.

## Universal Time and Earth Rotation

Greenwich Mean Time became Universal Time after changes in astronomical convention. UT is tied to Earth's rotation and the zero meridian.

Astronomers once began the Greenwich astronomical day at noon, but the convention changed so that civil and astronomical reckoning could align more clearly.

Different forms of UT correct for polar motion and changes in Earth's rotation. UT0 is observed locally; UT1 corrects for polar motion; UT2 added empirical seasonal correction but has largely been superseded by atomic scales.

Earth's rotation varies in secular, irregular, and periodic ways. Relative to dynamical time, the mean solar day slowly lengthens, and rotational time loses about 30 seconds per century squared.

## Coordinated Universal Time and Leap Seconds

Coordinated Universal Time, or UTC, links atomic precision with civil time based on Earth's rotation. It is kept close to UT1 by inserting leap seconds when needed.

Leap seconds show the tension between atomic time and astronomical time. Atomic clocks are uniform, but Earth does not rotate uniformly.

## Ephemeris and Dynamical Time

Ephemeris Time was created to provide a dynamical time scale based on Earth's orbital motion rather than its irregular rotation. It was adopted by the International Astronomical Union in the 1950s.

The ephemeris second was defined as a fraction of the tropical year beginning near 1900. It later helped connect astronomical time with the atomic definition of the SI second.

Later scales included Barycentric Dynamical Time and Terrestrial Dynamical Time, used for astronomical theories and ephemerides. These scales support precise calculation of celestial positions.

## Core Equations

Physical models often begin by relating force, energy, waves, and conserved quantities:

$$\vec{F}=m\vec{a}$$

This is Newton's second law: force is the product of mass and acceleration, so a larger mass or a faster change in motion requires a larger net force.

$$E=h\nu$$

This relation says that photon energy is proportional to frequency; higher-frequency radiation carries more energy per photon.

$$E=mc^2$$

This expresses mass-energy equivalence: mass is a form of energy, with the speed of light squared as the conversion factor.

## Key Events & Developments

- Ancient Egypt: the day was divided into 24 hours, with sexagesimal subdivisions later used for minutes and seconds.
- 525: Dionysius Exiguus devised Christian Era year numbering.
- 1582: the Gregorian calendar was introduced.
- 1928: the term Universal Time was adopted by the International Astronomical Union.
- 1952: Ephemeris Time was adopted as a dynamical scale.
- 1967: the SI second was redefined on the basis of atomic time.

## Interesting Facts

- Time ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Time)) is the most accurately measured physical quantity, even though its nature remains philosophically difficult.
- Leap seconds exist because atomic clocks are steadier than Earth's rotation.
- Atomic clocks make modern navigation, astronomy, telecommunications, and relativity tests possible.
- Civil calendars mix astronomy, religion, politics, and practical social needs.
