## Introduction

Electricity ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electricity)) is the set of phenomena connected with electric charges, whether the charges are at rest or moving. Electric charge is a basic property of matter, and in ordinary electrical effects the most important charged particle is the electron. By convention, the electron has negative charge. Many visible forms of electricity come from either a build-up of electrons in one place or the motion of many electrons through matter.

## Electrostatics

Electrostatics ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrostatics)) studies electric effects when charges are not moving, after a static equilibrium has been reached. Charges usually settle into equilibrium very quickly because electric forces are extremely strong. Once the charges are fixed, electrostatics can calculate the electric field and electric potential produced by known charges, conductors, and insulators.

### Coulomb's law

Static electricity ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Static_electricity)) is a familiar example of electrostatics. When two objects are rubbed together, especially when they are insulators and the air is dry, electrons can move from one object to the other. The object that loses electrons becomes positively charged, while the object that gains them becomes negatively charged. Opposite charges attract, so the two objects pull toward each other.

### Calculating the value of an electric field
The force between charges is explained through the idea of an electric field ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electric_field)). A charge creates a field in the space around it. Another charge placed in that field feels a force. The field made by a point charge is a vector field: its magnitude decreases as the square of the distance from the charge, and its direction points away from a positive charge and toward a negative charge.

### Superposition principle

The superposition principle says that a field made by several sources is found by adding the fields produced by each source separately. This is why the three-charge example can be solved by finding the field due to each nearby charge and then adding those vector fields.

### Electric potential

Electric potential is a scalar function connected with work. The potential difference between two points is defined by the work an external force must do to move a small test charge slowly from one point to the other while other charges remain at rest. Potential is measured in volts, and one volt is one joule per coulomb.

### Deriving electric field from potential

The electric field ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electric_field)) can be derived from the electric potential when the potential is known at every point. In vector calculus language, the electric field is the negative gradient of the potential. In simpler terms, the field points in the direction where the potential falls fastest, and its magnitude depends on how quickly the potential changes over distance.

## Capacitance

Capacitance concerns the ability of a device to store electric charge and energy. A basic capacitor has two conductors close to each other but insulated from each other. The parallel-plate capacitor is the simplest model, with two flat conducting plates of area A separated by a distance d.

### Principle of the capacitor

The principle of the capacitor is explained through the work needed to charge it. At first, when both plates are uncharged, it takes little work to move a small amount of negative charge from one plate to the other. As the upper plate becomes more negative and the lower plate more positive, the same movement becomes harder because the charge is being moved toward like charge and away from opposite charge.

### Dielectrics, polarization, and electric dipole moment

Dielectrics are insulating materials with no free charges. They can increase a capacitor's ability to store charge by reducing the electric field and potential difference for a given amount of charge. This allows the capacitance to increase by a factor connected with the dielectric constant of the material.

### Applications of capacitors

Capacitors are used in digital circuits to keep stored information from being lost during brief power failures. The stored electric energy can maintain memory for a short time when the main supply drops.

## Direct electric current

Direct electric current concerns electric flow under steady-state conditions. In such a state, current, voltage, and charge distribution do not change with time. A car headlight filament is one example: if the current through the filament is steady, the brightness stays steady.

### Basic phenomena and principles

In a steady electric circuit, charge keeps flowing even though the charge distribution remains constant. This requires a device that maintains fixed potentials while charge moves. Such a device is a source of electromotive force, or emf.

### Conductors, insulators, and semiconductors

Materials are classified as conductors, insulators, or semiconductors according to their electrical conductivity. this through energy levels in atoms and energy bands in solids. In a solid, many atomic levels combine into bands, and regions with no allowed levels are called band gaps.

### Electromotive force

Electromotive force is the ability of a device to maintain a potential difference while current flows. A 12-volt car battery, for example, keeps its terminals near 12 volts for a considerable time by continually replenishing separated positive and negative charge.

### Direct-current circuits

A simple direct-current circuit contains a resistor connected across a source of electromotive force. The source maintains a potential difference, and the connecting wires are treated as having negligible resistance. For example, a 12-volt emf is connected to a 60-ohm resistor.

### Resistors in series and parallel

Resistors in series are connected so that all charge must pass through them one after another. Their equivalent resistance is the sum of the individual resistances. This is like making a wire longer; a longer path gives more resistance. As an example, 5 ohms plus 2 ohms gives 7 ohms.

### Kirchhoff's laws of electric circuits

Gustav Robert Kirchhoff formulated two laws that make it possible to solve even fairly complex circuits. The first law applies at a junction of conductors. It says that the sum of currents entering a junction equals the sum of currents leaving it, which is a statement of charge conservation.

## Alternating electric currents

Alternating electric currents involve voltages and currents that change with time, often sinusoidally. Electric power transmitted over long distances uses alternating voltage: 60 hertz in the United States and Canada and 50 hertz in Europe. A frequency of 60 hertz means the current reverses direction 60 times each second in each direction.

### Oscillators and circuit components

Oscillators are circuits that generate oscillating currents. They may contain transistors or vacuum tubes, but they also depend on basic components such as resistors, capacitors, and inductors. Each component affects alternating current in a different way.

### Transient response

Transient response describes how a circuit behaves while it is moving from one state to another. A common example is an RC circuit with a resistor and capacitor. When a switch suddenly connects point a to a 50-volt battery, the capacitor initially has no charge and does not block current.

### Alternating-current circuits

Alternating-current circuits often use sources whose voltage is sinusoidal, such as V = V0 cos(omega t) or V = V0 sin(omega t). The voltage varies between positive and negative peak values. The angular frequency omega is measured in radians per second.

### Behaviour of an AC circuit

The series LRC circuit contains a resistor, an inductor, a capacitor, and a sinusoidal emf source in one loop. The letters R, L, and C stand for resistance in ohms, inductance in henrys, and capacitance in farads. Since there is only one current, Kirchhoff's second law is enough to write the loop equation.

### Resonance

Resonance occurs in the series LRC circuit when the phase angle is zero and the angular frequency equals 1 divided by the square root of LC. At this frequency, the impedance is at its minimum and equals the resistance. The current amplitude reaches its maximum.

## Electric properties of matter

Electric properties of matter include the ways solids, metals, semiconductors, and biological tissues respond to electric fields, temperature differences, light, stress, and charge flow. These effects begin with piezoelectricity and extend through optical, thermal, emission, light-sensitive, luminescent, and biological effects.

### Piezoelectricity

Piezoelectricity, literally pressure electricity, occurs in certain crystals when mechanical stress produces electric polarization. If a thin quartz slice is compressed between electrodes, a potential difference appears. Conversely, when quartz is placed in an electric field, the field changes its dimensions.

### Electro-optic phenomena

Electro-optic phenomena occur because the refractive index of a transparent substance is connected with its electric polarizability. Since the susceptibility of many materials changes slightly with electric field, applying a field can change the index of refraction.

### Thermoelectricity

Thermoelectricity begins when two metals touch. Electrons flow from the metal where they are less bound into the other metal until the electrostatic potential brings their Fermi levels to the same value. This potential difference is called the contact potential.

### Thermionic emission

Thermionic emission is the escape of electrons from a heated metal. Mobile electrons in the metal's conduction band are bound to the metal, and the energy needed to free one is the work function. Heating gives some electrons enough energy through thermal collisions to escape.

### Secondary electron emission

Secondary electron emission occurs when incoming electrons strike a surface in a vacuum and transfer energy to electrons near the surface. Some of those excited electrons escape from the material and form a secondary emission current.

### Photoelectric conductivity

Photoelectric conductivity begins with the photoelectric effect. If light with photon energy greater than the work function falls on a metal, some photons transfer energy to electrons and eject them from the metal. The excess energy becomes kinetic energy of the emitted electrons.

### Electroluminescence

Electroluminescence is light produced when electrons in a solid gain enough energy from an electric field to excite atoms, which then emit radiation as they return to lower energy states. A voltage across a thin layer of zinc sulfide powder can produce this effect.

### Bioelectric effects

Bioelectricity refers to electric currents or voltages produced or used in biological processes. It includes rapid signaling in nerves and the triggering of activity in muscles or glands. similarities among nerves, muscles, and glands in many organisms, possibly because efficient electrochemical systems evolved early.

## Key Events & Developments

- The 1930s: Robert J. Van de Graaff invented the Van de Graaff generator, a mechanical source of electromotive force later used as a particle accelerator for studying subatomic particles.
- The late 1980s: Higher-temperature superconducting materials were discovered, allowing cooling with liquid nitrogen rather than only liquid helium and opening possible uses in power transmission and high-speed computing.
- Nov. 7, 1940: The Tacoma Narrows Bridge collapsed after wind-driven resonance produced very large oscillations, an example used to clarify resonance in electrical circuits.
- Thermocouple development: The use of dissimilar metal and semiconductor junctions made temperature measurement and compact Peltier refrigeration practical through the Seebeck and Peltier effects.
- Semiconductor light emission: Electroluminescent injection diodes and semiconductor lasers developed from the way electron-hole recombination in compound semiconductors can produce visible, infrared, or communication-grade light.

## Key People

- Gustav Robert Kirchhoff ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gustav_Robert_Kirchhoff)): Formulated the circuit laws used to relate currents at junctions and potential changes around loops in direct-current and alternating-current circuits.
- Robert J. Van de Graaff ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_J._Van_de_Graaff)): Invented the Van de Graaff generator in the 1930s, showing a clear mechanical example of electromotive force.
- Thomas Young: Connected to Young's modulus, the elastic constant used in the analysis of stress, strain, and piezoelectric effects.
- John Kerr: Associated with the Kerr effect, in which the refractive index of certain materials changes in proportion to the square of an applied electric field.
- F. R. Pockels: Associated with the Pockels effect, where the refractive index of noncentrosymmetric crystals changes linearly with applied electric field.
- Thomas Johann Seebeck ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Johann_Seebeck)): Gave his name to the Seebeck effect, the generation of thermal electromotive force from a temperature difference between junctions.
- Jean-Charles Peltier ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jean-Charles_Peltier)): Gave his name to the Peltier effect, the absorption or release of heat at a current-carrying junction.
- Owen W. Richardson: Proposed Richardson's law for thermionic emission current from heated metals.

## Interesting Facts

- Electric fields are strongest near sharp outside corners of charged conductors, which is why electrical breakdown is most likely at those points.
- Field lines in electrostatics always meet equipotential surfaces and conductor surfaces at right angles.
- One farad equals one coulomb per volt, but many ordinary capacitors are measured in much smaller units such as picofarads or microfarads.
- Wet skin can have hundreds of times less resistance than dry skin, making the same voltage far more dangerous.
- Quartz oscillators can be extremely precise because quartz has a very large quality factor and a stable piezoelectric response.
- Radio tuning works by changing a circuit's capacitance until its resonant frequency matches the frequency of the desired radio wave.
- Silicon has a band gap of about 1.1 electron volts, so light can create electron-hole pairs and strongly change its conductivity.
- Electric eels can produce shocks as large as 1,000 volts by adding the voltages of many specialized biological cells in series.
