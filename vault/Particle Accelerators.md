# Particle Accelerators

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> Particle accelerators use electric fields to speed charged particles, magnetic fields to guide them, vacuum systems to prevent unwanted collisions, and detectors to measure results. Their development made nuclear and particle research possible while supporting radioisotopes, radiography, therapy, sterilization, and dating.

## Introduction

Particle accelerators ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Particle_accelerator)) are devices that produce beams of fast-moving electrically charged atomic or subatomic particles. Physicists use them to study nuclei, nuclear forces, unstable elements, transuranium elements, and fundamental interactions among elementary particles. They also serve practical purposes: radioisotope production, industrial radiography, radiation therapy, sterilization, and some radiocarbon dating.

Every accelerator needs a particle source, electric fields to accelerate particles, magnetic fields to guide them, a high vacuum to avoid collisions with air, and detectors to count and measure particles after acceleration. Electrons, protons, ions, antiparticles, and heavy ions may be accelerated depending on the machine.

## Principles of Acceleration

Electric fields ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electric_field)) supply the force that increases particle energy, while magnetic fields bend and focus the beam. In fixed-target experiments, much beam energy goes into recoil of the target because momentum is conserved. In colliding-beam machines, two beams collide head-on so more energy is available for creating new particles and probing subatomic structure.

## Colliding Beams and Antiparticles

Particle-antiparticle colliders ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Collider)) use the fact that antiparticles have opposite electric charge. An electron and positron traveling in opposite directions can be guided by the same magnets and accelerated by the same electric fields in a single ring. Because beams are bunched rather than continuous, only a tiny fraction of particles collide at each crossing, so beams must circulate repeatedly through interaction points.

## Early History

Accelerator development was driven by nuclear physics. After [[Rutherford (Ernest Rutherford)|Ernest Rutherford]]'s 1919 discovery of a nuclear reaction between nitrogen and an alpha particle, physicists sought artificial sources of higher-energy ions. George Gamow's 1928 calculation showed that lower-energy ions could still penetrate nuclei by quantum effects, encouraging practical accelerator construction.

## First Artificial Acceleration

In 1932, John Douglas Cockcroft and E.T.S. Walton used a voltage multiplier at Cambridge to accelerate protons to about 710 keV and split lithium nuclei into alpha particles. Robert J. Van de Graaff built the first belt-charged electrostatic generator in 1931. Cockcroft-Walton multipliers and Van de Graaff generators remained useful accelerator power sources.

## Linear Resonance and Cyclotrons

![[attachments/particle_accelerators_001.png|420]]

*Diagram of a cyclotron particle accelerator*

Rolf Wideroe demonstrated the linear resonance accelerator principle in 1928, using alternating voltage to accelerate ions beyond a single-voltage gain. Ernest O. Lawrence and David H. Sloan extended the idea in 1931. Lawrence then conceived the cyclotron, and M.S. Livingston demonstrated it in 1931. By the later 1930s, cyclotrons reached about 25 MeV.

## Phase Stability and Synchrotrons

After World War II, Edwin Mattison McMillan and Vladimir Iosifovich Veksler independently described phase stability in 1945. This principle allowed particles to remain synchronized with accelerating fields in cyclic machines, enabling synchrocyclotrons, electron synchrotrons, and later high-energy proton synchrotrons. Phase focusing removed major apparent limits on resonance accelerators.

## Betatrons

A betatron accelerates electrons by magnetic induction. Electrons circulate in a torus as a changing magnetic field both guides and accelerates them. If the average magnetic field inside the orbit remains twice the field at the orbit, the radius stays constant. Donald W. Kerst built the first betatron in 1940. Betatrons can produce intense X-rays but are limited by synchrotron radiation from electrons moving in curved paths.

## Classical Cyclotrons

Cyclotrons use a constant magnetic field and fixed-frequency alternating electric field. Ions begin at the centre of a flat cylindrical chamber and spiral outward through D-shaped electrodes called dees, gaining energy each time they cross the gap. The method works because nonrelativistic ions in a uniform magnetic field have nearly isochronous orbits, making repeated acceleration possible.

## Synchrocyclotrons

Synchrocyclotrons, also called frequency-modulated cyclotrons or phasotrons, vary the accelerating frequency as particles gain relativistic mass. Phase stability allows particles arriving early or late to be corrected toward stable acceleration. This overcomes the classical cyclotron's relativistic limitation and permits stronger magnetic focusing.

## Linear Electron Accelerators

Linear electron accelerators use microwaves, often near 3,000 MHz, in an evacuated waveguide slowed by metal irises. Electrons are injected at hundreds of keV, bunched, and accelerated nearly to light speed, gaining about 2 MeV every 30 centimetres in intense fields. Such machines are used in radiography, cancer treatment, and as injectors for electron synchrotrons. SLAC's 3.2-km accelerator produced beams up to 50 GeV.

## Proton Linear Accelerators

Large proton linear accelerators use drift tubes, resonant cavities, and radio-frequency fields to raise ion energies before injection into larger synchrotrons. Fermilab's 150-metre machine used Alvarez-type tanks and side-coupled cavities to reach 400 MeV. Linear accelerators avoid curved-orbit radiation losses but require long structures for high energy.

## Synchrotrons

In a synchrotron, particles travel in a ring while magnetic-field strength rises with energy to keep the orbit radius nearly constant. Radio-frequency voltages accelerate the particles, and phase stability keeps them synchronized. Early weak-focusing machines became costly at high energy, but alternating-gradient focusing enabled much higher energies with smaller magnets and better beam confinement.

## Storage Rings

Storage rings hold accelerated particles for repeated circulation and collisions. Electron-positron storage rings at Stanford, CERN, Tsukuba, Frascati, Beijing, and Novosibirsk have been important for particle physics. Electrons are produced from heated filaments; positrons are created when energetic electrons strike heavy targets, producing gamma rays that generate electron-positron pairs. Detectors surround collision points to record annihilation products.

## Core Equations

Physical models often begin by relating force, energy, waves, and conserved quantities:

$$\vec{F}=m\vec{a}$$

This is Newton's second law: force is the product of mass and acceleration, so a larger mass or a faster change in motion requires a larger net force.

$$E=h\nu$$

This relation says that photon energy is proportional to frequency; higher-frequency radiation carries more energy per photon.

$$E=mc^2$$

This expresses mass-energy equivalence: mass is a form of energy, with the speed of light squared as the conversion factor.

## Interesting Facts

- A television picture tube is, in principle, a particle accelerator.
- Colliders use beam energy more efficiently than fixed-target machines.
- Synchrotron radiation limits curved electron accelerators but can be useful as a light source.
- Electron-positron rings can use one ring because opposite charges circulate in opposite directions.
