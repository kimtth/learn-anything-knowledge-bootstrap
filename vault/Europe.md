# Europe

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> Europe is a western Eurasian region whose peninsulas, seas, climates, peoples, and states have produced a distinctive geographical and historical identity. Its physical diversity and cultural interactions have made it a major center of global history.

## Introduction

Europe ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Europe)) is the second-smallest continent, but it has long been treated as a distinct world region because its western edge faces the Atlantic and its many peninsulas, islands, and seas give it an unusually varied geography. It is a small western appendage of Eurasia ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eurasia)), yet its position, climate, and long history made it one of the main centers of civilization. Europe is bordered by the Arctic, Atlantic, Mediterranean, Black, and Caspian seas. Its irregular coastline and peninsulas, including the Scandinavian, Iberian, Italian, and Balkan regions, shape settlement, transport, and trade.
Europe is not only a physical place but also a cultural and historical idea. Its people, languages, states, and institutions have created strong regional identities, even though the continent contains many different landscapes and traditions. The combination of natural diversity and human history means that Europe is both a fragmented region and a coherent cultural unit.

## Geologic history

Europe's geology records continental growth across deep time. The oldest rocks are found in the Baltic Shield ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baltic_Shield)), the Ukrainian Massif ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ukrainian_Shield)), and parts of northwestern Scotland, while the youngest and most dramatic landforms are linked to the Alpine system. Between these older and younger belts lie sedimentary basins such as the Paris Basin, the North Sea basin, and the Russian Platform, which help explain much of the continent's plains, hills, and mineral wealth.

Major mountain-building periods include the Caledonian, Hercynian, and Alpine orogenies. These events created the mountains and basins that still shape drainage, soils, and human settlement. The opening of the Tethys and the Atlantic, together with later volcanic activity in Iceland and southern Europe, also left a strong mark on the continent. In the most recent period, the Pleistocene ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pleistocene)) ice ages reshaped northern and Alpine Europe by carving valleys, forming lakes, depositing loess, and changing drainage patterns.

## Land

![[attachments/europe_002.webp|420]]

*Europe satellite orthographic*

Europe's landforms are highly varied. The continent contains low plains, old shields, mountain chains, peninsulas, and island systems, and its relief is often more rugged where altitude rises. The European Plain stretches from western France through much of Poland, Germany, and Russia, while the Scandinavian highlands, the Alps, the Carpathians, and the Balkans create major contrasts in elevation and accessibility.

A useful distinction separates western Europe and eastern Europe. Western Europe has a greater proportion of coastline, maritime access, and navigable rivers, while eastern Europe is more continental and larger in scale. This physical difference helps explain why western Europe developed stronger trade links with the Atlantic and the Mediterranean, while eastern Europe remained more tied to large internal basins and long rivers.

Europe also combines broad lowlands with sharply contrasting highlands. The European Plain links the North European Plain with the much larger East European Plain, so much of the north and east has low relief. By contrast, the Scandinavian highlands, the Alps, the Apennines, the Carpathians, and the Balkan ranges create local barriers, watersheds, and distinctive regional climates. Europe has no continuous north-south mountain wall comparable to the Andes, however, which has helped maintain access between western coasts and the interior.

## Drainage and hydrology

Most European rivers begin in wet uplands shaped by the Caledonian, Hercynian, and Alpine mountain-building periods. They drain to the Atlantic and Arctic oceans, either directly or through the Baltic and Mediterranean seas, and to the enclosed Caspian Sea. Their present courses reflect erosion, faulting, river capture, and changes in land and sea level. The Rhine, for example, once drained toward the Mediterranean before geological change diverted it northward.

River behaviour depends on rainfall, snowmelt, and the ability of underlying rocks to store water. Western rivers usually have their greatest flow in winter rain, whereas mountain and continental rivers rise in spring and early summer when snow melts. The Rhine and Danube cross several climate zones, so their flow patterns are especially complex. Flooding can follow spring thaw in European Russia, prolonged oceanic rain, or rapid Alpine snowmelt; in parts of Mediterranean Europe, low summer rainfall, evaporation, and porous limestone can instead leave rivers nearly dry.

European river systems serve different purposes because of their age and gradient. Long, mature rivers of the central and eastern lowlands are comparatively navigable and support inland transport. Steeper, younger rivers in northern and southern Europe are less useful as waterways but have greater potential for hydroelectric power. Glacial erosion also created many northern lakes, and rivers entering the low-tide Baltic, Mediterranean, and Black seas commonly build deltas and spits rather than broad tidal estuaries.

## Soils

Europe's soils show how climate, vegetation, rock, drainage, and human use interact. In the far north, tundra soils are acidic, waterlogged, and nutrient-poor. South of them, conifer forests form cool-climate podzols, whose acidity, leaching, hardpan, and excess moisture make cultivation difficult. Mixed forests farther south produce more varied brown forest soils, often with enough humus to support farming.

The most productive areas include loess-derived loams, alluvial soils, and the chernozem, or black earth, of Ukraine and southwestern Russia. Chernozem is deep, friable, humus-rich soil formed under grassland and is highly fertile. Drier lands east of Ukraine have chestnut or saline soils, while erosion has removed much soil from the mountain and plateau landscapes of southern Europe. There, red, clay-rich terra rossa occurs in many valleys and depressions. These differences help explain regional patterns of crops, settlement, and agricultural productivity.

## Climate

![[attachments/europe_003.webp|420]]

*Rue des Marchands depuis le Koïfhus (Colmar) (4)*

Europe's climate is shaped mainly by air mass circulation. Atlantic air masses from the west, polar air from the north, tropical air from the south, and continental air from the east all meet across the continent. The westerlies dominate northwestern Europe, bringing wet and relatively mild conditions in winter, while summer brings cooler air from the north and northwest.

In southern Europe, the climate is more Mediterranean, with dry summers and wetter winters. The Alpine and northern regions are cooler and more continental, while the interior east can be colder and drier. Air-pressure systems over Iceland, the Azores, the Mediterranean, and Siberia help direct these air masses. Climate affects agriculture, soils, water flow, and human settlement, and it helps explain why some regions are densely populated and others are more limited for farming or habitation.

## Plant life

Europe has major vegetation zones that reflect both climate and geography. Tundra is found in the far north, especially in Iceland, northern Scandinavia, and Russia, while the boreal forest or taiga stretches across much of northern Europe. To the south, mixed forests once covered large areas, but centuries of human use have reduced them sharply.

In southern Europe, the natural vegetation is more Mediterranean, with evergreen shrubs, maquis, and scrub adapted to dry summers. In the southeast and east, wooded steppe and grass steppe appear, while semidesert vegetation exists around the Caspian Sea. Vegetation has changed greatly since the last ice age, and human land use has been a major force in shaping today's landscape.

After Pleistocene ice sheets retreated, forests spread northward as the climate warmed. Pollen preserved in peat and sediment records this changing pattern: conifers and birch established an early northern cover, and later mixed forests of oak, elm, lime, and other broad-leaved trees expanded. Human clearance, farming, grazing, and settlement later reduced much of this forest. Natural vegetation therefore shows both climate zones and the long environmental effects of human history.

## Animals and conservation

Europe's wildlife has been reduced by land development, hunting, pollution, and the conversion of natural habitats to farmland. Nature reserves and national parks now protect important habitats and threatened species. Examples include the Lofoten and Farne islands for seabirds, the Coto Doñana National Park in Spain for birds and the Iberian lynx, and the Belovezhskaya Forest of Poland and Belarus for European bison, deer, wolves, and other forest animals.

Protection works by preserving the conditions that species need rather than simply keeping individual animals alive. The Gran Paradiso National Park in Italy helped save the Alpine ibex, while wetland and delta reserves protect breeding birds and migratory routes. Conservation therefore connects water quality, forests, soils, hunting limits, and international cooperation with the survival of European animal populations.

## People

Europe has long been a center of human settlement, with modern humans arriving there about 45,000 to 43,000 years ago. Over time, Europe has experienced repeated immigration from Asia and, in more recent centuries, from Africa and other regions. Even so, Europe remains associated with a great variety of peoples, languages, and cultural traditions that have developed over many centuries.
Cultural differences in Europe are often more visible in language, religion, and historical experience than in physical appearance alone. The long history of settlement, the many borders between states, and the persistence of national identities have made Europe especially sensitive to ethnic, linguistic, and political divisions. This is one reason why the continent has been both highly creative and often politically unstable.

Many cultural groups share a region or language without sharing the same national identity. In the Balkans, for example, Serbs, Bosnians, and Croats use closely related forms of a language commonly called Serbo-Croatian by linguists, but historical and religious experience leads each group to prefer its own name. Across Europe, state boundaries, past empires, and migration have made language and historical memory powerful markers of belonging.

## Languages

![[attachments/europe_001.webp|420]]

*Deforestation central Europe - Rodungen Mitteleuropa*

Three major language families are especially important: Romance, Germanic, and Slavic. Romance languages developed from Latin and dominate western and Mediterranean Europe, including French, Spanish, Portuguese, Italian, and Romanian. Germanic languages are found across central and northern Europe, and Slavic languages dominate much of eastern and southeastern Europe.

Smaller but important language groups include Greek, Baltic, Celtic, Albanian, Basque, Uralic, and Turkic languages. These linguistic differences reflect long historical movements, local identities, and the persistence of regional cultures. Language is one of the strongest markers of Europe's internal diversity.

## Religions

Europe's religious life is shaped by a few major traditions. Most Europeans are linked to one of three forms of Christianity: Roman Catholicism in the west and southwest, Protestantism in the north, and Eastern Orthodoxy in the east and southeast. These divisions grew out of historical schisms in Christianity, including the split between East and West and the Reformation of the 16th century.

[[Judaism]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Judaism)) and [[Muhammad and the Religion of Islam (Islam)|Islam]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islam)) also have long histories in Europe. Jews have lived in Europe since Roman times, while Islamic influence entered the Iberian and Balkan regions in the Middle Ages. In modern Europe, Muslim communities have grown in both the Balkans and western cities, often through migration and labour movement.

Christian divisions also reflect a sequence of historical splits. The separation between the Roman bishop in the west and the patriarch of Constantinople in the east became final in 1054, leaving a durable cultural boundary in southeastern Europe. In the 16th century, the Reformation created Protestant churches that became strongest in many Germanic-speaking regions. Religious geography therefore combines doctrine with the political history of states, empires, and migration.

## Migration

European migration has worked in two directions across modern history. From the age of oceanic exploration onward, Europe became a major source of emigrants to the Americas, Australia, southern Africa, and New Zealand. Poverty, persecution, famine, land shortage, and industrial change pushed many people outward, while overseas colonies and settler societies offered land, work, and social mobility, often at the expense of Indigenous peoples already living there. The movement was especially large in the 19th century and early 20th century, when millions left Ireland, Britain, Scandinavia, the Low Countries, Italy, Spain, Portugal, central Europe, and eastern Europe.

Roughly 60 million people left Europe for overseas destinations during the 19th and 20th centuries, with more than half going to the United States. The scale peaked in 1913, when at least 1.5 million emigrated. Earlier emigrants often came from northwestern Europe and settled where English was spoken; later movement from central, eastern, and southern Europe followed cultural and language links to destinations such as South America. Transport made this movement easier, but famine, job loss, and persecution remained important causes.

Migration also reshaped Europe itself. Earlier movements from Asia, the spread of peoples across linguistic and religious frontiers, and later labour migration from Turkey, North Africa, Asia, the Caribbean, and former colonies all changed urban society. These movements helped create large Muslim, African, Asian, and mixed-heritage communities in western European cities, while also intensifying debates over citizenship, assimilation, minority rights, and national identity. Europe is therefore both a region of old cultural core areas and a continent repeatedly remade by mobility.

Europe's population history also has an economic dimension. The shift from farm work to industry concentrated people in towns and manufacturing regions, while overseas migration reduced population growth at home and redistributed European languages and institutions abroad. Postwar labour migration then supplied workers to expanding western European economies. Population change is therefore linked to industrialization, colonial history, labour demand, and changing ideas about citizenship.

## Economy
Europe is one of the first world regions to develop a modern economy based on agriculture, industry, and services. This transformation was helped by rich natural resources, a long history of innovation, and the movement of raw materials and ideas across connected regions. Agricultural improvement in the 17th century, particularly in England, helped create surplus wealth and labor, which then fed the Industrial Revolution ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Industrial_Revolution)) in the 18th century.

Continuous cropping and improved use of animal manure raised agricultural productivity, but they also reduced the farm labour needed for each unit of land. Capital accumulated by landowners and a growing pool of workers helped finance and staff factories. Water-powered textile machinery began this industrial change in northern England, and practical steam engines later freed factories from the need to stand beside fast-flowing water. Coal then supplied both fuel and a reason for industries to cluster near coalfields and improved transport routes.

Europe's economy also depends heavily on energy and minerals. Coal, petroleum, natural gas, nuclear power, hydroelectricity, geothermal energy, wind power, and tidal power all play roles. Heavy industry, chemical production, transport networks, and trade all became central to European life. Europe became a major center of manufacturing, services, and international commerce while facing strong competition from the United States, Japan, and China.

## Power

Coal was long the main source of industrial power in Europe, especially in Britain, Germany, France, Poland, and Ukraine. Later, petroleum and natural gas became more important, and nuclear energy, hydroelectricity, and renewable sources expanded. In some countries, such as Norway, hydroelectric power provides almost all electricity, while in others coal remains important for heavy industry and electricity generation.

Different energy sources are concentrated where geography makes them practical. Mountain rivers and heavy precipitation favour hydropower, volcanic areas in Iceland and Italy support geothermal power, and coastal or exposed regions can use wind and tidal power. These sources reduce dependence on imported fuel, while coal, oil, and gas remain important because they can supply transport, heating, and industrial processes at large scale.

## Manufacturing

Europe's manufacturing base is broad. It includes iron and steel, machinery, chemicals, vehicles, aircraft, pharmaceuticals, textiles, food processing, printing, and publishing. Coalfields were important for heavy industry, and many manufacturing centers grew where transport costs were low or where ore, coal, and ports were close together. Some industries, especially those producing specialized or high-quality goods, have become more widely distributed.

Heavy industry historically clustered where coal, iron ore, ports, or waterways lowered transport costs for bulky materials. Later improvements in transport, imported ore, electric furnaces, and technical efficiency weakened that dependence on local coal. Chemical industries grew from coal and refinery by-products as well as from deposits of salt, potash, phosphates, and sulfur. Light industries and specialist manufacturing could spread more widely because their products were less bulky or depended more on skilled labour and market access.

## Trade
Trade is essential to European prosperity. Europe imports raw materials and food, and it exports manufactured goods, services, and technology. Internal trade within the continent has long been vigorous, while external trade has expanded through ocean, air, and rail links. The political divide between western Europe and the former Soviet bloc shaped trade patterns for much of the 20th century, but this changed with the end of communist rule and the integration of newer markets.

Trade grows from regional specialization: regions exchange what they produce efficiently for materials, food, energy, or goods made elsewhere. European integration and improved transport made cross-border exchange easier, while the collapse of the Soviet bloc opened eastern markets more fully to international commerce. This makes trade an economic link between natural resources, factory locations, consumer markets, and political cooperation.

## Tourism

Tourism ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tourism)) is described as one of Europe's major growth industries. The continent's cities, beaches, mountains, and historical monuments attract visitors from within Europe and from other continents. Tourism is a major source of employment and foreign exchange, especially in the Mediterranean region.

## Roads, railways, and airways

Europe has an extensive transport network that includes highways, railways, inland waterways, pipelines, seaports, and airports. Roads and expressways are central to modern movement, while railways link ports, capitals, and international frontiers. Air services connect major European cities to the rest of the world, and this transport system helps explain the continent's economic integration.

Railways must sometimes solve the practical problem of different track gauges at national borders. Electrification, diesel locomotives, container freight, and high-speed passenger services have helped rail retain an important role despite competition from cars and trucks. Inland waterways move heavy goods slowly but cheaply, while modernized ports handle the large ships and high volumes required by international trade. Air transport is especially useful for passengers, mail, and small, high-value goods that need fast delivery.

## Key Events & Developments

- The geologic history of Europe was shaped by the Caledonian, Hercynian, and Alpine mountain-building periods.
- The Pleistocene ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pleistocene)) ice ages strongly altered northern and Alpine landscapes.
- The rise of agriculture and industry transformed Europe into a modern economic center.
- The Reformation and later religious schisms helped define Europe's cultural and political geography.
- Modern migration and trade reshaped Europe’s populations, languages, and economies.

## Key People

- James Watt: developed the practical steam engine that helped free factories from dependence on water power.
- Martin Luther: began the Reformation that reshaped the religious map of Europe.
- [[Francis bacon|Francis Bacon]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Francis_Bacon)): observed that changing winds bring changing weather, a useful insight into European climate.
