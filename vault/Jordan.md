## Introduction

Jordan ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jordan)) is an Arab country of Southwest Asia on the rocky desert edge of the northern Arabian Peninsula. It is a young state occupying an ancient land marked by biblical kingdoms, Nabataean Petra, Roman Arabia Petraea, Islamic desert palaces, Ottoman rule, British mandate administration, and independence as a kingdom in 1946.

The capital, Amman, was named for the Ammonites, who made it their capital in the 13th century BCE. Later known as Philadelphia in the Roman Decapolis, Amman became Jordan's largest city, a commercial and transportation centre, and one of the Arab world's important cultural capitals.

## Land And Climate

![[attachments/jordan_002.webp|420]]

Jordan's borders meet Syria, Iraq, Saudi Arabia, Israel, and the West Bank. The country has only 16 miles of coastline on the Gulf of Aqaba, where Al-Aqabah is its sole port. The West Bank was under Jordanian rule from 1948 to 1967, but Jordan renounced its claims there in 1988.

The landscape has three main regions: the eastern and southern desert, the uplands east of the Jordan River, and the Jordan Valley, part of the East African Rift System. The desert covers more than four-fifths of the country. Mount Ramm is Jordan's highest point, while the Dead Sea lies about 1,312 feet below sea level, the lowest natural point on Earth's surface.

The Jordan River drains Lake Tiberias, the Yarmuk River, and valley streams into the Dead Sea. The lower river area is saline, and the Dead Sea shores include salt marshes. Jordan's best soils occur in the Jordan Valley and southeast of the Dead Sea, where alluvial deposits support cultivation.

The climate ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Climate)) shifts from Mediterranean in the west to desert in the east and south, but the country is generally arid. Winter rains decline from the northwest toward the south, khamsin winds bring hot dusty weather, and water shortages in major towns became one of Jordan's most urgent problems.

## People And Settlement

Most Jordanians are Arabs, principally Jordanians and Palestinians. Bedouin were once the largest indigenous group and remain important to the Hashimite regime, though they became a minority after Palestinian influxes from the Arab-Israeli wars. Jordan is unusual among Arab states in granting wide-scale citizenship to Palestinian refugees.

Minorities include Iraqis, Circassians, Armenians, Turkmen, Druze, Bahai, and Chechens. Arabic is the official language, with colloquial dialects close to Levantine Arabic and Modern Standard Arabic taught in schools. Circassian Adyghe and Armenian survive in some communities, though assimilation to Arabic is common.

Religion ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Religion)) is overwhelmingly Sunni Muslim. Christians make up most of the remainder, especially Greek Orthodox, Greek Catholic, Roman Catholic, Syrian Orthodox, Armenian Orthodox, Armenian Catholic, and Protestant communities. Druze and Bahai groups are small, while Armenians, Druze, and Bahai are both religious and ethnic communities.

Settlement ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Human_settlement)) divides between desert and cultivated zones, though modern urbanization has changed older patterns. Tent-dwelling Bedouin became fewer as permanent settlement expanded, but more than one-third of Jordanians trace urban roots to Bedouin heritage. About three-fourths of the population live in urban areas, especially Amman, Al-Zarqa, Irbid, and Al-Rusayfah.

Demography is shaped by youth, migration, and refugees. Palestinians form about one-third of the population, with major influxes after 1948-1949, 1967, and the Persian Gulf War. Iraqi arrivals followed the Persian Gulf War and Iraq War, and UNRWA served about 1.6 million registered Palestinian refugees by the early 21st century.

## Economy

Jordan's economy is small, comparatively diversified, and vulnerable to regional instability. Trade, finance, transport, communications, utilities, construction, mining, manufacturing, services, government spending, remittances, tourism, foreign aid, debt, unemployment, refugees, and market size all shape its development.

Economic reforms accelerated after 1999, including Jordan's entry into the World Trade Organization in 2000 and partial privatization of state enterprises. The West Bank had supplied about one-third of domestic income before 1967, while later crises such as the Iran-Iraq War, Persian Gulf War, and Iraq War repeatedly redirected trade and services.

Agriculture ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agriculture)) is limited because only a tiny fraction of land is arable. Wheat and barley grow in rain-fed uplands, while the irrigated Jordan Valley produces citrus, fruits, potatoes, vegetables, and olives. Sheep and goats are the chief livestock, with smaller numbers of cattle, camels, horses, donkeys, mules, and poultry.

Resources include phosphates, potash, limestone, marble, dolomite, kaolin, salt, barite, quartzite, gypsum, feldspar, and unexploited copper, uranium, and shale oil. Jordan has little oil but modest natural gas in the eastern desert. Water became a regional flashpoint because the Jordan and Yarmuk rivers and aquifers were overused.

Industry and trade centre around Amman, phosphate extraction, petroleum refining, cement, food, clothing, and consumer goods. Exports include clothing, chemicals, potash, and phosphates, while imports include machinery, crude petroleum, and food. Tourism to Petra, biblical sites, and the Jordan Valley became important for foreign reserves.

## Government And Society

The 1952 constitution defines Jordan as a constitutional hereditary monarchy with a parliamentary form of government, Islam as the official religion, and Jordan as part of the Arab nation. The king remains the ultimate authority over executive, legislative, and judicial branches, appointing the prime minister and cabinet.

The National Assembly is bicameral, with an appointed Senate and an elected House of Representatives. Elections have often been suspended, and political parties were legalized in 1992 if they recognized the monarchy. The Muslim Brotherhood and its Islamic Action Front maintained a significant minority presence.

The judiciary is constitutionally independent but judges are appointed and dismissed by royal decree after a Justices Council decision. Regular courts, Shariah and other religious courts, and special courts handle civil, personal-status, land, municipal, tax, customs, and other matters.

The army developed from the Arab Legion, and Bedouin remained central to it even as their political influence declined. The king is commander in chief. Health services expanded, infant mortality remained lower than in several neighbouring countries, literacy became widespread, and education included government, private, and UNRWA schools.

Cultural life ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Culture)) is rooted in Arab family, hospitality, food, poetry, oral tradition, music, dance, and Bedouin heritage, while urban Jordanians participate in theatre, concerts, opera, ballet, cinema, cafes, and modern media. Petra, Qasr Amrah, and Umm al-Rasass are UNESCO World Heritage sites, and football and basketball are the most popular team sports.

## Ancient And Medieval History

![[attachments/jordan_003.webp|420]]

*The Treasury at Petra in Jordan*

Jordan's archaeology reaches back to the Lower Paleolithic Period, with flint tools, rock carvings at Mount Al-Tubayq, and sites such as Tulaylat al-Ghassul and Dhiban. Biblical traditions associate the region with Gilead, Moab, Midian, Edom, Ammon, Amorites, Bashan, and Israelite settlement east of the Jordan.

Ancient powers included Assyrians, Neo-Babylonians, Persians, Seleucids, Ptolemies, Nabataeans, and Romans. The Nabataeans prospered through caravan trade and made Petra famous. Rome absorbed Nabataea in AD 106, and after 313 Christianity gained official recognition and many churches were built.

Muslim rule followed the Battle of the Yarmuk River in 636, when forces led by Khalid ibn al-Walid defeated Byzantium. The Umayyads, ruling from Damascus, built desert palaces such as Qasr Amrah, while later Abbasid rule shifted power to Baghdad and Jordan became more marginal. Crusaders later established Oultre Jourdain east of the Jordan.

Ottoman rule came in the 16th century. In the 19th century, the Ottomans settled Circassian, Caucasian, and other refugees and, with German assistance, completed the Hejaz Railway linking Damascus and Medina in 1908.

## Hashimite State And Arab-Israeli Conflict

[[World Wars (World War I)|World War I]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_War_I)) brought the Arab revolt against the Ottomans, assisted by T.E. Lawrence. Faysal ibn Husayn's army captured Al-Aqabah in 1917. After the San Remo mandate settlement in 1920, Abdullah arrived in Maan, and Britain made him ruler of Transjordan in 1921.

Transjordan gained limited recognition under Emir Abdullah in 1923, full independence by treaty on March 22, 1946, and a new identity as the Hashimite Kingdom of Jordan in 1949. British support and the Arab Legion helped Abdullah secure Bedouin loyalty.

The 1948 war brought the West Bank and East Jerusalem under Jordanian rule. Jordan formally annexed the territory a year later, but most Arab countries and much of the world opposed the move. Palestinian refugees and West Bank incorporation created deep political and economic pressures. Abdullah was assassinated at Al-Aqsa Mosque in 1951.

King Hussein took the throne in 1953 and spent his reign balancing the monarchy, Palestinians, Arab states, Israel, Britain, and the United States. He dismissed British advisers in 1956, survived a 1957 coup attempt, and relied on East Bank Bedouins, British support, and American aid to secure the throne.

The 1967 war was a watershed. Jordan joined Egypt and Syria against Israel, lost the West Bank and East Jerusalem within 48 hours, suffered heavy casualties, lost fertile land, and absorbed new refugees. Hussein resumed secret contacts with Israel while also seeking the return of lost territory.

Black September came in 1970, when the PLO challenged Jordanian sovereignty and the Popular Front for the Liberation of Palestine hijacked aircraft to Dawson's Field. Hussein declared martial law, Syrian tanks entered northern Jordan, and by 1971 Jordan had forced PLO guerrillas out.

Hussein's diplomacy shifted repeatedly after 1973. The Arab League recognized the PLO as sole legitimate Palestinian representative at Rabat in 1974. Hussein alternated between coordination with the PLO, secret contacts with Israel, and efforts to preserve the kingdom. The 1987 intifadah ended Jordanian-Israeli plans for an economic path to peace.

In 1988, Hussein renounced all Jordanian claims to the West Bank, transferred responsibility to the PLO, dissolved parliament, and recognized the Palestinian entity after the Palestine National Council proclaimed independence. In 1989, Jordan held its first parliamentary elections in 22 years.

## Peace, Abdullah II, And Reform

The Persian Gulf War strained Jordan because Hussein leaned toward Saddam Hussein while trade with Iraq was vital. Aid was cut, blockade pressure mounted, and hundreds of thousands of Palestinians returned from Kuwait. By the early 1990s, the United States and Israel again sought Hussein's support for peace efforts.

Jordan and Israel signed a peace treaty about a year after the 1993 PLO-Israeli accord, with Hussein recognized as custodian of Muslim holy sites in East Jerusalem. Hussein also supported Palestinian autonomy, helped broker agreements on Hebron in 1997 and Wye Plantation in 1998, and named his son Abdullah II successor before dying in 1999.

Abdullah II continued his father's foreign policy, pursued economic reform, improved relations with Arab neighbours, and promised attention to women's status. His reign faced dissent over U.S. ties, relations with Israel, militant violence, disputed elections, identity politics between Palestinian-origin Jordanians and Bedouin-descended supporters of government, and infrastructure strain from Iraqi and Syrian refugees.

The Arab Spring reached Jordan in 2011, when protesters in Amman denounced high prices, unemployment, corruption, and limited democracy. Abdullah dismissed governments, appointed new prime ministers, created a royal committee for constitutional reform, and approved amendments, but opposition groups said the changes did not sufficiently limit royal power.
