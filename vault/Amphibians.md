# Amphibians

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> Amphibians are vertebrates whose life histories and body systems commonly bridge aquatic and terrestrial environments. Frogs, salamanders, and caecilians illustrate adaptation through moist skin, metamorphosis, diverse reproduction, and ecological specialization.

## Introduction

Amphibians ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amphibian)) are vertebrate animals able to exploit both aquatic and terrestrial habitats. The name comes from the Greek amphibios, meaning "living a double life," reflecting a strategy in which many species begin life in water and later move onto land, though some remain permanently aquatic and others fully terrestrial.

More than 6,500 species of living amphibians are known. They first appeared about 340 million years ago, among the earliest groups to diverge from ancestral fish-tetrapod stock as animals evolved from strictly aquatic to terrestrial forms. Amphibians were not the ancestors of reptiles, even though they shared some features with reptilian ancestors.

## The Three Living Orders

Today amphibians fall into three orders that share a single ancient origin in the subclass Lissamphibia ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lissamphibia)). Frogs and toads (Anura) are tailless and squat with long, powerful hind limbs for leaping; newts and salamanders (Caudata) have tails and two pairs of similar-sized limbs; and the limbless, wormlike caecilians (Gymnophiona) are adapted for burrowing.

Modern amphibians are united by several unique traits. They typically have a moist skin and rely heavily on cutaneous (skin-surface) respiration, and they possess a double-channeled hearing system, green rods in the retina for discriminating colours, and two-part (pedicellate) teeth.

## Size and Distribution

Amphibians vary enormously in size. The Chinese and Japanese giant salamanders of the genus Andrias exceed 1.5 metres (5 feet), while Mexican Thorius salamanders measure barely 25 mm. The West African goliath frog can reach 30 cm and weigh 3.3 kg, whereas some South American frogs mature at under 10 mm.

Amphibians occur almost worldwide, even north of the Arctic Circle in Eurasia, and are absent only from [[Antarctica]], remote oceanic islands, and the driest deserts. Frogs and toads are most diverse in humid tropics, while salamanders concentrate in the cool, moist montane forests of the Northern Hemisphere.

## Decline and Economic Importance

Since the 1980s many frog populations have declined sharply, with causes including habitat destruction, acid rain, global warming, and ozone depletion; but the parasitic amphibian chytrid fungus (Batrachochytrium dendrobatidis) has been identified as a major cause of severe die-offs in Australia, Central America, and elsewhere.

Amphibians are also economically useful. Anurans reduce crop pests and disease-carrying insects, frogs are harvested for food, and the skin secretions of tropical anurans yield substances such as magainin, a natural antibiotic, along with potential anesthetics and painkillers under study by biochemists.

## Reproduction and Life Cycle

![[attachments/amphibians_002.webp|420]]

*Diagram of frog metamorphosis*

The three groups show diverse life histories. Many amphibians are biphasic, laying large numbers of aquatic eggs that hatch into larvae and later metamorphose; a tiger salamander clutch may exceed 5,000 eggs and a large bullfrog 45,000. Egg size and water temperature strongly affect development time, which ranges from one or two days in warm water to many weeks in cold mountain streams.

A widespread trend is direct development, in which the aquatic egg and free-swimming larva are eliminated and juveniles hatch as miniature adults. This occurs in most lungless salamanders, some caecilians, and many anurans, while numerous caecilians and a few frogs and salamanders bear live young.

## Metamorphosis and Heterochrony

![[attachments/amphibians_001.webp|420]]

*Axolotl retaining larval gills into adulthood*

Metamorphosis is an abrupt change in physiology, biochemistry, structure, and behaviour, controlled ultimately by hormones. In salamanders and caecilians the changes are modest, but in anurans the transition is radical: limbs complete development, the tail is resorbed, gills give way to lungs, and adult mouthparts and a tongue appear.

The older term "neoteny" has been replaced by heterochrony, the change in timing and rate of developmental events. The classic example is the axolotl (Ambystoma mexicanum), a paedomorphic salamander that keeps its larval gills into sexual maturity, illustrating how shifts in development can produce strikingly different adults.

## Form and Function

Each order's body reflects a long, separate evolutionary history. Salamanders have generalized morphologies, walking with a diagonal-sequence gait and projecting fleshy tongues to capture prey. Caecilians show the least divergence, with compact spadelike skulls, a sensory tentacle, and concertina locomotion through soil.

Anurans are the most specialized, with broad flat heads, short inflexible trunks, and powerful hind limbs supported by complex pectoral and pelvic girdles that absorb landing shock. Modifications of the limbs let some anurans burrow with keratin tubercles or climb with adhesive toe pads.

## Key Events & Developments

- about 340 million years ago: Amphibians first appear during the evolution from fish-like ancestors toward land vertebrates.
- 20th century: The class is organized into the subclass Lissamphibia ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lissamphibia)) with three living orders.
- since the 1980s: Global amphibian declines accelerate, driven heavily by the chytrid fungus.
