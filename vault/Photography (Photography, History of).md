# Photography (Photography, History of)

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> Photography developed from optical and chemical experiments into a medium of scientific record, commerce, documentary evidence, personal expression, photojournalism, and fine art. Its history also challenges the claim that cameras simply tell the truth by emphasizing choices of subject, framing, timing, process, and manipulation.

## Introduction

![[attachments/photography_history_001.webp|420]]

*Camera obscura diagram*

Photography ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Photography)) is the recording of an image through the action of light or related radiation on light-sensitive material. The word comes from Greek terms meaning light and to draw and was first used in the 1830s. Photography's special aesthetic power comes from immediacy: the essential elements of the image are usually fixed at exposure, creating a sense of authenticity unmatched by other picture-making media.

The apparent objectivity of photographs encouraged the saying that the camera does not lie, though this belief is misleading. Photography depends on choice of subject, framing, timing, process, development, printing, and later manipulation. Its history moves between mechanical record, scientific tool, commercial practice, documentary evidence, personal expression, and fine art.

The basic process also depends on a latent image ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latent_image)), development, and fixing. Light changes sensitive material at exposure, but chemical processing makes the image visible and permanent.

## Antecedents

The main optical antecedent was the camera obscura ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Camera_obscura)), a dark chamber with a hole or lens projecting external images onto an opposite wall. Known in principle to ancient Chinese and Greek thinkers, it was described in detail by Giambattista della Porta in the 16th century. In 1727, Johann Heinrich Schulze proved that silver salts darkened because of light rather than heat, supplying the chemical basis for photography.

## Heliography

Nicephore Niepce developed heliography, or sun drawing, by using bitumen of Judea and lavender oil on plates exposed to light. From 1822, he copied engravings onto stone, glass, zinc, and pewter. In 1826/1827, he produced the first successful photograph from nature, a view from his estate at Gras, requiring about eight hours of exposure.

Niepce's aim was not only to make camera images but also to create printing plates, so his experiments linked photography to older reproductive print technologies.

## Daguerreotype and Photogenic Drawing

![[attachments/photography_history_002.webp|420]]

*Daguerreotype camera*

Louis-Jacques-Mande Daguerre, a scene painter and Diorama operator, partnered with Niepce in 1829. By 1835, he discovered that mercury vapour could develop a latent image on iodized silver, reducing exposure time to about 30 minutes. In 1837, he fixed images with salt, and in 1839 the French government acquired and announced the daguerreotype. In Britain, Thomas Wedgwood, Humphry Davy, and others pursued photogenic drawing with silver nitrate.

## Portraiture

![[attachments/photography_history_003.webp|420]]

*Early daguerreotype photograph*

Portraiture became one of photography's earliest popular genres. Many early commercial portraits relied on formulaic props and retouching, but artists such as Nadar, Etienne Carjat, and Julia Margaret Cameron broke conventions. Nadar used plain backgrounds and diffused light to capture prominent French figures. Cameron used soft focus, close-up lenses, and long exposures to seek the inner greatness of Victorian sitters such as Charles Darwin and Alfred, Lord Tennyson.

## Early Photojournalism

Photography entered the press soon after 1839, first through woodcuts and lithographs made from photographs. War reportage began with Roger Fenton's Crimean War photographs in 1855. James Robertson and Felice Beato documented India, while Mathew B. Brady, Alexander Gardner, and Timothy H. O'Sullivan recorded the American Civil War. The halftone process later allowed photographs to be printed with type, transforming newspapers and magazines.

## Documentary Photography

Documentary photography developed through landscape, architectural, social, and scientific recording. European photographers documented cathedrals, monuments, and restoration projects. In the American West, William Henry Jackson, O'Sullivan, and Carleton Watkins photographed landscapes for railroads and government surveys; Jackson's Grand Canyon and Yellowstone images helped support public preservation. Afong Lai and Marc Ferrez provided important non-European views of China and Brazil.

## Social Documentation

The camera became a tool for social reform. Lewis Hine documented child labour in mills, mines, factories, and street trades, helping prompt state and federal regulation. During the Great Depression, the Farm Security Administration under Roy Stryker sponsored over 270,000 images by photographers including Dorothea Lange and Walker Evans. Lange's migrant mother image became an icon of Depression anxiety, while Evans turned documentation into powerful visual expression.

## Photo-Secession and Pictorialism

The Photo-Secession, founded in 1902 by Alfred Stieglitz, promoted photography as fine art. With Edward Steichen, Stieglitz opened the Little Galleries of the Photo-Secession, known as 291, and published Camera Work from 1903 to 1917. Early Pictorialist processes such as gum-bichromate and bromoil softened images to resemble etchings or lithographs, but Stieglitz increasingly favoured straight photography.

## New Objectivity and Straight Photography

After World War I, photographers embraced sharp, objective imagery called New Objectivity, the new vision, Precisionism, or Neue Sachlichkeit. Paul Strand photographed machines, organic forms, and landscapes. Edward Weston, Ansel Adams, Imogen Cunningham, and Group f.64 valued sharp focus, deep depth of field, and unmanipulated prints. In Europe, Karl Blossfeldt and Albert Renger-Patzsch emphasized material detail, plant forms, industry, and everyday objects.

## Constructivism and Modern Photojournalism

Constructivist photography used fresh vantage points to reawaken perception of ordinary objects and processes. Photojournalism expanded as magazines multiplied and portable cameras improved. The Ermanox in 1924 and Leica in 1925 enabled fast, available-light photography. Andre Kertesz and Henri Cartier-Bresson used 35-mm cameras to capture informal scenes and the decisive moment.

## War, Picture Magazines, and Colour

World War I was censored, but later picture magazines made war photography central. Robert Capa, David Seymour, Margaret Bourke-White, Dmitry Baltermants, Yevgeny Khaldey, W. Eugene Smith, and others documented conflicts before and during World War II. Autochrome, introduced by the Lumiere brothers in 1907, was the first practical colour process. Kodachrome, developed by Leopold Godowsky Jr. and Leopold Mannes in 1935, began modern colour photography.

## Postwar and Contemporary Photography

After World War II, colour, abstraction, documentary renewal, fashion, celebrity, and art-world photography expanded. Eliot Porter and Ernst Haas showed colour's expressive power. Later photographers such as Annie Leibovitz, Helmut Newton, Mario Testino, Bruce Weber, and David LaChapelle crossed commercial and museum worlds. In the 1980s and 1990s, Robert Mapplethorpe, Cindy Sherman, Barbara Kruger, and Carrie Mae Weems helped make photography central to contemporary art. Digital technology transformed production, manipulation, and colour practice by the turn of the 21st century.

## Key Events & Developments

- 1727: Schulze proved silver salts darken by light.
- 1826/1827: Niepce made the first successful photograph from nature.
- 1839: Daguerreotype details were publicly announced.
- 1855: Fenton documented the Crimean War.
- 1888: The first U.S. newspaper halftone appeared.
- 1902: Stieglitz founded the Photo-Secession.
- 1907: Autochrome introduced practical colour photography.
- 1935: Kodachrome launched the modern colour era.
