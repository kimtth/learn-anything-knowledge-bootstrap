# Crime and Punishment (Crime)

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> Crime is conduct prohibited by law and subject to public sanction. Its study considers legal definitions, causes, social conditions, policing, courts, and the relationship between wrongdoing and social order.

## Introduction

Crime ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crime)) is the intentional commission of an act that is defined, prohibited, and punishable under criminal law as socially harmful or dangerous. This survey covers how crime is defined and classified, how it is measured and detected, the characteristics of offenders, and the stages of criminal proceedings, drawing mainly on common, or Anglo-American, law with supplementary treatment of civil-law, Islamic, African, and Chinese systems.

Crime is not a fixed universal category. Conduct that is lawful in one jurisdiction may be criminal in another, and a trivial infraction in one place can be a serious offense elsewhere. Social change regularly produces new criminal laws while making older ones obsolete.

## Criminal codes and general principles

Most countries define crime in a single criminal code ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Criminal_code)), although English law, the basis of many other systems, remains uncodified. A code must still be interpreted through principles that may not be written in it, such as the mental state of the accused, jurisdiction, and the classification of offenses so cases can be assigned to the appropriate court.

Several principles recur across systems. The rule against retroactivity forbids ex post facto laws, though Norway and Denmark both passed laws after World War II that retroactively criminalized wartime cooperation with the Nazis, executing about 40 people each. In the United States, the 1990s Megan's laws required sex offenders to register, and in Smith v. Doe (2003) the Supreme Court held that such registration was nonpunitive and constitutional.

Criminal responsibility ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crime)) normally requires both an act and intention, since ignorance of the law is no excuse. Insanity, defined as a mental disease or defect that prevents a person from knowing what they are doing or that it is wrong, brings acquittal but is rare, as does automatism such as sleepwalking. Most disorders like schizophrenia do not remove intention, and in Atkins v. Virginia (2002) the U.S. Supreme Court barred capital punishment for people with mental retardation while allowing life without parole. The old division of felony and misdemeanour, abolished in England in the late 1960s, survives in many U.S. states.

## Measurement and reporting

*Organized crime index*

Crime statistics are incomplete because many offenses are never reported. Victims may not realize a crime occurred, may doubt the police can catch the offender, may fear testifying, or may be embarrassed by the circumstances. Police recording also shapes the figures, since several stolen items may be logged as one theft or as many, so official numbers reflect both real behaviour and institutional choices.

Criminologists use victim surveys and other research methods to obtain a more accurate picture of how often crimes occur and how trends vary by period and jurisdiction. These methods improve the data but cannot remove every distortion, so measurement remains a problem of both law and method.

## Detection and forensic science

Crime detection falls into three phases: discovering that a crime has occurred, identifying a suspect, and collecting enough evidence to indict. Many crimes are reported by victims or witnesses, but consensual or victimless offenses such as drug dealing, prostitution, and obscenity are hard to detect without active police work, sometimes using surveillance, interception of communications, or infiltration of gangs.

Forensic science plays a central role in serious cases, and fingerprinting was one of its first achievements. In 1894 the Troup Committee in England accepted that no two people share the same fingerprints, a claim never seriously refuted; fingerprint evidence was admitted in an Argentine court in the 1890s and in an English court in 1902.

Modern detection mixes police organization, witness information, surveillance, records, laboratory analysis, and interrogation. Scientific evidence can be powerful, but it must be collected and interpreted through legal procedures that protect its reliability and the rights of suspects.

## Offenders and causation

Offenders are not all alike, and because the criminals who are caught may not represent all who offend, firm explanations of causes are hard to reach. Through the 19th and 20th centuries biological theories examined skulls, facial features, body type, and chromosomes, and later work linked criminality to hereditary, hormonal, and neurotransmitter factors, supported partly by adoptee studies.

Psychological explanations, rooted in the 19th century and partly in the work of Sigmund Freud (1856-1939), study how behaviour and self-restraint are learned through biological predisposition and social experience. Most criminologists now hold that no single theory explains crime, producing a cautious picture that combines individual choice with social conditions.

## Comparative legal systems

Criminal justice varies widely by region. Civil law, which originated in the Roman Law of the Twelve Tables of 451-450 BC, treats the legislature as the only valid source of law, expecting judges to apply detailed codes rather than interpret them, and uses an inquisitorial procedure led by the judge. Common law relies more on adversarial trial procedure and on judicial interpretation that builds up case law.

In Africa, penal codes generally reflect the assumptions of the former colonial power but add laws against customary practices such as witchcraft; Sierra Leone still applies customary law through tribal elders, and Nigeria runs a tripartite system of common law, Shariah, and customary law. In China, informal control rooted in the teachings of [[Confucius and Confucianism (Confucianism)|Confucius]] (551-479 BC) stressed mediation; after the Cultural Revolution (1966-76), Deng Xiaoping introduced formal legality, and the first criminal codes were adopted in 1979, with about 70 offenses punishable by death.

In Shariah systems such as those of Saudi Arabia and Iran, judges called qadis apply Islamic law, with fixed hadd punishments for the gravest crimes, judicial discretion for lesser tazir offenses, and retaliation by the victim's family permitted for certain jinayat. In Russia, the procuracy created by Peter I in 1722 became the central justice administrator under the Soviets before jury trial was reinstated after 1991. What counts as crime, and how it is handled, therefore reflects deeper ideas about order.

## Sentencing and social policy

Sentencing is shaped by both legal rules and social attitudes, and in the Anglo-American tradition it is usually separate from the verdict and left to the judge. Wide judicial discretion produced complaints of disparity, so U.S. states began enacting sentencing guidelines in the early 1980s and the federal system did so in 1987; in United States v. Booker (2005) the Supreme Court made the federal guidelines discretionary rather than mandatory.

Punishment can aim to protect society, deter future crime, or reform the offender, and these goals can conflict. As societies modernize, criminal-justice systems increasingly try to prevent future crime while preserving legitimacy, fairness, and public confidence.

## Key Events & Developments

- 451-450 BC: The Law of the Twelve Tables, posted in the Roman Forum, founds the civil-law tradition.
- 1894: The Troup Committee in England accepts that no two people share the same fingerprints.
- 1902: Fingerprint evidence is first accepted in an English court.
- 1979: China adopts its first codes of criminal law and procedure.
- 1987: U.S. federal sentencing guidelines take effect.
- 2005: United States v. Booker makes the U.S. federal guidelines discretionary.
