## Introduction

![[attachments/uruguay_001.webp|420]]

*Relief map of Uruguay*

Uruguay ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uruguay)) is a small country on the southeastern coast of South America, bordered by Brazil, Argentina, the Atlantic Ocean, the Uruguay River, and the Rio de la Plata. It is the second smallest country on the continent, but its low population density and open land have given it room for agriculture, ranching, and urban development. Montevideo, the capital, dominates national life and contains almost half the population in its metropolitan area, while Salto and Paysandu are much smaller.

Uruguay ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uruguay)) became independent in 1828 and developed strong European ties, especially with the United Kingdom, France, Italy, and Spain. For much of the 20th century it was known for political stability, advanced social legislation, a large middle class, and one of Latin America's higher living standards. Military rule from 1973 to 1985 damaged this reputation and left a legacy of human-rights disputes, emigration, and political tension after democracy returned.

## Land

Uruguay ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uruguay)) is wedge-shaped and mostly low. Its landscape consists of gently rolling land, broad valleys, plains, low plateaus, hills, and ridges. The Haedo Ridge in the north and the Grande Ridge in the southeast extend southward from the Brazilian Highlands. Mount Catedral, near the southeastern coast, is the highest point at 1,685 feet. Coastal plains, tidal lakes, sand dunes, clay, sand, and fertile alluvium shape lowland agriculture and settlement.

## Drainage

Uruguay ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uruguay)) is well watered, though no large river lies entirely within the country. The Uruguay River and Rio de la Plata form major western and southern waterways and are navigable for oceangoing ships as far as Paysandu and for smaller vessels above that point to the falls at Salto. The Negro River crosses the country from northeast to southwest and is navigable only in its lower part below Rincon del Bonete Lake. Other rivers include the Santa Lucia, Cebollati, and Queguay Grande, while Merin Lagoon is the largest natural lake.

## Climate

![[attachments/uruguay_003.webp|420]]

*Pampas grassland in Uruguay*

Uruguay ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uruguay)) has a temperate and generally pleasant climate. July averages range from about 54 degrees F at Salto to 50 degrees F at Montevideo, while January averages range from about 79 degrees F at Salto to 72 degrees F at Montevideo. Frost is rare along the coast. Weather changes quickly as storm fronts pass, and a hot northerly wind may be followed by a cold pampero from the Argentine Pampas. Rainfall is fairly even through the year, usually above 40 inches annually, with autumn bringing the heaviest precipitation and winter bringing frequent rains.

Uruguay ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uruguay)) once had tall-grass prairie across much of its surface, but planted and enclosed pastures now compete with native grasslands. Forests cover only a small area, mostly along streams, with ombu, alder, willow, eucalyptus, pine, poplar, acacia, aloe, algaroba, quebracho, and palms among the trees and shrubs. Many native animals have disappeared, though pumas and jaguars may still occur in remote areas. Foxes, deer, wildcats, armadillos, capybaras, spiders, owls, crows, lapwings, partridges, and quail remain part of the fauna.

## People

Uruguayans are mainly of European origin, especially descendants of 19th- and 20th-century immigrants from Spain and Italy, with smaller French and British elements. Earlier settlers came from Argentina and Paraguay. Few direct descendants of Indigenous peoples remain, and mestizos, Black Uruguayans, and mulattos form small shares of the population. Spanish is spoken throughout the country, while border towns near Brazil may use a Portuguese-Spanish mixture called portunol.

## Religion

Religion ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Religion)) is dominated nominally by Roman Catholicism, but many Catholics are not religiously active. Protestant and Mormon groups make up a smaller share of the population. Montevideo also has a Jewish community that is small nationally but one of the larger Jewish communities in South America. Secularization has long been important in public life, especially because reform governments reduced the political influence of the Roman Catholic church.

Settlement ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Human_settlement)) changed from open cattle ranges to an urban society. At independence in 1828, Uruguay had few permanent settlements outside Montevideo, Colonia del Sacramento, and villages along the Uruguay River. Italian and Spanish immigrants settled mainly in the south along the Rio de la Plata and Uruguay River, while the north remained pastoral. Railways, roads, sheep raising, barbed-wire pastures, cattle ranches, and crop farming expanded by the early 20th century. Nearly nine-tenths of Uruguayans now live in urban areas, with Montevideo dominating commerce, government, and manufacturing.

## Economy

![[attachments/uruguay_002.webp|420]]

*Montevideo skyline*

Uruguay ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uruguay)) has had one of Latin America's higher per capita incomes and a large urban middle class. Its prosperity traditionally depended on agricultural exports, especially wool and beef, which were vulnerable to world-market shifts. Governments encouraged domestic manufacturing and services to reduce dependence on exports. The state has operated corporations in electricity, petroleum refining, alcohol, cement, meat, fish, railways, and telecommunications, though privatization efforts grew from the 1990s.

Agriculture ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agriculture)) centres on sheep and cattle. Wool, beef, livestock, hides, skins, and other animal products account for a large part of export income even though agriculture forms a small share of GDP. Foot-and-mouth disease in 2001 damaged livestock and the wider economy. Crops include rice, wheat, corn, oranges, sugarcane, sunflower seeds, and grapes for wine. Fishing and limited forestry, mainly pine and eucalyptus, add smaller resource sectors.

## Manufacturing

Manufacturing ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manufacturing)) declined somewhat after the 1980s and accounts for about one-sixth of GDP. Major products include processed foods, beverages, chemicals, textiles, and tobacco products. Most factories are concentrated in and around Montevideo, reinforcing the capital's dominance over the national economy.

## Finance

Finance ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Finance)) is unusually important for Uruguay. Banking and financial services account for about one-fourth of GDP but employ only a small part of the workforce. Laws shield investors from many taxes, helping the country become an offshore financial centre. Much of the banking system uses U.S. dollars and other foreign currencies. The Central Bank of Uruguay, created in 1967, issues the peso, regulates foreign exchange, and oversees private banks. Major state banks include the Bank of the Eastern Republic of Uruguay and the Mortgage Bank of Uruguay.

## Trade

Trade ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trade)) has often produced deficits since the mid-20th century. The government reduced many import restrictions after the 1980s. Main exports include animal products, frozen beef, live animals, food products, wool, textiles, and hides. Imports include machinery, appliances, chemicals, transport equipment, and processed foods. Brazil is the main trading partner, while Argentina and the United States are also important.

## Services

Services such as public administration, education, computer programming, and tourism form a major part of the economy. Punta del Este and other coastal resorts attract visitors for much of the year and bring foreign exchange. The software industry has become increasingly important. Services and trade employ more than half the workforce, manufacturing about one-fifth, and finance and agriculture smaller shares. Women make up about half of workers but are concentrated in low-wage jobs. Most unionized workers belong to the Inter-Union Workers Assembly-National Federation of Workers.

## Justice

Justice is headed by a five-member Supreme Court elected by the General Assembly for 10-year terms. Appellate Tribunals and Courts of Record form lower levels, and Appellate Tribunal judges require Senate confirmation. Prison conditions are poor but better than in many Latin American countries, and trials may last for years.

Politics uses five-year national elections, and voting is required for citizens aged 18 and older. Secret and obligatory elections date from 1918, and women received voting rights under a 1932 law. The Electoral Court supervises voting, and elections are generally considered fair. The traditional Colorado Party had an urban liberal base, while the Blanco or National Party drew support from conservative landowners. The Broad Front, also called Progressive Encounter, brought together Christian democrats, socialists, communists, and dissidents from the older parties.

## Security

Security institutions are small. Police are poorly paid and have faced accusations of improper conduct, but the country has no secret police. The army, navy, and air force rely on volunteers, many serving one or two years. Uruguayan soldiers have joined many United Nations peacekeeping missions.

Welfare has been central to Uruguay's identity since social legislation in 1912 and 1929. Programs include subsidized health care and benefits for unemployed, low-wage, aged, and low-income workers. Maternity and child-care benefits support lower-income families. Montevideo's Hospital de Clinicas provides low-cost medical care and research. Life expectancy is relatively high, averaging about 73 years for males and 79 years for females in the period described.

## Education

[[Education]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Education)) is compulsory from ages 6 to 11 and free at primary, secondary, technical, and university levels. Literacy is high. Montevideo is the centre of higher education, led by the University of the Republic, founded in 1849, and the Catholic University of Uruguay, founded in 1985. Scientific research and vocational training are supported by specialized institutes.

Culture ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Culture)) is strongly European, especially Spanish and Italian, and shares many traits with Argentina. Indigenous influence is less visible than in many Latin American countries, while the gaucho tradition remains important in art and folklore. Beef is central to cuisine, including parrilladas and chivitos, and mate is a shared national drink. Major holidays include Constitution Day, Independence Day, and the April 19 commemoration of Juan Antonio Lavalleja and the Thirty-Three.

Arts include literature, theatre, music, dance, comics, and popular performance. Jose Enrique Rodo's Ariel argued for spiritual values and resistance to cultural domination, while Florencio Sanchez became a major playwright. Important writers include Juan Zorrilla de San Martin, Juana de Ibarbourou, Delmira Agustini, Horacio Quiroga, Juan Carlos Onetti, Mario Benedetti, and Eduardo Galeano. Uruguay shares tango roots with Argentina, and candombe preserves Afro-Uruguayan Carnival culture.

## History

Early Uruguay had a small Indigenous population, probably no more than 5,000 to 10,000 people. Charrua, Chana, and Guarani peoples lived by fishing, gathering, hunting, and small settlements. Juan Diaz de Solis explored the area in 1516 and was killed with several men. Magellan anchored near the future Montevideo in 1520, and Sebastian Cabot explored the Rio de la Plata in 1526, but Spain found little mineral wealth or easily controlled labour.

Colonial Uruguay grew from cattle, contraband, and rivalry. Cattle and horses introduced from neighbouring regions multiplied into millions and were hunted by gauchos for hides. Portuguese Brazil sent bandeirantes and founded Colonia do Sacramento in 1680 opposite Buenos Aires, creating a smuggling centre. Spain answered by founding fortified Montevideo in 1726. Colonia changed hands several times before Spain received it in 1777. Montevideo became the main Spanish South Atlantic port, and huge unfenced ranches spread across the Banda Oriental.

Independence ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Independence)) came through conflict among Spain, Buenos Aires, Portugal, Brazil, Britain, local elites, rural followers, and caudillos. Jose Gervasio Artigas led the fight against Spanish rule from 1811, supported by rural people, freed African slaves, and antiroyalist Montevideo leaders. His federalism and land redistribution frightened elites and Buenos Aires centralists. Portuguese Brazilian forces took the Banda Oriental in 1820, and Artigas went into exile. Juan Antonio Lavalleja and the Thirty-Three crossed from Argentina in 1825, sparking war. British mediation helped create Uruguay as a buffer state in 1828.

Early republic was unstable. The 1830 constitution governed a country of only about 74,000 people. War had reduced cattle and damaged old fortunes. Brazil and Argentina still coveted the territory. Rival followings of Fructuoso Rivera and Manuel Oribe became the Colorado and Blanco parties. The Guerra Grande devastated the interior, bankrupted government, and nearly destroyed independence before ending in 1851 without clear victory. Later Colorado-Blanco conflict, Brazilian and Argentine involvement, and the War of the Triple Alliance continued to disrupt development.

[[Modernization and Industrialization (Modernization)|Modernization]] accelerated in the late 19th century through immigration, sheep raising, fenced ranches, improved livestock, wool exports, British railways, utilities, and Montevideo improvements. The military took power in 1876 and strengthened control over the interior but lost support because of brutality and corruption. Civilian Colorados returned in 1890. Blanco demands led to the 1897 revolt under Aparicio Saravia. By 1900 the population reached one million, about 13 times the level of 1830.

Batlle reforms under Jose Batlle y Ordonez made Uruguay a social-reform model. After victory over a Blanco revolt in 1904 and a transparent 1905 legislative election, Batlle used stability and prosperity to expand state intervention, improve ranching, reduce dependence on imports and foreign capital, improve labour conditions, expand education, abolish the death penalty, allow women to initiate divorce, protect children born outside marriage, and reduce church influence. His attempt to replace the presidency with a plural executive split politics, but the 1918 constitution created a compromise between president and National Council of Administration.

Economic crisis weakened the system. After Batlle's death in 1929 and the Great Depression, Gabriel Terra blamed the plural executive and carried out a 1933 coup with Blanco support. Terra and Alfredo Baldomir limited unions, delayed reforms, protected British meat markets, built a dam, hired public employees, and encouraged industry. World War II and Korean War demand for meat, wool, and hides brought temporary prosperity, state purchases of British railways and utilities, and renewed plural executive government in 1951.

Decline followed the end of high wool prices in the mid-1950s. Ranching stagnated, living standards fell, inflation rose above 60 percent, public services declined, industries closed, and many workers and professionals emigrated. Blancos won in 1958 for the first time since 1865 but could not solve the crisis. A 1966 constitution restored the presidency, but inflation, production decline, protests, student and union repression, and Tupamaro guerrilla actions destabilized the country. The military defeated the Tupamaros through brutal counterinsurgency and took control in 1973.

Military rule from 1973 to 1985 brought arrests, torture, killings, disappearances, censorship, banned parties, dissolved unions, and an extremely high ratio of political prisoners to population. The regime held wages down, banned strikes, attracted foreign capital, and encouraged borrowing. Some exports, construction, finance, roads, and public works grew, but real wages fell, businesses failed, debt rose, and legitimacy collapsed after voters rejected the military's constitution in 1980.

Democratic return began with Julio Maria Sanguinetti's election in 1984 and inauguration in 1985. He sponsored a 1986 amnesty to appease the military, and voters upheld it in 1989. Luis Alberto Lacalle governed from 1990 to 1995, pursued economic reforms, and joined Mercosur in 1991, increasing trade but also vulnerability to Brazil and Argentina. Voters rejected telephone privatization. Sanguinetti returned from 1995 to 2000, and a 1996 constitutional amendment simplified presidential elections. Jorge Batlle won in 2000.

Left governments began when Tabare Vazquez won in 2004 and became Uruguay's first leftist president in 2005. His Progressive Encounter-Broad Front coalition gained majorities in both houses, improved a weak economy, financed social programs, and investigated crimes from military rule. Jose Mujica, a former guerrilla and senator, won the 2009 runoff. Uruguay continued growth and low unemployment. Congress annulled the amnesty law in 2011 after an Inter-American Court decision, though the Supreme Court later questioned parts of the law. In 2013 Mujica legalized same-sex marriage, making Uruguay the second country in Latin America to do so.
