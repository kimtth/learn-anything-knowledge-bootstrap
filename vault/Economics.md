# Economics

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> Economics studies how individuals, firms, and governments allocate scarce resources, organize production and exchange, distribute income, and respond to incentives and institutions.

## Introduction

Economics ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economics)) is a social science that studies how wealth is produced, distributed, and consumed. Its social importance has changed over time. In the 19th century, economics was mainly a subject for educated gentlemen and a few academics. Economists wrote about policy, but governments rarely asked them for advice before making decisions.

## Definition

No one has fully succeeded in giving a neat definition of economics. Alfred Marshall, a leading English economist of the 19th century, described economics as the study of mankind in the ordinary business of life. For him, it examined the part of individual and social action most closely connected with getting and using the material things needed for well-being.

## Production and Markets

The first key question concerns how labour, machines, and land are combined in production. The second concerns how buyers and sellers are brought together in a functioning market. Since the prices of many things are connected, economists ask how a price system or market mechanism holds together and what conditions are needed for it to survive.

## Marxism

Karl Marx is often called the last of the classical economists. The first volume of Das Kapital ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Das_Kapital)) appeared in 1867, and the second and third volumes appeared after Marx's death in 1885 and 1894. Marx built much of his economics on Smith and Ricardo, especially on the labour theory of value, which says products exchange roughly according to the labour costs needed to produce them.

## Microeconomics

Defines microeconomics as the branch of theory that treats the economy as a system of firms and households interacting in product markets and factor markets. Households buy goods in product markets and sell productive services, such as labour, machines, and land, in factor markets. Firms sell goods in product markets and buy productive services in factor markets.

## Macroeconomics

Macroeconomics studies the aggregate outcome of individual actions. It focuses on income and expenditure flows rather than on separate markets. Keynes's consumption function, for example, links aggregate consumption to national income as an empirical generalization rather than building it directly from each consumer's behaviour.

## Money

[[Money]] is one of the principal subfields of modern economics, partly because government control of the medium of exchange is one of the oldest public functions. Economists had already studied the powerful effects of money on prices and activity in the 18th century. In the 19th century, the quantity theory of money became a major tradition.

## International Trade

The Heckscher-Ohlin theory, associated with Eli Heckscher and Bertil Ohlin, explains international trade through countries' relative supplies of land, labour, and capital. A country tends to export goods that use its abundant factors of production. Countries with cheap labour, for example, are expected to export labour-intensive goods.

## Labour

Labour economics is an old specialty, like money and international economics. Its special difficulty comes from the fact that labour is not a normal commodity. Land or machinery can be bought and sold, but labour services are hired from people who cannot be separated from their own services. This means nonmonetary factors often matter in labour markets.

## Agriculture

Agricultural economics matters because farming has long been a favourite example of perfect competition. Yet agriculture is also heavily regulated and supported by governments. Price controls, income supports, output ceilings, and marketing cartels make farming a rich field for studying both markets and public intervention.

## Key Events & Developments

- 1776: [[Adam Smith]] published The Wealth of Nations, giving economics its first major full-scale treatise and helping found classical political economy.
- 1798: Thomas Malthus stated the population principle used by David Ricardo in his model of growth, land scarcity, and diminishing returns.
- 1817: David Ricardo published Principles of Political Economy and Taxation, developing economic modeling and the doctrine of comparative advantage.
- 1848: [[John Stuart Mill]] restated Ricardian economics and extended its authority for another generation.
- 1867: Karl Marx published the first volume of Das Kapital ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Das_Kapital)), building Marxian economics on the labour theory of value and surplus value.
- 1870s: Stanley Jevons, Carl Menger, and Leon Walras helped launch the marginal revolution, shifting value theory toward marginal utility.
- 1890: Alfred Marshall's Principles of Economics helped join classical and marginalist ideas into neoclassical economics.
- 1929: The stock market crash ended the long period of marginalist consolidation and set the stage for deeper challenges to orthodox economics.
- 1933: Edward Chamberlin and Joan Robinson published major works on monopolistic and imperfect competition.
- 1936: John Maynard Keynes published the General Theory, transforming macroeconomics through the study of effective demand, income, and employment.
- 1948: Roy Harrod's Towards a Dynamic Economics helped establish growth theory by adding time to Keynesian analysis.
- 1963: Milton Friedman and Anna Schwartz published A Monetary History of the United States, 1867-1960, a key work of monetarism.
- 1968: Milton Friedman's address on the natural rate of unemployment challenged Keynesian full-employment policy.
- 1969: The first Nobel Prize in Economic Sciences was awarded, marking the growing prestige and practical importance of economics.
- 1970: George Akerlof's Market for Lemons article helped create information economics around asymmetric information.
- 1990s: Endogenous growth theory, experimental economics, and renewed doubts about efficient financial markets became important developments.
- 2001: George Akerlof, Joseph Stiglitz, and Michael Spence won the Nobel Prize for work on asymmetric information.

## Key People

- [[Adam Smith]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adam_Smith)): Founded classical political economy through The Wealth of Nations and explained how competitive markets can produce unintended social results.
- David Ricardo ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Ricardo)): Developed the economic model, the theory of diminishing returns on land, and the law of comparative costs.
- Thomas Malthus ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Robert_Malthus)): Formulated the population principle that Ricardo used to explain pressure on food production and land rents.
- [[John Stuart Mill]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Stuart_Mill)): Restated Ricardian economics and prolonged its authority in British political economy.
- Karl Marx ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karl_Marx)): Built Marxian economics on labour value and surplus value, though his technical influence on economics was limited.
- Stanley Jevons ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stanley_Jevons)), Carl Menger ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carl_Menger)), and Leon Walras ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leon_Walras)): Led the marginal revolution, shifting value theory toward marginal utility and consumer choice.
- Alfred Marshall ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alfred_Marshall)): Joined classical and marginalist ideas, emphasized both supply and demand, and shaped neoclassical economics.
- Arthur Pigou ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arthur_Pigou)): Developed welfare economics through the distinction between private and social costs.
- John Maynard Keynes ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Maynard_Keynes)): Created Keynesian economics, focusing on effective demand, national income, unemployment, and government policy.
- Roy Harrod ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roy_Harrod)) and Evsey Domar ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Evsey_Domar)): Built the Harrod-Domar model of growth from Keynesian ideas about investment, saving, and income.
- Milton Friedman ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Milton_Friedman)): Led monetarism and challenged Keynesian policy through the natural rate of unemployment and the importance of money supply.
- Robert Lucas: Advanced rational expectations, arguing that anticipated policy may have little effect on real economic activity.
- Eli Heckscher ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eli_Heckscher)) and Bertil Ohlin ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bertil_Ohlin)): Explained [[International Trade|international trade]] through countries' relative supplies of land, labour, and capital.
- Wassily Leontief ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wassily_Leontief)): Tested Heckscher-Ohlin theory and found the Leontief Paradox in U.S. trade patterns.
- Ronald Coase ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ronald_Coase)): Founded key ideas in law and economics through transaction costs, firms, and social cost.
- George Akerlof ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Akerlof)), Joseph Stiglitz ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph_Stiglitz)), and Michael Spence ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michael_Spence)): Developed information economics and won the Nobel Prize for asymmetric information.
- Friedrich A. Hayek ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Friedrich_A._Hayek)), Ludwig Mises ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ludwig_Mises)), and Israel Kirzner ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Israel_Kirzner)): Represented Austrian economics, especially free markets, knowledge, entrepreneurship, and spontaneous learning.
- Piero Sraffa ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Piero_Sraffa)): Inspired Sraffian economics, which tried to explain prices through input-output relations rather than consumer preferences.
- Gary Becker ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gary_Becker)): Extended rational choice theory into family, education, marriage, and other social questions beyond traditional markets.

## Interesting Facts

- Economics ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economics)) was once mostly the hobby of gentlemen and a few academics, but modern governments, banks, and international agencies commonly employ economists.
- In the [[United States of America (United States)|United States]], about 400 higher-learning institutions were said to grant about 900 new economics Ph.D.'s each year.
- Jacob Viner's playful definition says economics is simply what economists do, because formal definitions are so hard to make precise.
- [[Adam Smith]]'s invisible hand works only when competition is supported by proper legal and institutional conditions.
- Ricardo's comparative advantage says trade can benefit a country even when another country is absolutely better at producing many goods.
- [[Marx and Marxism (Marx)|Marx]]'s social influence was enormous, but Marxian economics had little direct effect on economics as a technical social science.
- Walras's abstract general equilibrium work was called the Magna Carta of economics by Joseph Schumpeter.
- oligopoly remains hard to explain because many modern industries are dominated by a few firms, while older theory works best with many small competitors.
- Keynes argued that an economy has no automatic tendency to reach full employment, a claim that changed macroeconomics.
- [[Game Theory|Game theory]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Game_theory)) helped economics answer the criticism that it lacked laboratory-style testing by encouraging controlled experimental games.
- George Akerlof's secondhand-car example showed how asymmetric information can make markets fail or disappear.
- Treats environmental economics as a growing applied field because pollution and global warming made external costs central to policy.
- Health economics is described as one of the most promising applied fields because rising health costs in Western countries continue to demand economic analysis.
- Spatial economics has a rich history, but economists have never given location the steady attention one might expect.
