# Los Angeles

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> Los Angeles is a vast southern California metropolis whose coastal plain, mountains, automobile culture, diverse population, industry, tourism, and global entertainment role define its modern character. Its rapid twentieth-century growth transformed a small settlement into a world city while producing environmental and urban challenges.

## Introduction

Los Angeles is the seat of Los Angeles county in southern California and the second most populous city and metropolitan area in the [[United States of America (United States)|United States]]. The city spreads across a coastal plain between mountains and the Pacific, while the county includes about 90 other cities, two Channel Islands, Mount San Antonio, deserts, and 75 miles of coast.

The city and county ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Consolidated_city-county)) are geographically, culturally, and economically intertwined. Population density ranges from almost empty mountain areas to dense downtown districts, and the region's great size makes any account of the city also partly an account of the county.

By the early 21st century, the Los Angeles metropolitan area included millions across divisions such as Los Angeles-Long Beach-Glendale and Los Angeles-Long Beach-Santa Ana. Its scale helped make the region a world city.

Los Angeles became world-class only recently. At the start of the 20th century, it still seemed like a large village and lacked a natural harbour, yet it became a centre of commerce, agriculture, tourism, industry, Hollywood celebrity, and outdoor leisure.

The Angeleno lifestyle depends heavily on the automobile, single-family housing, informality, sunshine, and recreation. Critics have attacked the city as shallow or troubled by earthquakes, fires, smog, gangs, riots, and inequality, while defenders point to climate, diversity, geographic variety, and modern American energy.

## Landscape

Los Angeles county contains inland valleys, coastal plain, low mountains, higher mountain arcs, beaches, rivers, deserts, and seacoast. Nearly half the county is mountain chain, and its landscape is shaped by earthquakes, firestorms, mudslides, and seasonal floods.

The San Gabriel and San Bernardino mountains stand to the north and northeast, with the Santa Monica Mountains, Puente Hills, Repetto Hills, and San Jose Hills marking valleys such as the San Fernando, San Gabriel, and San Bernardino. The Santa Ana Mountains lie farther south.

The Los Angeles River, San Gabriel River, and Santa Clara River cross the county. Historic floods shifted the Los Angeles River in 1825, covered the basin in water in 1861-1862, and pushed humans to confine waterways in concrete channels.

## Climate

The Los Angeles climate is semiarid or Mediterranean. Latitude, marine air, and mountain shelter create mild temperatures, two seasons, and many sunny days, while local temperatures vary sharply between beaches, downtown, valleys, and mountain foothills.

Rain averages about 15 inches annually, though El Nino can bring much more. Long rains or intense downpours can trigger debris slides, especially after fires strip hillsides, and Santa Ana winds bring hot, dry, unsettling air through mountain passes.

The same basin shape and warm climate help create photochemical smog, a Los Angeles problem since the 1940s. State and local antipollution laws reduced motor-vehicle emissions, but air quality remained a serious regional issue.

Downtown Los Angeles includes districts such as Bunker Hill, Civic Center, Music Center, Broadway, Chinatown, Olvera Street, Little Tokyo, Library Square, and the Staples Center area. Many are busy by day and quiet by evening, and downtown long lacked middle- and lower-income housing, street amenities, and residents.

## Downtown Renewal

Since the 1980s, the city has tried to revive downtown through housing, recreation, culture, pedestrian activity, loft conversions, and renewed attention to the river. Skid Row, also called Central City East, remained one of the area's deepest problems.

## People

Los Angeles's ethnic and racial composition shifted repeatedly. The 1781 Spanish-era settlers included many people of African, Native American, or mixed ancestry, but white Midwestern migrants later became dominant enough to give the city the nickname “seacoast of Iowa.”

Mexican migration increased after the 1910 Mexican Revolution, and federal immigration changes from the mid-1960s helped make Los Angeles one of the world's most diverse cities. Los Angeles county has especially large Hispanic, Asian, and Native American populations.

African Americans formed about one-tenth of the population in the early 21st century, though some middle-class families moved outward and formerly African American cities such as Compton and Inglewood became predominantly Latino. Population change came through both immigration and birth rates.

The city contains many languages, including Spanish, Vietnamese, Hmong, Cantonese, Tagalog, Korean, Armenian, Russian, Farsi, Cambodian, and Hebrew. Radio and newspapers serve many language communities.

Religion is also diverse. Roman Catholics remain numerous, Protestant and Evangelical groups expanded, William J. Seymour's Azusa Street revival of 1906 launched Pentecostalism, and Jewish, Mormon, Eastern Orthodox, Muslim, Buddhist, Hindu, Bahai, and other communities became part of the region.

## Economy

The Los Angeles economy is huge, diversified, and constantly changing. Agriculture, manufacturing, services, finance, technology, fashion, music, toys, jewelry, and movies all contributed, and the metropolitan economy would rank among the world's largest if treated as a country.

After strong 20th-century growth, recession hit in the 1990s, especially after reduced military spending. Recovery came mid-decade, with growth in construction, transportation, utilities, finance, insurance, real estate, government, and high technology.

[[Globalization and Culture (Globalization, Cultural)|Globalization]] shifted jobs. Higher-paying manufacturing declined, lower-paying work increased, immigrant labour became more important, sweatshop conditions appeared in some industries, and unions later organized service workers, janitors, hotel workers, teachers, and nurses while supporting living-wage politics.

## Agriculture

Los Angeles county once had abundant agriculture ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agriculture)), including citrus orchards, vegetables, fruit, nuts, hay, and dairy cattle. By the mid-20th century, it was the most productive agricultural county in the United States.

Urban sprawl consumed most orchards and farmland, but agriculture continued through nursery and greenhouse plants, vegetables, fruits, nuts, seeds, and hay. The change shows how urban expansion transformed the county's economic landscape.

## Manufacturing

Edward L. Doheny's 1892 oil discovery helped make Los Angeles a major petroleum field and encouraged industrialism. After the 1906 San Francisco earthquake, some manufacturers moved south for lower wages.

World War II federal spending turned Los Angeles into the “Pittsburgh of the West” through warplane and merchant-vessel production. During the Cold War, aerospace made the region central to the military-industrial complex.

Postwar construction boomed as veterans' loans encouraged homes, malls, and offices. Older industries such as fish packing, shipbuilding, auto and airplane assembly, oil, steel, tires, and glassmaking later declined or vanished, while electronics, computers, and links to NASA's Jet Propulsion Laboratory became more important.

## Transportation

Automobile-dependent Los Angeles struggled to build balanced transit. The Pacific Electric Railway, created by Henry E. Huntington, served real estate development but lost money, could not match suburban automobile convenience, and ran its last Red Car in 1961.

Freeways became necessities after the late 1940s. The Arroyo Seco Parkway opened on the last day of 1939, the Hollywood Freeway followed in 1948, and the four-level downtown freeway stack became a defining image.

Traffic delays and smog revived interest in public transit. Voters eventually approved a plan to rebuild bus service and add light rail and subway lines, showing the region's attempt to balance freeways with mass transportation.

## Government

Los Angeles county has a complex web of jurisdictions: municipal, county, special district, regional, state, and federal. The powerful five-member County Board of Supervisors governs unincorporated areas, contracts services to some cities, and manages a huge population and budget.

The 15-member Los Angeles City Council controls contracts, permits, zoning, planning, leases, licenses, and department funding, while the mayor prepares the budget, nominates officials, and can veto ordinances. Elections are nonpartisan, reflecting Progressive-era reform.

Postwar politics were once dominated by white downtown businessmen, but the spread of population westward and into the San Fernando Valley changed power. In 1973, Tom Bradley became the city's first African American mayor, backed by white progressives and African Americans.

## Education

Los Angeles has major public and private higher-education institutions. UCLA, established in 1919, is the largest University of California branch; the California State University system has four county campuses; and USC, Caltech, the Claremont Colleges, Occidental, and Loyola Marymount are important private institutions.

The region pioneered two-year community colleges. Its school system includes many independent districts, and the Los Angeles Unified School District, the country's second largest, struggled with busing, segregation litigation, white flight, private-school growth, rising Latino enrollment, funding limits, and enrollment growth.

Los Angeles's cultural image changed from overgrown village to Tinseltown and beyond. Its arts culture includes theatres, outdoor amphitheatres, music, dance, literature, architecture, museums, and sports, often shaped by Hollywood and the region's large creative labour force.

## Theatre

Theatre began in Los Angeles by the 1850s, and eastern touring companies visited by the 1890s. Early 20th-century theatres could often serve both film and stage, while outdoor venues such as the Hollywood Bowl, Greek Theatre, and John Anson Ford Amphitheatre became lasting performance sites.

The Ahmanson Theatre and Mark Taper Forum opened in 1967 at the Music Center, helping make Los Angeles the country's second major theatre city. Small theatres multiplied because many professional actors, writers, and directors lived in the region.

Music ranged from the Los Angeles Philharmonic, founded in 1919, to jazz on Central Avenue, big band performance, surf music, rock, opera, ballet, and experimental dance. The Walt Disney Concert Hall opened in 2003, and the Los Angeles Opera opened in 1985.

Conductors such as Alfred Wallenstein, Zubin Mehta, Carlo Maria Giulini, Andre Previn, and Esa-Pekka Salonen helped build the Philharmonic's reputation. European musicians fleeing Nazism, including Otto Klemperer, Kurt Weill, Igor Stravinsky, and Arnold Schoenberg, strengthened the local classical scene.

## Literature

Southern California fiction began a durable romantic mode with Helen Hunt Jackson's Ramona of 1884. Hollywood novels by Nathanael West and F. Scott Fitzgerald, satire by Evelyn Waugh and Aldous Huxley, and hard-boiled fiction by James Cain, Raymond Chandler, Chester Himes, and Walter Mosley presented Los Angeles as both sunny and corrupt.

Later Los Angeles fiction included Joan Didion's Play It As It Lays, Carolyn See's Making History, and Janet Fitch's White Oleander. The Los Angeles Times Festival of Books, held since 1996, became the country's largest literary event of its kind.

## Architecture

Los Angeles architecture includes many styles, especially Spanish Mission Revival, Craftsman, and the California bungalow. Irving Gill, Frank Lloyd Wright, Richard Neutra, and R.M. Schindler did original work there.

Sunshine, landscape, and weak stylistic traditions encouraged experiment, from playful commercial buildings such as the Brown Derby and Tail o' the Pup to Case Study Houses by Craig Ellwood and Charles and Ray Eames. After the 1956 height limit ended, skyscrapers rose.

## Museums

Los Angeles has more than 200 [[Museums (Museums, History of)|museums]]. LACMA, founded in 1910, anchors Museum Row; other major institutions include the Huntington, Norton Simon, Getty Center and Villa, MOCA, Natural History Museum, Page Museum-La Brea Tar Pits, California African American Museum, Japanese American National Museum, and Skirball Cultural Center.

Sports culture includes the 1932 Summer Olympics, the arrival of the Dodgers in 1958, the Lakers in 1960, and later professional teams, recreational landscapes, beaches, and stadiums that made sport a central part of civic identity.

## History

For centuries the region was home to Tongva and Chumash peoples. Juan Rodriguez Cabrillo entered Santa Monica Bay in 1542, and Spanish colonization later brought missions, presidios, and civilian settlements.

Junipero Serra's Franciscans founded San Gabriel in 1771 and San Fernando in 1797. In 1781, Felipe de Neve and 44 settlers from Sonora and Mazatlan founded El Pueblo de la Reina de los Angeles near the Native village of Yang-na.

Spanish rule ended in 1822, Mexican authorities made the pueblo a city in 1835, and California became part of the United States after the Mexican-American War. Los Angeles officially became American when California joined the Union in 1850.

## Water and the Aqueduct

Water transformed Los Angeles. William Mulholland and thousands of labourers built the 233-mile Los Angeles Aqueduct, which delivered Owens Valley water by gravity to the San Fernando Valley on November 5, 1913.

In the 1920s, Owens Valley residents attacked the aqueduct during the “water wars,” and the St. Francis Dam collapse of 1928 killed hundreds. The city later extended water systems toward Mono Lake and imported water from the Colorado River and Feather River.

The 1920s and 1930s brought oil, speculation, suburban growth, aircraft, film, religious excitement, and corruption. Aimee Semple McPherson, Hollywood, and automobile migration all belonged to this boom culture.

Later modernization included the Los Angeles State Historic Park, the Alameda Transportation Corridor linking harbour and downtown, dredging and cargo expansion at the nation's busiest seaport, and renewed attention to the river.

Despite riots, earthquakes, smog, and economic disruption, Los Angeles continued attracting visitors and residents. Its history is one of turbulent expansion, crisis, reinvention, and persistent optimism among people who choose to stay.
