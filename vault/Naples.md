# Naples

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> Naples is southern Italy's commercial and cultural center, located on the Bay of Naples beneath Vesuvius. Its dense historic fabric of monuments, churches, museums, markets, port activity, and modern environmental and suburban pressures reveals a city where long urban history remains intensely lived.

## Introduction

![[attachments/naples_001.webp|420]]

*Bay of Naples and Vesuvius*

Naples, Italian Napoli and ancient Neapolis, is the capital of Naples province in Campania, southern Italy. It lies on the Bay of Naples between Mount Vesuvius and the Campi Flegrei, about 120 miles southeast of Rome. Its volcanic setting, bay, views of Capri, and Greek origins give it a strong sense of continuity with the classical past. The historic centre was designated a UNESCO World Heritage site in 1995.

Naples remains the commercial and cultural centre of southern Italy even though its port has declined in relative importance. Since [[World Wars (World War II)|World War II]], bombing damage, modernization, industrial pollution, speculative growth, and suburban expansion have altered the city. Yet Naples retains a dense popular setting in which monuments, churches, palaces, markets, and neighbourhood life remain closely mixed.

## Landscape

The city's landscape is shaped by hills, sea, volcanic zones, and the curve of the bay. The Sorrentine peninsula, Posillipo, Capri, Vesuvius, the Gulf of Pozzuoli, and the Gulf of Salerno frame the city. Vesuvius last erupted in 1944, an earthquake damaged the area in 1980, and Pozzuoli has suffered from bradyseism, the rising and falling of land.

## The Castel Nuovo

![[attachments/naples_002.webp|420]]

*Castel Nuovo in Naples*

The Castel Nuovo ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Castel_Nuovo)), founded in 1279 by Charles I of Naples, is one of Naples's major landmarks. It is also called Maschio Angioino, recalling Angevin power. In the 14th century, King Robert's court welcomed Petrarch and Boccaccio, and Giotto painted now-lost frescoes there. Alfonso V of Aragon later added Renaissance sculpture celebrating his 1443 entry into Naples.

Nearby civic and religious buildings show the layered waterfront history of Naples. The city hall incorporates a 16th-century church, Santa Maria del Carmine stands near the market and old execution ground, and San Martino Hill holds a former Carthusian monastery and Castel Sant'Elmo. World War II bombardment erased much of the old portside character.

## Monte di Dio and San Carlo

![[attachments/naples_003.webp|420]]

*San Carlo theatre in Naples*

The Monte di Dio and royal palace area contains churches, the San Carlo opera house, and the Galleria Umberto I. San Carlo remains central to European musical life and recalls Naples's exceptional 18th-century musical creativity. The Galleria became a wartime and postwar setting remembered by writers such as John Horne Burns, Norman Lewis, Curzio Malaparte, and Raffaele La Capria.

## Via Toledo

Via Toledo, laid out in 1536 by the Spanish viceroy Don Pedro di Toledo, runs north into dense central Naples. It is lined with shops, churches, and palazzi, while steep alleys rise toward San Martino through the Spanish Quarter. Piazza Dante marks a boundary of the ancient Greek and Roman core, whose main decumani preserve the city's oldest street structure.

## Santa Chiara

The Santa Chiara complex and nearby Gesu Nuovo stand at a central point of historic Naples. Santa Chiara was built for the Franciscans in the 14th century, transformed in the 18th, damaged by bombing in 1943, and restored toward Gothic form. Spaccanapoli continues east through palaces, churches, workshops, philosophical memory, and the popular Christmas creche industry of Via San Gregorio Armeno.

## The Duomo

The Duomo is dedicated to St. Januarius, or San Gennaro, whose liquefying blood inspires two festivals each year. Its interior, chapel, antique columns, artworks, sculpture, and access to the early basilica of Santa Restituta and baptistery of San Giovanni in Fonte form a compressed history of Naples. Nearby medieval, Baroque, Renaissance, and Aragonese monuments lead toward the Capuana Gate, Via Carbonara, and the railway-station district reshaped by late-19th-century slum clearance after the 1884 cholera epidemic.

## Museums

Naples has two great [[Museums (Museums, History of)|museums]] founded under Bourbon rule. The National Archaeological Museum contains major Greco-Roman antiquities from Herculaneum, Pompeii, and other Campanian sites, along with the Farnese marbles and Egyptian material. The Capodimonte museum and gallery holds paintings, tapestries, porcelain, and works by artists such as Simone Martini, Masaccio, Botticelli, [[Titian]], El Greco, and Pieter Bruegel the Elder.

The city's people and urban life are not concentrated only in grand squares. Naples is understood through small populous enclaves that change through the day and preserve distinctive local character. Tourism declined after World War II, port travel decreased, and crime and poverty deterred visitors, but the city remains a gateway to Pompeii, Herculaneum, Capri, Vesuvius, Sorrento, and the broader bay.

## Industry

Industry makes Naples the industrial centre of southern Italy. Bourbon-era manufacturing included Capodimonte porcelain, silk, and textiles. Later industries include food processing, wine, steel, electronics, petroleum refining, automobile assembly, and tourism. After World War II, state planning, public companies, infrastructure upgrades, and energy expansion supported industrial development, though Naples and the south still lag behind northern Italy.

## Transportation

Naples has long been a transport centre. The first Mediterranean steamboat was launched at its royal shipyards in 1818. Harbour reconstruction began in 1826, and postwar modernization kept Naples a major Mediterranean port. Italy's first railway ran from Naples to Portici in 1839, the first funicular opened to Vomero in 1880, and the city became a railway, road, air, and sea junction for southern Italy.

## History

Naples was founded about 600 BC as Neapolis near older Palaepolis, tied to Greek colonies at Pithecusa and Cumae. It preserved Greek language and manners long after coming under Roman power in the 4th century BC. Under Rome, Naples and the bay became centres of Greek culture, learned retreat, and elite villas, including sites later buried or preserved near Vesuvius.

Ancient Neapolitan culture is linked with classical writers and places: Horace, [[Virgil]], Statius, Capri's Villa Jovis, Oplontis, Pausilypon at Posillipo, and the Villa of the Papyri near Herculaneum. Finds from buried Vesuvian sites enriched Naples's museums and helped make the region essential to cultivated travel.
