## Introduction

United Nations ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_Nations)) is a worldwide international organization established on October 24, 1945, after World War II. It followed the League of Nations, which had been created by the Treaty of Versailles in 1919 and ended in 1946. The UN is headquartered in [[New York City]] and also has major offices in [[Geneva]], [[Vienna]], and other cities. Its official languages are Arabic, Chinese, English, French, Russian, and Spanish.

United Nations ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_Nations)) was founded to prevent war, defend human rights, support international law, improve living standards, and help states coordinate action. It continued some League of Nations structures, but it differed in its broader commitment to peacekeeping, economic and social development, decolonization, and human rights. During the Cold War, rivalry between the [[United States of America (United States)|United States]] and the Soviet Union limited many security functions. After 1991, the organization faced more requests for conflict resolution, peacekeeping, humanitarian relief, and responses to new threats such as terrorism and AIDS.

## General Assembly

![[attachments/united_nations_002.webp|420]]

*General Assembly of the United Nations*

General Assembly is the only UN body in which all member states are represented. It discusses issues, makes recommendations, admits new members, elects members of other organs, supervises reports, helps choose judges of the International Court of Justice, and participates in selecting the secretary-general. Most decisions use a simple majority, while major questions such as budget, membership, peace, and security require a two-thirds majority.

General Assembly works through annual sessions, special sessions, a general debate, and six main committees covering disarmament, economic and financial issues, social and cultural issues, special political and decolonization questions, administration and budget, and law. It cannot force states to obey its resolutions, but it brings public attention to major issues such as decolonization, apartheid, terrorism, AIDS, human rights, and North-South economic disputes. Its voting blocs changed over time, from Cold War alignments to regional and economic groupings.

## Security Council

Security Council ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_Nations_Security_Council)) has primary responsibility for international peace and security. It originally had 11 members, but a 1965 Charter amendment expanded it to 15: five permanent members and 10 nonpermanent members. The permanent members are [[China]], [[France]], [[Russia]], the [[United Kingdom]], and the [[United States of America (United States)|United States]]. Nonpermanent members are elected for two-year terms to reflect regional balance.

Security Council ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_Nations_Security_Council)) decisions on substantive matters require nine affirmative votes and no veto by a permanent member. This veto power made action difficult during the Cold War, but the council still developed tools such as peacekeeping, sanctions, monitoring missions, and authorization of force. Debate about reform grew as membership expanded and as countries such as [[Germany]], [[India]], and [[Japan]] sought permanent seats.

## Economic and Social Council

Economic and Social Council, or ECOSOC, coordinates UN work on economic, social, humanitarian, cultural, health, education, science, technology, population, narcotics, statistics, women’s status, and human-rights-related issues. It conducts studies, prepares recommendations and conventions, and connects the General Assembly with specialized agencies and programs. Its membership expanded from 18 to 54 through Charter amendments in 1965 and 1974.

ECOSOC also grants consultative status to nongovernmental organizations. These NGOs may attend meetings, issue reports, and sometimes testify. By the early 21st century, more than 2,500 NGOs had consultative status, showing how UN work increasingly involved civil society as well as governments.

## Trusteeship Council

Trusteeship Council ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_Nations_Trusteeship_Council)) was created to supervise trust territories and guide them toward self-government or independence. It grew from the idea that territories taken from defeated powers should not simply be annexed by victors. Unlike the League of Nations mandate system, the UN trusteeship system allowed petitions from territories and required visiting missions.

Trusteeship Council ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_Nations_Trusteeship_Council)) became inactive after Palau, the last trust territory, gained independence in 1994. Earlier trust territories included Nauru, New Guinea, Ruanda-Urundi, Togoland, Cameroon, Pacific Islands, Western Samoa, Tanganyika, and Palestine-related mandates, while South West Africa, later Namibia, was kept outside the system by South Africa. Later proposals imagined new roles for the council, such as oversight of global commons or a forum for minority and Indigenous peoples.

## International Court of Justice

International Court of Justice ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Court_of_Justice)), often called the World Court, is the principal judicial organ of the UN. Its roots go back to Hague arbitration ideas in 1899 and the Permanent Court of International Justice under the League of Nations in 1919. It received its present name with the UN in 1945. Its decisions are binding, but states generally must consent before disputes can be heard.

International Court of Justice ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Court_of_Justice)) can decide cases referred by states and give advisory opinions when asked by authorized UN organs or agencies. It has helped settle some disputes, including a 1992 border dispute between Honduras and El Salvador, but governments often avoid submitting sensitive issues. The court has 15 judges elected separately by the General Assembly and Security Council for nine-year terms, with no two judges from the same state and with major legal systems represented.

## Secretariat

Secretariat is led by the secretary-general, the UN’s chief administrative officer and most visible public representative. The secretary-general is elected for a renewable five-year term by the General Assembly on Security Council recommendation. The role includes preparing budgets, supervising reports and studies, managing meetings and translation, and bringing threats to peace and security before UN organs.

Secretary-general also serves as negotiator, mediator, spokesperson, and political figure. Secretaries-general often come from small or neutral countries. Dag Hammarskjold and Kofi Annan received the Nobel Peace Prize, with Annan sharing the 2001 award with the UN. Secretariat staff are international civil servants who swear loyalty to the UN rather than to national governments.

## Specialized Agencies and Global Conferences

United Nations system includes specialized agencies and related institutions such as UNESCO, WHO, the World Bank, and the IMF. These bodies are linked to UN goals but often have separate budgets, memberships, and decision processes. The World Bank and IMF are especially powerful and relatively independent, while UN programs and agencies handle work in health, education, development, finance, labour, food, refugees, and culture.

Global conferences became major UN tools, especially in the 1990s. They addressed disarmament, nuclear weapons, small arms, racism, population, hunger, crime, water, environment, women, and human rights. These meetings worked as world forums: they gathered governments, experts, and NGOs; spread knowledge; raised attention; and placed emerging issues on the international agenda. Some governments complained of conference fatigue, but the conferences helped build public support for global action.

## Finances

UN finance depends on assessed member contributions, voluntary contributions, and separate budgets for peacekeeping, specialized agencies, and special programs. The secretary-general submits a biennial regular budget to the General Assembly. A Committee on Contributions proposes each state’s share based on economic capacity. The [[United States of America (United States)|United States]] remained the largest contributor, but its share fell from about two-fifths at the UN’s founding to about one-fifth by 2000.

Financial crisis became a recurring problem because some states delayed or refused payments and because peacekeeping and assistance demands grew after the Cold War. The U.S. contribution became especially controversial in the 1990s, leading to negotiation over budget shares. UN privileges and immunities, approved in 1946, protect the organization’s independent work.

## Headquarters

![[attachments/united_nations_001.webp|420]]

*Flag of the United Nations*

UN headquarters was placed in New York after the General Assembly’s first session in London. John D. Rockefeller, Jr. donated land in Manhattan, while temporary offices operated at Lake Success on Long Island. The Secretariat building was completed and occupied in 1951-52, and the General Assembly and council buildings followed in 1952. The UN flag, adopted in 1947, shows a world map from the North Pole surrounded by olive branches on a light blue background. October 24 is United Nations Day.

## Functions

Peace and security are central UN functions. Chapter 6 of the Charter supports peaceful settlement through negotiation, mediation, arbitration, judicial decisions, and Security Council recommendations. Chapter 7 supports collective security, allowing sanctions and force when peaceful methods fail. Cold War rivalry limited enforcement, so preventive diplomacy and peacekeeping became especially important. After the Cold War, peacekeeping, humanitarian protection, terrorism, disease, and internal conflicts became larger parts of UN work.

Economic welfare and cooperation involve the General Assembly, ECOSOC, the Secretariat, regional commissions, specialized agencies, and development bodies. UNRRA began relief and rehabilitation in 1943, before the UN formally existed, and the International Refugee Organization operated from 1947 to 1951. The World Bank, International Finance Corporation, and International Development Association financed reconstruction, private investment, and development loans. Regional commissions supported Europe, Asia and the Pacific, Latin America, Western Asia, and Africa.

Specialized agencies make the UN system more than a diplomatic meeting place. Organizations concerned with health, labour, food, education, science, culture, finance, aviation, shipping, telecommunications, and development turn broad principles into technical standards, data, loans, campaigns, and field programs. Global conferences added another layer by bringing states, experts, and nongovernmental groups together around issues such as environment, population, women, housing, food, racism, and development. This is one reason the UN can influence everyday policy even when the Security Council is blocked.

Refugee protection became a major UN responsibility. The International Refugee Organization helped more than one million European and Asian refugees after World War II. In 1951, the refugee convention and the UN High Commissioner for Refugees created a continuing structure. By the end of the 1990s, about 20 million people had been forced to migrate or flee oppression, violence, or hunger. UNHCR worked in more than 120 countries with more than 450 NGOs and received the Nobel Peace Prize in 1954 and 1981. UNRWA separately served Palestine refugees in the Near East.

[[Human Rights|Human rights]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Human_rights)) were built into the UN Charter more directly than they had been in the League of Nations. The General Assembly and ECOSOC created bodies to study and promote rights without distinction of race, sex, language, or religion. The Commission on Human Rights, first chaired by Eleanor Roosevelt in 1946, helped develop international standards, including an international bill of rights and conventions on civil liberties, women’s status, and freedoms.

## Assessment

United Nations ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_Nations)) is the only global organization with broad functions across peace, security, rights, development, humanitarian relief, law, environment, and social cooperation. Its founders understood that peace could not be separated from political, legal, economic, social, environmental, and individual rights. The UN helped complete decolonization, bring newly independent states into international life, create new peacekeeping practices, develop specialized agencies, and make NGOs and individuals more visible in global politics.

United Nations ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_Nations)) also reflects the limits of international politics. Its Security Council structure still mirrors the power balance of 1945, especially through permanent membership and veto power. General Assembly blocs, Secretariat tensions, global conference debates, and budget conflicts reveal political and economic divisions among states. Respect for national sovereignty and noninterference remains a basic rule, so the UN can transform some parts of world politics but cannot escape the realities of state power.

## Key Events & Developments

- League of Nations was created by the Treaty of Versailles in 1919 and became the main predecessor to the UN.
- United Nations ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_Nations)) was founded on October 24, 1945, with worldwide scope and broad peace, rights, and development goals.
- Security Council ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_Nations_Security_Council)) membership expanded from 11 to 15 through a Charter amendment in 1965.
- People’s Republic of China replaced the Republic of China in the UN seat in 1971.
- Palau became independent in 1994, ending the active work of the Trusteeship Council.
- Post-Cold War conditions after 1991 increased demands for UN peacekeeping, mediation, relief, and development action.

## Key People

- Eleanor Roosevelt ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eleanor_Roosevelt)) chaired the early Commission on Human Rights and helped shape UN rights work.
- Dag Hammarskjold ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dag_Hammarskj%C3%B6ld)) served as secretary-general and received the Nobel Peace Prize in 1961.
- Kofi Annan ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kofi_Annan)) served as secretary-general and shared the 2001 Nobel Peace Prize with the UN.
- John D. Rockefeller, Jr. ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_D._Rockefeller_Jr.)) donated the Manhattan land used for the UN headquarters.
