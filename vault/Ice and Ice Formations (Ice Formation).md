Ice formations are natural bodies or layers of frozen water that occur on land, in oceans, rivers, lakes, soil, and the atmosphere. They include snowpacks, glaciers, ice sheets, ice shelves, sea ice, lake ice, river ice, icebergs, ground ice, and permafrost. Ice matters because it stores fresh water, reflects sunlight, shapes landscapes, affects ocean circulation, controls habitats, and creates hazards for people and infrastructure.

Water freezes when enough heat is removed for molecules to arrange into a crystalline solid. Freezing point depends on pressure and dissolved substances: salt lowers the freezing point of seawater, while pressure and impurities affect ice behaviour in other settings. Ice is less dense than liquid water, so it floats. This unusual property allows a layer of ice to insulate the water beneath it, helping aquatic life survive winter and influencing how lakes and seas exchange heat with the atmosphere.

## Glaciers and Ice Sheets

A glacier forms on land where snow accumulates year after year and survives summer melting. Weight compacts snow into firn and then dense glacial ice. Once thick enough, ice deforms and flows slowly under gravity, carrying rock debris and reshaping the landscape. Mountain glaciers carve cirques, U-shaped valleys, arêtes, and moraines; ice sheets cover far larger areas and can alter sea level and continental drainage.

The largest ice masses are the Antarctic and Greenland ice sheets. Their health is measured by mass balance: the difference between gains from snowfall and losses from melting, runoff, sublimation, and iceberg calving. A glacier can advance even during a warming period if snowfall rises enough, and it can retreat even during a cold period if losses exceed gains. For this reason, glacier length alone is not a complete measure of climate, though widespread retreat is a strong signal of long-term change.

## Sea Ice and Icebergs

Sea ice forms when ocean water freezes. Salt is mostly rejected from the growing ice crystals, creating salty brine channels and denser water below. Seasonal sea ice expands in winter and shrinks in summer; some remains through several years and becomes thicker and less salty. Wind and ocean currents break, raft, pile, and move the ice into ridges and openings called leads. Sea ice is therefore a dynamic habitat and transport surface, not a fixed cap.

An iceberg is floating freshwater ice that has broken from a glacier or ice shelf. Ice shelves are extensions of land ice that float on the ocean while still attached to the coast. Their breakup does not directly raise sea level because they already float, but it can remove a buttress that slows land-based glaciers behind them. Faster flow of those land glaciers can then increase sea level. Icebergs also create risks for ships and offshore structures, especially where currents carry them into busy waters.

## Ground, River, and Lake Ice

Permafrost is ground that stays at or below freezing for at least two consecutive years. It may contain large amounts of ground ice, and only a surface layer thaws seasonally. When ice-rich permafrost warms, the ground can settle unevenly, damaging roads, buildings, pipelines, and runways. Thaw can also change drainage, release stored organic carbon, and alter northern ecosystems.

River and lake ice forms when surface water loses heat to cold air. Flowing water often produces frazil ice—small crystals that can collect below the surface or around obstructions—before a continuous cover develops. Lake ice can affect winter transport, recreation, fish habitat, and local climate, but its safety depends on thickness, temperature history, currents, snow cover, and water chemistry. A visible surface does not guarantee uniform strength.

## Climate and Society

Ice has a high albedo, meaning it reflects much incoming sunlight. When snow and ice melt, darker land or water absorbs more energy, which can amplify warming locally. Changes in glaciers, snowpack, and sea ice affect water supplies, hydropower, agriculture, sea level, coastal risk, shipping routes, fisheries, and Indigenous communities whose travel and livelihoods depend on seasonal ice.

Ice records past climate in layers and bubbles trapped within ice cores. Scientists use these archives to study historical temperature, atmospheric composition, volcanic eruptions, and dust. Understanding ice therefore requires both short-term weather knowledge and long-term Earth-system thinking: ice responds to climate, but it also helps regulate climate and reshape the conditions in which people and ecosystems live.

## Key Events & Developments

- Pleistocene Epoch: repeated glaciations reshaped continents, sea levels, rivers, and ecosystems.
- 20th century: ice cores and satellite observations made long-term ice and climate records more precise.
- 21st century: rapid glacier retreat and changing sea ice increased concern about sea-level rise and Arctic change.

## Key People

- Louis Agassiz: helped establish the scientific recognition of past ice ages.
- Glaciologists: study glaciers, ice sheets, snow, and the processes that change them.
- Polar communities: hold practical knowledge of sea ice, travel routes, wildlife, and seasonal environmental change.

## Interesting Facts

- Ice cores preserve bubbles of ancient air that reveal past atmospheric composition.
- Sea ice floating on the ocean does not directly raise sea level when it melts, unlike land ice.
- Albedo makes snow and ice important climate regulators because bright surfaces reflect sunlight.
