# Diagnosis and Therapeutics (Diagnosis)

## Introduction

Diagnosis is the process of deciding what disease or disorder is present and telling it apart from other possible conditions. It depends on careful reading of symptoms, signs, and medical clues, so it is both a practical test of judgment and a way to turn uncertain complaints into a clear medical explanation. The aim is to reach the most likely answer with careful clinical reasoning.

The diagnostic process looks at how symptoms begin, how they develop, and what has happened before them, including past illness, risk factors, and recent exposure to disease. Doctors also use physical signs, emotional or nonverbal signs of distress, and findings from laboratory work, radiology, and other imaging tests. From this information they create a differential diagnosis, place the most likely cause first, and choose further tests that can narrow the list or confirm one disease.

Historically, diagnosis was understood as the art of identifying disease from signs and symptoms, and it depended mainly on medical history, observation, and examination. In the 20th century, new tests and imaging methods changed this work and made diagnosis much more accurate. The older medical tradition also valued hygiene, bathing, fresh air, diet, and exercise, showing that observation and prevention have long been part of good medical practice.

## Inspection

Inspection is the first and often most revealing part of the physical examination, because careful looking can show clues that are easy to miss by touch or testing alone. The doctor begins with the patient’s general appearance, nutrition, symmetry, and posture, since wasting, poor grooming, or slack posture may point to chronic illness, depression, or low self-esteem. Good inspection means noticing the whole person before focusing on one body part.

The skin, hair, nails, and hidden areas such as the scalp, back, and buttocks also need close attention. Nail clubbing can suggest heart or lung disease, and pitting may be seen in psoriasis. Skin changes can point to infection or cancer, including melanoma, squamous-cell carcinoma, and basal-cell cancer. Careful viewing of moles, pigments, and recent changes in lesions supports early detection and can reveal serious disease before it spreads.

## Palpation

Palpation means feeling the body with the hands to learn about the organs and tissues beneath the skin. It may be light or deep, depending on the question being asked. Light palpation is useful for tenderness, spasm, and rigidity, while deeper palpation helps judge the size of organs such as the liver, spleen, and kidneys, or find an abnormal mass. When pain is present, the examiner begins away from it, because gentle palpation helps locate the point of greatest tenderness without adding unnecessary discomfort.

The abdomen often gives the clearest examples of what palpation can show. Appendicitis may cause guarding and localized tenderness, an abdominal aortic aneurysm may feel like a pulsating mass, and an inflamed gallbladder may be tender in the right upper abdomen and hurt more on inspiration. Palpation is also used for the breast, prostate, lymph nodes, and testes. Breast examination should be methodical, and suspicious lesions are often hard, fixed, and associated with skin retraction or asymmetry, while benign lumps are more likely to be movable and well defined. This makes palpation a key tool for finding hidden disease.

## Percussion

Percussion is done by tapping the body and listening to the sound that comes back, which helps judge the density of the tissue below. It can detect fluid in the abdomen, bowel gas in obstruction, or an enlarged liver, and it is especially useful in chest examination. A healthy lung sounds resonant, while fluid or solid tissue makes the sound dull; emphysema creates hyperresonance, and a stomach full of air gives a tympanic sound. These sound changes provide simple but valuable clues about what lies beneath the surface.

## Auscultation

Auscultation uses a stethoscope to listen to sounds from the heart, lungs, blood vessels, and bowels. Absent bowel sounds may mean a paralyzed bowel, while high-pitched tinkling sounds can suggest obstruction. Bruits are vascular sounds heard over partly blocked arteries, and they may point to stroke risk when heard in the carotids or to narrowing in major abdominal vessels. Listening carefully to airflow can also help detect asthma, bronchitis, or pneumonia, so sound becomes evidence in diagnosis.

The chest and heart give further diagnostic clues. Crackles often reflect fluid in small airways and are heard in heart failure and pneumonia; wheezes are linked to narrowed airways; and pleural rubs sound rough and leathery when inflamed pleural surfaces move against each other. Cardiac auscultation looks for murmurs caused by turbulent blood flow or valve deformity. Some murmurs are harmless, but others are pathological and need attention, especially when they suggest structural heart disease.

## Emergency

In an emergency, the first concern is whether the body systems that sustain life are working, especially breathing, circulation, and the central nervous system. A distressed person should be checked quickly to see whether air is moving well enough to oxygenate the blood. If breathing and circulation have stopped, CPR can provide temporary artificial respiration and blood flow until emergency staff arrive. In this setting, speed and priority matter more than a full examination.

Emergency diagnosis also asks whether circulation is strong enough to carry oxygen to the tissues. Low blood pressure can signal shock, and the cause may be loss of blood volume, poor heart function, or failure of the vascular system. Severe allergy or drug overdose can also collapse blood pressure by lowering vascular tone and causing blood to pool. Rapid weak pulse, pale skin, sweating, and confusion are warning signs, and the brain, heart, lungs, kidneys, and liver are especially at risk. An unconscious person may be in coma, which makes rapid life-saving action even more important.

## Pediatric

Examinations in children begin at birth, and one of the most important first checks is the Apgar score. This score is taken at one minute and five minutes after birth and rates heart rate, breathing, muscle tone, reflex irritability, and colour. It helps show whether the newborn is doing well or needs support such as stimulation, breathing help, or longer resuscitation. The Apgar system gives doctors a fast way to judge the newborn’s immediate condition.

Child assessment also uses growth charts and developmental tests. Height and weight are followed over time and compared with normal patterns, while the Denver Developmental Screening Test checks motor, language, and social skills in children up to age six. This gives a fuller picture of growth than weight alone, because healthy development depends on physical and psychosocial progress together. When a child grows or develops too slowly, the pattern can point to a medical or social problem.

## Geriatric

As people grow older, they lose organ reserve, which is the extra function the body can call on during stress or illness. This steady loss makes older adults more vulnerable to disease and disability and means that exam findings must be read with age in mind. The same symptom may have a different meaning in an older person because normal reserve is lower and recovery is often slower.

## Age-Related Changes

Aging affects many body systems at once. Cells decline in number and function, collagen rises, tissues become stiffer, and muscle mass decreases. The liver, brain, and kidneys also lose mass. Heart output falls, the response to stress weakens, and blood flow to vital organs decreases. Lung function drops because the number of alveoli falls, the expiratory muscles weaken, and elastic recoil is reduced. These changes together make the older body less able to adapt to stress.

Digestive, excretory, endocrine, neurological, and sensory changes also appear. Stomach acid falls, intestinal movement slows, constipation and dehydration become more common, drug metabolism slows, gallstones increase, and tooth loss can make chewing and digestion harder. Kidney mass and functioning nephrons decline, thyroid and adrenal activity may fall, insulin production may decrease, and insulin resistance can lead to type II diabetes. Nerve conduction slows, brain substance is lost, sleep becomes lighter, cerebral blood flow drops, and vision, hearing, taste, and smell all weaken. Vision is especially limited in dim light, glaucoma and cataracts become more common, height decreases, and osteoporosis raises the risk of fracture, showing how aging reshapes the whole diagnostic picture.

## Key Events & Developments

- classical medicine: Diagnosis begins with observation of symptoms, history, and physical signs.
- modern medicine: Laboratory testing and imaging add precision to disease identification.
- contemporary practice: Diagnosis becomes a step-by-step process that links symptoms to treatment decisions.
