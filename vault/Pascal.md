# Pascal

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> Blaise Pascal was a French mathematician, physicist, inventor, religious writer, and moralist whose early geometry and calculating machine preceded major work on probability, pressure, and theology. His conversion, Provinciales, and Pensees connect scientific brilliance with Jansenist controversy and a searching account of human condition.

## Life and Work

![[attachments/pascal_001.webp|420]]

*Portrait of Blaise Pascal*

Blaise Pascal, born June 19, 1623, in Clermont-Ferrand and died August 19, 1662, in Paris, was a French mathematician, physicist, religious philosopher, and master of prose. He helped found modern probability theory, formulated Pascal's law ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pascal%27s_law)) of pressure, built an early digital calculating machine, and defended a religious doctrine that emphasized knowing God through the heart rather than through reason.

Pascal's father, Etienne Pascal, educated his children after moving the family to Paris in 1631. Blaise showed early mathematical brilliance. In 1640, he wrote Essai pour les coniques on conic sections, drawing on Girard Desargues's projective geometry. Between 1642 and 1644, he constructed a calculating machine to help his father with tax computations at Rouen.

## Science and Conversion

In 1646, Pascal encountered Jansenism ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jansenism)) through disciples of the abbe de Saint-Cyran and began turning from worldly life toward God. Yet he remained scientifically active, testing ideas of [[Galileo]] and Evangelista Torricelli with barometers and experiments on atmospheric pressure. He invented the syringe and hydraulic press, and his law states that pressure applied to a confined liquid is transmitted undiminished in all directions.

During his so-called worldly period of 1651-1654, Pascal actually pursued intense scientific work. He wrote on liquid equilibrium, air weight and density, and the arithmetic triangle. His Traite du triangle arithmetique laid foundations for the calculus of probabilities.

## Les Provinciales

Les Provinciales ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lettres_provinciales)) were 18 letters written in defense of Antoine Arnauld, a Jansenist opponent of the Jesuits. Pascal attacked what he presented as Jesuit moral laxity and defended inner religion, grace, charity, and evangelical seriousness. The letters were immediately successful and helped mark the beginning of modern French prose through their precision, brevity, variety, and force.

## Pensees

Pascal planned an unfinished Christian apologetic, Apologie de la religion chretienne, whose fragments were later published as Pensees ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pens%C3%A9es)). He argued that humanity without grace is a contradiction of greatness and misery, and that Christianity uniquely explains this condition. His famous wager proposes that if God does not exist, belief loses little, but if God exists, belief gains eternal life.

## Assessment

Pascal's genius united mathematics, physics, literary polemic, and spiritual intensity. His scientific training gave rigour to his religious writings, while his religious concerns gave urgency to his prose. His influence extended to [[Jean-Jacques Rousseau]], Henri Bergson, and existentialist thinkers.
