# The Concept And Components Of Culture (Urban Culture)

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> Urban culture concerns the lifeways, social organization, interaction, work, government, and meanings produced in cities. The source treats dense settlement, markets, migration, communication, institutions, and built environments as forces that make city life culturally distinct from rural life.

## Introduction

![[attachments/urban_culture_001.webp|420]]

*Catalhoyuk archaeological exhibit*

Urban culture ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Urban_culture)) means the behavioural patterns and lifeways of cities and urban areas, past and present. It studies how people live, organize, interact, work, govern, and create meaning within urban settings.

Research on urban cultures focuses on the city as the defining institution. Cities generate distinctive cultural forms because dense settlement, markets, government, social groups, communication, migration, and built environments create ways of life different from villages or rural communities.

Modern urban scholarship has moved toward definitions of cities and urban cultures that avoid ethnocentrism. Instead of treating one civilization's city as the normal or true city, scholars try to compare urban forms across history and cultures.

## Defining Cities

Older theories often treated one historical type of city as the model for all cities. Henri Pirenne, studying medieval Europe, argued that urban culture developed through a commercial middle class and a communal organization of citizens.

For Pirenne, the bourgeoisie depended on trade for wealth and political autonomy from feudal rulers. The urban commune gave citizens municipal unity and helped free the city from local feudal or religious control.

Pirenne's view was influential but limited. It made the European medieval city and its burgher culture seem like the standard form of the city, even though many cities in other times and places had different foundations.

## Weber's Urban Definition

![[attachments/urban_culture_002.webp|420]]

*Portrait of Max Weber*

Max Weber also defined the city through features drawn heavily from Western experience. In The City, he contrasted Occidental and Oriental urbanism and listed key traits of an urban community.

Weber's five attributes were fortification, a market, a separate law code and court system, an association of urban citizens producing municipal identity, and enough political autonomy for citizens to choose governors.

This definition emphasized corporate urban citizenship and political self-government. Weber believed many Oriental cities did not fully develop these traits because family, tribe, sect, or other solidarities limited municipal autonomy.

Like Pirenne's approach, Weber's theory helped clarify important features of some cities but risked treating Western urban history as the general rule.

## Cross-Cultural Urban Study

Urban anthropology and urban history broaden the study of cities by comparing many urban forms: medieval commercial cities, imperial capitals, industrial cities, colonial cities, border cities, shantytowns, religious centres, administrative cities, and modern global cities.

This broader view asks how urban life is shaped by class, capital, state power, migration, kinship, ethnicity, gender, poverty, neighbourhoods, markets, social movements, informal networks, and built design.

Urban culture is not only elite culture or municipal law. It includes everyday life: housing, work, transport, street use, markets, festivals, neighbourhood ties, crime, family strategies, religious activity, and political participation.

## Modern and Third World Cities

Studies of advanced capitalist cities connect local neighbourhoods with larger forces such as capital, class, state policy, planning, land markets, industry, and mass communication.

Studies of neocolonial and Third World cities examine poverty, migration, shantytowns, marginality, informal work, urban politics, and the survival networks of migrants and poor residents.

These studies challenge the idea that poor urban communities are simply disorganized. Research on shantytowns and migrant neighbourhoods often shows complex social networks, political strategies, economic adaptation, and community organization.

## Urban Culture as Process

Urban culture ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Urban_culture)) is a process rather than a fixed list of traits. Cities change through migration, economic shifts, government policy, war, trade, infrastructure, religious change, and social movements.

Urban life also produces tension. Cities can create freedom, innovation, markets, political autonomy, and cultural diversity, but also inequality, crowding, poverty, segregation, and conflict.

Because cities connect local life with large systems, urban research often combines micro-level study of neighbourhoods and households with macro-level analysis of states, economies, and global change.

## Key Events & Developments

- 1921: Max Weber's The City offered a major theory of urban community.
- 1925: Henri Pirenne's Medieval Cities shaped the study of European urban culture.
- Late 20th century: Urban anthropology expanded beyond Western models to cities across cultures.
- Modern urban studies: Linked neighbourhood life with class, capital, state power, migration, and social movements.

## Key People

- Henri Pirenne ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henri_Pirenne)): Interpreted medieval European cities through trade, bourgeois autonomy, and urban communes.
- Max Weber ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Max_Weber)): Defined urban community through fortification, market, law, citizen association, and political autonomy.
- Ulf Hannerz ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ulf_Hannerz)): Associated with anthropological exploration of city life.
- Manuel Castells ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manuel_Castells)) and David Harvey ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Harvey)): Linked urban culture with capitalism, social movements, and political economy.
- Larissa Adler Lomnitz ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Larissa_Adler_Lomnitz)) and Janice E. Perlman: Associated with studies of marginality, poverty, and urban networks.

## Interesting Facts

- City definitions once often treated the European medieval city as the model for all cities.
- Urban culture ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Urban_culture)) includes everyday practices as much as architecture or government.
- Shantytowns may contain organized networks rather than simple social breakdown.
- Urban anthropology studies both industrial cities and migrant or informal settlements.
