# Baghdad

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> Baghdad is Iraq's capital on the Tigris River and a historic centre of Arab and Islamic civilization. Founded as the Abbasid capital in 762, it became a major city of scholarship, trade, administration, and cultural life before the Mongol conquest, later remaining central to Ottoman and modern Iraqi politics despite recurrent destruction and conflict.

## Introduction

Baghdad ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baghdad)), capital of [[Iraq]], stands on the Tigris River in central Iraq and occupies a central place in the history of the Islamic world. Founded in 762 as the capital of the Abbasid ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abbasid_Caliphate)) caliphate, it became for centuries one of the world's greatest cities and the leading cultural centre of Arab and Islamic civilization.

## Character and Symbolic Importance

Baghdad has an exceptional cultural aura. Many Muslims remember it as the seat of the last legitimate caliphate, while others know it as the setting of stories from The Thousand and One Nights and as a symbol of the height of Arab-Islamic civilization.

## Site and Physical Setting

The city lies on a flat alluvial plain about 112 feet above sea level, near the point where the Tigris comes closest to the Euphrates. The Diyala River joins the Tigris southeast of the city.

## Climate

Baghdad has a hot, dry summer and a cool, damp winter. Summer temperatures often rise into the low 100s F and can exceed that, though the heat is moderated somewhat by low humidity and large drops at night.

## Layout and Districts

The city stretches along both sides of the Tigris. The east-bank settlement is known as Rusafah, the west-bank settlement as Al-Karkh. A system of bridges links the two sides.

## Monuments and Urban Identity

The city has several major modern monuments, many of them linked to war, revolution, and state symbolism. These include Jawad Salim's Liberation Monument, the Unknown Soldier monument, the Martyr's Monument, and the Victory Arches.

## Population and Communities

Baghdad's population expanded dramatically after World War II. The overwhelming majority of inhabitants are Arab and Muslim, but the city has long contained important internal divisions and minorities.

## Economy

Baghdad has long concentrated much of Iraq's manufacturing, finance, commerce, and government employment. A large share of Iraq's large-scale manufacturing and many major state institutions are located in and around the city.

## Manufacturing, Services, and Transport

Modern manufacturing in Baghdad began in the early 20th century and included textiles, food processing, bricks, cigarettes, and later a wide range of consumer and industrial goods. Yet traditional activities such as retail trade, handmade goods, repairs, and personal services remained significant employers.

## Government, Services, Health, and Education

As both provincial and national capital, Baghdad contained the central organs of the Iraqi state. Municipal functions, especially police services, were repeatedly politicized.

## Cultural Life

Baghdad has long been one of the major cultural centres of the Arab world. It produced poets, writers, sculptors, and painters, and played an important role in modern Arabic literary developments such as the free-verse movement.

## Foundation and Abbasid Greatness

![[attachments/baghdad_001.webp|420]]

*Plan of the Round City of Baghdad in the time of al-Mansur*

Though the site had long been inhabited and lay near earlier centres such as Babylon, Seleucia, and Ctesiphon, the true founding of Baghdad dates to 762, when the Abbasid caliph al-Mansur selected it as his capital.

## Decline, Revival, and Modern War

The city's first great age ended with political shifts and eventually with the Mongol conquest by Hulegu in 1258, after which Baghdad's importance declined sharply. It later survived as a provincial Ottoman city.

## Conclusion

Baghdad ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baghdad)) is a city where geography, empire, religion, memory, and modern conflict all intersect. It was once one of the supreme capitals of the world and later became the political and administrative centre of modern Iraq.

## Key Events & Developments

- Baghdad was founded as an Abbasid capital and quickly became a major center of power.
- It flourished as a hub of scholarship, trade, and administration in the medieval Islamic world.
- The Mongol destruction of 1258 was a major turning point.
- Under later Ottoman and modern Iraqi rule, the city remained politically central.

## Key People

- Al-Mansur ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Mansur)): founder of Abbasid Baghdad.
- Harun al-Rashid ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harun_al-Rashid)): associated with the city's cultural peak.
- Mongol leaders under Hulagu: responsible for the 1258 conquest.
- Later Iraqi rulers and administrators: shaped the modern city.

## Interesting Facts

- Baghdad was planned as a round city in its early Abbasid form.
- It became famous for learning, translation, and commerce.
- Baghdad is tied to the wider history of Islamic civilization.
- The city has been repeatedly rebuilt after periods of destruction.
