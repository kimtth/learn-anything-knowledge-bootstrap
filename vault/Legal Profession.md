# Legal Profession

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> The legal profession consists of occupations requiring specialized legal knowledge and authority to advise, represent, or decide matters of law. Its organization differs among jurisdictions through divisions such as advocates, solicitors, notaries, judges, and public legal officials.

## Introduction

The legal profession ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Legal_profession)) is a vocation based on expertise in law and in applying law. The definition is simple, but it varies by country because some systems divide legal work into several professions, while other occupations also require legal knowledge without being treated as part of the profession.

## History

![[attachments/legal_profession_001.webp|420]]

*Inns of Court in London*

Distinct legal systems are ancient, but large and independent legal professions ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lawyer)) are relatively modern. Early judges were often chiefs, princes, kings, priests, wise men, or royal officials who judged as part of wider political or religious authority rather than as separate legal specialists.

A separate class of legal specialists first became important in Greco-Roman civilization. In early Greece and Rome, paid legal specialization was viewed with suspicion because citizens were expected to know customary law and act with advice from kin.

In [[Rome]], patrician legal experts became jurisconsults, respected advisers who did not act as trial advocates and were not supposed to receive fees. Later Roman practice produced advocates, procurators, law teachers at Rome, Constantinople, and Berytus, and paid document experts known as tabelliones.

## Medieval Europe

The late Roman pattern shaped [[Europe]] after about 1000 CE. The Christian church developed canon law, courts, and practitioners, and its jurisdiction over family law and inheritance helped preserve the idea of legal representation despite Germanic and feudal resistance.

After the 12th-century revival of Roman law at Bologna, continental Europe developed four major legal roles. Procurators handled formal and documentary litigation steps; advocates advised and argued; notaries drafted and authenticated documents; and university teachers explained Roman law mixed with Germanic custom.

England after the 1066 Norman Conquest developed differently. The Inns of Court trained common-law practitioners, serjeants became the highest advocates, attorneys and solicitors handled litigation work, and by the 17th and 18th centuries the division between barristers and solicitors became rigid.

England differed from the Continent because it lacked a major class of university commentators on national law, developed through precedent rather than legislation, appointed judges from senior practitioners, and placed most transactional work on solicitors because no strong notarial profession developed.

Legal professions outside Europe existed before colonial influence, including unofficial Chinese legal advisers who helped with documents despite official suspicion of litigation. Modern state-recognized professions outside Europe, however, usually spread through colonial models based on English common law or Romano-Germanic civil law.

Colonial inheritance was mixed. Lawyers such as Mohandas Gandhi and Lee Kuan Yew led independence movements, while others supported colonial rule. Many countries also fused older divisions so one practitioner could perform both advocate and procurator, or solicitor and barrister, functions.

The profession has always had an ambiguous social role. Leading lawyers, judges, advocates, notaries, and scholars often held high prestige, but lawyers also drew distrust because law is complex, litigation is adversarial, and advocacy can appear to make weak or false claims look strong.

The profession is often conservative because it works through inherited institutions, yet individual lawyers have sometimes joined revolutionary or justice movements. Robespierre, [[Lenin]], [[Gandhi]], Thurgood Marshall, and Nelson Mandela show the range from revolution to social-justice leadership.

In private practice, client-directed lawyers advise on transactional and litigious matters. Transactional work includes land transfers, wills, family settlements, commercial agreements, corporate formation, share rights, taxation, and estate planning, often through documents and sometimes notarization.

Litigation normally has three stages: preparing the case, conducting the trial, and executing judgment. In divided professions, barristers or advocates focus on argument and trial work, while solicitors or procurators often handle preparation, formal steps, documents, and enforcement.

Public-directed practice includes judges, prosecutors, government lawyers, legislative draftsmen, and law-reform bodies. Anglo-American judges usually come from senior practice and carry high prestige, while many civil-law judges enter after training and examinations as career officials.

Prosecution is especially sensitive because criminal prosecution can become an instrument of oppression. Commonwealth systems often use public prosecutors under the attorney general, the United States relies heavily on elected district attorneys, and civil-law systems often make prosecution a career service related to the judiciary.

Teaching and scholarship have been part of the profession since Roman times. Robert Joseph Pothier influenced the Napoleonic Code, William Blackstone became the first Vinerian professor of English law at Oxford, and full-time law teaching became more distinct during the 20th century.

Legal scholarship is influential in both civil-law and common-law systems, though civil-law judges traditionally give more weight to treatises and commentaries. Computerized legal-information services and the Internet made access easier while also increasing the problem of excessive data.

Judicial independence is central to rule of law. Democracies protect judges through constitutional structures, salary protections, and limits on dismissal, while lawyers and courts together may defend legal autonomy against political pressure.

The bar's independence is also threatened by markets. In the United States, growth of large firms, competition for business clients, multidisciplinary practices, and nontraditional legal-service providers have made it harder for lawyers to keep distance from clients and serve broader public duties.

Since about 1800, most countries have regulated the profession through statutes and bar associations. Integrated bars, voluntary associations, the Inns of Court, regional professional bodies, and national law associations control admission, education, discipline, ethics, and unauthorized practice.

## Notarization

Notarization is central to many Romano-Germanic systems, where notaries handle nonlitigious documents and authenticate legal acts. Typical transactional work includes land, inheritance, family property, corporate entities, commercial agreements, tax planning, and management of assets for beneficiaries.

## William Blackstone

William Blackstone represents the bridge between practice, judging, and university law teaching. His Vinerian professorship at Oxford showed how English common law gradually entered academic study after long reliance on the Inns of Court.

## Independence in Developing Countries

In developing countries, the independence of the bench and bar is often fragile because supporting institutions are newer. After the late-20th-century collapse of Soviet communism and the end of apartheid in South Africa, many programs tried to strengthen independent courts, lawyers, democracy, markets, and rule of law.

Recent changes also transformed the profession's demography. In the United States, women now make up about half of law students, and underrepresented minorities about one-fifth to one-quarter, although partnership levels in major firms changed more slowly.

The paralegal profession grew after efforts in the late 1960s to increase access to legal services. Paralegals conduct research, gather affidavits, draft documents, and assist lawyers, but they may not give legal advice, set fees, or represent clients in court.

Large law firms expanded from the late 20th century, absorbing smaller domestic and foreign firms to build national or international reach. This growth changed the internal structure of the legal profession and increased pressure for efficiency and specialization.

## Key Events & Developments

- 200 BCE-600 CE: [[Rome]] developed jurisconsults, advocates, procurators, legal schools, and document specialists.
- 1066: Norman rule helped bring Roman and clerical influence into English legal organization.
- 12th century: Renewed Roman-law study at Bologna strengthened continental legal professions.
- 17th-18th centuries: England's barrister-solicitor division became more rigid.
- 1878: The American Bar Association was established as a leading voluntary professional body.
- late 20th century: Demographic change, paralegals, large firms, and rule-of-law programs reshaped the profession.

## Interesting Facts

- Jurisconsults in early Rome gained prestige partly because they advised without acting as paid trial advocates.
- England never developed a strong notarial profession, so solicitors took on much transactional work.
- Legal advocacy was distrusted so strongly that trained representation in serious criminal cases was not generally recognized until the 18th century.
- Integrated bar systems make admission to practice automatically include membership in a professional association.
- Paralegals can perform many lawyer-like tasks but cannot practice law independently.
