# Calendar

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> A calendar is a social and astronomical system for ordering days, months, and years. Lunar, solar, and lunisolar traditions reconcile the unequal cycles of the Sun and Moon for agriculture, religion, government, and historical memory, while reforms such as the Julian and Gregorian calendars pursue greater civil regularity and accuracy.

## Introduction

A calendar is any system for dividing time over extended periods, such as days, months, and years, and arranging them in a definite order. The word comes from the Latin calendarium, meaning account book, from calendae, the first day of the Roman month, when market days and feasts were proclaimed. Calendars serve civil life, religious observance, history, and science, and arose from the practical need to keep agriculture, ritual, and administration aligned with the sky.

The central problem of calendar making is that the motions of the Sun, Moon, and Earth do not fit neatly together. The synodic month ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lunar_month)), the cycle of the Moon's phases, lasts 29.53059 days, while the tropical year runs 365.242199 days; twelve lunar months total only 354.37 days, almost 11 days short of the solar year. Civilizations solved this mismatch in different ways, producing solar, lunar, lunisolar, and sidereal systems and repeated reforms to keep the calendar in step with nature and society.

## Time Units and Calendar Types

The basic unit of calendar computation is the day. Days are now measured from midnight, but astronomers counted them from noon to noon from about the 2nd century CE until 1925. A month may follow the Moon's phases or its orbit, and a year may be defined by the Sun, the seasons, or civil convention; the mean solar day ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solar_time)) is the period actually used in calendar reckoning.

From these building blocks came several types of calendar: lunar, solar, lunisolar, and sidereal or schematic. No single calendar is purely natural; all are human arrangements that put natural time signs, such as the Moon's phases, to work for society's needs, rearranging reality to fit civil and religious life.

Intercalation is the central technical problem. Because lunar months do not fit exactly into a solar year, lunisolar calendars must add extra days or whole months to keep festivals and harvests in season. Solar calendars face the opposite difficulty: the tropical year is not a whole number of days, so leap days must be inserted at regular intervals.

Calendars also require an epoch, or starting point, for counting years. Some systems count from creation, a ruler’s accession, a religious event, or a dynastic cycle. This makes calendars historical and political as well as astronomical.

## Ancient Near Eastern Calendars

![[attachments/calendar_001.webp|420]]

*Close-up of the ancient Gezer calendar inscription*

The earliest major practical calendar was Egyptian, and Mesopotamia developed influential lunisolar systems that later shaped Persian, Greek, Jewish, and other calendars. Babylonian practice was especially important because it standardized month naming, regnal dating, and methods of intercalation across a large imperial world.

Iranian, Egyptian, and Near Eastern traditions all show the same tension between lunar months and solar seasons. The lunisolar formula was probably invented in Mesopotamia in the 3rd millennium BCE, and cuneiform tablets trace time reckoning back to the 27th century BCE. After conquering Babylonia in 539 BCE, Persian kings made the Babylonian cyclic calendar standard from the Indus to the Nile, and Egyptians created their own stable civil year of 365 days.

The Egyptian calendar set a civil year of 365 days as three seasons of four 30-day months plus five intercalary days. It was simple and stable but slowly drifted against the seasons because it lacked a leap day. Egyptians regulated their older lunar calendar by the seasonal rising of the star Sirius, which fell only 12 minutes short of the true solar year, and the annual Nile flood gave their timekeeping great practical importance.

Babylonian astronomy made Mesopotamian calendars especially influential. Months began at the first visibility of the New Moon, which court astronomers still reported to Assyrian kings in the 8th century BCE, and month names differed from city to city. Regular observation and increasingly systematic intercalation shaped later Jewish, Persian, Greek, and scientific chronology.

## Greek, Roman, and Jewish Calendars

Greek calendars were strongly civic and religious. Each polis had its own month names, festivals, and intercalations, so the Greek world shared no uniform civil calendar; the astronomer Meton calculated a 19-year lunisolar cycle in 432 BCE, but politicians ignored it to keep their power over the calendar. This diversity made historical dating hard but tied time closely to local cult and political identity.

The early Roman calendar began in March with ten months totalling 304 days; Numa Pompilius reputedly added January and February to reach 355 days. Because the Pontifices controlled intercalation in secret, the calendar fell into seasonal chaos, and by 50 BCE the spring equinox fell eight weeks late. Julius Caesar's reform replaced this unstable system with the Julian solar calendar, using an Egyptian-style 365-day year and a regular leap day, making civil time far more predictable.

Jewish practice developed from lunar months tied to agriculture and festivals; the earliest source, the Gezer calendar of the late 10th century BCE, lists agricultural tasks across twelve lunations. After the conquest of Jerusalem in 587 BCE the Jews adopted Babylonian month names and a fixed lunisolar structure, intercalating a 13th month in seven years of a 19-year cycle.

## Muslim, Hindu, and Chinese Calendars

The Muslim calendar is purely lunar and dates from the Hijrah, Muhammad's migration from Mecca to Medina in 622 CE; the caliph Umar I set its first day as 1 Muharram, July 16, 622. Its twelve months alternate 30 and 29 days for 354 days, and because it never intercalates months it regresses through all the seasons about every 32.5 years. Ramadan, the ninth month, is the month of fasting, begun only when the New Moon is seen with the naked eye.

The Hindu calendar is lunisolar and highly refined, dividing the year into lunar months marked by 27 or 28 constellations called naksatra and adapting them to solar reckoning with a leap month every 60 months. The Chinese calendar, attested in Shang oracle bones from the 14th century BCE, set the solar year at 365.25 days and used a 19-year cycle of 235 months with seven intercalations, perhaps a century before Meton calculated the same cycle.

The Chinese calendar combined lunar months with 24 solar terms, the ershisi jieqi, intercalary months, and a sexagenary 60-year cycle. Correct timekeeping was tied to imperial legitimacy because it showed harmony between heaven and government, so calendar reform could carry real political significance.

The Hindu calendar is especially complex because regional systems differ and because it combines lunar days (tithi), solar months, sidereal references, multiple eras, and a 12-year cycle of the planet Jupiter. It is a refined astronomical and religious system rather than a single uniform calendar, and India later adopted the seven-day week, naming the days after the planets.

## Calendars of the Americas

![[attachments/calendar_002.webp|420]]

*Page from the Maya Dresden Codex*

The Mayan, Aztec, and Inca systems were among the most elaborate in the pre-Columbian world. The Maya combined a ritual cycle of 260 named days, formed by meshing numerals 1-13 with 20 day names, and a year of 365 days; running together these formed a Calendar Round of 18,980 days, or 52 years. The Aztecs used a similar 260-day cycle, the tonalpohualli, for divination and ritual.

The Inca evidence is more uncertain, but the strongest reconstruction is a sidereal-lunar calendar at Cuzco of 328 nights based on the 27.3-day sidereal month, beginning on June 8/9 with the heliacal rising of the Pleiades and corrected by intercalating 37 days. North American Indian groups had no integrated calendar, counting moons and seasons instead, with most tribes reckoning 12 moons to a year and some New England and Cree tribes counting 13.

The Mayan calendar divided its 365-day year into 18 months of 20 days plus five unlucky days called Uayeb, and added long-count reckoning for historical dating. The 260-day Tzolkin, recorded in the surviving Dresden Codex, was used mainly in divination, while Aztec priests called Tonalpouhque consulted the days for lucky omens and named children after their birthdate.

## Western Calendar Reform

![[attachments/calendar_003.webp|420]]

*First page of the papal bull Inter gravissimas introducing the Gregorian calendar reform*

The Western calendar combined Roman and Egyptian ideas. In the mid-1st century BCE Julius Caesar invited the astronomer Sosigenes of Alexandria, who advised abandoning the lunar calendar entirely in favour of a fixed solar year of 365.25 days with a regular leap day. A dominical-letter code, derived from a Roman market-day system, was then used to fix the weekdays and to help calculate the date of Easter.

The Julian year was still about 11 minutes too long, and the error accumulated over centuries until the spring equinox slipped well away from late March. The Gregorian calendar later corrected this drift, became the standard of international civil life, and remains the dominant global system today.

## Modern Reforms and Proposals

Reform proposals continue, including the International Fixed Calendar of 13 months of 28 days plus an extra day, which inserts a new month named Sol between June and July, and the World Calendar with equal quarters. Earlier experiments include the French republican calendar of 1793, abandoned under Napoleon on January 1, 1806, and the Soviet move from the Julian to the Gregorian calendar in February 1918, which dropped 13 days so that February 1 became February 14.

Even with its imperfections, the Gregorian calendar remains dominant because it balances astronomical accuracy, civil convenience, and historical continuity better than any competing system, providing a year accurate to less than half a minute.

Perpetual-calendar schemes try to make dates fall on the same weekday every year, simplify quarters, or stabilize holidays, but they face resistance because calendars preserve tradition as well as convenience.

Calendar reform is difficult because a calendar organizes religion, business, memory, and public administration at once. A technically simpler system can fail if it disrupts festivals, the weekly cycle, historical continuity, or national custom.

## Conclusion

Calendars are not merely technical instruments; they are social agreements that organize religion, labour, memory, and government. The history of calendars is essentially the history of civilization's attempts to reconcile human needs with the motions of the Sun, Moon, and stars.

The broader lesson is that time reckoning always combines observation and convention. The Sun, Moon, and stars provide the cycles, but societies choose how to name, count, correct, and remember them, which is why calendars can be scientifically precise and culturally particular at the same time.

## Key Events & Developments

- 3rd millennium BCE: Mesopotamian lunisolar methods emerge.
- 26th-27th century BCE: Early Egyptian and Sumerian time-reckoning systems appear.
- 1st millennium BCE: Jewish, Greek, and Persian calendrical systems develop.
- 45 BCE: Julius Caesar introduces the Julian calendar.
- 16th century: The Gregorian reform corrects the Julian drift.
- 1918: Soviet Russia adopts the Gregorian calendar.
