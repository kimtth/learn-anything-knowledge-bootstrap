# Yiddish Literature

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> Yiddish literature is the writing of Ashkenazic Jewish communities in the Yiddish language, developed alongside a religious culture in which Hebrew held greater prestige. The source traces Old Yiddish, Haskala, Hasidic storytelling, and modern fiction, poetry, drama, journalism, and criticism across Europe, North America, and Israel.

## Introduction

Yiddish literature ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yiddish_literature)) is the body of writing created in the Yiddish language of Ashkenazic Jews, especially the Jews of central and eastern Europe and their descendants. It grew beside a religious culture that gave higher status to Hebrew prayer, commentary, and scripture. Because Yiddish ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yiddish)) was the everyday language, it often served ordinary readers rather than only highly educated men. Women, who were less often taught Hebrew, formed an important audience for Yiddish books.

The history of Yiddish literature ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yiddish_literature)) is usually divided into three broad periods. Old Yiddish literature ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yiddish_literature)), from about 1300 to 1780, began in areas now called [[Germany]] and [[Italy]] and then moved east with Jewish migration. The Haskala ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haskalah)), or Jewish Enlightenment, from about 1755 to 1880, spread from [[Berlin]] and often treated Yiddish as a temporary tool for educating the masses. Hasidism ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hasidic_Judaism)), beginning about 1740 in the Polish-Lithuanian world of present-day [[Ukraine]], used Yiddish storytelling to reach common people and to carry religious feeling.

Modern Yiddish literature, beginning in 1864, accepted Yiddish as a full literary language. It developed fiction, poetry, drama, journalism, and criticism in Europe, North America, and Palestine or Israel. The period from 1864 to 1939 was the great flowering of Yiddish writing, but the [[Holocaust]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Holocaust)), called the Yiddish khurbn, destroyed many of its European centres. Soviet oppression and North American assimilation also weakened the language, though from the 1980s onward there was renewed interest through translation, university study, secular cultural groups, and klezmer revival. Hasidic communities remained the main places where Yiddish continued as an everyday language.

Early Yiddish ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yiddish)) grew from medieval Germanic speech written in Hebrew characters, enriched by Hebrew words and later by Slavic loanwords. By the 12th century, Jewish communities along the Rhine, including Worms, Speyer, and Mainz, were culturally developed, though scholars differ on whether Yiddish began in the Rhineland or farther east near the Danube. As Jewish life moved eastward, printing centres such as [[Prague]] and Krakow helped spread Yiddish books.

Old Yiddish writing served practical, devotional, and entertaining needs. It included biblical retellings, romances, ethical works, customs books, prayers, and stories for women and less learned readers. The language linked learned Jewish tradition with daily speech. This double role remained central later: Yiddish could sound intimate and oral, but it could also carry allusions to Hebrew scripture and rabbinic culture.

The Haskala ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haskalah)) changed Yiddish writing in a conflicted way. Enlightenment writers wanted to modernize Jewish society, teach useful knowledge, reduce superstition, and encourage social reform. Yet many of them disliked Yiddish and preferred German or Hebrew. Their use of Yiddish showed what has been called a suicidal principle: they used the language to educate common readers, hoping that after this work was done it would be abandoned.

Hasidic literature gave Yiddish a very different status. Legends about Israel Baal Shem Tov, the founder of Hasidism, were told orally in Yiddish and later collected. Shivhei ha-Besht, published in 1815, presented him as a hidden spiritual leader and wonder-worker, with episodes involving miracles and demons. These stories used vivid language and helped make popular storytelling a serious part of Jewish religious literature.

Rabbi Nahman ben Simhah of Bratslav, the great-grandson of the Baal Shem Tov, told allegorical tales to his disciples in 1806-1810. His scribe Nathan Sternharz published the Sippurey mayses in a bilingual Hebrew-Yiddish edition in 1815. The Hebrew gave religious authority, while the Yiddish preserved the living language of narration. Stories such as The Loss of the Princess, The Simple Man, and The Master of Prayer work as folktales, spiritual guidance, and hidden mystical teaching. Later writers, including I.L. Peretz and Martin Buber, drew on Hasidic tales, and Peretz argued in 1908 that modern Yiddish literature had roots in Nahman's stories.

The eastern Haskala ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haskalah)) often attacked Hasidism. Yisroel Aksenfeld, once close to the Bratslav circle, later viewed Hasidism as an obstacle to progress. His novel Dos shterntikhl, or The Head-Covering, published in 1861, satirized the Jewish world and helped establish the Yiddish novel. Shloyme Ettinger wrote the comedy Serkele, which criticized social corruption and distorted pretensions. Isaac Meir Dik became one of the first mass authors of secular Yiddish fiction, writing hundreds of didactic stories from the 1850s to the 1880s about education, reform, women, migration, and superstition.

Modern Yiddish fiction took shape through three classic figures: S.Y. Abramovitsh, known by the persona Mendele Moykher Sforim; Sholem Aleichem, the pen name of Sholem Rabinovitsh; and I.L. Peretz. Abramovitsh gave Yiddish fiction a new satirical and artistic power. His works used an oral narrator, social criticism, and mock-heroic comedy to describe poverty, ignorance, disabled bodies, failed dreams, and Jewish communal life. He later translated and revised many works in Hebrew, helping both Yiddish and Hebrew modern literature.

Sholem Aleichem became the most popular Yiddish writer and called Abramovitsh the grandfather of Yiddish literature. In Odessa, his circle included Abramovitsh, Shimon Dubnov, and H.N. Bialik. His early novels of the 1880s, including Stempeniu and Yosele Solovey, adapted European fiction to Jewish subjects such as klezmer music and cantorial life. His greatest strength was the speaking voice. Characters such as Tevye create the illusion of oral speech, mixing ordinary Yiddish with Hebrew phrases, jokes, self-defense, and sorrow. After the pogroms of 1905, Sholem Aleichem left Russia permanently.

I.L. Peretz brought modernist compression, symbolist drama, and artistic reworking of Jewish folklore into Yiddish literature ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yiddish_literature)). His circle helped give Yiddish writing a high literary ambition. He used Hasidic materials not simply as pious legend but as symbolic art, psychological exploration, and national culture. Together, Abramovitsh, Sholem Aleichem, and Peretz became the classic foundation for later Yiddish fiction.

Other major modern writers expanded the field. Sholem Asch became one of the best-known 20th-century Yiddish authors and reached wide audiences in translation. His Christian-themed works, including Der man fun natseres, or The Man from Nazareth, first published in English translation as The Nazarene in 1939, alienated some Jewish readers, and the Forverts refused to print it after decades of publishing his work. His career anticipated the international success later achieved by Isaac Bashevis Singer.

## Writers in New York

![[attachments/yiddish_literature_001.webp|420]]

*Menke Katz*

Several immigration waves after 1881 brought Yiddish writers and readers to the [[United States of America (United States)|United States]]. The earliest important group in New York was known as the Sweatshop Poets. Their poems responded to the harsh lives of immigrant workers, especially in the textile industry, and expressed socialist, anarchist, and revolutionary ideas.

Morris Winchevsky, born Ben-Zion Novakhovitsh, worked in socialist Hebrew and Yiddish journalism, was arrested and expelled from Prussia, founded a socialist Yiddish journal in [[London]] in 1885-1886, and moved to the United States in 1894. He worked with the daily Forverts after its founding in 1897 and became the first major proletarian Yiddish poet. Morris Rosenfeld described sweatshop labour and family estrangement, especially in Mayn yingele, or My Little Boy, from 1887. David Edelstadt, who knew factory hardship directly, joined the anarchist movement, edited Fraye arbeter shtime, and died young of tuberculosis. Yehoash, the pen name of Solomon Bloomgarden, wrote poetry and made a major poetic Yiddish translation of the Hebrew Bible.

American Yiddish poetry then developed modernist groups. Di Yunge, or The Young, began around the journal Di yugend in 1907-1908. It rejected direct political goals and cultivated pure poetry, lyric beauty, and refined form. Di Inzikhistn, or The Introspectivists, began with the journal In zikh in 1920. They wanted poetry to explore inner life, modern perception, free rhythm, and the self's changing experience.

A. Leyeles, N.B. Minkoff, and Jacob Glatstein shaped the Introspectivist movement. Leyeles used urban montage, abrupt rhythm, exotic references such as the dao and the Ganges, and an alter ego named Fabius Lind. Glatstein, born in Lublin and in New York from 1914, used exotic and sound-rich poetry before returning strongly to Jewish themes. Yidish-taytshn in 1937 began this turn; A gute nakht, velt in 1938 angrily rejected Western civilization after the Nazi annexation of Austria; Gedenklider in 1943 and Shtralndike yidn in 1946 responded to the Holocaust with mourning, resistance, and renewed attachment to Jewish culture.

Yiddish writing also continued through journals and literary centres outside New York. In the Soviet Union, journals such as Der shtern, Di royte velt, Prolit, Sovetish, and Sovetishe literatur printed Yiddish work before [[World Wars (World War II)|World War II]], while Sovetish heymland survived from 1961 to 1991. In [[Poland]], Yung-yidish in Lodz and Khaliastre, Albatros, and Literarishe bleter in [[Warsaw]] supported innovative writers, including Peretz Markish and I.J. Singer. In Tel Aviv, Di goldene keyt became important, and later Naye vegn, Chulyot, and Toplpunkt showed continuing interest in Yiddish literature.

Yiddish theatre began in older European Jewish performance traditions. Late medieval dancers, mimics, jesters, wedding entertainers, and Purim players used Bible stories, songs, improvisation, and current references. By the 16th century, Purim plays were being performed in Yiddish. Enlightenment writers in Berlin and Russian Jewish intellectuals later wrote plays, but professional Yiddish theatre is usually dated to 1876.

Abraham Goldfaden, often called the father of Yiddish theatre, joined with two traveling musicians in a Romanian tavern in 1876 and then founded a professional troupe in Iasi. His touring theatre relied on music, slapstick, spectacle, and popular plots. Important plays included Di tsvey Kuni-Leml, Di kishefmakherin, and Bar Kokhba. After Tsar Alexander II was assassinated in 1881, conditions worsened for Jews, and Yiddish theatre was banned in [[Russia]] in 1883. Goldfaden later tried New York, and his final play Ben Ami succeeded there in 1907 under Boris Thomashefsky.

Jacob Gordin brought western European and Russian literary models to the Yiddish stage after emigrating to the [[United States of America (United States)|United States]] in 1891. He wrote more than 70 plays and helped move theatre toward serious drama. Got, mentsh, un tayvl, first performed in 1900, drew on Goethe's Faust. Der yudisher kenig Lear and Mirele Efros drew on Shakespearean patterns. Gordin's work competed with shundteater, a popular low theatre of exaggeration, songs, comic routines, and shtick.

Peretz Hirshbein wrote avant-garde and naturalistic drama. Eynzame veltn, published in Hebrew in 1905 and Yiddish in 1906, showed experimental interests; Tkies kaf in 1908 anticipated Ansky's Der dibek ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Dybbuk)); and Grine felder in 1916 dramatized a yeshiva student's turn from Talmudic study toward rural life. In 1918, Maurice Schwartz founded the Yiddish Art Theatre, became a leading actor-director, and trained performers including Muni Weisenfreund, later known as Paul Muni.

S. Ansky wrote the most famous Yiddish play, Der dibek ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Dybbuk)), or The Dybbuk, written in 1914 and first performed in 1920. Originally written in Russian and also known as Tsvishn tsvey veltn, it joined Hasidic folklore, spirit possession, psychological depth, and ethnographic knowledge from Ansky's expeditions. Konstantin Stanislavsky's Moscow Art Theatre considered it, but Ansky died before seeing it staged. After his death, it became central to the Vilna Troupe and to Habima in Hebrew. H. Leivick, born Leyvik Halpern, continued the mystical and folkloric line in works such as Der goylem, written in 1921 and performed in Yiddish in 1927.

The Yiddish stage spread widely with migration. The New York Folksbiene, or People's Stage, formed in 1915 and continued for generations. Yiddish plays were performed in [[Montreal]], Tel Aviv, [[Warsaw]], Bucharest, [[Argentina]], South Africa, and [[New Zealand]]. In the 1930s, Yiddish film adapted stage and fiction classics, including Der dibek ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Dybbuk)), Tkies kaf, and Grine felder in 1937, as well as Onkl Moses in 1930, Tevye in 1939, and Fishke der krumer in 1939.

The achievements of Yiddish literature ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yiddish_literature)) are closely tied to orality and intertextuality. Because Yiddish was the spoken language of Ashkenazic Jews, it was especially good at representing everyday speech. The oral-style narrator, related to Russian skaz, became a core feature in Abramovitsh and Sholem Aleichem. At the same time, educated readers knew Hebrew, so Yiddish writing often drew power from allusions to scripture, prayer, and rabbinic language. Itsik Manger's poetry shows how folk tone and scriptural reference could work together.

Later Yiddish writing remained multilingual and transnational. Some authors became famous in other languages as well. H.N. Bialik, a major Hebrew poet, also wrote in Yiddish. S.Y. Agnon, who received the Nobel Prize for Literature in 1966, published Yiddish poetry and prose before turning to Hebrew. Elie Wiesel's Holocaust work La Nuit, or Night, had an earlier Yiddish version, Un di velt hot geshvign, published in 1956.

Yiddish culture also entered English-language Jewish writing indirectly. Israel Zangwill wrote about Jewish life in London's East End in Children of the Ghetto in 1892 and later coined the phrase the melting pot in a play. Abraham Cahan's Yekl in 1896 used Yiddish words and represented Americanized Yiddish speech through English. Mary Antin, whose family moved from Russia to Boston in 1894, used Yiddish words in stories and in The Promised Land in 1912. Anzia Yezierska gave English fiction a Yiddish-inflected immigrant voice in works such as Hungry Hearts in 1920.

By the late 20th century, Yiddish literary activity continued in smaller circles. Kobi Weitzner wrote for the Yiddish Forverts and edited Yidisher kemfer. The New York journal Yugntruf published writers such as Hershl Glasser, Shmoyl Nydorf, Avrom Rosenblatt, Gitl Schaechter, Yermiahu Aaron Taub, and Sheva Zucker, and sponsored a writers' circle from the 1970s. Cultural clubs supported poets such as Sarah Moskovitz and Troim Katz Handler, sometimes in bilingual editions or with Roman-character transcriptions.

- 1861: Yisroel Aksenfeld's Dos shterntikhl helped establish the Yiddish novel.
- 1864: Modern Yiddish literature began its period of full literary self-confidence.
- 1876: Abraham Goldfaden founded professional Yiddish theatre in Romania.
- 1881: Pogroms and political turmoil pushed mass Jewish emigration and carried Yiddish writing to North America.
- 1883: [[Russia]] banned Yiddish theatre after conditions worsened for Jews.
- 1905: Pogroms shook writers such as S.Y. Abramovitsh and Sholem Aleichem and contributed to emigration.
- 1915: The New York Folksbiene formed and became a long-running Yiddish stage institution.

- 1939-1945: The [[Holocaust]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Holocaust)) destroyed the major European centres of Yiddish literature and culture.
- 1961-1991: Sovetish heymland preserved a Soviet Yiddish literary forum after most others had disappeared.
- 1980s: Renewed interest in Yiddish appeared through scholarship, translation, music, and cultural revival.
