# Bacteria and Other Monerans

> [!abstract] Reference navigation

## Introduction

Bacteria ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bacteria)) are microscopic single-celled organisms that live in enormous numbers in almost every environment on Earth. They are members of the prokaryotes, organisms whose cells lack a membrane-bound nucleus and other internal structures typical of eukaryotes.

They are among the oldest and most adaptable forms of life, perhaps present for three-quarters of Earth's history. Although some bacteria cause disease, most are harmless or beneficial, and many are essential to ecology, agriculture, and industry.

Some bacteria also live as symbionts, fixing nitrogen for plants or helping animals and invertebrates digest materials such as cellulose.

## Bacteria and Prokaryotes

Prokaryotic cells differ from eukaryotic ones. Traditionally, all prokaryotes were grouped together in the kingdom Monera, but this older classification understated the extraordinary genetic and metabolic diversity of these organisms.

Bacteria are therefore treated not simply as tiny primitive cells but as a major and varied form of life with their own deep evolutionary importance.

## Discovery and Observation

![[attachments/bacteria_002.webp|420]]

*Scanning electron micrograph of Escherichia coli bacteria*

Bacteria were first seen in the 17th century by Antonie van Leeuwenhoek using primitive microscopes. Their minute size meant that their existence remained unknown until optical instruments became powerful enough to reveal them.

Modern study uses both light microscopes and electron microscopes. The Gram stain, developed by Hans Christian Gram, is important as a way of distinguishing major types of bacterial cell envelopes.

The stain works because gram-positive and gram-negative bacteria differ in surface structure, especially the thickness and organization of peptidoglycan layers.

## Cell Envelope and Gram Types

The bacterial cell surface is central to bacterial life. All bacteria possess a cytoplasmic membrane that encloses the cell contents and carries out crucial functions such as energy generation, transport, and protein secretion.

Outside this lies the cell wall, made mainly of peptidoglycan. In gram-positive bacteria the peptidoglycan layer is thick and retains the blue Gram stain; in gram-negative bacteria it is much thinner and loses that stain, later taking up a pink one. These structural differences are both microscopically visible and biologically important.

## Growth, Reproduction, and Budding

Bacteria most commonly reproduce by division, but special methods also occur, such as budding, where a smaller outgrowth enlarges and then separates from the mother cell. In some species the mother and offspring differ structurally. Bacterial population growth has a characteristic generation time and proceeds through phases: lag, log, stationary, and death. Under ideal conditions some bacteria can reproduce with extraordinary speed, while others living in extreme environments may divide only after very long intervals.

In a rich medium, the log phase can produce balanced exponential growth, while nutrient depletion or toxic products lead first to stationary growth and then to population decline.

## Sporulation and Dormancy

Many environmental bacteria survive difficult conditions by producing dormant forms such as endospores, cysts, or heterocysts. These structures are not an obligatory stage of life but a survival response to adverse conditions, often nutritional stress.

Endospores in genera such as Bacillus and Clostridium are especially resistant to heat, dehydration, chemicals, and radiation. This durability results from a complex specialized structure and extremely low internal water content.

Only one endospore forms inside a bacterial cell, and it returns to active growth only when suitable nutrients become available.

## Distribution in Nature

Bacteria are ubiquitous. They occur in oceans, soils, deep underground, in extreme thermal and chemical environments, and inside the bodies of plants, animals, and humans. Their ecological role is correspondingly enormous.

Without bacteria, soil fertility would collapse, decomposition would slow drastically, and many key cycles of matter would be interrupted. Bacterial life sustains higher life-forms in countless indirect ways.

## Oxygen Relationships

Bacteria differ sharply in their relation to oxygen. Obligate aerobes require it, while obligate anaerobes cannot tolerate it. Facultative anaerobes can switch between respiration and fermentation depending on oxygen availability.

Some bacteria are microaerophilic or aerotolerant, preferring low oxygen levels. These differences reflect both metabolism and the ability, or inability, to detoxify reactive oxygen by-products.

Aerobic cells commonly use enzymes such as catalase and superoxide dismutase to break down hydrogen peroxide and superoxide, which helps explain why oxygen tolerance differs among species.

## Temperature Adaptation

Bacteria also occupy a vast thermal range. Psychrophiles grow at low temperatures, mesophiles at moderate temperatures, and thermophiles at high ones. Some archaea and bacteria thrive in hot springs, compost heaps, or deep-sea hydrothermal environments. These examples show that bacterial life is not marginal but extraordinarily adaptable. Extreme conditions that would kill many other organisms can serve as normal habitats for some prokaryotes.

## Metabolism and Photosynthesis

The metabolic diversity of bacteria is one of its strongest themes. Many are lithotrophs, gaining energy from inorganic substances, while others oxidize sulfur, ferrous iron, carbon monoxide, hydrogen, or many organic compounds. Many are also phototrophic, using light energy in forms of photosynthesis. Some bacterial groups carry out oxygen-free photosynthesis, while cyanobacteria perform oxygen-evolving photosynthesis and were crucial in Earth's long environmental history.

## Biosynthesis and Nutritional Requirements

Many bacteria can synthesize a broad range of needed compounds from simple sources, carefully regulating these pathways so they make only what is required. Others, such as Mycoplasma, have lost many biosynthetic abilities and therefore require richer nutrition.

This variation reflects evolution under different ecological circumstances. Bacterial simplicity in cell structure does not imply simplicity in metabolism or adaptation.

## Classification and Evolutionary Relationships

Modern bacterial classification increasingly relies on genetic divergence, especially comparison of the 16S rRNA gene, which provides a measure of evolutionary relatedness. This approach has revolutionized bacterial systematics. An older structural classification grouped them into Gracilicutes, Firmicutes, Tenericutes, and Mendosicutes, including the archaebacteria. Even where terminology has shifted, the underlying point remains that bacteria and related prokaryotes are vastly diverse and not easily contained within a single simplistic system.

The 16S rRNA gene is useful because it occurs in all bacteria, changes slowly, and contains regions whose variation can reveal deep evolutionary branching.

## Conclusion

Bacteria and other monerans are dominant, ancient, and metabolically versatile forms of life. They inhabit nearly every environment, sustain major ecological processes, and display extraordinary biochemical ingenuity.

Far from being merely agents of disease, bacteria appear here as the hidden foundation of much life on Earth. Their study also remains central to understanding evolution, cell biology, ecology, and biotechnology.

The logistic-growth equation models population growth that slows as population size N approaches carrying capacity K.

## Key Events & Developments

- Microscopic life was first observed and later classified into bacterial forms.
- Microbiology arose alongside the discovery of disease-causing microbes.
- Moneran classification grouped very simple organisms before modern taxonomy changed the system.
- Later research distinguished major bacterial lines and the prokaryote/archaeal split.
