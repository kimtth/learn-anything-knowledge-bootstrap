## Introduction

Cities are relatively permanent, highly organized centres of population, larger or more important than towns or villages. The name is given to certain urban communities by some legal or conventional distinction that varies between regions, but the deeper idea is that of the urban community ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Residential_community)) and its culture, known as urbanism. City government is almost everywhere the creation of a higher political authority, usually the state or nation, which devolves limited self-government to local bodies. Size, area, and density alone do not define a city, since features such as the division of labour ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Division_of_labour)), nonagricultural activity, and central-place functions appear in all settlements from the country town to the giant metropolis.

## History of cities

![[attachments/cities_001.webp|420]]

*Ruins of the ancient city of Ur near Nasiriyah, Iraq*

In the Neolithic Period ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neolithic)) (roughly 9000 to 3000 BC) settlements grew as irrigation and cultivation improved, and transport advanced from human carrying to draft animals and then to the wheel, invented in the Tigris-Euphrates valley about 3500 BC, which in turn required roads later perfected by the Romans. The first recognizable cities had emerged by about 3500 BC, marked by literacy, metalworking, and religious-legal codes symbolized in temples and walls. They first appeared in the Nile valley and at Sumerian Ur, then at Mohenjo-daro in the Indus valley during the 3rd millennium, and in the Wei River valley of [[China]] by 2000 BC; their base in agriculture and trade gave them unprecedented occupational specialization and social stratification, and they lent coherence to the surrounding countryside.

## Classical and medieval cities

The city idea reached its peak in the Greek polis, which grew from a religious association of clans into a small, self-governing community of citizens. For its citizens the city and its laws formed a moral order symbolized by the acropolis, fine public buildings, and citizen assemblies—in [[Aristotle and Aristotelianism|Aristotle]]'s phrase, "a common life for a noble end." As the strict requirements for citizenship were relaxed and new commercial wealth overtook the old landed families, the social basis of the city widened, and later medieval and early modern cities developed through trade, craft production, and new political arrangements within feudal and monarchical power.

## Modern cities

![[attachments/cities_002.webp|420]]

*World map showing the percentage of each country's population living in urban areas*

Modern cities became larger, more specialized, and more tightly tied to national systems of transport, government, and economy. Urbanization is a continuing process rather than a fixed form, with cities constantly changing in scale, function, and social structure.

## Conclusion
The city is one of humanity’s central social inventions: a dense, organized, culturally important settlement that transforms both economic life and political organization.

## Key Events & Developments

- Neolithic era: early settlements grow into towns and cities.
- About 3500 BC: the first recognizable cities emerge.
- Classical antiquity: the Greek polis defines civic city life.
- Medieval and modern periods: trade and state power reshape cities.

## Key People

## Interesting Facts

- The city is a cultural as well as a spatial form.
- Early cities combined literacy, craft specialization, and political-religious power.
- Urban government is usually delegated from higher authority.
