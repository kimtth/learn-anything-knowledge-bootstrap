# Endocrine Systems

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> Animal endocrine systems use hormones and neurosecretory signals to regulate development, reproduction, metabolism, and adaptation. Comparative studies of invertebrates and vertebrates reveal how endocrine control evolved from simpler cellular signaling to specialized glands.

## Introduction

An endocrine system ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Endocrine_system)) is any animal system that produces hormones, the chemical signals that regulate body functions, development, reproduction, metabolism, and adaptation. Endocrine organization ranges from simple neurosecretory cells in the nervous system to the complex glandular networks found in vertebrates.

Comparative endocrinology ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Endocrinology)) studies endocrine evolution and hormonal adaptation. Nonmammalian research clarified mammalian endocrinology: prolactin's role in water and salt balance was first found in fishes, ovarian paracrine secretions were studied in starfishes, and thyroid hormone effects on embryonic development were investigated in tadpoles in the early 1900s.

Nonmammalian animals also served as bioassay systems for identifying mammalian hormones and as models for hormone receptors. Chicken oviduct receptors for progesterone and estrogens helped explain how gonadal hormones act on target tissues.

## Prostaglandins

Many tissues of nonmammalian animals produce prostaglandins, local hormone-like substances that influence reproduction in ways similar to their roles in humans and other mammals. These substances show that endocrine regulation is not limited to large glands; tissues can also release local chemical signals that affect nearby reproductive processes.

The liver of several nonmammalian species produces somatomedin-like growth factors after stimulation by growth hormone, and prolactin may stimulate a related factor in targets such as the pigeon crop sac.

## Unique Endocrine Glands in Fishes

![[attachments/endocrine_systems_001.webp|420]]

*Endocrine system glands*

[[Fishes]] possess endocrine organs with no direct counterpart in amphibians, reptiles, birds, or mammals. These include the corpuscles of Stannius, ultimobranchial glands, and the urophysis, a neurosecretory neurohemal organ associated with the spinal cord at the base of the tail.

The urophysis produces urotensin I and urotensin II, though its full function remains uncertain. These peptides are related to families that include somatostatin and mammalian corticotropin-releasing hormone.

## Invertebrate Endocrine Systems

![[attachments/endocrine_systems_002.webp|420]]

*Negative feedback loop - Endocrine system*

Invertebrate endocrinology developed more slowly than vertebrate endocrinology because methods designed for large vertebrates are hard to adapt to small animals, and many invertebrates are difficult to keep under laboratory conditions. Even so, research has shown that every animal phylum with a nervous system also has neurosecretory neurons.

The distribution of neurosecretory neurons and epithelial endocrine cells suggests that neurohormones were probably the earliest hormonal regulators in animals. Invertebrate systems therefore help explain how nervous and endocrine control became linked before the evolution of complex vertebrate glands.

## Phylum Nemertea

Nemertine worms are primitive marine animals that lack a coelom but differ from other acoelomates because they have a complete digestive tract. Their simple brain contains three known neurosecretory centres.

One neurosecretory centre controls gonad maturation, while all three centres appear to take part in osmotic regulation.

## Phylum Annelida

The cerebral ganglion, or brain, of Nereis, a marine polychaete worm, produces a small peptide hormone called nereidine. This hormone apparently prevents premature sexual development.

Below the brain is an infracerebral complex that functions as a neurohemal organ. Neurohormones are released from this complex into the coelomic fluid and travel to target tissues. In the lugworm Arenicola, evidence points to a brain neuropeptide that stimulates oocyte maturation.

## Phylum Mollusca

Within Phylum Mollusca, the class Gastropoda, including snails and slugs, has been studied most closely for endocrine function. The cerebral ganglion of species such as Euhadra peliomorpha, Aplysia californica, and Lymnaea stagnalis secretes a gonadotropic peptide hormone that stimulates the hermaphroditic gonad.

These gonadotropic hormones are stored in neurohemal organs until release is triggered. In some mollusks, phototropic information from an optic gland near the eye can direct hormone release, and egg-laying hormones in Aplysia and Lymnaea are similar small peptides.

The hermaphroditic gonad of Euhadra secretes testosterone identical to vertebrate testosterone, which stimulates formation of a gland that releases a mating pheromone. In cephalopods such as octopus, the optic gland influences seasonal reproductive development, though the exact role of neurohormones remains unclear.

## Phylum Arthropoda

Phylum Arthropoda is the largest and most ecologically successful invertebrate group, including insects, crustaceans, and arachnids. Only a limited number of species have been studied in detail, especially large insects such as cockroaches, grasshoppers, cecropia moths, and crustaceans.

Arthropod endocrine systems parallel vertebrate endocrine organization: brain neurohormones are stored in neurohemal organs and released to stimulate endocrine glands or nonendocrine targets.

## Class Insecta

The insect endocrine system contains neurosecretory, neurohemal, and endocrine structures. The largest brain neurosecretory centre is the pars intercerebralis, while the corpora cardiaca and corpora allata store brain neurohormones and also contain endocrine cells.

The ventral nerve cord and its ganglia also contain neurosecretory cells and their own neurohemal organs, called perisympathetic organs. These systems regulate molting, diapause, reproduction, water and salt balance, metabolism, heart rate, muscle contraction, and digestive activity.

## Molting in Insects

Molting is the periodic shedding of the hardened cuticle, or exoskeleton, so an insect can grow. A brain neurohormone called prothoracotropic hormone, or PTTH, is secreted by the pars intercerebralis and stored in the corpora cardiaca or corpora allata depending on the insect group.

PTTH stimulates the prothoracic glands to release ecdysone, the steroid molting hormone. In the 1940s, Sir Vincent (Brian) Wigglesworth showed that a blood meal in Rhodnius prolixus distends the abdomen, sends neural impulses to the brain, triggers PTTH release, and begins molting.

Juvenile hormone ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Juvenile_hormone)), secreted by the corpora allata, modifies the outcome of molting. When juvenile hormone is present, the epidermis remains larval and secretes larval cuticle; when it is absent at a critical time, the insect can enter a pupal stage or become adult.

## Diapause

Diapause is a state of suspended development or reproduction marked by dehydration, reduced metabolism, and nutrient accumulation that enlarges the fat bodies. It is often triggered by environmental factors such as temperature, day length, or food availability.

During diapause, neurohormones may be stored and the corpora allata may become inactive. Reversing the environmental conditions can end diapause, while juvenile hormone may either terminate or trigger it depending on species and life stage.

## Reproduction in Insects

In many insects, the pars intercerebralis secretes a neurohormone that stimulates vitellogenesis, the synthesis of vitellogenin by the fat body. Oocytes use vitellogenin to make egg yolk proteins, so this pathway links the brain, fat body, and ovary.

Juvenile hormone ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Juvenile_hormone)) often enhances vitellogenin uptake by the ovary and stimulates vitellogenin synthesis by the fat body. Other neurohormones influence reproduction by causing tissues to release pheromones that affect mating behaviour. In the live-bearing tsetse fly Glossina, a neurohormone from the corpora allata stimulates milk glands that nourish larvae.

## Osmoregulation in Insects

All insects produce a diuretic hormone, and many also produce an antidiuretic hormone. These hormones regulate water and salt balance by acting on the malpighian tubules, the insect kidney-like organs.

Insects that feed only on liquids, such as plant sap or blood, especially need diuretic regulation to remove excess fluid and salts. Osmoregulatory neurohormones are produced in both the brain and ventral nerve cord.

Small peptide myotropic factors stimulate heart rate and contractions of kidney tubules and the digestive tract. The corpora cardiaca also produce hyperglycemic activity that raises trehalose, while adipokinetic hormone releases diglycerides during and after flight in locusts and grasshoppers.

## Class Crustacea

The principal neuroendocrine system of crustaceans centres on the X-organ and its associated neurohemal organ, the sinus gland. One X-organ and sinus gland occur in each eyestalk, forming the eyestalk complex.

Crustaceans also have the Y-organ and androgenic gland, whose hormones help regulate molting, reproduction, osmoregulation, metabolism, heart rate, and colour change.

## Molting in Crustaceans

In crustaceans, the Y-organ secretes ecdysone, which stimulates molting. After release into the blood, ecdysone is converted into 20-hydroxyecdysone, the active molting hormone.

The eyestalk complex produces molt-inhibiting hormone, which blocks ecdysone secretion. Experimental evidence suggests additional molting factors also exist, so crustacean molting is likely more complex than a simple Y-organ and eyestalk opposition.

## Reproduction in Crustaceans

The eyestalk complex produces a neurohormone that inhibits vitellogenesis by the fat body and blocks vitellogenin uptake by ovarian oocytes. Older ovarian follicles may release a vitellogenin-stimulating hormone that can override this inhibition.

In shrimps and other crustaceans with sequential hermaphroditism, the androgenic gland produces a peptide hormone that masculinizes the gonad. These animals first function as males; when the androgenic gland degenerates, they become females. Removing the gland surgically can cause an early male-to-female change.

## Osmoregulation in Crustaceans

Water and ionic balance in crustaceans is controlled by factors from four sources: the brain, thoracic ganglion, eyestalk complex, and pericardial organs. These factors act on organs such as the antennal glands, intestine, gills, stomach, heart muscle, and other tissues involved in salt and water metabolism.

The pericardial organs release a factor that accelerates heart rate and may resemble an insect cardioacceleratory factor, pointing to biochemical similarities across arthropods.

## Colour Changes

Several chromatophorotropins, or neurohormones that regulate colour by acting on chromatophores, have been isolated from crustacean eyestalk extracts, including light-adapting hormone and red-pigment-concentrating hormone.

Chromatophore regulation allows crustaceans to become lighter, darker, or better matched to different backgrounds. The red-pigment-concentrating hormone is chemically similar to insect adipokinetic and myotropic factors.

## Phylum Echinodermata

Among echinoderms, female sea stars, or starfishes, are the only group studied extensively for endocrine function. About one hour before spawning, a neuropeptide called gonad-stimulating substance, or gamete-shedding substance, is released from radial nerves into the body cavity.

This gonad-stimulating substance has been reported in more than 30 sea star species. It acts directly on ovaries and causes formation of 1-methyladenine, which induces oocyte maturation and spawning. A similar hormone has been demonstrated in sea urchin ovaries.

## Phylum Chordata

Phylum Chordata is divided into three subphyla: Tunicata, Cephalochordata, and Vertebrata. The invertebrate tunicates include ascidians and salps, while cephalochordates include amphioxus, a small marine animal resembling the larva of jawless fishes.

The invertebrate chordates are important because their organs may show early forms of the vertebrate endocrine system. Structures such as the endostyle, neural gland, and Hatschek's pit help connect simple chordate endocrine organization with thyroid and pituitary evolution.

## Subphylum Tunicata

Ascidians, or sea squirts, begin as free-swimming tadpolelike larvae and then attach to a solid surface before metamorphosing into sessile adults. Both larva and adult have an endostyle, a mucus-secreting gland believed to be an evolutionary ancestor of the vertebrate thyroid gland.

Thyroid hormones can induce ascidian metamorphosis. The cerebral ganglion contains gonadotropin-releasing hormone, the neural gland may be a pituitary forerunner, neural-gland extracts stimulate toad testes, and a prolactin-like protein occurs in Styela plicata.

## Subphylum Cephalochordata

The brain of cephalochordates, especially amphioxus, contains neurosecretory neurons that may be related to Hatschek's pit, a structure near the brain. Hatschek's pit appears related to the tunicate neural gland and therefore to the vertebrate pituitary gland.

Treatment of male amphioxus with gonadotropin-releasing hormone or luteinizing hormone reportedly starts spermatogenesis. Extracts from Hatschek's pit can stimulate a toad testis, and the amphioxus endostyle can synthesize thyroid hormones, suggesting early roots of vertebrate endocrine organization.

## Core Equations

Biological systems are often summarized with growth, heredity, and energy-transfer relations:

$$\frac{dN}{dt}=rN\left(1-\frac{N}{K}\right)$$

The logistic-growth equation models population growth that slows as population size N approaches carrying capacity K.

$$p^2+2pq+q^2=1$$

Hardy-Weinberg proportions describe expected genotype frequencies when allele frequencies are p and q under idealized assumptions.

$$\Delta G=\Delta G^\circ+RT\ln Q$$

This equation adjusts standard free-energy change for actual concentrations or pressures through the reaction quotient.

## Key Events & Developments

- Early 1900s: Role of thyroid hormones in embryonic development studied in tadpoles, predating comparable mammalian investigations.
- 1940s: Sir Vincent (Brian) Wigglesworth demonstrates that abdominal distention after a blood meal in Rhodnius prolixus triggers neural signals, PTTH release, and molting.
- Modern comparative research: Studies of fishes, starfishes, chicken oviducts, insects, crustaceans, tunicates, and amphioxus clarify hormone receptors, hormone actions, and the evolutionary roots of vertebrate endocrine organs.

## Key People

- Sir Vincent (Brian) Wigglesworth — British entomologist who discovered in the 1940s that neural signals from a blood-engorged abdomen in Rhodnius prolixus ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rhodnius_prolixus)) trigger PTTH and molting; foundational figure in insect endocrinology.

## Interesting Facts

- Testosterone produced by the hermaphroditic gonad of the gastropod Euhadra peliomorpha is chemically identical to vertebrate testosterone, showing that this steroid hormone evolved before the vertebrate lineage.
- The fish urophysis produces urotensin peptides but has no homologous structure in amphibians, reptiles, or birds.
- Juvenile hormone ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Juvenile_hormone)) can preserve larval features during insect molting, but in some insects it can also help terminate or initiate diapause depending on life stage.
- The endostyle of tunicates and amphioxus points toward the evolutionary origin of the vertebrate thyroid gland.
