# The History of the Byzantine Empire

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> The Byzantine Empire was the eastern Roman Empire, a Christian, increasingly Greek-speaking polity centred on Constantinople that survived until the Ottoman conquest of 1453. The source shows how Roman law, religion, strategic geography, diplomacy, military adaptation, and pressure from neighboring powers shaped its thousand-year history.

Byzantine Empire ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Byzantine_Empire)) refers to the eastern Roman Empire, which survived about 1,000 years after the western empire broke into feudal kingdoms and finally fell to the Ottoman Turks in 1453.

The empire's people called themselves Romans, or Romaioi, not Byzantines. They believed their Christian Roman empire was founded by divine grace and reflected heavenly order.

The term Byzantine comes from Byzantium, the old Greek city on the Bosporus that Constantine refounded as Constantinople in 330. Constantinople became the political, administrative, religious, and intellectual centre of the empire until 1453.

The empire was Roman in law and administration, Christian in ideology, Greek in language and culture over time, and strategically placed between Europe and Asia.

Its history linked the medieval worlds of Europe and Asia. Constantinople stood on transit routes between the Mediterranean, the Black Sea, Anatolia, and the Balkans, so imperial survival depended on controlling passages, merchants, armies, and religious loyalties as much as on holding territory. The empire repeatedly turned geographic position into diplomatic leverage, but the same position exposed it to Germans, Huns, Persians, Arabs, Slavs, Bulgars, Crusaders, Venetians, Genoese, Serbs, and Ottoman Turks.

## East Rome and Germanic Pressures

The eastern empire initially remained part of the Roman Empire, but after 476 the West had no emperor. The East survived by using gold, manpower, diplomacy, and military adaptation.

Theodosius I allowed Goths to settle as foederati, or allied troops, but this policy was controversial. The Goths were Arian Christians, considered heretical by Orthodox Romans, and their autonomous military groups troubled imperial society.

At Constantinople in 400, citizens rose against the Gothic commander Gainas and killed him and many followers. Later Germanic leaders still served the East, but usually as individuals rather than semi-independent armed peoples.

The eastern government used [[Diplomacy|diplomacy]] to play enemies against one another. Subsidies kept Attila's Huns in uneasy peace until Marcian stopped payments and Attila turned westward.

## Justinian and Transformation

Justinian, ruling from 527 to 565, represents the transition from ancient East Rome toward medieval Byzantium. His reign had years of achievement, crisis in the 540s, and later strain.

Justinian was a builder, lawgiver, theologian, and conqueror. He sponsored the codification of Roman law, built Hagia Sophia, reorganized administration, and tried to restore Christian Roman authority in the West.

The empire fought Persia, Huns, Danubian enemies, Vandals in North Africa, Ostrogoths in Italy, and other foes. War transformed institutions and drained resources.

Justinian's early success rested on Constantinople's ability to combine classical civilian administration with exceptional military effort. Campaigns on the Persian frontier trained generals later used in western reconquest, while the Danube and Crimean theatres showed that the East could still fight on several fronts. Tribute and diplomacy were not signs of passivity; they bought time for carefully chosen offensives.

The reconquest of North Africa also shows the conditional nature of Byzantine expansion. Justinian acted when Vandal politics, Catholic persecution, merchant interests, and military caution made a limited expedition plausible. Its speed encouraged broader ambitions, but the administrative reforms that followed reveal how every victory required taxation, officials, courts, supply, and provincial restructuring.

In 532, Justinian negotiated an Endless Peace with Persia and then sent Belisarius to recover Vandal North Africa. Victory came quickly, and North Africa was reorganized as Roman territory.

Justinian's officials, especially John of Cappadocia, simplified administration, sometimes combining civil and military authority, increasing salaries, reducing overlapping jurisdictions, and demanding taxes to support military effort.

The 540s brought pestilence, warfare, social upheaval, and fiscal crisis. Justinian's late empire looked less like ancient Rome and more like the militarized medieval Byzantine state.

## Church and Society under Justinian

Justinian regulated churches, monasteries, clergy, property, liturgy, and doctrine. Bishops increasingly took over civic roles: collecting taxes, dispensing justice, organizing charity, negotiating with barbarians, and even mustering soldiers.

By the early 7th century, many Byzantine cities looked outwardly like fortresses and inwardly like religious communities under ecclesiastical leadership.

Justinian also shaped monasticism ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monasticism)) through rules based partly on St. Basil and church councils. Later monastic codes developed from Justinianic foundations.

His attempt to reconcile Monophysites and Chalcedonians failed. Efforts to win moderate Monophysites alienated western Chalcedonians and damaged earlier gains with Rome.

## Heraclius and the Arab Conquests

Heraclius faced near disaster against Persia and then Arab Islam. He drew strength from military government, land grants, and reorganized armies in Asia Minor.

The theme system grew from army groups settled in territories under generals, or strategoi, with civil and military authority. Themes such as Opsikion, Armeniakon, and Anatolikon became the backbone of survival.

Byzantium and Persia were exhausted by war when Arab Muslim armies emerged after 632. Both had demobilized, lost frontier client support, and faced internal religious tensions.

Heraclius tried Monothelitism to placate Monophysites in Syria and Egypt, but it failed. Muslim conquerors often allowed Christian communities more religious autonomy than Constantinople had, weakening loyalty to the empire.

## The 7th Century and Survival

Heraclius's successors, including Constans II, Constantine IV, and Justinian II, struggled with Arabs, Bulgars, succession conflict, and western losses.

Arab sea power grew, leading to attacks on Constantinople ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constantinople)). The Arab siege of 674-678 failed partly because Byzantines used Greek fire, a mysterious incendiary weapon.

Justinian II was deposed, mutilated, restored with Bulgar help, and killed after renewed tyranny. From 711 to 717 the empire weakened until Leo III, strategos of the Anatolikon theme, founded a new dynasty and saved the empire from Arabs and Bulgars.

## Iconoclasm and the West

The 8th century brought Iconoclasm, the imperial attack on religious images. Leo III and successors provoked opposition from Rome and many eastern monks and believers.

Rome also clashed with Constantinople over jurisdiction in Illyricum and Calabria. After Ravenna fell to the Lombards in 751, the papacy sought Frankish protection.

Pope Stephen II anointed Pippin III king of the Franks in 754. Later, Pope Leo III crowned [[Charlemagne]] emperor of the Romans on Christmas Day 800.

Even the restoration of image veneration in 787 could not heal the widening divide. Frankish advisers rejected both iconoclast and iconodule positions, while Byzantines could not accept that Irene's rule left the imperial title vacant. Ecclesiastical arguments therefore concealed a larger political question: whether Christian universal authority still belonged only to Constantinople.

Byzantium could not accept a rival Roman emperor in Aachen. The coronation revealed deepening political and ecclesiastical separation between Orthodox Byzantium and Catholic western Europe.

## Macedonian Era

![[attachments/the_history_of_the_byzantine_empire_002.webp|420]]

*Byzantine empire 1355*

The Macedonian dynasty, from Basil I in 867 to Basil II's death in 1025, was a golden age of military revival, law, learning, administration, and cultural renaissance.

Although the empire was theoretically elective, dynastic feeling was strong. Basil I secured succession through his sons, and even usurping soldier-emperors claimed to protect legitimate Macedonian heirs.

Macedonian strength also depended on disciplined succession management after periods of usurpation. Romanus I Lecapenus, Nicephorus Phocas, and John Tzimisces could seize power, but they usually framed their rule as guardianship for legitimate heirs. That compromise let military commanders govern during minorities without fully destroying dynastic continuity.

Byzantine armies regained initiative against Arabs in the East. New themes were created between Vaspurakan and Antioch, Armenia was annexed or brought under treaty, and Antioch and other frontiers were strengthened.

Missionaries extended Byzantine influence among Slavs. Cyril and Methodius created Slavonic alphabets and translated scripture and liturgy, spreading Christianity and literacy to Moravia, Serbia, Bulgaria, and beyond.

The conversion of the Bulgars became a contest between Rome and Constantinople, but Boris chose Byzantine Christianity in 870. Later relations with Bulgaria included war, diplomacy, and eventual Byzantine conquest under Basil II.

## Decline after Basil II

Basil II died childless in 1025. His relatives kept the throne until 1056 more through dynastic prestige than ability. Zoe and Theodora continued the Macedonian line through marriages and brief reigns.

From 1025 to 1081, 13 emperors ruled. The state suffered from conflict between provincial military aristocracy and Constantinopolitan civilian bureaucracy.

The empire's 11th-century weakness resembled Rome's 3rd-century crisis: external pressures worsened internal tensions. The Seljuqs, Normans, Pechenegs, Serbs, Bulgars, and western merchants all pressed against Byzantine capacity.

## Alexius I and the Crusades

Alexius I Comnenus restored stability in 1081 and founded the Comnenian dynasty. He faced Normans, Pechenegs, Seljuqs, Venetians, and a weakened treasury.

In 1082, Alexius granted [[Venice]] major trading privileges in Constantinople and elsewhere in return for naval help against Normans. This became the cornerstone of Venetian commercial power in the eastern Mediterranean and weakened Byzantine merchants.

Alexius wanted western mercenary help to recover [[Turkey and Ancient Anatolia (Anatolia)|Anatolia]], not a mass crusade to liberate Jerusalem. Pope Urban II's call in 1095 produced the First Crusade, whose scale and motives Byzantium had not expected.

The [[Crusades]] brought temporary help but deepened Greek-Latin suspicion. Byzantines saw westerners as unruly and dangerous; westerners accused Byzantines of treachery.

## Later Comneni

John II Comnenus and Manuel I Comnenus continued Alexius's policies. They relied on diplomacy, marriage, war, and alliances amid complex relations with Venice, Hungary, Serbia, Normans, Seljuqs, Crusader states, and western emperors.

Manuel admired aspects of western life, married western wives, welcomed Latins, and granted offices and estates to some. His policies angered many Byzantine subjects.

The Second Crusade in 1147 worsened Greek-Latin bitterness. Manuel allied with Conrad III against Normans, but relations with Frederick Barbarossa were strained because the western emperor did not regard Byzantium as equal.

Manuel recovered influence in the Balkans and Antioch but overreached in Anatolia. His defeat at Myriocephalon in 1176 ended the Byzantine counteroffensive against the Turks begun under Alexius.

## Latin Conquest and Palaiologan Restoration

The Fourth Crusade captured Constantinople in 1204, creating a Latin Empire and scattering Byzantine authority into successor states such as Nicaea, Epirus, and Trebizond.

Michael VIII Palaiologos recovered Constantinople in 1261 and founded the Palaiologan dynasty. He repopulated and defended the city, restored trade, and favoured Genoese merchants who had helped against Venice.

Genoese and Venetian rivalry made Byzantium a victim of Italian commercial power. Michael spent scarce resources in the Morea, islands, Epirus, Thessaly, and diplomacy against western reconquest threats.

To block Charles of Anjou's crusading plans, Michael offered church union with Rome at the Council of Lyon in 1274. Most Byzantines opposed the union, seeing it as betrayal of Orthodoxy.

## Andronicus II and Ottoman Rise

Andronicus II rejected his father's western policy, restored Orthodoxy, cut the army, and disbanded the navy to save money. This left Byzantium dependent on Genoese sea power and exposed to Turkish expansion.

In 1302, Ottoman warriors under Osman I defeated Byzantine forces near Nicomedia. The Osmanli, or Ottoman, people soon overran much Byzantine territory.

Andronicus hired the Grand Catalan Company, which defeated Turks but then turned on Byzantium after its leader was murdered. The Catalans ravaged Thrace, invited Turks into Europe, and eventually conquered Athens.

## Civil Wars and Final Decline

Andronicus III and John Cantacuzenus represented a younger aristocratic generation but inherited a weakened empire. Civil war from 1321 to 1328 deepened vulnerability.

The Byzantines lost Nicaea in 1331 and Nicomedia in 1337 to Orhan's Ottomans. Northwestern Anatolia, once the imperial heartland, was lost.

Byzantine rulers increasingly hired Turkish soldiers against Italians, Serbs, Bulgars, and internal rivals. Serbia under Stefan Dusan expanded and claimed imperial status over Serbs and Greeks.

The final centuries saw economic weakness, civil war, western pressure, church union debates, Ottoman expansion, and shrinking territory. Constantinople survived as a symbolic capital long after the empire's resources had collapsed.

Late Byzantine rulers gained occasional successes, including the recovery of Epirus and Thessaly under Andronicus III, but these achievements could not restore the tax base or strategic depth lost in Anatolia. Civil wars made foreign soldiers indispensable, and each hired ally carried political costs. Ottoman, Serbian, Italian, and internal pressures therefore reinforced one another until imperial policy became a sequence of emergency bargains.

## Fall of Constantinople

In 1453, the last Constantine defended Constantinople against the Ottoman Turks. The city walls that had resisted Germans, Huns, Avars, Slavs, Persians, Arabs, Bulgars, and Crusaders were finally breached by modern artillery.

The fall joined ancient, medieval, and modern worlds. The new Rome built by Constantine fell under the last Constantine, while European cannon technology helped the most successful Central Asian-descended invaders.
