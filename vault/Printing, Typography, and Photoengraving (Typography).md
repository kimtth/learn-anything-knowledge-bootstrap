# Printing, Typography, and Photoengraving (Typography)

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> Typography is the functional design and arrangement of letterforms for readable, durable pages. Its history follows movable type from Italian Roman faces through French and English book design, mathematical and industrial type systems, and Arts and Crafts reforms that shaped modern visual communication.

## Introduction

Typography ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Typography)) is the design or selection of letter forms and their organization into words, sentences, and blocks of type on a page. It may also involve paper, ink, printing method, and binding, but its central concern is the appearance and readability of the printed page.

Strictly speaking, typography began only after movable type appeared in the mid-15th century. Manuscript writing came before it, and transient electronic letters on signboards or screens are only analogically related. Whether the letter is made by metal type or photographic image matters less than whether it becomes a stable designed page.

## The Nature of Typography

![[attachments/typography_002.webp|420]]

*Metal movable type*

Typography ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Typography)) is a useful art, not primarily a fine art. Books and printed pages usually exist because writers have something to say, publishers want to disseminate it, and readers need to understand it. The typographer's task is to make the connection between writer and reader usable.

This does not mean typography must be invisible in a crude sense. Beatrice Warde's idea that printing ought to be invisible means that typography should not obstruct reading. Aesthetic judgment, taste, and imagination still matter, but they operate within the function of communication.

Typography is also a secondary art. Its basic material, the alphabet or other notation system, is inherited rather than invented by the typographer. Reading conventions are also largely inherited. The typographer can refine, arrange, and clarify, but cannot freely overturn the system readers must use.

It is secondary in another sense because the typographer's design becomes real only through a mechanical or technical process. In the 20th century this often meant working through printers, typesetting machines, or electronics specialists, especially in early computer-generated typefaces.

## Typeface Classifications

![[attachments/typography_001.webp|420]]

*Garamond type specimen*

Typeface classification commonly distinguishes old face, transitional, modern, and later old-style revivals. These categories are based on letter design, not simply on date. Old faces grew from calligraphic and pen-based models, with bracketed serifs and relatively moderate thick-thin contrast.

Transitional types stand between old face and modern. Modern types, associated with the late 18th and early 19th centuries, show stronger contrast, thinner and longer serifs, sharper transitions, and a more vertical stress influenced by engraving rather than the pen.

Old-style revivals are modern reinterpretations of earlier faces. Designers such as Stanley Morison reworked historical models, creating new typefaces that owed much to old forms while becoming independent creations.

## Italy and the Roman Typeform

Printing spread from Germany first to [[Italy]], where German and German-trained printers established presses. Sweynheim and Pannartz opened a press at Subiaco in 1465 and printed Cicero in an early Antiqua type that helped lead toward roman.

The brothers Johann and Wendelin von Speyer opened Venice's first press in 1469 and used a clear, legible face moving toward roman. Nicolas Jenson, a Frenchman trained in Germany, set up in Venice and cut a roman type for Cicero's Epistolae ad Brutum in 1470.

Jenson's roman type has been praised as one of the most perfect roman faces ever cut. His training as a medalist may explain its precision. He did not use roman for ecclesiastical or legal works, which still often used Gothic or related forms.

## France and England

In [[France]], printing began as a sponsored activity, especially in Paris, where the Sorbonne invited German printers around 1470. French printers helped establish the dominance of roman and italic type, influenced by Simon de Colines, Henri and Robert Estienne, Geoffroy Tory, and Claude Garamond.

French books gained a reputation for careful elegance. Antoine Verard's luxurious books of hours, beginning in 1485, were precise, delicate, illustrated, and ornamented. Henri Estienne and Geoffroy Tory linked scholarship, publishing, and typographic refinement.

In England, printing was introduced by William Caxton. He printed The Recuyell of the Historyes of Troye in Bruges about 1474, returned to England in 1476, and set up at Westminster. The first dated book printed in England was Dictes and Sayenges of the Phylosophers in 1477.

Caxton printed about 90 books, most in English, before Wynkyn de Worde continued the business. Richard Pynson was an early English printer of higher typographic quality, using the first roman type in England in 1518 and issuing more than 400 works.

## Maturation of the Printed Book

By the end of the first century of typography, most basic book elements had taken shape. The main type families were formalized, business and craft functions were established, and printers had developed title pages, page layout, type hierarchy, and book formats.

Claude Garamond's mid-16th-century roman and italic designs became central to European typography. His roman drew on Griffo's cutting, and his italic drew on Ludovico degli Arrighi. So many adaptations appeared that Garamond became almost a generic term.

By the end of the 16th century, European typography had declined in energy and quality. A major revival began in France with the Imprimerie Royale, founded under Louis XIII in 1640, and later with Louis XIV's commission for a new scientifically designed royal type.

## Romain du Roi and Later Designers

The Romain du Roi ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Romain_du_Roi)) was designed on mathematical principles with letters drawn on grids divided into thousands of parts. Philippe Grandjean cut the punches, using the drawings as guidance rather than mechanical command. First used in 1702, the type was precise, regular, and influential.

The Romain du Roi shifted influence from the calligrapher toward the engineer. Pierre-Simon Fournier copied and extended the style and also produced decorative printer's devices. The calligraphic model would not be powerfully reintroduced until William Morris in the 19th century.

William Caslon issued his first specimen sheet in 1734 and refined Garamond-style forms into sturdy, traditional faces still used later. Other important designers, including Baskerville in England and Bodoni in Italy, pushed typography toward greater contrast, precision, and modernity.

## Type and Book Design Since the 19th Century

Two late 19th-century developments changed book typography: mechanical composition and the Arts and Crafts movement. Linotype was patented by Ottmar Mergenthaler in 1884, and Monotype by Tolbert Lanston in 1887. These machines shaped the look of the 20th-century book.

Mechanical composition increased speed but also limited type choice because new machine faces were expensive. It curbed Victorian exuberance, where many typefaces might appear in one book, and pushed printers toward more economical typographic systems.

The Arts and Crafts Movement, led in typography by William Morris, reacted against industrial ugliness and the loss of joy in work. Morris's private-press ideals looked back to craft, decoration, and early printing while influencing modern book design and industrial design.

The private-press movement encouraged better paper, type, margins, spacing, and harmony between page and book. Its paradox was that anti-industrial craft ideals helped shape later industrial typography by raising standards of design.
