# Amsterdam

## Introduction

Amsterdam is the capital and chief commercial and financial centre of the [[Netherlands]], a city and port on the IJsselmeer linked to the North Sea. More than 700 years old, it is often called a living museum for its centuries-old canals, patrician houses, and atmosphere of tolerance, yet the modern city also faces pollution, traffic, and housing shortages. It is the nominal capital but not the seat of government, which is The Hague, and it lacks grand squares or monuments; its inner city is divided by canals into some 90 islands joined by about 1,300 bridges and viaducts.

## The Old City and Canals

![[attachments/amsterdam_001.webp|420]]

*Herengracht canal in Amsterdam*

The three concentric canals—Herengracht, Keizersgracht, and Prinsengracht—and their radial branches form a characteristic spiderweb laid out during the prosperous Golden Age ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Golden_Age)) of the 17th and early 18th centuries. The old town holds the 13th-century Old Church ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oude_Kerk%2C_Amsterdam)) and the 15th-century New Church, beside which stands the 17th-century city hall, now the Royal Palace in Palladian style. Other landmarks include the Mint Tower, the Zuiderkerk, and the Westerkerk, where [[Rembrandt]] is buried. Fine patrician houses line the canals; the main squares are the Dam, Leidseplein, and Rembrandtplein; and the former Jewish quarter contains the Portuguese Synagogue and the Rembrandt House.

## The Modern City

Major change came in the late 19th and early 20th centuries, when booming colonial trade fueled industrialization: the North Sea Canal was completed in 1876, Central Station opened in 1889, and great institutions such as the Rijksmuseum, the Concertgebouw (1888), and the Olympic Stadium (1928) were built. Amsterdam suffered less wartime damage than many European cities, but its old Jewish quarter was razed. Postwar growth produced garden suburbs such as Slotermeer and the modernist Bijlmermeer of the 1970s, later partly demolished and rebuilt, after which low-rise mixed housing became the norm. The population passed 800,000 after the war and is now sustained partly by Surinamese, Moroccan, and Turkish immigrants.

## Industry

Industry no longer dominates Amsterdam's economy, but varied activities remain, from shipbuilding and heavy engineering to petrochemicals, food processing and brewing, and diamond polishing. To reduce unemployment, the city pursues an active economic policy, improving regional infrastructure links and offering training and grants to attract investment, creating thousands of subsidized jobs late in the 20th century.

## Tourism

Tourism is a major and growing activity. Many visitors come for business or for conferences at the large RAI Exhibition and Congress Centre, and because much of the compact centre can be seen on foot in a day, day trips are also very popular.

## Transportation

![[attachments/amsterdam_002.webp|420]]

*Amsterdam Central Station facade*

Amsterdam has excellent rail, water, road, and air links. Schiphol Airport is among the busiest in Europe and a major world hub, while the seaport, though overshadowed by nearby Rotterdam-Europoort, remains important. Superhighways connect the city with the rest of the Netherlands and with Germany and Belgium. Since the 1960s planners have favoured public transport: a metro line opened in 1976 and a fast rail link to Schiphol in 1988, but trams remain the main means of travel in the inner city.

## Administration

Under the Dutch constitution, each municipality is headed by a council whose size reflects its population. The 45-member Amsterdam City Council is elected for four-year terms, and its College of Aldermen consists of eight aldermen and the mayor, who is appointed by the crown for six years and usually represents the largest party. The Labour Party has dominated the council since World War II, and a new city hall and opera house were built at Waterloo Square in the mid-1980s.

## Culture and Recreation

Amsterdam offers more than 200 live-performance venues, including the Concertgebouw, home of the world-famous Royal Concertgebouw Orchestra, and the Muziektheater, where the national ballet and opera perform. It has two universities—the University of Amsterdam (1632) and the Free University (1880)—plus many academies and over 100 galleries, and the arts employ thousands and earn close to a billion dollars a year. Recreation is abundant, with the Amsterdam Woods, lakes, some 40 sports parks, and world-class venues such as the Amsterdam Arena, home of the Ajax football club.

## History

Although a Roman-era settlement at the mouth of the Amstel cannot be ruled out, none has been found, and Amsterdam is known to have begun as a small fishing village in the 13th century. Its people built dikes and, about 1270, a dam that gave the city its name, and their ships soon traded as far as the Baltic. Count Floris V granted a toll privilege in 1275 that first records the name Amsterdam, and a full charter followed in 1306. In 1489, Emperor Maximilian I allowed the city to bear the imperial crown, and after the Netherlands passed to Spain, a bloodless revolution in 1578 brought Amsterdam into the Dutch revolt led by William I.
