# Stalin

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> Joseph Stalin ruled the Soviet Union through party control, forced industrialization and collectivization, terror, wartime leadership, and a pervasive personality cult. The source follows his rise through Bolshevik organization, elimination of rivals, transformation of Soviet power, and the immense human costs of his dictatorship.

## Introduction

![[attachments/stalin_001.webp|420]]

*Portrait of Joseph Stalin*

Joseph Stalin, born Ioseb Dzhugashvili, was Communist Party secretary-general from 1922 to 1953 and Soviet premier from 1941 to 1953. He ruled dictatorially for about a quarter century and transformed the Soviet Union into a major world power.

Stalin industrialized the Soviet Union, forced collectivization, used police terror, helped defeat Germany, and extended Soviet control over eastern Europe. He created a military-industrial state and led the Soviet Union into the nuclear age, but at the cost of mass death, repression, and poverty.

Soviet propaganda created a false heroic cult of Stalin, praising him as genius, teacher, father, and benefactor. Busts, statues, icons, and public rituals promoted his image.

## The Young Revolutionary

Stalin was born in Gori, Georgia, the son of a poor cobbler and a devout washerwoman. His mother hoped he would become a priest, and he learned Russian at church school.

At the Tiflis Theological Seminary, he secretly read Karl Marx and other forbidden works. He left or was expelled in 1899 and entered revolutionary politics. He was strong, secretive, patient, and implacable in revenge.

In 1900, Dzhugashvili joined the political underground in the Caucasus. After the Russian Social Democrats split in 1903, he joined Lenin's Bolsheviks. Between 1902 and 1913 he was repeatedly arrested, exiled, and able to escape.

## Rise to Power

Stalin rose slowly in the Bolshevik Party, using organization rather than public brilliance to advance. After the February Revolution of 1917, he returned from Siberia to Petrograd and resumed work on Pravda.

At first he favoured cooperation with the provisional government, but under [[Lenin]]'s influence he supported armed seizure of power. In the November 1917 coup he played a real but less prominent role than Trotsky.

During the Civil War, Stalin served as a political-military leader and held posts as commissar for nationalities and state control. His decisive position was secretary-general of the party's Central Committee from 1922 until death, giving him control over appointments and bureaucracy.

[[Lenin]], near death, warned against Stalin and called for his removal as secretary-general. Stalin's skill and luck allowed him to neutralize Lenin's testament. His rivals underestimated him as unintellectual and bureaucratic, an error that proved fatal.

## Lenin's Successor

![[attachments/stalin_002.webp|420]]

*Lenin and Stalin seated together*

After Lenin's death in 1924, Stalin promoted a cult of Lenin while building his own authority. He first joined Zinoviev and Kamenev against Trotsky, then allied with Bukharin and Rykov against his former partners. He advocated Socialism in one country ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Socialism_in_one_country)), a policy attractive to party managers.

Stalin gradually destroyed all major rivals. Trotsky was expelled from the Soviet Union in 1929 and assassinated in Mexico in 1940. Zinoviev, Kamenev, Bukharin, and Rykov were disgraced and later executed. Stalin's organizational control became personal dictatorship.

In 1928, he abandoned Lenin's New Economic Policy and launched forced industrialization and collectivization. This was a second revolution from above, more socially destructive than 1917 for millions of peasants.

## Collectivization, Industrialization, and Terror

Collectivization forced about 25 million peasant households into collective or state farms. Resistant peasants, labelled kulaks, were arrested, shot, exiled, or sent to camps. Grain continued to be exported during famine, especially in Ukraine. Millions died.

Crash [[Modernization and Industrialization (Industrialization)|industrialization]] expanded Soviet heavy industry through five-year plans, but failures were blamed on managers through show trials. Forced confessions made scapegoats for policy disasters.

Stalin's second wife, Nadezhda Alliluyeva, tried to moderate him but died by suicide in 1932. His family life was harsh; his son Vasily later died as an alcoholic, and his daughter Svetlana emigrated after his death.

The Great Purge destroyed party officials, military leaders, police officials, intellectuals, professionals, and ordinary citizens. Denunciations, forced confessions, and executions spread fear. Stalin's victims numbered in the tens of millions.

## Role in World War II

![[attachments/stalin_003.webp|420]]

*Yalta Conference with Churchill, Roosevelt, and Stalin*

In August 1939, Stalin made a pact with Adolf Hitler after failing to secure an anti-Hitler alliance with Western powers. The pact encouraged Germany's attack on Poland and the beginning of World War II. Stalin then annexed eastern Poland, Estonia, Latvia, Lithuania, and parts of Romania, and attacked Finland.

When Germany invaded the Soviet Union on June 22, 1941, Soviet defenses were badly exposed. Stalin soon became supreme commander, remained in threatened Moscow, and helped organize resistance and counteroffensives.

The battles of [[Moscow]], Stalingrad, and Kursk turned the war against Germany. Stalin maintained close control over fronts, reserves, and the war economy, though he gradually learned to delegate more to commanders. The Soviet Union played a central role in defeating Hitler by 1945.

Stalin met Winston Churchill and [[Franklin D. Roosevelt]] at Allied conferences. His wartime leadership made the Soviet Union one of the two dominant postwar powers, despite his earlier purges and mistakes.

## Last Years

After World War II, Stalin imposed Soviet control over eastern Europe through nominally independent Communist regimes. This expanded his subjects by about 100 million people. Yugoslavia's break under Tito in 1948 damaged the image of a unified Stalin-led Communist bloc.

To prevent other satellites from following Tito, Stalin encouraged show trials in eastern Europe. Leaders confessed to Titoism and other crimes, and many were executed. The Cold War replaced wartime alliance.

At home, Andrey Zhdanov led attacks on artists and intellectuals, foreign achievements were derided, and Russian priority was asserted in many fields. Hopes for postwar relaxation disappeared.

In his final years, Stalin became increasingly paranoid. The Doctors' Plot, announced in January 1953, accused mostly Jewish Kremlin doctors of murdering Soviet leaders. Stalin may have been preparing another terror when he died on March 5, 1953.

## Assessment

Stalin was a politician of extraordinary manipulative talent. He understood bureaucratic power better than other Bolshevik leaders and used party offices, ministries, police, unions, armed forces, and administrative networks to dominate society.

His main achievement was rapid [[Modernization and Industrialization (Industrialization)|industrialization]]. By 1937 Soviet industrial output was second only to that of the United States. After World War II, the Soviet Union remained the second great industrial and military power and exploded an atomic bomb in 1949.

Against this stood the immense human cost. Consumer goods and living standards remained poor, and much wealth went to the military, police, and further industrialization. Collectivization failed economically but succeeded as a tool for controlling peasants.

Historians disagree sharply about Stalin. George F. Kennan stressed his criminality, Robert C. Tucker compared him to Ivan the Terrible, E.H. Carr saw him as ruthless but unoriginal, and Isaac Deutscher treated him as a deviant element in Marxism. His narrow genius lay in political manipulation.

## Major Works

Stalin's writings include Anarchism or Socialism?, Marxism and the National and Colonial Question, Foundations of Leninism, Problems of Leninism, writings on agrarian policy, the Great Patriotic War, linguistics, and economic problems of socialism.

Many works attributed to Stalin were propaganda or collective productions shaped by his authority, including official biographies and the Short History of the Communist Party of the Soviet Union.

## Key Events & Developments

- 1899: Stalin left or was expelled from Tiflis Theological Seminary.
- 1917: Stalin returned to Petrograd and joined the Bolshevik seizure of power.
- 1922: He became secretary-general of the Communist Party.
- 1924: Lenin died, and Stalin began consolidating succession power.
- 1928: Stalin abandoned the New Economic Policy for forced industrialization and collectivization.
- 1930s: The Great Purge killed or imprisoned millions.
- 1939: Stalin signed the pact with Hitler.
- 1941-1945: Stalin led the Soviet war effort against Germany.
- 1949: The Soviet Union exploded an atomic bomb.
- 1953: Stalin died in Moscow.

## Key People

- Joseph Stalin ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph_Stalin)): Soviet dictator and architect of Stalinist totalitarianism.
- Vladimir Lenin ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vladimir_Lenin)): Bolshevik leader whose succession Stalin captured.
- Leon Trotsky ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leon_Trotsky)): Stalin's chief rival, exiled and later assassinated.
- Adolf Hitler ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adolf_Hitler)): German dictator whose invasion turned Stalin into wartime leader.
- Winston Churchill ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Winston_Churchill)) and [[Franklin D. Roosevelt]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Franklin_D._Roosevelt)): Allied leaders who negotiated with Stalin during World War II.

## Interesting Facts
