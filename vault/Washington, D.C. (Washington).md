# Washington, D.C. (Washington)

## Introduction

Washington, D.C., in full Washington, District of Columbia ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Washington%2C_D.C.)), is both a city and the capital of the [[United States of America (United States)|United States of America]]. It is the same territory as the District of Columbia, often called simply D.C., and stands on the northern bank of the Potomac River at the point where river travel met land transport. Maryland borders the district on the north, east, and west, while Virginia lies across the Potomac to the south.

Congress created a federal territory of 100 square miles in 1790 to serve as the permanent seat of government. The site was central among the eastern seaboard states, about 90 miles inland from the Atlantic Ocean, and was formed from land given by Maryland and Virginia. In the mid-19th century, the land south of the Potomac was returned to Virginia, leaving the District with its present area. After the American Civil War ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Civil_War)), the city expanded beyond its original planned limits and became legally identical with the District.

The city has several identities at once. It is a working federal capital, an international metropolis, a major tourist centre, a storehouse of national history, and a local community with neighbourhood life. About half its land is owned by the federal government, which pays no local taxes on it. Since 1974, Washington has had an elected mayor and council, but Congress still holds veto power. The wider metropolitan area stretches across parts of Maryland and Virginia, tying the District's economy to its suburbs.

## Landscape

Washington was built on a low, broad, gently rolling peninsula shaped by the Potomac River and the Anacostia River. The names reflect older local geography and trade: Potomac comes from an Algonquian word linked with trading, and Anacostia comes from the Nacostines, a people who traded along that river. Terraces around the city rise in places to about 400 feet above sea level, and later neighbourhoods grew across these higher areas.

Development was uneven because natural features slowed growth. A long shallow ravine, now Rock Creek Park, separated early Washington from the older port of Georgetown. Expansion north and west of this ravine remained slow until bridges and public transportation made movement easier near the end of the 19th century.

## Climate

The [[Climate and Weather (Climate)|climate]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Climate)) is temperate but humid. Rainfall is fairly even through the year, with about 3 to 4 inches each month. Winters are damp rather than severe. Snow is usually light and wet, daytime winter temperatures average in the mid-30s Fahrenheit, and melted snow can freeze into ice at night.

Summers in Washington can bring short periods of high heat and dense humidity. Daytime summer temperatures average in the mid-70s Fahrenheit, but temperatures above 100 F sometimes occur. Spring and autumn are usually mild and often last longer than the colder and hotter seasons.

## City Layout

Pierre-Charles L'Enfant, a French engineer who had served in the American Revolution, planned the capital. His design drew on 18th-century Baroque landscape architecture and on his knowledge of [[Paris]] and Versailles. He placed the U.S. Capitol on a high ridge, linked it symbolically to the presidential palace, later the White House, by Pennsylvania Avenue, and made the Capitol the centre of the street plan.

The city was divided into four quadrants: Northwest ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Points_of_the_compass)), Northeast, Southeast, and Southwest. North Capitol, East Capitol, and South Capitol streets follow three of the survey lines; the fourth runs west through the Mall toward the Potomac. Numbered north-south streets, lettered east-west streets, and broad diagonal avenues combine to create long vistas, circles, squares, and public spaces. Many diagonal avenues carry the names of U.S. states.

## Architecture and Monuments

![[attachments/washington_d_c_washington_002.webp|420]]

Three forces strongly shaped Washington's architecture: height limits, Classicism, and design conservatism. Congress passed height restrictions as early as 1899, and the Height of Buildings Act of 1910 preserved the city's low skyline. Most buildings therefore spread outward instead of rising into skyscrapers, giving the capital a horizontal appearance unlike many other large American cities.

Classical design has marked federal Washington since 1800. The White House, designed by James Hoban, reflects an 18th-century Palladian style, while the Capitol, first designed by William Thornton, reflects Greek Revival and later additions such as marble wings and a cast-iron dome. Later public buildings used Georgian, Palladian, Greek Revival, Second Empire, Neoclassical, Art Deco, Modernist, and postmodern ideas, but new styles usually arrived slowly.

Public sculpture and memorials fill the city with political and historical memory. Civil War figures such as George H. Thomas, William Tecumseh Sherman, and Philip H. Sheridan are honoured, as are naval dead, battlefield nurses, African American Civil War soldiers, and Abraham Lincoln. International figures and foreign heroes of the American Revolution also appear in public statues, including Tadeusz Kosciuszko, Friedrich von Steuben, Rochambeau, and Lafayette.

## The District

The District of Columbia grew through a mixture of planning and accident. Federal building, immigration, public transportation, suburban flight, urban renewal, and renewed interest in urban life all reshaped the neighbourhoods. Fair-housing changes in the 1950s and 1960s encouraged many middle-class European Americans to move to suburbs, while middle-class African Americans moved into areas that had earlier been closed to them.

Some neighbourhoods became strongly identified with particular groups and incomes. Wealthier European Americans concentrated in upper Northwest, wealthy African Americans in the Gold Coast near upper 16th Street, and poorer African Americans in Anacostia. Other areas, such as Capitol Hill and Brookland, included both black and white residents who tried to build integrated communities.

## Northwest

Northwest ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Points_of_the_compass)) is the largest quadrant and contains many of the city's federal buildings, tourist destinations, and wealthy neighbourhoods. It includes Downtown, Lafayette Square, Foggy Bottom, Georgetown, Dupont Circle, Adams-Morgan, and other areas. Its mixture of government offices, historic houses, universities, embassies, nightlife, and residential districts shows the city's layered character.

## Downtown

![[attachments/washington_d_c_washington_003.webp|420]]

*Aerial view of White House and downtown, Washington, D.C LCCN2010630891*

Downtown Washington lies between the Capitol, the White House, and Georgetown. During the 19th century, it was both a workplace and home for shopkeepers, office workers, labourers, craftspeople, politicians, lobbyists, and hotel workers. It also included immigrant communities from China, Greece, Italy, Germany, and Jewish and Catholic German backgrounds. Center Market, the city's main farmers' market, operated near 7th Street until 1931.

The area declined in the mid-20th century as suburban growth and the 1968 riots reduced activity. In the early 21st century, however, Downtown revived through a sports arena, hotels, restaurants, a convention centre, museums, restored historic buildings, condominiums, and luxury apartments. Chinatown, Metro Center, the Federal Triangle, and the K Street office corridor became part of this renewed central district.

## Lafayette Square

Lafayette Square, north of the White House, was once one of Washington's grandest addresses. In the 19th century, cabinet officials, diplomats, vice presidents, social leaders, philanthropists, and military commanders lived or were entertained there. Octagon House, built in 1800, was the first major home, and St. John's Church, built in 1816, became known as the Church of the Presidents.

The neighbourhood changed in the 20th century as mansions gave way to banks, offices, and institutional buildings. By 1962, the federal government planned to replace remaining houses with offices, but Jacqueline Kennedy helped support preservation. A compromise kept historic fronts while placing new office structures behind them. Blair House remained the presidential guesthouse, Decatur House was saved, and St. John's Church remained intact.

## Foggy Bottom

Foggy Bottom lies between the White House and Georgetown. Its name came from wharves, warehouses, brickyards, breweries, gas works, and glassmaking, where smoke and river humidity sometimes produced fog. The first U.S. Naval Observatory was built there in 1844, and Union troops used the low fields during the Civil War.

After the war, German immigrants settled near light industrial jobs, but industry later declined and the area became poorer. George Washington University bought inexpensive land and gradually replaced row houses with classrooms, dormitories, and offices. Federal buildings, the Watergate complex, the Kennedy Center for the Performing Arts, hotels, apartments, and university facilities later transformed the area, though some 19th-century row houses survived.

## Georgetown

Georgetown is the oldest neighbourhood in the District. Native people knew the trading village as Tohoga, and by 1751 it was a colonial tobacco port named for King George II. It became part of the District when Maryland transferred land for the capital, and Georgetown University, founded in 1789, became the first Roman Catholic academic institution in the United States.

The Chesapeake and Ohio Canal brought mills, foundries, and lime kilns to the waterfront in the 19th century. Georgetown kept its own government and identity until 1871, when it was absorbed into the larger city. After losing fashion by the end of the century, it was rediscovered in the 1930s by New Deal officials who restored old houses. Congress made much of Georgetown a historic district in 1951, and sites such as Old Stone House, Tudor Place, Dumbarton House, and Dumbarton Oaks became public landmarks.

## Dupont Circle

Dupont Circle grew from neglected marshland into an elite Gilded Age neighbourhood after the Civil War. Wealthy businesspeople and investors built mansions and fashionable row houses around the circle, making the area a symbol of Washington's social importance. Diplomatic buildings and winter residences strengthened its prestige.

During the [[Great Depression]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Depression)), many mansions became boarding houses, offices, clubs, or embassies. By the 1960s, the neighbourhood was no longer exclusive and had become a centre for students, counterculture, and anti-Vietnam War protest. Historic district designation in 1978 helped preserve major houses and pushed real-estate prices upward. Nearby areas include Adams-Morgan, Mt. Pleasant, Tenleytown, Chevy Chase, American University Park, Shaw, U Street, and LeDroit Park, each with its own ethnic, educational, musical, or residential history.

## Northeast

Northeast Washington contains residential neighbourhoods founded mainly in the 19th century. Brookland grew between 1887 and 1901 and includes the Shrine of the Immaculate Conception, the Franciscan Monastery, and the Catholic University of America. Deanwood, established in 1888, began as a semirural mixed community and became mostly African American by the mid-20th century.

The area northeast of the Capitol was once called Swampoodle because of flooding and swampy ground. Working-class Irish immigrants lived there after the Irish Potato Famine. The name later disappeared, and by the 1980s the area became known as NoMa, or North of Massachusetts Avenue. New streets, renovated Union Station, the National Postal Museum, condominiums, a subway station, and federal offices reshaped it in the late 20th and early 21st centuries.

## Southeast

Southeast Washington is dominated by Capitol Hill and also includes Anacostia. The quadrant combines the oldest residential area of the planned city, a major federal and military waterfront, and communities strongly affected by race, income, suburban flight, and redevelopment.

## Capitol Hill

Capitol Hill is the oldest residential community in the original city. Boarding houses and taverns served members of Congress by 1805, and low-rise row houses, shops, and businesses soon replaced farms. Eastern Market, operating since 1802 and completed in its later form in 1873, became the neighbourhood's centre.

Federal expansion changed the area between 1880 and 1980. Whole blocks were cleared for the Library of Congress, the Supreme Court, congressional offices, and the Folger Shakespeare Library. The Washington Navy Yard, founded in 1799, provided skilled work for African Americans and European immigrants and later became a major centre for ordnance production and engineering research. After World War II, many houses became rentals and boarding houses, but later gentrification restored much of the housing stock while displacing lower-income residents.

## Anacostia

Anacostia, southeast of the Anacostia River, has a mostly African American population and lower average incomes. Development began in 1854, and the 11th Street Bridge connected it to Capitol Hill in 1874. After the Civil War, many former slaves lived there, and by the end of the 19th century it was ethnically and economically mixed.

White families largely left after school desegregation in the late 1950s and during the 1968 riots. The neighbourhood includes the Frederick Douglass National Historic Site and the Anacostia Community Museum. In the early 21st century, the waterfront opposite the Navy Yard saw residential and commercial development, helped by the Washington Nationals baseball stadium, opened in 2008.

## Southwest

Southwest Washington was planned as a waterfront commercial and residential district. L'Enfant included a military defence site there, and Fort Lesley J. McNair began as an army arsenal in 1794. Before the Civil War, free and enslaved black residents lived in a large Southwest community, and the 6th Street wharf served as a stop on the Underground Railroad.

After the Civil War, freed blacks and European immigrants settled in Southwest, where small row houses and hidden alley dwellings created crowded poor communities. Jewish immigrants from Germany and eastern Europe built shops in the 1880s. By the 1930s, the area was poor and largely African American. Urban renewal after 1946 demolished thousands of buildings, displaced many residents, and replaced the old district with federal buildings, highways, apartment complexes, and later waterfront redevelopment.

## Virginia

Arlington County was part of the District until 1846. Once mostly farmland, it was badly damaged during the Civil War. The plantation of George Washington Parke Custis was seized by the federal government, became a military stronghold, and later became Arlington National Cemetery. In the 20th century, farms gave way to modest homes and garden apartments for government workers, making Arlington a close bedroom community for the capital.

Northern Virginia also includes major national and regional landmarks. The Marine Corps War Memorial was inspired by the Iwo Jima flag-raising photograph by Joe Rosenthal. The U.S. Air Force Memorial, dedicated in 2006, rises near the cemetery. The Pentagon was built during World War II to bring military offices together. [[Alexandria]], part of the District from 1791 to 1846, kept its port character and later revived through historic preservation. Fairfax County includes Mount Vernon, Woodlawn Plantation, Gunston Hall, Great Falls Park, and the Steven F. Udvar-Hazy Center.

## Maryland

Nearby Montgomery County and Prince George's County form important parts of the Washington region. Bethesda-Chevy Chase grew into a centre of shops, offices, restaurants, expensive housing, and cultural activity. Silver Spring became a large, diverse northern suburb, while Takoma Park kept a smaller community character with Victorian houses, shops, and festivals.

Maryland's side of the region also includes major institutions. NASA's Goddard Space Flight Center and the University of Maryland are in Prince George's County. These suburbs show how Washington's economy, transportation, education, and population spread far beyond the District line.

## Parks and Open Spaces

Washington is rich in parks. Rock Creek Park, bought by Congress in 1890, covers nearly 1,800 acres in Northwest and includes the National Zoological Park. Potomac Park was created in 1897 from reclaimed river flats and tidal reservoirs and later gained cherry trees given by the people of [[Japan]] in 1912.

Many national memorials stand in these parklands, including the Lincoln Memorial, the Vietnam Veterans Memorial, the Korean War Veterans Memorial, the Jefferson Memorial, the Franklin Delano Roosevelt Memorial, and the National World War II Memorial. Other green spaces include the National Arboretum, Theodore Roosevelt Island, Meridian Hill Park, Montrose Park, Glover-Archibald Park, and Kenilworth Aquatic Gardens.

## People

Washington has always been ethnically diverse. In 1800, one community consisted mainly of affluent people of European descent, while another consisted of labourers who built the capital, including free blacks, enslaved people, and immigrants. Early immigrants came especially from Scotland and [[Ireland]], followed in the 19th century by people from Germany, Italy, eastern Europe, Greece, and China.

The city's African American history is central. In 1800, about one-third of the population was black, and about one-fifth of that group was free. By 1860, more than four-fifths of black residents were free. During and after the Civil War, tens of thousands of freed people came to the city, and by 1900 Washington had the largest African American urban population in the United States. By the 1970s, nearly three-fourths of city residents were African American, though that share later declined. At the start of the 21st century, the city remained racially and economically divided, with many European Americans in Northwest and many African Americans in Southeast.

## Economy

The service sector ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tertiary_sector)) dominates Washington's economy because of federal civil-service work, tourism, lobbying, research, and development. Most businesses have some connection to the federal government. Thousands of residents work as lobbyists, seeking laws or policies that help the interests they represent. The idea of the lobbyist is often linked in popular memory to people who approached Ulysses S. Grant in the lobby of the Willard Hotel.

## Manufacturing

Washington is not mainly a manufacturing city, but local natural resources have mattered. Sandstone and granite from nearby Maryland and Virginia were used in buildings such as the Capitol and the White House. Clay and soil from the Potomac Valley made bricks and terra cotta, common building materials in the city. Gold was even mined within 10 miles of the city.

Finance also grew around the federal presence. Banking began after Congress arrived and the Bank of the United States opened a branch office. The financial district surrounds the Treasury Department, and important institutions include the Federal Reserve Board, the Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation, the Bureau of Engraving and Printing, the World Bank, the International Monetary Fund, and the Inter-American Development Bank.

## Transportation

Washington began as a river-port city. The Potomac River connects through Chesapeake Bay to the Atlantic, allowing oceangoing ships to reach [[Alexandria]] and Georgetown. [[George Washington]] supported the Potomac Canal Company in the 1780s, and the later Chesapeake and Ohio Canal extended canal traffic from Georgetown toward Cumberland, Maryland. The canal operated until 1924 and became a recreational national park in 1971.

Railroads arrived in 1835, horse-drawn streetcars in 1860, and electric streetcars by the 1880s. Streetcars served the city and Maryland streetcar suburbs until buses replaced them in 1962. The Capital Beltway, a 64-mile interstate loop through Maryland and Virginia, became both a major highway and a political symbol through the phrase Inside the Beltway. The region is also served by Ronald Reagan Washington National Airport, Dulles International Airport, and Baltimore-Washington International Thurgood Marshall Airport.

## Government

Washington's [[The Forms Of Government Their Historical Development (Government)|government]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Government)) is unique because the U.S. Constitution gives Congress exclusive legislative authority over the national seat of government. Congress first granted a city government in 1802, with a president-appointed mayor and an elected council limited to taxpaying white male residents. Later changes allowed qualified male property owners to elect a mayor.

In 1871, Congress created a territorial government that joined Georgetown, the City of Washington, and Washington County under the District of Columbia. This system lasted only three years because of corruption charges and financial crises. The Organic Act of 1878 created a commissioner government. Later reforms gave residents a nonvoting delegate, the right to vote in presidential elections, and in 1974 limited home rule through an elected mayor and council under congressional oversight.

## Education

Washington's schools changed deeply after Brown v. Board of Education of Topeka in 1954 ended public-school segregation. Integration improved opportunities for many African American students, but it also encouraged middle-class white families to leave for suburbs or private schools. Later, poorly run schools led many middle-class African American families to leave for nearby Prince George's County.

The city also developed important selective and specialized schools, including the Duke Ellington School of the Arts, opened in 1974. Higher education is a major part of the city. Georgetown University dates from 1789, George Washington University from 1821, Catholic University of America from 1889, and Howard University became a leading historically black university. Research and learned institutions include the Brookings Institution, the Smithsonian Institution, the National Geographic Society, and the National Academy of Sciences.

## Literature

Washington's literature often focuses on national politics, government, and society. Henry Adams, who lived near Lafayette Square, wrote [[Democracy]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Democracy)) in 1886. Margaret Leech won a Pulitzer Prize for Reveille in Washington, 1860-1865, a study of Civil War life in the capital.

Other writers used the capital as a setting for political, historical, or crime stories. Gore Vidal wrote Washington, D.C. ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Washington%2C_D.C.)), Robert Coover wrote The Public Burning, and Joe Klein was later identified as the author of Primary Colors. George Pelecanos used the city in crime fiction, William Peter Blatty set The Exorcist in Georgetown, and Carl Bernstein and Bob Woodward wrote All the President's Men about Watergate.

## Music

Washington has made major contributions to American music. John Philip Sousa grew up in the city and led the U.S. Marine Band from 1880 to 1892. Duke Ellington, born in Washington, first developed as a jazz musician on U Street, and the city's jazz world also included Shirley Horn, Billy Taylor, Ramsey Lewis, Frank Weiss, and Pearl Bailey.

Washington also helped launch or shape later styles. Ruth Brown was performing in a D.C. club when she was signed by Atlantic Records. Emmylou Harris and Roberta Flack were discovered in Washington clubs, while Marvin Gaye began there before building fame elsewhere. Go-go, a bass- and percussion-heavy funk style pioneered by Chuck Brown and the Soul Searchers, became the city's most popular dance-hall music by the early 1980s. The city also shaped hardcore punk through Ian MacKaye, Minor Threat, Fugazi, and Dischord Records.

## Theatre

Washington's [[The Art of the Theatre (Theatre)|theatre]] tradition began early. The first legitimate theatre opened in 1800 in Blodgett's Hotel, and the National Theatre, opened in 1835, remained in operation. Ford's Theatre, opened in 1862, became a memorial as well as a theatre because Abraham Lincoln was assassinated there on April 14, 1865.

The John F. Kennedy Center for the Performing Arts opened in 1971 and became the city's major performing-arts centre. Other venues include Constitution Hall, the Folger Shakespeare Theatre, the Shakespeare Theatre Company, Arena Stage, the Lincoln Theatre, the Warner Theatre, the Studio Theatre, and the Gala Hispanic Theatre. Free military and summer performances also connect the arts to public space.

## History

Washington became the U.S. capital after years of debate over how to create a federal enclave. The Residence Act, passed on July 17, 1790, created a permanent seat of government. [[George Washington]] chose the Potomac site because it stood near Georgetown and Alexandria, linked North and South, and could connect the eastern seaboard with western territories. The federal territory was named the District of Columbia for Christopher Columbus, and the city was named for George Washington.

Andrew Ellicott surveyed the territory with help from Benjamin Banneker, a self-educated free black man. Construction of the Capitol, the presidential palace, and other buildings was nearly complete when Congress moved from [[Philadelphia]] in December 1800, but housing and services were still limited. During the War of 1812, British troops burned major public buildings in 1814, forcing rebuilding and testing the young capital.

During the Civil War, Washington stood close to the front because Richmond, the Confederate capital, was nearby. Lincoln's assassination at Ford's Theatre soon after the war ended brought a period of grief. After the war, the city slowly became a showplace. Territorial self-government in 1871 led to paved streets, schools, markets, lighting, sewers, and tree planting, though it also created financial problems. The Washington Monument, completed in 1884, and the Library of Congress, completed in 1897, helped mark the capital's growing dignity.

In the 20th century, Washington changed from a small Southern town into a major metropolis in the northeastern corridor. The subway's Metro Center station opened in 1976, and later revitalization reached Downtown, Chinatown, Anacostia, and areas damaged by the 1968 riots. Bicycle routes, crosstown buses, tourist ambassadors, neighbourhood pride, and expanded tourism beyond the Mall showed a city still guided by L'Enfant's plan while constantly changing.
