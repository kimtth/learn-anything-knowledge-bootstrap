# Mimicry

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> Mimicry is evolved resemblance in which a mimic, model, and receiver form a system of deceptive signaling. It can involve visual, acoustic, chemical, or behavioral cues among animals and plants, and differs from mere convergence because selection acts through the receiver's response.

## Introduction

![[attachments/mimicry_001.webp|420]]

*Batesian mimicry in butterflies*

Mimicry ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mimicry)) in biology is the superficial resemblance of two or more organisms that are not closely related. The resemblance gives an advantage, often protection from predators, because an animal or other receiver of signals mistakes one organism for another.

A mimicry system usually includes a mimic, a model, and a receiver. The receiver may be a predator, prey, symbiont, host, mate, or parasite host. Mimicry differs from ordinary convergence because selection acts through deception and information flow between signal sender and receiver.

Many cases are one-sided: the mimic benefits by resembling the model. Resemblance may involve appearance, sound, smell, behaviour, or chemistry, and it can occur between animals, between plants, or even between plants and animals.

Batesian mimicry ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Batesian_mimicry)) was explained by Henry W. Bates in 1862. Edible butterflies gained protection by resembling conspicuous, unpalatable butterflies. Predators that learned to avoid the protected model also avoided the harmless mimic.

Mullerian mimicry, explained by Fritz Muller in 1878, occurs when several noxious or inedible species share the same warning pattern. Each species benefits because the cost of educating predators is spread across the whole warning group.

Aggressive mimicry reverses the protective pattern. A predator, parasite, or plant resembles something useful to the victim, such as prey, a mate, a host egg, or a food signal, so the victim moves closer or accepts the mimic.

## Automimicry

![[attachments/mimicry_002.webp|420]]

*Mullerian mimicry diagram*

Automimicry occurs within a single species. Defenseless male bees and wasps may gain protection by resembling stinging females. Some butterflies contain toxins only if their larvae fed on poisonous plants, yet unprotected individuals may be avoided because predators learned from protected members of the same species.

Some mimicry systems combine several roles and are difficult to classify. Coral snakes and false coral snakes show how poisonous, mildly poisonous, and nonpoisonous species can share similar red, black, and yellow rings.

Warning systems often depend on secondary metabolites and other defensive chemicals. Plants store alkaloids, terpenes, resins, flavonols, and related compounds; animals may produce or store similar substances in glands or tissues. Milkweeds contain cardenolides, which protect monarch and queen butterflies after their larvae feed on them.

Protection is rarely absolute. Some birds can eat bees after removing stingers, rabbits can eat poisonous Amanita mushrooms, and some insects feed on dangerous plants. Still, warning signals reduce attacks by teaching predators to avoid costly or unpleasant prey.

Aposematic warning may use colour, smell, sound, or behaviour. Skunks combine black-and-white markings with a threat display; rattlesnakes warn with sound while remaining hidden from prey; some foul-tasting moths click in response to bat ultrasound. Edible moths may then mimic those clicks in Batesian fashion.

Warning signals work best when receivers learn quickly and the signal is clear. Many protected animals also combine concealment and warning, hiding bright flash colours until threatened, as tiger moths do with orange or yellow hind wings.

Batesian examples are widespread. Bees, wasps, hornets, ladybird beetles, and leaf beetles are copied by many harmless insects. The African swallowtail Papilio dardanus is especially important because females occur in several colour morphs, each mimicking a different inedible butterfly, while males remain nonmimetic.

Mullerian mimicry often forms mimicry rings of unrelated protected species. In tropical America, inedible butterflies from several nymphalid subfamilies share warning patterns, and some Heliconius species show matching geographic forms shaped by local mimicry rings.

Parasitic worms provide striking aggressive mimicry. The trematode Leucochloridium macrostomum infects snails and makes their eyestalks swollen, colourful, and pulsing. Birds mistake the eyestalks for insect larvae, eat them, and become the parasite's final host.

Insect-luring plants use similar deception. Parnassia flowers imitate glistening droplets, sundews lure and digest insects with sticky glandular drops, and pitcher plants use flowerlike markings, nectar, slippery surfaces, and digestive fluids to trap prey.

The cleaner-fish system shows aggressive mimicry among vertebrates. The cleaner wrasse Labroides dimidiatus removes parasites from reef fish and is protected by its recognizable colours and dance. The sabre-toothed blenny Aspidontus taeniatus imitates the cleaner, approaches trusting fish, and bites pieces from their fins.

## Fireflies

Some fireflies use aggressive mimicry entirely through behaviour. Male Photinus fireflies flash species-specific signals, and females reply with matching flashes. Predatory female Photuris fireflies imitate the reply of Photinus females, then seize and eat the approaching males.

The deception is precise. Photuris females adjust timing and brightness to resemble smaller Photinus females, while using a different signal when responding to males of their own species.

## Cuckoos

The European cuckoo is a brood parasite that lays eggs in the nests of small songbirds. Host birds often reject foreign eggs, so cuckoo eggs resemble the eggs of particular host species.

Each female cuckoo lays one colour pattern and usually parasitizes a matching host. A survey showed that matching cuckoo eggs were lost less often than mismatched ones, supporting the importance of host choice and egg mimicry. Egg colour is probably genetic, while correct host choice may be learned from the foster parents that raised the female cuckoo.

African whydahs and widow birds parasitize estrildid finches. Their eggs are plain white like host eggs, but their nestlings mimic the host's mouth markings, begging calls, head movements, juvenile plumage, and even food adaptations. Males also learn and use host songs, which helps prevent hybridization among similar whydah species.

## Orchids

![[attachments/mimicry_003.webp|420]]

*Orchid mimicry flower*

Plants may use mimicry for pollination and dispersal. Some orchids imitate nectar-bearing flowers without offering nectar, while Ophrys orchids imitate female bees or wasps so closely that males attempt to copulate with the flower.

The labellum of the orchid acts as a dummy female. During attempted mating, the male insect picks up pollinia and later carries them to another flower. Size, colour, surface texture, dark spots, movement, and odour all help guide the male into the correct position.

Odour is especially important because many female insects attract males with pheromones. Some Ophrys flowers produce odours similar to, or stronger than, those of the appropriate female insect.

Carrion flowers, stinkhorn mushrooms, and some mosses attract insects by imitating dung, rotting flesh, nectar-bearing flowers, or carrion odours. Blowflies, dung beetles, and other insects then pollinate flowers or disperse spores. The cuckoopint traps visitors long enough for pollen transfer before releasing them.

Some plants use defensive egg dummies. Passiflora and Streptanthus can produce marks that resemble butterfly eggs, causing females to avoid laying more eggs on the plant and reducing damage from larvae.

Mimicry can also occur within one species during reproduction. Male Corynopoma riisei fish wave a gill-cover stalk that females mistake for prey, bringing them close enough for mating. Male Haplochromis cichlids carry egglike spots on the anal fin; females trying to pick up the false eggs take in sperm that fertilizes the real eggs in the mouth.

## The Evolution of Mimicry

The mirror ophrys, Ophrys speculum, shows how detailed orchid mimicry can be. Its colouring, dark central shine, and resemblance to folded insect wings imitate the female insect closely enough to attract males.

The evolution of mimicry depends on receiver behaviour. Experiments with chickens and starlings show that predators can learn warning signals from punishment and may avoid mimics even when mimics are edible. A close resemblance can protect mimics even when they are fairly common.

Reconstructing mimicry requires knowledge of morphology, behaviour, ecology, and relationships among model, mimic, and receiver. Mimetic traits often begin as pre-existing features that only partly resemble a useful signal; natural selection then strengthens the resemblance.

The receiver's interests shape the system. Models may benefit, lose, or be unaffected. Parasites and hosts often enter evolutionary arms races: cuckoos that are too successful reduce the host nests they need, while hosts evolve better egg discrimination and more varied egg patterns.

Geographic overlap is useful but not always required; model and mimic mainly need a receiver in common. A migratory predator may connect species in different regions. Mimics also do not always need to be fewer than models, because negative learning experiences may be stronger than positive ones.

Mimicry became important for evolutionary theory during the Darwinian controversy. It provided strong evidence for Charles Darwin and Alfred Russel Wallace's theory of natural selection and remains a major way to study how signals acquire meaning.

## Key Events & Developments

- 1862: Henry W. Bates explained protective resemblance in butterflies, giving rise to Batesian mimicry ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Batesian_mimicry)).
- 1878: Fritz Muller explained why several defended species may share one warning pattern.
- 1930s and later: Experimental studies of predator learning helped test warning colours and mimic protection.
- 20th century: Detailed studies of cuckoos, whydahs, orchids, cleaner fish, fireflies, and cichlids expanded mimicry beyond visual resemblance.

## Key People

- Henry W. Bates ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henry_W._Bates)): Naturalist who explained how harmless species gain protection by resembling noxious models.
- Fritz Muller ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fritz_Muller)): Zoologist who explained shared warning patterns among inedible species.
- Charles Darwin ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_Darwin)): Evolutionary theorist whose natural selection was supported by mimicry studies.
- Alfred Russel Wallace ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alfred_Russel_Wallace)): Evolutionary theorist whose ideas on natural selection were strengthened by mimicry evidence.
- L. de Ruiter: Biologist whose stick-caterpillar experiments helped clarify mimicry, mimesis, and receiver behaviour.

## Interesting Facts

- Ophrys orchids can attract male insects by imitating female insects in shape, colour, texture, movement, and smell.
- Male Haplochromis fish use egglike fin spots to help fertilize eggs carried in the female's mouth.
