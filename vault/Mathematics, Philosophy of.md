# Mathematics, Philosophy of

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> Philosophy of mathematics asks how mathematical claims should be understood and whether numbers and other mathematical objects exist. Its debates among Platonism, nominalism, realism, and antirealism connect questions of meaning with metaphysics and mathematical knowledge.

## Introduction

![[attachments/mathematics_philosophy_001.webp|420]]

*Plato Academy mosaic*

Philosophy of mathematics ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philosophy_of_mathematics)) asks how ordinary mathematical sentences should be interpreted and whether abstract objects exist. Sentences such as 4 is even seem to make claims about numbers, but philosophers disagree about what numbers are and whether they exist.

The main divide is between realists, who think mathematical objects exist, and antirealists, who deny this. Some realists treat numbers as mental or physical objects, but mathematical Platonists treat them as abstract objects: nonphysical, nonmental, and outside space and time.

The semantic question and the metaphysical question are linked. If ordinary mathematics is best read as making true claims about abstract objects, then it becomes natural to believe that abstract objects exist. This issue goes back to [[Plato and Platonism (Plato)|Plato]] and [[Aristotle and Aristotelianism|Aristotle]] and remains unresolved after more than 2,000 years.

## Mathematical Platonism

Mathematical Platonism ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematical_Platonism)) says that abstract objects exist and that true mathematical sentences describe them. Abstract objects are usually defined as nonspatiotemporal, nonphysical, nonmental, unchanging, and causally inactive, though people can still have mental ideas of them.

For Platonists, [[Arithmetic|arithmetic]] describes the sequence of positive integers just as astronomy describes the solar system. Numbers, functions, sets, vectors, circles, and mathematical structures are all abstract objects or structures. Euclid's proof that infinitely many primes exist is treated as a discovery about such objects.

Classical Platonism was developed without major change for centuries. Gottlob Frege gave a powerful argument for it in the late 19th century, while Kurt Godel and Willard Van Orman Quine later offered explanations of how humans might know abstract objects without changing the basic view.

Nontraditional [[Plato and Platonism (Platonism)|Platonism]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Platonism)) emerged in the 1980s and 1990s. Penelope Maddy argued that sets are abstract yet located where their physical members are. Mark Balaguer and Edward Zalta defended full-blooded Platonism, where every consistently conceivable mathematical structure exists. Michael Resnik and Stewart Shapiro defended structuralism, where numbers are positions in patterns.

## Nominalism

Nominalism ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nominalism)) denies that mathematical objects such as numbers, sets, and circles exist. Nominalists allow that there are piles of three eggs and ideas of the number 3, but they deny that either is the number 3 itself.

Realistic anti-Platonism includes psychologism and physicalism. Psychologism treats mathematics as about mental objects, an idea associated with Edmund Husserl, L.E.J. Brouwer, and Arend Heyting. Physicalism treats mathematics as about physical objects, physical properties, or piles, with versions linked to [[Aristotle and Aristotelianism|Aristotle]], David Armstrong, and [[John Stuart Mill]].

Paraphrase nominalism says mathematical sentences do not really make claims about objects. Deductivism, rooted in David Hilbert and developed by Hilary Putnam and Geoffrey Hellman, reads 4 is even as meaning that if numbers existed, 4 would be even.

Fictionalism, first proposed by Hartry Field, reads mathematics at face value but says it is not literally true because abstract objects do not exist. Mathematics is like a useful fiction. Neo-Meinongianism, linked to Alexius Meinong and later figures such as Richard Sylvan, says mathematical sentences can be literally true even though their objects do not exist.

Logicism, intuitionism, and formalism arose from the search for secure foundations after Bertrand Russell discovered a contradiction in naive set theory in 1901. Logicism, developed by Frege, Russell, and Alfred North Whitehead, treats mathematics as logic. Intuitionism rejects nonconstructive proof and was developed by Brouwer, Heyting, and Michael Dummett. Metamathematical formalism treats mathematical statements as statements about formal sentences and axioms.

Arguments for [[Plato and Platonism (Platonism)|Platonism]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Platonism)), especially Fregean ones, claim that psychologism makes mathematics depend on human psychology, physicalism cannot handle infinite set theory or mathematical necessity, and paraphrase nominalism misreads ordinary mathematical discourse. Platonists argue that mathematical sentences are best interpreted as about objects that are neither mental nor physical.

The main remaining anti-Platonist challenge is fictionalism. Platonists answer with the indispensability argument, associated with Frege, Quine, and Putnam: mathematics must be true because it is woven into successful empirical science. Fictionalists such as Field, Balaguer, Gideon Rosen, and Stephen Yablo reply that science can be understood without literal mathematical truth or that useful false mathematics can still support accurate physical theories.

## The Epistemological Argument

The strongest argument against [[Plato and Platonism (Platonism)|Platonism]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Platonism)) is epistemological. Humans live in space-time, while abstract objects, if they exist, are outside space-time, so it is unclear how humans could know them.

Platonists answer in three ways. [[Plato and Platonism (Plato)|Plato]] and Godel deny that the mind is wholly physical, using recollection or mathematical intuition. Maddy denies that all abstract objects are outside space-time. Resnik and Shapiro appeal to pattern recognition, while full-blooded Platonism says mathematicians stipulate structures through axioms and then learn about them through proof.

The debate ends in an ongoing impasse. Platonism and fictionalism agree that mathematical sentences are best read as about abstract objects, but disagree about whether such objects exist. Traditional arguments have not forced agreement, though they continue to shape positions in the philosophy of mathematics.

## Core Equations

Philosophy of mathematics ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philosophy_of_mathematics)) uses symbols, sets, functions, and logical relations to examine mathematical structure:

$$f:X\to Y$$

This notation represents a function that maps each input from one set to an output in another set.

$$A\subseteq B \quad \Rightarrow \quad (x\in A \Rightarrow x\in B)$$

This states the meaning of subset: every member of A is also a member of B.

## Key Events & Developments

- More than 2,000 years ago: [[Plato and Platonism (Plato)|Plato]] and [[Aristotle and Aristotelianism|Aristotle]] framed the enduring dispute over abstract objects.
- 1901: Bertrand Russell discovered a contradiction in naive set theory, intensifying the search for foundations.
- The late 19th century: Gottlob Frege developed logicism and a major argument for mathematical Platonism.
- The 1980s and 1990s: New forms of Platonism appeared through Maddy, Balaguer, Zalta, Resnik, and Shapiro.

## Key People

- [[Plato and Platonism (Plato)|Plato]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plato)): Early source of the view that abstract objects exist independently.
- [[Aristotle and Aristotelianism|Aristotle]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aristotle)): Early critic of separate abstract objects and source of physicalistic property views.
- Gottlob Frege ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gottlob_Frege)): Founder of modern mathematical logic and major defender of Platonism and logicism.
- Bertrand Russell ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bertrand_Russell)): Exposed a contradiction in naive set theory and helped develop logicism.
- Hartry Field ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hartry_Field)): First proposed mathematical fictionalism.
- Paul Benacerraf ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_Benacerraf)): Formulated the influential epistemological challenge to Platonism in 1973.

## Interesting Facts

- Structuralism treats the number 4 as a position in the positive-integer pattern, like shortstop is a position in a baseball defense.
- Fictionalism compares arithmetic to a useful story: not literally true, but valuable and systematic.
- Neo-Meinongianism claims mathematical sentences can be literally true even when the objects they mention do not exist.
- Full-blooded Platonism says every internally consistent axiom system describes some part of the mathematical realm.
