# Medicine

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> Medicine seeks to maintain health and prevent, relieve, or cure disease through clinical care, scientific knowledge, and public-health systems. Its broad view of health includes physical, mental, and social well-being and therefore connects medical practice to sanitation, housing, nutrition, education, and social organization.

## Introduction

![[attachments/medicine_001.webp|420]]

*Portrait of Hippocrates*

Medicine ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medicine)) is the practice of maintaining health and preventing, easing, or curing disease. The World Health Organization's Alma-Ata declaration in 1978 defined health as complete physical, mental, and social well-being, not only the absence of illness, and treated it as a human right. That view made health care a social goal requiring education, economics, sanitation, food, housing, and community organization as well as doctors.

## Harold Scarborough

Primary health care ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Primary_health_care)) is first-contact care. In many developing countries it is often delivered by medical auxiliaries rather than fully qualified physicians because populations are large and needs are urgent. Wealthier countries also reconsidered the doctor's role because training and service costs rose, though industrial countries still usually expect a trained physician to provide first contact.

## Britain

![[attachments/medicine_002.webp|420]]

*Historical hospital ward*

Before 1948, British general practitioners worked where they could earn a living. Insured wage earners received free panel care, while many others paid fees directly. The National Health Service began in 1948 and gave everyone access to a registered general practitioner without direct payment, though doctors could still treat private patients not on their NHS lists.

## United States

![[attachments/medicine_003.webp|420]]

*Modern medicine and surgery painting*

In the [[United States of America (United States)|United States]], first-contact care became less uniform than in Britain. General practice declined in the second half of the 20th century, especially in cities, while family practice grew after the American medical organizations created a board for the specialty in 1969. Internists, pediatricians, obstetricians, emergency units, and clinics also shared first-contact work.

## Russia

[[Russia]] used general practitioners mainly in rural areas, while pediatricians, internists, and occupational physicians divided care elsewhere. Polyclinics treated many diseases and connected outpatient work with district hospitals. Small towns often had one polyclinic; large cities had separate adult, child, women's, mental health, and sexually transmitted disease clinics.

## Japan

[[Japan]] once had a strong tradition of self-treatment because pharmaceuticals were less restricted. Health insurance programs begun in 1961 increased visits to outpatient and private clinics. Western medical practice, adopted from the 1870s with strong German influence, encouraged university status and research careers, though private practice and hospital clinics also expanded.

## China

After the Cultural Revolution, [[China]] emphasized decentralized, local health care. Short-trained workers, preventive campaigns, community responsibility, and self-reliance helped serve rural areas where much of the population lived. The system valued equality over hierarchy and tried to narrow divisions between categories of health workers.

## India

[[India]] preserved strong traditional systems alongside modern medicine. Ayurvedic medicine, perhaps 3,000 years old, remained widely used for prevention and treatment, sometimes alone and sometimes with modern medicine. The Indian Medical Council, established in 1971, set educational standards and recognized systems such as Ayurvedic, Unani, and Siddha practice.

## Magnetic

Magnetic resonance imaging ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnetic_resonance_imaging)) shows how technology expanded medical diagnosis and required specialized expertise. Specialist physicians and surgeons often worked in teams, for example in heart disease, with nurses, technologists, and operating-room technicians. Such specialization improved service but raised institutional costs and consumed a larger share of health budgets.

## Teaching

Teaching hospitals attract newly qualified physicians seeking career experience and senior physicians seeking consultant, specialist, or professorial prestige. Medical schools commonly organize clinical professors in major specialties such as surgery, internal medicine, obstetrics, psychiatry, pediatrics, and radiology, linking patient care with education and research.

## Geriatrics

Geriatrics became more important as developed countries gained larger elderly populations from the mid-20th century. In Japan the elderly population grew especially fast in the late 20th century. In the United Kingdom, about one-third of hospital beds were occupied by patients over 65, many of them psychiatric patients, placing heavy demands on physicians and health services.

## Public Health in Britain

British public health services are organized locally under the National Health Service. A medical officer of health advises the local council, supervises sanitary inspectors, enforces laws on food, drugs, milk, dairies, factories, and nuisances, and responds to notifiable infectious diseases. Duties also include child welfare, school health, vaccination, and environmental health.

## Public Health in the United States

Public health in the [[United States of America (United States)|United States]] is divided among federal, state, county, and city governments. The Public Health Service, surgeon general, state health departments, boards of health, and local physicians all have roles. State codes guide county and city rules, while local health departments inspect, regulate, control communicable disease, collect vital statistics, and provide clinics.

## Public Health in Japan

[[Japan]]'s Ministry of Health and Welfare directs national public health while prefectures and large municipalities operate health centers. Programs combine preventive medicine, medical care, welfare, insurance, maternal and child health, communicable-disease control, health education, family planning, health statistics, food inspection, and sanitation, with private physicians helping through local medical associations.

## Former Soviet Union

In the former Soviet Union, public health had been centrally directed by the Ministry of Health through the 15 republic ministries. After dissolution, responsibility passed to successor governments. The Soviet model had little local autonomy, with republics, provinces, districts, and cities carrying out central plans for medical institutions and public health services.

## Surgery

Surgery develops differently from drug research because clinicians often play a central role in new operations, though laboratory scientists, engineers, chemists, and materials specialists also contribute. Advances in anesthesia, machines, drugs, alloys, plastics, and artificial joints made many operations possible. When practical, new procedures are tested on animals first, especially when they involve replacement or transplantation.

## Key Events & Developments

- 1948: Britain began the National Health Service and changed access to general practitioners.
- 1961: [[Japan]]'s health insurance programs increased use of clinics and physicians.
- 1969: [[United States of America (United States)|United States]] family practice was recognized as a specialty.
- 1978: The World Health Organization Alma-Ata declaration treated health as a human right and social goal.

## Key People

- Harold Scarborough ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harold_Scarborough)): Named editor or contributor associated with the primary-care discussion.
- World Health Organization ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_Health_Organization)): Framed health care planning through the Alma-Ata declaration.
- Indian Medical Council ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_Medical_Council)): Set standards and recognized Indian traditional medical systems.

## Interesting Facts

- Health is defined in the Alma-Ata declaration as physical, mental, and social well-being, not merely absence of disease.
- Family practice grew in the United States partly because older general practice declined.
- Medical specialization improves technical service but also raises hospital and health-budget costs.
