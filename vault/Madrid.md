# Madrid

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> Madrid is Spain's capital and a major administrative, financial, and arts center on the central Iberian plateau. Its selection by Philip II, high-elevation climate, historic core, later boulevards and institutions, and central position made it the political heart of modern Spain.

## Introduction

Madrid is the capital of Spain and of Madrid province, as well as a major arts, financial, and administrative center in central Spain. Its rise as capital reflected the centralizing policy of Philip II and later kings, but its selection also came from its earlier obscurity and neutrality rather than from river access, mineral wealth, pilgrimage importance, or obvious strategic advantages. Philip II moved the court there in 1561, and Philip III later made it the official national capital.

The city lies near the geographic center of the Iberian Peninsula on the Meseta, about 2,120 feet above sea level, making it one of Europe's highest capitals. Its site near the Sierra de Guadarrama helps produce cold, windy winters, hot dry summers, sharp temperature changes, and relatively low rainfall. Spring and fall are the most comfortable seasons.

Madrid's layout joins an old center of tight streets near the Plaza Mayor with Neoclassical buildings, boulevards, modern offices, apartment districts, parks, and newer housing areas. The city long felt crowded because early residents avoided lodging duties by building low or deceptive houses, while later public buildings, convents, and walls limited space. The Manzanares River was not crossed by urban growth until 1948, yet the city has large parks such as El Pardo, Casa de Campo, and Retiro.

The population grew through migration, especially during industrial expansion in the 1950s and 1960s. Madrilenos, nicknamed gatos, are associated with late hours, street life, cultural activity, and strong attachment to the city. Madrid's economy rests on government, finance, insurance, tourism, transport, services, publishing, the stock market, and manufacturing that expanded after the Spanish Civil War.

## Transportation

Road and rail systems converge on Madrid from across Spain. The Metro serves the city, Barajas Airport lies about 8 miles east, motorways encircle and radiate from the capital, and buses and suburban trains serve commuters. Road programs from the 1960s favored cars so strongly that some overpasses were later removed.

Democratic Spain and regional autonomy brought stronger local consultation. In 1982, a public survey led to the General Ordinance Plan, which aimed to direct growth, modernize services, and improve quality of life. Madrid remains Spain's governmental focus, with the Supreme Court, ministries, Cortes Generales, military offices, and a bishopric. Its cultural life includes theatres, film, music, universities, publishing, bullfighting at Las Ventas, football clubs Real Madrid and Club Atletico de Madrid, fiestas, zarzuela, museums, libraries, bookshops, and the spring book fair.

The Prado Museum holds royal art collections shaped by Spanish alliances and dynastic tastes, with Venetian, Flemish, French, Spanish, and other works. Other institutions include the Queen Sofia Museum, whose collection includes Pablo Picasso's Guernica ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guernica)), the Thyssen-Bornemisza Museum, the National Archaeological Museum, the Royal Academy museum, the Museum of the Americas, the National Library, and the Royal Palace library.

## Universities

The Polytechnical University belongs to Madrid's higher-education concentration in Ciudad Universitaria, alongside the Open University and the Complutense University, while the Autonomous University stands to the north.

## History

![[attachments/madrid_003.webp|420]]

*Plaza Mayor in Madrid*

The early Arab town grew around an alcazar above the Manzanares River. The name Majerit appears in AD 932, when Ramiro II of Leon destroyed the walls, and Alfonso VI of Castile and Leon captured the town from Muslims in 1083. Madrid remained small, but kings visited, the Cortes met there by 1309, and medieval streets around the Calle Mayor, Calle de Segovia, and Moreria preserved older patterns.

Habsburg Madrid expanded after the court settled there. Population reached about 60,000 by 1598 and about 100,000 by 1656. Buildings and works from the period included the foreign ministry, Casa de Cisneros, Segovia Bridge, San Isidro el Real, and the Plaza Mayor, designed by Juan Gomez de Mora and built between 1617 and 1619. The plaza hosted markets, bullfights, plays, fireworks, Inquisition ceremonies, and public punishments.

Under the Bourbon kings, especially Philip V and Charles III, Madrid gained the Royal Palace, academies, libraries, the Puerta de Alcala, Puerta del Sol's symbolic zero-kilometre point, the Botanic Garden, improved avenues, and later the Prado. The city endured French occupation during the Napoleonic Wars; the May 2, 1808 uprising began Spain's War of Independence. In the 19th century, mayors and planners introduced street numbers, lighting, refuse collection, new boulevards, the Salamanca district, and the 1860 Plan Castro, though speculation and population growth frustrated orderly expansion.

## Modern Madrid

Modern plans included Arturo Soria's 1892 linear city and Nunez Granes's 1910 satellite-city idea, neither of which gained support. The Gran Via, begun in 1910, became a lively main street of cinemas, shops, banks, and coffeehouses. During and after the Civil War, many streets were renamed, including Gran Via as Avenida Jose Antonio, though most names returned after Franco's death.

The 1931 Republic extended roads and the Paseo de la Castellana, but the Spanish Civil War brought two years of bombing and combat near University City. Afterward, Madrid expanded rapidly, reaching 205 square miles between 1948 and 1951. The 1960s brought sprawl, speculation, industry, and damage to heritage, while laws in the 1980s and 1990s protected important buildings and encouraged preservation.

Late-20th-century projects included new housing, a National Music Auditorium in 1990, cultural institutions, a new railway station, and the Queen Sofia Museum. Almudena Cathedral, begun in 1883, was consecrated by John Paul II in 1993. Madrid was European Capital of Culture in 1992. On March 11, 2004, Islamist militants detonated 10 train bombs, killing 191 people and injuring about 1,800, yet the city remained a major tourist, political, and cultural center.
