# Jesus - The Christ and Christology (Jesus Christ)

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> Jesus of Nazareth was a Jewish teacher executed in Roman Jerusalem about AD 30 and regarded by Christians as the incarnate Christ. The historical study of his life interprets New Testament sources alongside Jewish, Roman, and early Christian contexts, while his significance anchors Christian belief and practice.

## Introduction

![[attachments/jesus_christ_001.webp|420]]

*Mosaic of Jesus Christ in Hagia Sophia*

Jesus of Nazareth, also called Jesus of Galilee, was a Jewish religious leader born shortly before the death of Herod the Great and executed at Jerusalem about AD 30. Most Christians regard him as the incarnation of God. The New Testament records his teaching and deeds, but those writings are theological documents, so the historical Jesus must be reconstructed through Jewish, Roman, and early Christian context.

The name Christ began as a title, not a surname. It comes from Greek christos, translating Hebrew meshiah, or Messiah, meaning anointed one. Early followers believed Jesus was the anointed Davidic figure expected by some Jews to restore Israel. In the New Testament, especially in Paul, title and name soon fused into Jesus Christ, Christ Jesus, or simply Christ.

The basic life outline is that Jesus was a Galilean from Nazareth, legally son of Joseph and born of Mary according to Matthew and Luke. Joseph and Jesus were described as craftsmen. Jesus was baptized by John the Baptist, became an itinerant preacher and healer, attracted notice during a brief public career, went to Jerusalem for Passover between AD 29 and AD 33, was arrested and crucified, and was then proclaimed alive by his followers.

## The Historical Setting

The infancy material is limited. Matthew and Luke place Jesus' birth at Bethlehem and connect him with Davidic descent, but little is known about childhood. Luke alone portrays him as precociously learned as a child. The source of historical knowledge is therefore not a full biography but a small body of traditions centred on public ministry, death, and the belief in resurrection.

Jewish Palestine in Jesus' lifetime belonged to the Roman Empire. When Jesus was born, Herod ruled Jewish Palestine as Rome's client king. After Herod's death, his realm was divided. Herod Antipas governed Galilee and Peraea, while Judaea, Idumaea, and Samaria became a Roman province in AD 6 under a prefect supported by a small army.

Pontius Pilate was Roman prefect during Jesus' public career, but daily government in Jerusalem rested largely with the high priest Caiaphas and his council. Roman troops came to Jerusalem during pilgrimage festivals because Passover, Weeks, and Booths combined crowds, national memory, and the possibility of revolt. Caiaphas had to mediate between Roman power and a population hostile to pagan rule.

Jewish and Gentile territories were close enough for trade and language contact, yet Jewish areas resisted pagan culture. Palestinian Jews mainly spoke Aramaic, though some merchants knew Greek. Rome generally allowed Jews to keep Sabbath, dietary law, Temple support, circumcision, and monotheistic customs, and before the First Jewish Revolt of AD 66-74 it did not colonize Jewish Palestine.

Economic conditions were mixed but not uniquely desperate. Galilee was relatively prosperous because land and climate supported harvests and sheep, while public works gave employment. Poverty existed and the rich-poor gap was distressing, but compared with many ancient regions it was not extreme. Jewish aristocrats and Temple suppliers could become prosperous, while most people produced food or clothing.

## First-Century Judaism

First-century Judaism was based on one God, one Jerusalem Temple, and Torah. The law regulated worship, diet, circumcision, Sabbath rest, pilgrimage, sacrifice, purity, recompense, and ordinary ethical dealings such as honest weights and love of fellow Jews and foreigners. Unlike many ancient religions, Judaism made daily life as well as ritual a matter of divine concern.

Jewish diversity existed inside shared commitments. The Pharisees, about 6,000 in Herod's time, were respected interpreters of law and believed in resurrection and traditions of the fathers. The Essenes, about 4,000, included the strict Qumran community linked with the Dead Sea Scrolls. The Sadducees were mainly aristocratic priests and leading laymen who rejected resurrection and held power around the Temple.

Jewish hope usually expected God to intervene in history and restore Israel to peace, freedom, and prosperity. Some expected a Davidic Messiah, while others expected angelic or divine action without a messianic king. Figures such as John the Baptist, Theudas, the Egyptian, Honi the Circle Drawer, Hanina ben Dosa, and apocalyptic writers show the range of prophets, healers, sages, and visionaries in Jesus' world.

## The Sources

The main sources for Jesus' life are the New Testament Gospels, especially the Synoptic Gospels: Mark, Matthew, and Luke. Mark was written about AD 60-80, while Matthew, Luke, and John followed about AD 75-90. Paul's letters, beginning around AD 50, are earlier but give only limited facts, chiefly that Jesus was crucified, raised, and appeared to followers.

Noncanonical sources add little secure evidence. The Gospel of Judas and Gospel of Thomas are 2nd-century Gnostic texts with theology far from the Synoptic picture of Jesus, where Jewish eschatology is central. Some individual sayings may preserve old material, but apocryphal gospels are usually unreliable for reconstructing Jesus' historical life.

Roman and Jewish references are brief. Suetonius mentions disturbances connected with Chrestus, and Tacitus says Christians followed Christus, executed by Pilate. Josephus wrote about Jesus, but Christian scribes heavily revised the paragraph. These notices confirm early Christian presence more than they independently explain Jesus.

The Synoptics are preferred over John for historical teaching. In Matthew, Mark, and Luke, Jesus teaches about the kingdom of God in aphorisms and parables drawn from village life, seldom making himself the main subject. In John, Jesus gives long discourses about himself, performs signs to validate claims, and appears to have a ministry lasting more than two years rather than less than one.

Historical reconstruction is difficult because the Gospels are theological works built from short units, or pericopes, rearranged for teaching. Original contexts of sayings are often unknown, and early Christians did not sharply distinguish the earthly Jesus from the heavenly Lord. Tests such as multiple attestation and material against a Gospel's editorial tendency can give broad confidence, but not detailed biography.

## Ministry and the Kingdom

The context of Jesus' ministry was Jewish eschatology. John the Baptist preached repentance before imminent judgment, and Jesus' baptism by John shows at least partial agreement. Jesus called twelve disciples, apparently symbolizing restored Israel's twelve tribes, proclaimed the kingdom of God, predicted the Temple's destruction, entered Jerusalem on a donkey, and expected a final meal in God's kingdom.

The kingdom of God could be described as heavenly, partly present in Jesus' words and deeds, or gradually growing like leaven. Yet the main Synoptic emphasis is eschatological: God's kingdom would come to earth in power, God's will would be done on earth as in heaven, and a heavenly Son of Man would come with judgment and redemption.

Hebrew prophetic texts shaped this hope. The day of the Lord in prophets such as Amos, Zephaniah, Joel, and Obadiah pointed to judgment, while Daniel 7 pictured beastly kingdoms destroyed and a Son of Man receiving dominion. Jesus' language about the Son of Man seems to draw from these traditions, though whether he meant himself or another figure is uncertain.

## Entering the Kingdom

Paul expected the Lord to descend from heaven, the dead in Christ to rise, and living believers to meet him in the air. Paul's letters are earlier than the Gospels and expect most contemporaries still to be alive at Jesus' return. The Synoptic wording that some standing there would not taste death probably shows that the expectation had begun to soften by the time the Gospels were written.

Inclusion in the kingdom required attention to God rather than possessions, but not necessarily formal loyalty to Jesus in every case. Jesus called some people to leave everything and follow him, yet much of his teaching stressed returning to God, loving others, becoming like children, and the reversal by which the poor, meek, lowly, sinners, customs officers, and prostitutes could enter before the powerful.

Jesus' social message had two sides. He and his disciples were not destitute; some came from families with boats and houses. Yet he favoured those below or near his social level and promised that the last would be first. He may have imagined a transformed society in which relationships and even property were reorganized under God's rule.

## Self-Understanding

Jesus' self-understanding is clearer in mission than in titles. Evidence that he directly claimed titles such as Messiah, Son of God, Son of David, or heavenly Son of Man is uneven. He did not make titles central. He seems to have thought he was God's final emissary, that he and the disciples would rule in the coming kingdom, and that those who accepted his message would share in it.

Jesus and Jewish law cannot be reduced to simple opposition. Some Gospel passages, especially in the Sermon on the Mount, make him insist on strict law observance. Others show him less strict about Sabbath healing or purity debates. These disputes fit normal 1st-century Jewish legal disagreement. Jesus accepted Mosaic purification for lepers and probably did not directly abolish food laws, though Mark later inferred that all foods were clean.

Autonomy made Jesus suspicious to some observers. Legal disputes normally occurred between recognized schools, but Jesus interpreted the law on his own authority and defended himself from Scripture. He was not unique in acting from a personal sense of God's will, but an autonomous teacher with followers could appear unpredictable and dangerous.

## Ethics

Jesus' ethics demanded complete devotion to God above self and family. In Matthew, observance of law became inward as well as outward: hatred and lust were wrong alongside murder and adultery. His moral perfectionism fits his eschatological expectation, because if divine intervention was near, people had to be blameless only for a short time before the Son of Man arrived.

Perfectionism became difficult when Christian life continued beyond the expected short interval. Paul repeated Jesus' prohibition of divorce but made an exception for a Christian abandoned by an unbelieving spouse. Matthew's disciples react to Jesus' strict view of divorce by saying it might be better not to marry. Such developments show how an interim ethic had to be adapted to continuing society.

## Miracles

Miracles ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miracle)) made Jesus known as healer and exorcist, but in the 1st century healers and miracle workers were not automatically viewed as divine or messianic. The issue was not whether miracles could happen, but by what power. Opponents accused Jesus of casting out demons by the prince of demons; he answered that he acted by the spirit of God.

Crowds were the historical consequence of Jesus' reputation. They helped him spread his message, but they also drew people seeking cures and created political risk. Herod Antipas had executed John the Baptist partly because his crowds suggested possible uprising. Jesus did not attack Judaism's core, but crowds, social reversal, welcome to sinners, and autonomous legal teaching made him suspect in Galilee.

Scribes and Pharisees challenged Jesus because they were legal experts. Scribes drafted documents and knew law; Pharisees were a party devoted to resurrection and ancestral legal traditions. The Gospels differ in naming Jesus' Galilean opponents, but both groups likely scrutinized him. Their opposition did not directly cause his death; mortal danger came in Jerusalem.

## The Last Week

Jesus' last week began when he went to Jerusalem for Passover about AD 30. He entered on a donkey, recalling Zechariah 9:9, and his followers hailed him with royal or Davidic language. The demonstration was probably small, because a large one in festival Jerusalem would likely have caused immediate arrest.

The Temple action was decisive. Jesus taught near the Temple, predicted its destruction, and overturned tables where coins were exchanged and pigeons bought for offerings. Chief priests and scribes then planned his death. At the Passover meal, he interpreted bread and wine as his body and covenant blood and said he would not drink wine again until God's kingdom came.

Arrest and trial followed betrayal by Judas Iscariot. Jesus was taken to Caiaphas, where a charge about threatening the Temple failed. Caiaphas then questioned him about being Christ and Son of God; the Gospel answers differ. Caiaphas declared blasphemy, but the charge sent to Pilate likely framed Jesus as king of the Jews.

The execution is historically understandable. Pilate probably did not agonize as later Gospel portraits suggest; other evidence depicts him as cruel and quick to execute. Jesus was crucified as a would-be king and mocked as one who would destroy and rebuild the Temple. Caiaphas and Pilate acted to prevent unrest in a pilgrim-filled city where talk of kingdom, Temple destruction, and liberation was dangerous.

## The Resurrection

The Resurrection claim changed history in a way Jesus may not have anticipated. Followers said they saw him after death, but accounts differ. Matthew emphasizes appearances to women and disciples connected with Galilee; Luke and Acts place events in and near Jerusalem; John includes Jerusalem and Galilee; Paul lists appearances that do not align closely with the Gospel accounts.

The risen Jesus is not portrayed simply as a revived corpse or a ghost. Luke says disciples did not recognize him at first and that he could disappear and reappear, while Paul speaks of a transformed spiritual body that is still a body. The accounts are inconsistent, but the inconsistency makes deliberate fraud less likely, and some witnesses were willing to die for the belief.

## The Passion

Artistic Passion imagery later fixed Jesus' death and burial in Christian memory, but the earliest sources are already centred on those events. The Gospels culminate in the Passion, and Paul treats Jesus' death and resurrection as the core of proclamation. The historical detail is uncertain, yet the general conviction that followers experienced Jesus as risen is secure.

## The Christ of Faith

Early Christian faith was not a late addition to a simple biography. Before the Gospels were written, believers were already worshipping, witnessing, confessing, and interpreting Jesus. The Jesus of history is reached only through the Christ of faith, because the Gospel narratives themselves were shaped by early Christian belief and worship.

## Creed

The Apostles' Creed is a later form, but its Christological core has very early ancestry, perhaps reaching toward the 1st century. Creeds summarized what Christians meant in worship. To say I believe or We believe about God and Christ meant that the confession rested on faith, not only observation.

## Preexistence

Early theology distinguished three stages in Jesus Christ's career: preexistence with the Father, Incarnation and humiliation, and glorification beginning with Resurrection. John identified Christ as the Word through whom all things were made. Colossians and Hebrews also present Christ as preexistent and above creation. Worship of Christ as divine preceded fully developed theological vocabulary.

Jesus, Christ, and Son of God became closely linked. Jesus meant Yahweh saves; Christ was originally the Messiah title; and Son of God became a universal New Testament confession. Baptism, Transfiguration, Resurrection, and later Trinitarian doctrine all deepened the special meaning of sonship. The title opposed both Jewish rejection of divine sonship and Greek myths of many divine offspring.

## The Lord

Lord, or Kyrios, expressed early Christian claims about Jesus against several audiences. It denied many lordly beings, denied Caesar's ultimate lordship, and claimed that Yahweh had come in Jesus Christ to establish the new covenant. Sometimes Lord stressed the risen Christ; sometimes Old Testament echoes stressed preexistence. Eventually it became a common name of devotion.

Incarnation and humiliation were confessed in the creed through conception by the Holy Spirit and birth from the Virgin Mary. This asserted both the true humanity of Christ and the supernatural origin of that humanity. The Passion section of the creed then moved quickly to suffering under Pontius Pilate, crucifixion, death, and burial, because early Christianity saw these events as the means by which God saved humanity.

Atonement language in the New Testament used many metaphors: sacrifice, ransom, new life, example in suffering, Second Adam, and the revelation of God's love. The descent into hell entered the creed late, drawing especially on 1 Peter. It came to mean Christ's entry into the dead, rescue of faithful souls, or proclamation of victory over hell, but it was not central to earliest apostolic preaching.

## Glorification

Glorification began with the Resurrection. The New Testament assumes rather than argues it, treating it as God's witness to the Son, the basis of Christian hope after death, and the ground of a new life. Differences among Gospel and Pauline resurrection traditions show variety, but also how universal resurrection faith was from the beginning.

Ascension and session expressed Christ's presence with the Father between Resurrection and Second Advent. Luke-Acts narrates the Ascension directly, while some Pauline texts may identify Resurrection and Ascension. Sitting at God's right hand drew on Psalm 110:1 and meant elevation or restoration to honour. The creed's final expectation was that Christ would come to judge living and dead and reign without end.

## Nicaea

Ancient councils turned germinal New Testament and creedal beliefs into dogma because teachings arose that seemed to threaten Christian confession. Adoptionism guarded humanity but treated Jesus as adopted Son; docetism protected divinity from matter but denied real body; modalism lost personal distinction from the Father; subordinationism made the Logos less than the supreme God.

Arius forced the issue by teaching that the Logos was the first creature, divine in a lesser sense but not God. Constantine convened Nicaea in 325. The council, following the position associated with Athanasius, declared Christ begotten not made, creator not creature, and of the same essence, or homoousios, with the Father.

## Constantinople

Constantinople ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constantinople)) in 381 clarified Trinitarian language after Nicaea created new controversies. The formula of one divine essence and three divine persons distinguished Father, Son, and Holy Spirit while affirming equality in eternity and power. This protected Nicaea from the charge of modalism and prepared later debate about how divine and human are joined in Christ.

Ephesus and Chalcedon addressed the relation of two natures in one person. Alexandria stressed Christ's divine unity and risked absorbing humanity; Antioch stressed true humanity and risked dividing Christ. Nestorius objected to calling Mary Theotokos, God-Bearer, and Cyril of Alexandria charged that he divided Christ. Ephesus in 431 did not settle the matter fully.

## Chalcedon

Chalcedon ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chalcedon)) in 451 used Leo I's Tome to affirm one and the same Son, perfect in deity and humanity, in two natures without mixture, change, division, or separation. It preserved both Alexandrian concern for unity and Antiochian concern for distinct humanity. Most of the church has treated this as the basic statement of Christ's person.

Later Eastern controversies over Monophysite and Monothelite interpretations tried to clarify Chalcedon. The resulting doctrine affirmed Christ's oneness with God, oneness with humanity, and one person. The source notes that liturgy had preserved this faith before theologians found precise vocabulary, so Chalcedon was both development and articulation of older worship.

## Western and Modern Christology

Western theology then focused on Christ's work. [[Augustine]] stressed Jesus' humanity as the assurance of salvation: the link between human physical nature and God's spiritual nature, the sacrifice humanity offered to God, and the foundation of a new humanity in Christ as the old humanity began in Adam.

Medieval atonement used many images: ransom from Satan, victory over the Devil, sacrifice offered to God, patient example, and divine love. Anselm of Canterbury gave the most influential synthesis in Cur Deus homo. Sin violated God's honour; only a truly human life of infinite worth could satisfy it; Jesus' life and death were valid for humans because he was human and for all humans because he was God.

Medieval devotion joined dogma and mysticism. Bernard of Clairvaux expressed union with Christ, and Francis of Assisi emphasized imitation of Jesus' humility and obedience. This renewed appreciation of Jesus' humanity influenced art, as painters such as Giotto moved away from highly stylized Byzantine images toward more human portrayals.

The Reformation remained conservative about Christ's person and work while relating Christology to justification by grace through faith. [[Luther]] revived victory language for atonement and defended Christ's real presence in the Lord's Supper by saying divine omnipresence was communicated to Christ's humanity. [[Calvin and Calvinism (Calvin)|Calvin]] rejected both Luther's and Zwingli's Eucharistic theories but held firmly to ancient dogma.

The threefold office became Protestantism's chief constructive contribution. Christ as prophet fulfilled and continued the prophetic word; Christ as priest ended sacrifice by being both priest and victim and continues intercession; Christ as king fulfilled royal expectation and rules through appointed means. This scheme preserved the complexity of biblical and patristic images.

Modern debate widened the gap between Reformers and many later Protestants. Michael Servetus and the Socinians first attacked nonbiblical terms while emphasizing Jesus as moral example. The Enlightenment made historical study of Jesus indispensable, gradually questioning miracles, Virgin Birth, Resurrection, Second Advent, and vicarious atonement.

Nineteenth-century criticism separated the Jesus of history from the Christ of faith. David Friedrich Strauss treated Gospel portraits as myths, while Ernest Renan wrote a psychological life of Jesus. Many scholars sought the religion of Jesus rather than the religion about Jesus, influenced by Kantian ethics, Hegelian history, natural causality, evolution, and Adolf von Harnack's call to recover Christianity's essence.

Twentieth-century scholarship transformed the debate again. Johannes von Weiss and Albert Schweitzer argued that Jesus expected the imminent end and taught an interim ethic. Form criticism studied Gospel units such as parables, sayings, miracles, and Passion stories as products of oral community tradition, making old-style biography impossible but not eliminating historical knowledge.

Modern churches remained divided less by official Christological formulas than by how far historical criticism could go. Karl Barth, Lionel Thornton, Karl Heim, and Karl Adam sought modern restatements of classical doctrine. Many Protestants tolerated critical inquiry, while Roman Catholic Modernism was condemned in 1907-1910, though Catholic scholars still pursued critical literary study within limits.

- 1907-1910 Modernism crisis: Roman Catholic authority set limits on critical theological use of historical methods.

## Interesting Facts

- Christ was originally a title meaning Messiah, but it became part of Jesus' name very early.
- The Gospels give very little information about Jesus' childhood, education, travel, or personality.
- Galilee was probably more prosperous than a simple picture of desperate poverty suggests.
- Most Jews in Jesus' Palestine did not belong to Pharisee, Essene, or Sadducee parties.
- Miracles ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miracle)) in Jesus' world did not by themselves prove divinity or messiahship.
- The Resurrection stories disagree in details, yet all preserve the early conviction that Jesus was transformed and alive.
- The crucifix reinforced Western satisfaction atonement theology and was reinforced by it.
- Modern Christology is divided partly by how much historical criticism churches permit around Jesus' person.
