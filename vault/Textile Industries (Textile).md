# Textile Industries (Textile)

## Introduction

![[attachments/textile_001.webp|420]]

*Spinning jenny textile machine*

Textiles are fibres, filaments, yarns, fabrics, cloths, and related structures made by weaving, knitting, bonding, felting, tufting, braiding, netting, lace making, embroidery, and similar processes. The word comes from Latin and French roots meaning to weave, but it now covers far more than woven cloth.

Textiles include threads, cords, ropes, nets, braids, lace, embroidery, woven fabrics, knitted fabrics, bonded materials, felts, and some paperlike products with fabric properties. Textile production converts fibres into yarns, yarns into fabrics, and fabrics into finished goods for clothing, household, industrial, and technical uses.

## Early Development

Textile structures came from ancient handicrafts and modern invention. Early forms included nets made from one thread by repeated looping and basketry made by interlacing reeds, cane, and other flexible materials.

Prehistoric textiles are rare because fabrics perish easily, but evidence of weaving related to basketry dates to about 5000 BCE. Cotton, silk, wool, and flax were early textile fibres, and weaving probably preceded fully developed yarn spinning.

## Spinning

Spinning draws out and twists fibres into continuous yarn or thread. It is essential for fibres that are not naturally long enough to be woven directly.

Early spinning used a distaff to hold fibres and a weighted spindle to twist the drawn fibre. The spinner repeatedly whirled the spindle, drew out fibre, wound the new yarn, and repeated the process.

The spinning wheel, invented in India and introduced to Europe in the Middle Ages, mechanized twisting and winding. The Saxony wheel in the 16th century allowed continuous spinning of coarse wool and cotton yarn.

John Kay's flying shuttle increased loom output and created demand for faster spinning. James Hargreaves's spinning jenny, patented in 1770, used multiple spindles, while Richard Arkwright and later inventors further mechanized yarn production.

## Looms and Weaving

![[attachments/textile_002.webp|420]]

*Power loom weaving machine*

The earliest evidence of a loom, from about 4400 BCE in Egypt, shows a horizontal two-bar loom. Warp threads were stretched between bars, and lease rods separated warp yarns to form a shed through which weft yarn passed.

The heddle rod was a major improvement because it raised alternate warp threads quickly and accurately. Primitive looms also included warp-weighted looms and vertical two-bar looms.

The shaft loom handled plain and simple patterned fabrics, while the drawloom handled complex figured fabrics requiring many warp-thread combinations. Drawlooms used cords operated by a drawboy to raise selected warp groups.

French inventors worked to automate selection. Basile Bouchon used perforated paper in 1725, later systems improved the mechanism, and Joseph-Marie Jacquard introduced the Jacquard attachment in 1804-05.

The Jacquard loom used punched cards to control warp threads automatically, allowing intricate figures and even large woven pictures. It became one of the most important steps toward programmable mechanical control.

John Kay's flying shuttle, patented in 1733, was a decisive step in loom automation. Later power looms and shuttleless looms increased speed and reduced problems of noise, danger, vibration, power consumption, and wear.

## Basic Weaves

![[attachments/textile_003.webp|420]]

*Basic textile weaving diagram*

Plain weave, or tabby, is the simplest and most common weave. Weft yarn passes over and under alternate warp yarns, then reverses on the next row. Balanced plain weave can be strong because warp and weft interlace frequently.

Plain weave variations include taffeta and poplin, where different warp and weft sizes create ribs. Extended tabby uses two or more warps or wefts together.

Other basic weaves include twills and satins. Twill produces diagonal lines by shifting interlacings, while satin reduces visible interlacings to create smooth, lustrous surfaces.

## Knitting

Knitting forms fabric by interlooping yarns. Warp knitting uses many parallel yarns, while weft knitting usually builds loops across the fabric.

Raschel knitting uses coarser yarns and guide bars that feed yarn to needles moving in a trick plate. Raschel fabrics are often open and coarse and can include crochet-like pillars joined by zigzag inlays.

Tricot is a warp knit made with two sets of threads, producing fine vertical ribs on the face and horizontal ribs on the back. Tricot machines make lightweight fabrics and can create plain or patterned knits.

Warp knitting expanded through inlaying, in which nonknitted threads add colour, density, texture, or structural support. Co-We-Nit machines produce fabrics with properties of both woven and knitted cloth.

## Lace, Felt, Bonding, and Laminating

Machine lace making grew from efforts to imitate handmade lace, including Brussels lace. Bobbinet and related machines arranged threads to create netlike and decorative open fabrics.

Felts are fibrous structures formed when wool, fur, or some hair fibres interlock under heat, moisture, and pressure. Felt is among the earliest textile goods and is still made for clothing, shelter, hats, rolls, sheets, and industrial uses.

Bonded nonwovens are made by forming fibre webs and joining fibres with adhesives, thermoplastic fibres, needles, stitches, or mechanical entanglement. Webs can be formed by cards, garnett machines, airstream systems, or cross laying.

Needle punching uses barbed needles to entangle fibres into stronger webs. Stitch bonding, such as on the Arachne machine, uses needles like warp knitting to give fibrous webs longitudinal strength.

Laminating joins fabrics or fabrics and foams with adhesives, flame bonding, powders, or coatings. Laminates are used in rainwear, linings, stable composites, and fabrics needing combined layers with minimal stiffness.

## Finishing

Finishing includes mechanical and chemical processes that improve fabric after weaving or knitting, except direct colouring. It makes gray goods or greige goods cleaner, softer, brighter, smoother, stronger, more attractive, or more useful.

Preparatory finishes include scouring, which removes dirt, oils, sizing, and lint, and bleaching, which removes natural colour. Cotton is often bleached with alkaline hydrogen peroxide; wool may be treated with sulfur dioxide or mild oxidizers.

Mercerization treats cotton or cotton blends under tension with caustic soda, then neutralizes them. It increases lustre, strength, and dye affinity by permanently swelling the fibre.

Drying removes water after processing. Mechanical methods such as centrifuges and vacuum suction rolls remove much moisture before heated dryers finish the process. Careful control avoids harsh hand or damage.

## Appearance Finishes

Napping raises a soft surface by lifting short fibres with wire-covered cylinders. It adds warmth and is used on woollens, cottons, spun silks, spun rayons, blankets, and knits. Shearing cuts the nap or pile to an even height.

Brushing removes loose threads and fibre ends or raises a nap. Singeing, or gassing, burns off projecting fibres, yarn ends, and fuzz by passing yarn or fabric over flame or heated plates.

Beetling pounds damp linen or linenlike cotton around an iron cylinder to create a hard, flat, lustrous surface. Decating applies heat and pressure to woollens, worsteds, man-made fabrics, blends, and knits to set lustre, hand, grain, or shrinkage.

Tentering stretches fabric width under tension on a tenter frame, straightens the weave, removes wrinkles, dries fabric, and sets final dimensions. Related processes include crabbing for wet wools and heat-setting for synthetics.

Calendering passes fabric between heated rollers to give a flat, smooth, glossy surface. Variations include moireing, embossing, glazing, cireing, polishing, and pressing.

Creping produces puckered effects by heated rollers or caustic soda treatment. Optical brightening uses fluorescent colourless dyes that reflect extra blue light, making fabrics look whiter and brighter.

## Tactile and Performance Finishes

Sizing adds substances such as starch, gelatin, glue, casein, or clay to form a film around yarn or fibres, increasing crispness, weight, and lustre. Weighting adds metallic salts to silk to increase body.

Fulling, also called milling or felting, makes wool thicker and more compact through moisture, heat, friction, and pressure. Softening agents such as glycerin or sulfonated oils improve hand and sometimes absorbency.

Performance finishes improve shrinkage control, durability, washability, and shape retention. Compressive shrinkage relaxes cotton or cotton blends, resin treatment stabilizes rayon, heat-setting stabilizes polyesters and nylons, and chlorine or resin treatments reduce wool felting.

Durable press finishes help fabrics keep shape, pleats, creases, and smoothness after laundering. They became important with resin chemistry and heat-sensitive synthetic fibres.

## Dyeing

Dyeing can occur at many stages: loose fibre, tow, sliver, tops, yarn hanks, yarn packages, piece goods, knitted fabrics, or finished garments. Manufacturers often prefer piece dyeing because white goods can be stocked until colour demand is known.

Dye selection depends on fibre type, cost, lightfastness, wash resistance, rubbing resistance, and end use. Preparation is essential because dirt, oils, or finishing residues interfere with level dyeing.

Major dye classes include acid dyes for wool, silk, and nylon; direct dyes for cellulose; mordant dyes requiring metal salts; sulfur dyes for inexpensive cellulose colours; azoic dyes formed inside fibres; vat dyes such as colourfast insoluble pigments; disperse dyes for hydrophobic fibres; and reactive dyes that bond chemically with fibres.

True dyeing requires relatively permanent colour not easily removed by washing and not rapidly faded by light. Dye molecules attach through ionic forces, hydrogen bonding, van der Waals forces, or covalent chemical linkages.

The movement of dye from bath to fibre is called exhaustion. Heat helps transfer dye molecules and swells fibres to make them receptive. Even colour, called levelness, is a key quality.

Fastness standards measure resistance to [[Light|light]], water, bleach, acid, alkali, detergents, perspiration, washing, and rubbing. Lightfastness is often rated on an 8-point scale, while other fastness measures often use a 5-point scale.

## Printing

Textile printing decorates fabric with pigments, dyes, or related materials in patterns. It grew from hand painting and has ancient examples from India, Egypt, Peru, and Mexico.

The four main printing methods are block printing, roller printing, screen printing, and heat transfer printing. Colour is usually applied as a thickened paste, fixed by steam or heat, and followed by washing to remove excess material.

Printing styles include direct printing, discharge printing, and resist printing. Direct printing applies colour directly. Discharge printing removes a dyed background to make a white or coloured design. Resist printing protects selected areas from taking colour before dyeing.

Block printing ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Woodblock_printing)) uses carved wooden blocks with raised designs. It is slow but historically important and can produce rich hand-crafted effects.
