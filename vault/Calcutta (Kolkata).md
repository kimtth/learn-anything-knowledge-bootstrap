# Calcutta (Kolkata)

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> Kolkata, formerly Calcutta, is West Bengal's capital and a major Indian port city shaped by British colonial rule, Bengali cultural life, commerce, manufacturing, and migration after partition. Its historic institutions and dense urban fabric coexist with persistent pressures of housing, pollution, congestion, and uneven development.

## Introduction

Calcutta, officially renamed Kolkata in 2001, is the capital of West Bengal and one of India's largest cities and ports. Built on the east bank of the Hugli River, the city developed as a point of transshipment and became the dominant urban centre of eastern India.
Kolkata is a city of sharp contrasts: colonial grandeur and deep poverty, cultural vitality and urban strain, intellectual energy and persistent overcrowding. Its identity was shaped first by the British empire and later by the social and political realities of independent India.

## Landscape and Climate

Kolkata occupies low, swampy land near the river, with the urban area stretching in a narrow band along both banks of the Hugli. Expansion has been limited by geography, though reclamation and suburban growth have pushed the city outward into adjacent districts and newer satellite areas.

The city has a subtropical monsoon climate. Summers are hot and humid, the monsoon brings heavy rain, and the winter months are comparatively pleasant. Air pollution, however, became a major urban problem in the modern period.

## City Layout and Housing

The city grew haphazardly outside its former European core, and its layout still reflects colonial history. Administrative and commercial activity is concentrated around Barabazar and the Maidan, while narrow streets, old neighbourhoods, and dense commuter flows place constant strain on municipal systems.

Housing shortages are severe. A large share of the population lives in bastis, many of them poorly serviced and overcrowded. Housing as one of Kolkata's most persistent social problems, even as the government has attempted improvement and resettlement programs.

## Architecture and People

Kolkata's built environment mixes British, classical European, Indo-Gothic, Mughal, and modern styles. Landmarks such as the Raj Bhavan, High Court, Writers' Building, Indian Museum, Victoria Memorial Hall, and Sahid Minar reflect the city's layered history and its colonial ambitions.

The city's population is mostly Hindu, with substantial Muslim, Christian, Sikh, Jain, and Buddhist minorities. Bengali is the dominant language, but Kolkata remains cosmopolitan, with communities from elsewhere in India and from abroad. Its population growth was intensified by partition and later refugee movements.

## Economy and Transportation

![[attachments/calcutta_kolkata_002.webp|420]]

*Howrah Bridge over the Hugli River in Kolkata*

Kolkata's economy rests on manufacturing, finance, trade, printing, and port activity. Jute processing and engineering were especially important, while banks, stock-market institutions, and service businesses reinforced the city's role as a regional commercial centre.

The city also depends on a complex transport network of trams, buses, bridges, rail terminals, roads, the airport, and the metro. Traffic congestion, road congestion, and the port's decline as a cargo centre have all challenged Kolkata's modern development.

## Government, Health, and Education

Local government is handled by the Kolkata Municipal Corporation, while state institutions and national agencies are also concentrated in the city. Kolkata is the seat of the West Bengal governor, the state legislature, the High Court, and several national institutions.

The city has major medical and educational institutions. It is a regional medical centre and home to the University of Calcutta, Jadavpur University, and Rabindra Bharati University, along with several important research institutes. Education and public health are central to the city's role in eastern India.

## Cultural Life

Kolkata as perhaps India's most important cultural centre. It was a birthplace of modern Indian literary and artistic thought and of Indian nationalism, and it remains closely associated with books, theatre, music, cinema, and political debate. Rabindranath Tagore stands as the defining cultural figure of this tradition.

The city also has important recreation spaces such as the Maidan, Eden Gardens, racecourses, golf courses, and botanical gardens. These spaces stand out in a city otherwise marked by density and limited open land.

## History

![[attachments/calcutta_kolkata_003.webp|420]]

*Writers' Building in Kolkata*

Kolkata's origins lie in the British trading post established by Job Charnock in 1690. The site was chosen for strategic and commercial reasons, and the city later grew into a major colonial settlement. In 1772 it became the capital of British India, and in the 19th century it expanded through trade, railways, industry, and urban reform.

After the partition of Bengal in 1947, Kolkata lost part of its economic hinterland and received large refugee inflows from what became Bangladesh. The city experienced stagnation, industrial decline, and worsening congestion, though later public works and planning helped stabilize conditions somewhat. Its renaming to Kolkata in 2001 marked a symbolic break with the colonial past.

## Conclusion

Kolkata is a city of enduring contradictions: colonial and Indian, rich in culture but burdened by poverty, historically powerful yet economically strained. Its long urban history, intellectual life, and social intensity make it one of the most distinctive cities in modern India.
