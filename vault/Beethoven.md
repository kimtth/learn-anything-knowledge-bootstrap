# Beethoven

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> Ludwig van Beethoven was the German composer who transformed Classical forms into a forceful, expressive instrumental language central to Romantic music. Working in Vienna despite progressive deafness, he developed symphonies, sonatas, quartets, concertos, and sacred works whose formal innovation and ideals of human freedom made the Eroica and Ninth Symphony enduring landmarks.

## Introduction

Ludwig van Beethoven was a German composer, baptized on December 17, 1770, in Bonn and died on March 26, 1827, in Vienna. He is the predominant musical figure in the transition between the Classical and Romantic eras and, in the view of many, the greatest composer who ever lived.

Rooted in the traditions of Haydn and [[Mozart]], his art reached toward the new humanism and the ideals of the French Revolution, with its concern for the freedom and dignity of the individual. He showed more vividly than any predecessor how instrumental music could convey a philosophy of life without words, and his work became a fountainhead for the Romantics who followed.

## The Early Years in Bonn

Beethoven's earliest compositions, written at Bonn, interest the student more than the ordinary listener, because they reveal both his roots and the gaps in his early training. Three piano sonatas of 1783 show that musically Bonn was an outpost of Mannheim, the cradle of the modern orchestra in Germany.

From the Mannheim school he absorbed a love of sudden contrasts between soft and loud, abrupt outbursts, and wide leaping figures known as Mannheim rockets. What was an occasional effect for Mozart became central to Beethoven's musical personality and helped him free instrumental music from its dependence on vocal style.

## Arrival in Vienna

Before leaving Bonn, Beethoven had already won a reputation across northwest Germany as a piano virtuoso with a special gift for improvisation, by some accounts surpassing even Mozart. He was taken up by the Viennese aristocracy almost as soon as he arrived, aided by patrons such as Baron van Swieten and Prince Lichnowsky.

To master technical problems, he studied secretly with teachers including Johann Georg Albrechtsberger, a learned contrapuntist of the old school. In the 1790s Vienna, music had become the favourite pastime of a cultured aristocracy, and amateur and semiprofessional music-making reached an exceptionally high standard.

## The First Period and Approaching Deafness

The turn of the century closed what is usually called Beethoven's first period, during which his art stayed mainly within 18th-century technique. Most of his published works were for the piano, with important exceptions such as the Six String Quartets, Opus 18, and the First Symphony.

A change of direction came as Beethoven realized he was becoming deaf. By 1802 the malady was clearly permanent, and during a summer at Heiligenstadt he wrote the Heiligenstadt Testament, an anguished document addressed to his brothers in which he confessed thoughts of suicide but declared that only his art held him back. He resolved instead to "seize fate by the throat."

## The Heroic Middle Period

From about 1804 the masterworks followed one another in rapid succession. The Third Symphony (Eroica) answered the Heiligenstadt Testament with an unprecedented assertion of the human will; originally meant for [[Napoleon]], Beethoven struck out the dedication in fury on hearing that Napoleon had named himself emperor.

There followed the Waldstein and Appassionata sonatas, the Fourth Piano Concerto, the three Razumovsky Quartets, and the Fourth, Fifth, and Sixth symphonies. Beethoven supported himself by the sale of his works and by patrons such as the archduke Rudolf, Prince Lobkowitz, and Prince Kinsky, who in 1809 granted him an annuity of 4,000 florins simply to remain in Vienna and compose.

## Personal Life and the Immortal Beloved

Beethoven never married, though several attachments shaped his life. He dedicated the Moonlight Sonata to the countess Giulietta Guicciardi and proposed marriage to her cousin Josephine, but family pressure and his own indecisiveness ended each hope.

After his death, three unsent letters to an unnamed "Immortal Beloved" were found locked in a drawer. Scholars such as Maynard Solomon have argued that the most likely recipient was Antonie Brentano, the Viennese wife of a Frankfurt merchant. In 1815 the death of his brother Carl left Beethoven the guardian of his nephew Karl, a relationship that brought him lasting anguish.

## The Late Works

In his final years Beethoven produced his most profound music. He completed the last three piano sonatas, the Diabelli Variations, the Missa Solemnis, and the Ninth Symphony ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Symphony_No._9_%28Beethoven%29)), which combined vocal and instrumental forces as never before and was performed with great success in 1824, the deaf composer unaware of the applause until a soloist turned him to face the audience.

His last commission came from Prince Nikolas Golitsyn, who asked for three string quartets; Beethoven wrote five late quartets in all, including the Grosse Fuge. He died of cirrhosis of the liver on March 26, 1827, and his funeral three days later was attended by some 20,000 people, with Schubert among the torchbearers.

## Reputation and Influence

Beethoven's greatest achievement was to raise instrumental music, long considered inferior to vocal music, to the highest plane of art. After him it was no longer possible to regard music merely as the art of pleasing sounds.

His biographer Wilhelm von Lenz first divided the output into three periods, running from the Opus 1 trios of 1794 to the works of his last decade. Heir above all to Haydn in the symphony and the quartet, Beethoven combined intense feeling with perfection of design and became, by his example, a model for the entire Romantic age.

## Key Events & Developments

- 1770: Beethoven is born in Bonn and trained within the declining Mannheim tradition.
- 1792: He settles in [[Vienna]] and is embraced by its musical aristocracy.
- 1802: Facing deafness, he writes the Heiligenstadt Testament but resolves to keep composing.
- 1804: The Eroica Symphony opens his heroic middle period.
- 1824: The Ninth Symphony ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Symphony_No._9_%28Beethoven%29)) premieres in Vienna.
- 1827: Beethoven dies in Vienna, mourned by a crowd of about 20,000.

## Key People

- Joseph Haydn ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph_Haydn)): An early teacher and a model whose forms Beethoven extended.
- [[Mozart]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mozart)): A guiding example, especially in concerto and opera.
- Archduke Rudolf ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Archduke_Rudolf)): A pupil and patron who helped guarantee Beethoven's income.
- Karl: His nephew and ward, a source of deep personal strain.

## Interesting Facts

- Beethoven was the first major musician to receive a salary with no duties other than to compose as he pleased.
- Many of his greatest works, including the Ninth Symphony ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Symphony_No._9_%28Beethoven%29)), were written after he had become almost totally deaf.
- His surviving sketchbooks show simple melodies being reworked patiently into their final forms.
