## Introduction

Geometry is the branch of mathematics concerned with shapes, spatial relationships, and the properties of space itself. Its practical roots lie in land measurement, building, and storage, but it developed into an abstract science of proof and deduction. It also notes that geometry is not limited to flat or solid objects; it now extends into multidimensional, projective, differential, and topological forms. The field’s core value is its ability to describe and analyze patterns in space with great precision.

## Major branches of geometry

Geometry comprises several main branches. Euclidean geometry ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euclidean_geometry)) is the classical system codified in Euclid's *Elements* about 300 BCE, in which several hundred theorems were proved by deductive logic from ten axioms. Analytic geometry ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Analytic_geometry)), initiated by René Descartes (1596–1650), uses rectangular coordinates and algebra to represent lines and curves. Projective geometry ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Projective_geometry)), originated by Girard Desargues (1591–1661), studies properties that remain true under projection, such as the way parallel lines seem to meet on a horizon. Differential geometry ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Differential_geometry)) examines curves and surfaces using calculus, and [[Topology|topology]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Topology)) — the youngest branch, given general methods by L.E.J. Brouwer in 1911 — studies features that survive continuous deformation such as stretching and folding but not tearing.

## History of geometry

Geometry developed from ancient Egypt and Mesopotamia to classical Greece and later civilizations. Early practical geometry helped with surveying, building, and measuring volumes. Greek mathematicians transformed it into a deductive science, especially through Euclid’s system of proof. Geometry was also used in astronomy, cartography, and perspective, and Islamic and Renaissance scholars expanded its methods and applications.

## Ancient Greek methods and proof

The Greeks turned practical measurement into logical proof. Proclus credited Thales with the very idea that obvious propositions still need proof (the "Bridge of Asses"), while Democritus boasted that he could prove what the Egyptian "rope pullers" merely asserted, and by [[Plato and Platonism (Plato)|Plato]]'s time proof had become customary. The method of exhaustion ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Method_of_exhaustion)), developed by Eudoxus of Cnidus, approximated curved areas and volumes using inscribed and circumscribed polygons; [[Archimedes]] used 96-sided polygons to bound the value of π between 3 10/71 and 3 1/7. It also explains how Menaechmus's discovery of the conic sections — circle, ellipse, parabola, and hyperbola — grew from efforts to duplicate the cube.

## Geometry and astronomy

The Greeks "geometrized the heavens," modelling the Sun, Moon, and planets as moving on rotating circles around a stationary Earth. Ptolemy worked out complete sets of circles for every planet in his *Almagest*, using a secondary epicycle riding on a primary deferent and a table of chords that anticipated the trigonometric sine. Eratosthenes estimated the Earth's circumference at about 250,000 stadia by comparing the Sun's noon angle at Alexandria and Syene, while Aristarchus of Samos measured the relative sizes of Sun, Moon, and Earth and proposed an early heliocentric model. That the abundance of observationally equivalent geometric models led the Greeks to doubt whether geometry could disclose the true principles of nature.

## Non-Euclidean geometry and modern developments

*Hyperbolic triangle drawn in the Poincare disk model*

Girolamo Saccheri, building on the quadrilateral of Omar Khayyam, tried to prove the parallel postulate by contradiction but instead established several theorems of non-Euclidean geometry, while Johann Heinrich Lambert linked the acute-angle case to an imaginary sphere. The independent work of [[Gauss]], Bolyai, and Lobachevsky then established non-Euclidean geometry ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-Euclidean_geometry)) as a consistent system; Lobachevsky noted it would differ measurably from Euclid's only across vast interstellar triangles. [[Einstein]]'s 1916 general theory of relativity made geometry central to gravity, replacing Newtonian force in flat space with motion along the geodesics of a curved, four-dimensional spacetime, and successfully predicting the bending of light near massive bodies.
Geometry also links to Renaissance perspective — the visual pyramid of Brunelleschi and Alberti's *De pictura* (1435) — as well as map projection, the calculus of Cavalieri and Fermat, optics, and planetary motion, showing how spatial reasoning moved between art, measurement, and physics.

## Core Equations

Euclidean geometry connects distance, area, and curvature through compact formulas:

$$a^2+b^2=c^2$$

The Pythagorean theorem relates the sides of a right triangle, with c as the hypotenuse.

$$A=\pi r^2, \qquad C=2\pi r$$

These formulas give the area and circumference of a circle from its radius.

## Key Events & Developments

- Ancient societies used geometry for surveying, building, and trade.
- Greek mathematicians turned geometry into a deductive science.
- [[Descartes and Cartesianism (Descartes)|Descartes]] and Fermat introduced analytic geometry in the 17th century.
- [[Gauss]], Bolyai, and Lobachevsky developed non-Euclidean geometry.
- [[Einstein]] used curved geometry to explain gravity in the 20th century.
