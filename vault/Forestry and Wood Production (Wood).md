## Introduction

Wood ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wood)) is the principal strengthening and nutrient-conducting tissue of trees and one of the most important natural materials used by humans. that wood is strong for its weight, provides insulation, has useful acoustic properties, and feels warm compared with metals or plastics. It is also easily worked and remains valuable in traditional uses such as construction, furniture, and fuel, while also serving in modern products such as plywood, paper, and chemicals.
Wood is a renewable resource and a major economic material. It is produced by many plant species, but only some are suitable for industrial use. The world’s wood supply comes mainly from [[Gymnosperms|gymnosperms]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gymnosperm)) (softwoods) and [[Angiosperms|angiosperms]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flowering_plant)) (hardwoods), whose properties differ widely.

## Production and Economic Importance

Wood is produced by an estimated 25,000 to 30,000 plant species, although only about 3,000 to 4,000 produce wood suitable for use as a material. The two categories are [[Gymnosperms|gymnosperms]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gymnosperm)) (cone-bearing softwoods such as pine and spruce) and [[Angiosperms|angiosperms]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flowering_plant)) (hardwoods such as oak, beech, teak, and balsa). Softwoods account for about 40 percent of the world's lumber and hardwoods about 60 percent. The distinction is not always literal: balsa, a hardwood, is softer than the softwood yew.
Wood is a renewable resource, unlike coal, ores, and petroleum, which are gradually exhausted. By being harvested, transported, processed, and traded, it provides jobs and even basic subsistence in some countries. In the late 1990s yearly world production of roundwood, or logs, reached about 3.5 billion cubic metres, up from 1.5 billion cubic metres earlier, and demand continues to grow.

## Management and Harvesting

Wood harvesting differs from the harvesting of annual crops because a tree must be grown for many years before its wood can be used. Rotation periods run 2-3 years on energy plantations, 6-8 years for eucalypt pulpwood, 30-50 years for fast-growing pines, and 100 years or more for large temperate and tropical timber. Sustainable management therefore depends on a management plan that sets the yearly yield and method of removal.

Harvesting may involve clear-cutting large areas or selective cutting of individual trees, and the sustained-yield concept removes only timber matched by net growth, as estimated by statistical sampling, combined with natural and artificial seeding. The importance of ecolabeling, which assures buyers that marketed wood was not produced in a way harmful to the environment. Felled trees are vulnerable to fungi and insects, so harvest timing also helps protect the wood and the remaining forest.

## Processing and Products

![[attachments/forestry_and_wood_production_wood_002.webp|420]]

*Worker handling veneer sheets in a plywood factory*

Wood is turned into veneer, plywood, particleboard, fibreboard, and pulp. Veneer is a thin sheet, commonly 0.5-1.0 mm thick; more than 90 percent is rotary-cut on a lathe, while figured woods for furniture are sliced. Plywood is made by gluing veneers with alternating grain at right angles, usually in an odd number of layers, using phenol-formaldehyde resin for exterior use and urea-formaldehyde for interior use; its main advantage is dimensional stability. Particleboard is bonded from wood particles and residues, and fibreboard from wood fibres, often using the thermomechanical or the explosion (Masonite) process.

Pulp and paper production are also covered. Pulping is mechanical (grinding), chemical (cooking with chemicals), or semichemical, with yields from about 40 percent by chemical methods to 95 percent by mechanical ones. Chemical processes use acids in sulfite pulping or alkalies in the soda and sulfate (kraft) processes, after which the pulp is washed, screened, bleached, and run into a paper machine. ## Drying, Preservation, and Quality

Freshly produced wood usually contains a great deal of moisture, so drying is essential before use. that drying reduces shrinkage, protects wood from microorganisms, lowers weight, and improves strength and finish. Drying can be done in the open air or in kilns; air-drying wood 2.5 cm thick from green to about 20 percent moisture takes roughly 20 to 300 days depending on species, place, and season.
Preservation methods using three groups of chemicals: oils, oil-soluble chemicals, and water-soluble salts. Creosote from coal tar greatly extends the life of railroad ties, poles, and pilings but resists painting and pollutes; pentachlorophenol keeps wood cleaner but is banned in several countries, including the [[United States of America (United States)|United States]]. Water-soluble salts such as the copper-chromium-arsenic (CCA) compound resist leaching, while fire retardants containing silica create a barrier to flame.

## Structure and Properties

![[attachments/forestry_and_wood_production_wood_001.webp|420]]

*Oak log cross-section showing annual growth rings*

Wood can be examined at both the macro and micro scale. At the visible level it has growth rings, pith, bark, earlywood, latewood, and pores, and counting the growth rings near the ground can reveal a tree's age. At the cellular level, wood is composed of tracheids, vessel members, fibres, and parenchyma; one cubic metre of spruce wood contains an estimated 350 billion to 500 billion cells, with axial tracheids averaging 3-5 mm in length. Common temperate woods weigh about 300 to 900 kg per cubic metre when air-dry, while tropical woods range from balsa at 80 to lignum vitae at 1,300 kg per cubic metre.
Hygroscopicity is the ability of wood to absorb moisture from air or water. Cell walls hold roughly 30 percent moisture at the fibre saturation point, beyond which water fills the cell cavities; balsa can hold up to 800 percent moisture, pine 250 percent, and beech 120 percent. Under shelter in temperate zones wood settles to about 12 to 15 percent moisture, and these changes matter for storage, processing, and use in buildings and furniture.

## Degradation and Durability

Wood can be degraded by bacteria, fungi, insects, marine borers, and environmental factors. Some discoloration, such as bacterial wetwood or blue stain in pines, is mainly aesthetic, but decay fungi are by far the most important cause of real wood loss. Wood does not decay simply with the passage of time; furniture found in ancient Egyptian pharaohs' tombs survived in excellent condition because durability depends on whether wood is protected from moisture, insects, and other damaging agents.
