# Democracy

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> Democracy is a form of government in which political authority rests ultimately on the people, commonly through representation, elections, participation, and protection of rights.

## Introduction

Democracy ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Democracy)) means rule by the people, and the term comes from the Greek words demos and kratos. This origin raises the main problem of democracy: if power belongs to the people, then a society must decide what the people is, who counts as part of it, how they govern, and what should happen when the majority and minorities disagree. Democracy is not just a question of words. It is a practical system that must answer hard questions about citizenship, scale, institutions, voting, and the balance between freedom and common rule. From the start, democracy is tied to choices about the right political unit, the right voters, and the right decision-making rules.

## Classical Greece

Classical Greek democracy grew in the polis, the city-state, not in a modern nation-state. In Athens, popular rule began under Cleisthenes in 507 BC and lasted for almost two centuries. The Athenian demos was limited to adult male citizens, so it was far smaller than the full population, and the system still depended on exclusion as much as participation. The Assembly was the center of government, and citizens voted directly, while the Council and the courts helped manage public business. This early model showed that direct democracy can work in a small political community, but it also showed how narrow citizenship could be in practice.

## The Roman Republic

Rome developed a republic at almost the same time as Greece developed democracy. The Roman system belonged to the Roman people in theory, but in practice it remained tied to a city-state structure even as Rome expanded across the Mediterranean world. Roman citizenship could grow through birth, naturalization, and manumission, yet many citizens who lived far from the city could not really take part because the assemblies still met in the Forum. The republic therefore faced a basic democratic problem of size and distance, and this is a major reason why later democrats did not simply copy Rome. Its Senate, assemblies, and magistracies were powerful, but the system never solved the problem of representation for a wide territory. That failure became important centuries later, when representative government offered a new answer.

## Continental Europe

Popular institutions in Continental Europe developed slowly after the early Middle Ages. Local assemblies appeared in northern Europe, in the Alps, in Scandinavia, and especially in Iceland, where the Althing became an early national legislature. Over time, these bodies helped shape later representative institutions in places such as Switzerland, Norway, Sweden, Denmark, and the Netherlands. This development is a gradual move from local participation to broader political forms. It was not yet modern democracy, but it created habits of assembly, election, and shared rule that later democratic systems could use.

## England

England gave the modern world one of its most important democratic inventions: Parliament. Parliament did not begin as a fully planned democratic body; it grew out of royal councils that handled grievances, justice, and finance. Over time it became a legislature, and by the late 15th century lawmaking already needed approval by both houses and the monarch. Later, the English Civil Wars and the Commonwealth briefly interrupted monarchy, and by about 1800 Parliament had gained major power, including influence over the prime minister. The rise of Whigs and Tories also mattered because party competition made majority support inside the Commons necessary for government to function.

## The United States

The United States showed that representation and democracy could work together on a large scale. Colonial America had many conditions that helped this development: long distance from London, local legislatures, wider suffrage for many white men, land ownership, and strong ideas about rights and popular sovereignty. The conflict over taxation and representation, the Stamp Act, the war for independence, and the Declaration of Independence all helped make Americans think of themselves as one people. After independence, the new states and later the federal system built on representation instead of direct rule, and this made a large democratic republic possible. The American experience is a turning point because it linked democracy to a nation-state rather than a city-state.

## Suffrage

Democracy changed most deeply when the demos itself expanded. In the 19th and 20th centuries, property limits on voting were reduced and then removed, women gained the vote in more and more countries, and African Americans in the United States eventually won real political inclusion after long legal and illegal exclusion. Open questions also remain about permanent legal residents and other people who live inside a country but may not yet be full citizens. In the same period, factions became parties, and electoral systems took different forms. Some countries used winner-take-all rules, others proportional representation, and these choices shaped whether one party could govern alone or whether coalitions and bargaining were necessary. The result was that democracy no longer meant only voting; it also meant choosing how voting should be organized.

## Pericles

*Discurso funebre pericles*

Pericles presented Athens as a proud model of democratic life. In his funeral oration, he praised a constitution that favored the many instead of the few and said that public life was open to merit rather than birth or wealth. He also linked democracy with equal justice and a wider freedom in ordinary life. Pericles shows that democracy was not only a set of procedures. It was also an image of civic dignity, fair treatment, and public confidence in the abilities of ordinary citizens.

## Aristotle

[[Aristotle and Aristotelianism|Aristotle]] gave democracy a more systematic and comparative analysis. He treated a constitution as an arrangement of offices and argued that different societies can have different forms of government because they organize power in different ways. He divided governments into ideal and corrupted forms, based on whether rulers seek the common good or only their own interest. In his famous scheme, rule by the many was called polity when it served the common good, but democracy when it served only the majority for its own benefit. Even though his definition of democracy was not friendly, his wider work still helped later thinkers compare real regimes, measure stability, and think about the relation between liberty and government.

## Locke

John Locke gave democracy a strong modern foundation by linking government to consent and majority rule. He argued that people are naturally equal and free, and that political society becomes legitimate only when individuals consent to form a community in which the majority can decide for the whole. Locke is a thinker who moved democratic thought beyond the city-state, because he understood that a nation-state could also be the proper unit for democratic government. He still accepted limits that now look narrow, especially on women, but his ideas about consent, rights, and majority decision became central for later democratic theory. Locke turns democracy into a question of legitimacy, not just participation.

## The Declaration of Independence

The Declaration of Independence makes Locke's ideas public and political. It says that all men are created equal, that rights are unalienable, and that governments gain just powers only from the consent of the governed. It also says that when a government destroys those ends, the people have a right to alter or abolish it and form a new government. The Declaration shows how democratic language crossed from philosophy into revolution.

## Montesquieu

Montesquieu influenced both the American founders and later democratic theory by reworking the study of government. He rejected Aristotle's categories and instead contrasted monarchy, despotism, and republican government, which could be either democratic or aristocratic depending on who held supreme power. He argued that republics need public virtue, meaning a concern for the common good, and he warned that without it democracy can break down under factional conflict. He helped later democrats understand why institutions matter and why a democracy needs moral habits as well as laws.

## Hume

David Hume sharpened the discussion of factions. Hume, and later Madison through Hume, argued that bigger political associations can actually be safer from factional capture than small ones, because wider societies contain more interests and force representatives to think more broadly. This is an important reversal of the old idea that small communities are always better for self-government. Hume therefore helped shift democratic theory toward representative government on a larger scale, where conflict among interests could be managed rather than simply avoided.

## Rousseau

Rousseau is one of the strongest defenders of direct democracy, but also one of the most pessimistic. He argues that sovereignty cannot be represented and that a people becomes free only when it governs itself directly. At the same time, he thinks such direct democracy is too difficult for large states and too fragile in a world of stronger neighbors. His view makes democracy feel morally pure but politically hard to maintain. Rousseau shows the tension between democratic ideals and the practical limits of scale.

## Mill

John Stuart Mill gives democracy a liberal defense. His harm principle says that power may be used against a person only to prevent harm to others, not simply to protect that person's own good. That idea supports freedom of association, free discussion, and other rights that a democracy needs if citizens are to think and act for themselves. Mill also argues for representative government and strongly defends woman suffrage, which makes him a key bridge between classical liberalism and modern democratic inclusion. His work connects democracy with liberty, discussion, and equal citizenship.

## Dewey

John Dewey treats democracy as more than a state form. For him, it is a way of life in which people cooperate, solve common problems through inquiry, and keep their institutions open to criticism and change. He insists that citizens need habits of mind such as curiosity, cooperation, and public spirit, and he places great weight on education because those habits must be learned early. His thought moves toward a democratic welfare state, where social conditions make self-development possible for more people. His approach makes democracy feel active, educational, and always unfinished.

## Habermas

Jürgen Habermas builds a model of democracy around public reason. His ideal speech situation is a setting in which people argue with one another only through reason and evidence, free from coercion, pressure, and time limits that distort discussion. Habermas uses this idea not as a literal description of everyday politics but as a standard for judging real democratic debate. It gives democracy a communicative ideal: citizens should be able to speak, listen, and reach agreement in conditions that are as fair as possible.

## Rawls

John Rawls offers a nonutilitarian defense of democracy based on fairness and equal rights. He imagines people choosing principles of justice behind a veil of ignorance, where they do not know their own class, race, religion, talents, or life goals. Because they cannot know whether they will be in a majority or minority, they would reject rules that let majority happiness justify unfair treatment of some citizens. Rawls therefore supports equal liberty and fair opportunity as core democratic principles. His work gives modern democracy a strong moral base that does not depend only on producing the greatest happiness for the greatest number.

An ideal democracy must allow effective participation, equal voting, an informed electorate, citizen control of the agenda, inclusion, and fundamental rights. These features are connected rather than separate: people need the right to speak, gather information, participate equally, and help shape the agenda if democracy is to be real. It also explains that representative institutions are necessary in large states because direct participation by everyone is no longer practical. At this point the discussion moves from ideal theory to the institutions of representative democracy.

## Institutions of Representative Democracy

Representative democracy depends on free, fair, and frequent elections, freedom of expression, independent sources of information, and freedom of association. These institutions did not always begin as democratic ideas, but they became essential for large modern states because they make participation possible at national scale. They also support the ideals listed above: representation helps citizens control the agenda, elections protect equality in voting, and open discussion and independent media help create an informed electorate. Actual democracies show that no real system reaches the ideal completely, but many systems combine these institutions in different ways. In practice, democracies vary according to their laws, histories, party systems, and social conditions, so democratic design is something that must fit the country rather than follow one universal model. That is why the discussion moves from ideal democracy to the working structures of real democracies.

## Immigration

After World War II, immigration rose sharply in western Europe, Australia, and the United States. Immigrants often arrived with little money or education and worked in low-paid jobs, while language and cultural differences made their presence politically controversial. Anti-immigrant feeling sometimes fed radical parties and movements, some of which turned openly racist, neofascist, or hostile to democracy itself. Immigration is both a social challenge and a test of democratic tolerance.

## Terrorism

Terrorism became a growing threat from the 1970s onward, and especially after the attacks of September 11, 2001. Democratic governments responded with stronger police and security powers, but these measures sometimes raised fears that civil and political liberties were being weakened. The central democratic problem is balance: a democracy must protect people from violence without abandoning the rights that make it democratic. This remains an open issue in the early 21st century, because security and freedom can pull in opposite directions.

## International systems

International organizations created after World War II, especially the United Nations, pushed democracy beyond the nation-state. These systems solve problems too large for one country alone, but they also create new democratic dilemmas because they shift final decision-making away from citizens in a single state. This raises questions of accountability, control, and legitimacy at a higher level than national politics. Democratic thought must now address not only cities and nations but also how international power can be governed in ways that still respect popular rule.

- 18th century: Enlightenment thinkers connect democracy with rights, representation, and constitutional government.
- 19th and 20th centuries: Democracy expands through wider suffrage, parties, and mass politics.
