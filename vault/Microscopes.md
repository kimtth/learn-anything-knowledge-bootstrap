## Introduction

![[attachments/microscopes_001.png|420]]

*Compound microscope diagram*

A microscope is an instrument that enlarges small objects so their fine structures can be examined and analyzed. Optical microscopes use glass lenses and visible light, but other systems use acoustic waves, X-rays, electron beams, lasers, or digital imaging.

Magnifying power describes how many times larger an object appears, while resolution measures the smallest detail that can be seen separately. A hand lens may magnify about 3 to 20 times, a single-lens microscope may reach about 300 times, and a compound light microscope may reach about 2,000 times with resolution near 0.2 micrometre.

Modern microscopes often capture images through photomicrography and digital detectors. Digital microscopes can show images directly on screens, record time-lapse sequences, and make low-cost imaging available to students and amateur users.

Electron microscopes extend microscopy far beyond ordinary light. A transmission electron microscope ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transmission_electron_microscopy)), or TEM, images thin specimens in near vacuum and can magnify more than 1,000,000 times. A scanning electron microscope, or SEM, usually has lower resolution but shows solid surfaces and relief.

## Transmission

![[attachments/microscopes_003.webp|420]]

*Electron microscope*

The transmission electron microscope ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transmission_electron_microscopy)) belongs to a larger group of microscopes that use physical effects beyond ordinary optical lenses. Other instruments include laser, sound, X-ray, scanning tunneling, and environmental scanning electron microscopes, each extending the range of materials and structures that can be examined.

Optical microscopy developed from older ideas about magnification. Roger Bacon described the visual enlargement of tiny particles in the 13th century, and Girolamo Fracastoro wrote in 1538 that two spectacle lenses could make objects appear larger. Dutch spectacle makers, including Hans Jansen, Zacharias Jansen, and Hans Lippershey, are credited with the compound microscope around 1590.

In the 17th century, Robert Hooke used compound microscopes for demonstrations at the Royal Society and published Micrographia in 1665, coining the term [[Cells - Their Structures and Functions|cell]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cell_%28biology%29)). Antonie van Leeuwenhoek used tiny single-lens microscopes to observe freshwater microorganisms in the 1670s, helping launch microbiology.

Early compound microscopes were beautiful but optically weak because lens errors were magnified with the image. Work by Chester Moor Hall, Benjamin Martin, Francois Beeldsnijder, Joseph Jackson Lister, and Ernst Abbe gradually corrected chromatic and geometric errors. By the late 19th century, light microscopes had approached the effective limits of optical microscopy.

## Principles

The simple microscope uses a single magnifying lens, or loupe. Modern loupes may use two glass elements for colour correction, but the basic idea remains the same: a lens lets the eye view a virtual image of a nearby object at a comfortable distance.

## Magnification

Magnification depends on the relationship between the eye, the object, and the lens focal length. Without a lens, the closest clear viewing distance is about 25 cm for many people. With a magnifier, the object can be brought closer while remaining clear, making it appear larger.

The field of view and magnifying power are linked. A short focal length gives higher magnification but a smaller field and often more aberration; a longer focal length gives lower magnification but a wider view. Brightness depends partly on whether the lens diameter fills the eye's pupil.

## Chromatic

Chromatic aberration occurs because different wavelengths of light focus at different distances. Red light focuses farther from the lens than blue light, so high-contrast edges can show coloured fringes.

## Different

Different wavelengths create different focal points, which makes lens design difficult. Correcting this effect was one of the main historical problems in improving optical microscopes.

## Spherical

Spherical aberration occurs when a spherical lens does not bring central and edge rays to the same focus. Distortion can also bend straight lines, and both problems become worse as aperture and magnifying power increase.

Low-power magnifiers may use simple double-convex or aspheric lenses, while higher-power magnifiers use compound forms such as Huygenian, Coddington, Steinheil, or Hastings designs. Reflecting microscopes use mirrors rather than lenses, avoiding chromatic aberration and working especially well in infrared or ultraviolet analytical instruments.

## Optics

A compound microscope ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Optical_microscope)) overcomes the limits of a simple lens by using an objective and an eyepiece. The objective, placed near the specimen, forms a real image; the eyepiece enlarges that image for the observer. Total magnification is the product of objective and eyepiece magnification.

The instrument also needs a body tube, condenser, illumination system, specimen stage, and focusing controls. Monocular microscopes use one viewing tube, binocular microscopes split the same image for two eyes, and stereoscopic microscopes use separate optical paths to give a three-dimensional view.

Resolution depends on the eye, illumination, contrast, diffraction, wavelength, and numerical aperture, or N.A. Numerical aperture measures how much diffracted light the objective can collect. Dry objectives may reach about 0.95 N.A., while oil-immersion objectives can reach about 1.4, improving resolution by using a medium with a higher refractive index.

Mechanical precision matters because high-N.A. objectives have very shallow depth of focus, sometimes only 1 or 2 micrometres. The body tube must keep lenses aligned; the stage must move slides accurately; and modern systems may use infinity-corrected optics, zoom eyepieces, computer-controlled stages, and digital return to selected specimen positions.

Illumination may use critical illumination or Kohler illumination, which averages light-source irregularities and fills the objective aperture evenly. Specialized condensers create dark-field or Rheinberg illumination, useful for bacteria, flagellated cells, and visually striking colour contrast.

Objectives are defined by focal length, magnification, numerical aperture, tube length, field of view, and aberration correction. Low-power objectives may be simple, but high-power objectives require special glasses, apochromatic correction, flat-field design, short working distances, aplanatic elements, and sometimes oil or water immersion.

Image capture has shifted from film to digital systems using CMOS or CCD detectors. Digital imaging allows still pictures, video, time-lapse recording, measurement of particles and areas, image processing, electronic sharing, and easy printing.

Ernst Abbe founded the modern theory of microscope image formation in 1873. Object details diffract light, and the objective must collect enough diffracted waves to reconstruct fine structure. Higher spatial frequencies require higher numerical aperture; otherwise extra eyepiece magnification produces only empty magnification without new detail.

Specialized optical microscopes adapt the basic design to particular tasks. Inverted microscopes examine living cell cultures from below while the specimen remains in liquid. Stereomicroscopes support dissection, microsurgery, and electronics work. Polarizing microscopes reveal birefringent crystals, stresses, and asbestos fibres.

Metallographic microscopes use vertical illumination to examine opaque metals, minerals, forensic samples, and diagnostic specimens. Reflecting microscopes use mirror objectives for ultraviolet, infrared, and broad-spectrum work where ordinary glass lenses fail.

## Compound

The compound microscope ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Optical_microscope)) depends on a coordinated optical and mechanical system. Objectives, eyepieces, condensers, tube length, illumination, specimen mounting, focusing, and stage movement all affect whether magnification becomes useful resolution or only a larger blurred image.

## Phase-contrast

Many living cell structures are nearly transparent in ordinary light because they transmit almost as much light as their surrounding medium. Phase-contrast microscopy ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phase-contrast_microscopy)) turns small refractive-index differences into visible intensity differences, making transparent structures visible without staining.

Frits Zernike developed phase contrast in 1934 and received the Nobel Prize for Physics in 1953. By shifting the phase of diffracted and direct light, the microscope makes phase differences cancel or reinforce, so invisible structures appear as contrast in the image.

Interference microscopy compares light that passes through the specimen with a reference beam that does not. Systems by Francis Smith, Maurice Francon, and Georges Nomarski led to differential interference contrast, which uses polarized beams and prisms to create strong relief-like contrast.

Confocal microscopy detects only light from the focal plane, so out-of-focus regions appear dark. Laser scanning confocal microscopes build optical sections, combine Z-series images into three-dimensional structures, and can record living-cell changes rapidly with fluorescent labels.

Ultraviolet microscopy, developed by August Kohler and Moritz von Rohr, used shorter wavelengths for higher resolution but required quartz lenses or reflecting systems. Its fluorescence applications helped point toward the use of electron beams as very short-wavelength illuminants.

## Core Equations

Engineering and technology topics often combine efficiency, signal flow, and computational cost:

$$\eta=\frac{\text{useful output}}{\text{input}}$$

Efficiency compares useful output with total input, so losses appear as the gap between the two.

$$SNR=10\log_{10}\left(\frac{P_s}{P_n}\right)$$

Signal-to-noise ratio compares signal power with noise power on a logarithmic decibel scale.

$$T(n)=O(n\log n)$$

## Key Events & Developments

- c. 1590: Dutch spectacle makers were credited with inventing the compound microscope ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Optical_microscope)).
- 1665: Robert Hooke published Micrographia and coined the term [[Cells - Their Structures and Functions|cell]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cell_%28biology%29)).
- 1670s: Antonie van Leeuwenhoek observed microorganisms with single-lens microscopes.
- 1830: Joseph Jackson Lister described a theoretical approach to microscope objective design.
- 1873: Ernst Abbe published the modern theory of image formation and numerical aperture.
- 1934: Frits Zernike developed phase-contrast microscopy.
- 1947: C.R. Burch produced a large reflecting microscope using ultraviolet rays.

## Interesting Facts

- Leeuwenhoek's tiny microscopes weighed less than 15 grams but could resolve details around 0.7 micrometre.
- The largest optical microscope put into production, the Burch reflector, weighed about 200 kg.
- Extra eyepiece power without enough numerical aperture creates empty magnification, not more detail.
- Oil immersion improves resolution by replacing air with a medium closer to glass in refractive index.
- Confocal microscopes can build three-dimensional images by stacking optical sections from different depths.
