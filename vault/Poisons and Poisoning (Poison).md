# Poisons and Poisoning (Poison)

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> Poisons are natural or synthetic substances that harm living tissue when absorbed, inhaled, ingested, or injected. The source organizes toxic effects by dose, route, chemistry, distribution, excretion, metabolism, target organs, and uses, connecting Paracelsus's dose principle to modern toxicology and environmental hazards.

## Introduction

Poison ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poison)) is a natural or synthetic substance that damages living tissue and has injurious or fatal effects when ingested, inhaled, absorbed, or injected. Modern toxicology began with Paracelsus, who stressed chemical nature, dose, and experimentation, and with Matthieu Orfila, who correlated toxin chemistry with biological effects in poisoned individuals.

Poisoning involves four elements: the poison, the organism, cellular injury, and symptoms, signs, or death. A toxic chemical must enter the organism, accumulate to a toxic level in target cells, disrupt structure or function, and produce clinical effects. Toxicity depends on dose, exposure route, absorption, distribution, biotransformation, excretion, and target-organ sensitivity.

Poisons are classified by origin, physical form, chemical nature, chemical activity, target site, and use. Microbial, plant, animal, and synthetic poisons differ in source, while solids, liquids, gases, vapours, and aerosols differ in how easily they are absorbed. Metallic poisons often accumulate slowly, organic chemicals pass membranes more readily, and acids or alkalis injure tissues by corrosion.

## Exposure by Injection and Ingestion

Injection introduces the entire dose directly into the body, making intravenous exposure especially rapid and dangerous. Intramuscular, subcutaneous, and intraperitoneal routes differ by blood flow and absorption rate. Ingestion is the most common exposure route. Absorption depends on ionization, pH, lipid solubility, surface area, and blood supply, with most absorption occurring in the small intestine.

## Inhalation and Skin Absorption

Inhalation of gases and vapours leads mainly to lung absorption, though water-soluble or reactive gases may be retained in nasal mucosa. Gas transfer depends on partial-pressure gradients between air, blood, and tissues. Skin absorption depends on the stratum corneum, lipid solubility, skin condition, exposure duration, and body site; babies can be especially vulnerable to some topical toxicants.

Inhaled aerosols behave differently from gases. Particles must be small enough to enter and deposit in the respiratory tract; larger particles tend to lodge in upper passages, while smaller ones can reach deeper lung regions. Dusts and fibres can therefore be more dangerous in the lungs than on the skin.

Gas absorption is rapid because alveoli lie close to capillaries and present a huge exchange surface. Highly water-soluble or reactive gases may be trapped before reaching the lung, while less reactive gases continue until partial pressures in air, blood, and tissues approach equilibrium.

## Dose, Accumulation, and Risk

![[attachments/poisons_and_poisoning_poison_001.png|420]]

*Dose-response curve showing biological response as dose increases*

All chemicals can be toxic at sufficient dose. The dose-response relationship links concentration at the active site to biological effect. LD50 measures the dose killing 50 percent of exposed subjects and helps compare potency. Hazard or risk depends not only on toxicity but also on exposure. Repeated exposure may cause toxic accumulation when elimination is slower than dosing intervals.

## Distribution, Excretion, and Biotransformation

After absorption, toxicants enter [[Blood|blood]], lymph, interstitial spaces, tissues, and target organs. Excretion occurs through urine, bile, sweat, milk, and lungs. Kidneys filter and secrete chemicals; lipid-soluble compounds are often reabsorbed, while water-soluble ones are excreted. Biotransformation, mainly in the liver, modifies chemicals through phase I reactions and phase II conjugation to increase excretion, though sometimes it creates more toxic products.

Orally absorbed chemicals often meet the liver before reaching the rest of the body, creating a first-pass effect that can reduce systemic exposure. Phase I reactions add or expose functional groups, and phase II reactions attach endogenous molecules; the result is usually easier excretion but sometimes a reactive intermediate.

## Toxic Responses

Toxic responses may be morphological, functional, or both. Silicosis, for example, creates structural lung nodules that eventually impair breathing. Allergies are immune-system responses requiring prior sensitization and far lower doses than ordinary toxicity. Teratogenesis produces prenatal defects, growth retardation, fetal death, or later cancer. Carcinogenesis creates benign or malignant tumours, while mutagenesis alters genes and may cause cancer, birth defects, stillbirth, or heritable change.

The therapeutic index helps compare drug safety by relating a lethal dose to an effective therapeutic dose. A wide index gives more room for dosage error, while a narrow one makes ordinary treatment and poisoning closer together, as with some cardiac and psychiatric drugs.

Developmental toxicity depends strongly on timing. During organ formation, injury to dividing cells can malform the organ whose critical period is underway; later fetal injury may slow growth, kill the fetus, or create effects that appear after birth.

## Agricultural Chemicals

Agricultural poisons include pesticides: insecticides, herbicides, fungicides, fumigants, and rodenticides. Organophosphate and carbamate insecticides inhibit acetylcholinesterase, exaggerating parasympathetic effects and potentially causing respiratory depression. Chlorinated hydrocarbons such as DDT stimulate the central nervous system and can cause convulsions; DDT also persists in food chains and damages bird reproduction. Pyrethrins are less toxic but can cause allergy.

## Herbicides and Rodenticides

Herbicides usually have low human toxicity, though high doses of 2,4-D can cause muscular and neurological symptoms. Agent Orange combined 2,4-D and 2,4,5-T and was contaminated by dioxin, which causes chloracne and cancer or birth defects in laboratory animals. Warfarin, first developed as an anticoagulant drug, also serves as a rodenticide by causing fatal internal bleeding.

Other agricultural chemicals show that intended use does not equal harmlessness. Daminozide improved apple appearance and storage life, yet animal carcinogenicity concerns sharply reduced its use; PCBs, though not herbicides, likewise illustrate skin toxicity through chloracne caused by sebaceous-gland damage.

## Industrial Chemicals

Industrial chemicals include household and workplace substances not primarily agricultural or medicinal. Hydrocarbons such as gasoline, toluene, n-hexane, and benzene often depress the central nervous system; n-hexane damages peripheral nerves, and benzene can damage bone marrow and cause leukemia. Alcohols can depress the nervous system, while methanol may cause blindness through formic acid and acidosis.

## Drugs and Health Products

Painkillers cause many poisonings. Aspirin disrupts cellular fuel use, heat regulation, pH, and the nervous system; acetaminophen damages the liver; morphine and narcotics depress respiration. Benzodiazepines are relatively safe but depress coordination and speech, while barbiturates and related sleeping pills can cause respiratory and cardiovascular failure. Antipsychotics, antidepressants, lithium, cardiovascular drugs, and antiasthmatics each have characteristic toxic patterns.

## Antiseptics, Vitamins, and Drugs of Abuse

Antiseptics may irritate the gastrointestinal tract or injure organs; hexachlorophene is especially dangerous in babies. Vitamin excess can poison: vitamin A damages skin, liver, and nerves; vitamin C can cause kidney stones; vitamin K can promote red-cell destruction and kernicterus in newborns. Drugs of abuse such as amphetamines, cocaine, phencyclidine, heroin, and methaqualone act strongly on the central nervous system.

## Biological Poisons

Biotoxins include microbial toxins, phytotoxins from plants, and zootoxins from animals. They may aid predation, defense, territorial competition, or ecological exclusion. Microbial toxins are widespread, while poisonous plants and animals are most abundant in warm-temperate and tropical regions. Some substances toxic to one organism may serve vital functions in the source organism.

## Mycotoxins and Mushrooms

Mycotoxins are fungal poisons from microfungi and larger mushrooms. Ergot and poisonous mushrooms have long been known, but modern mycotoxicology expanded after turkey X disease and the discovery of aflatoxin in 1960. Mycotoxins matter because some are carcinogenic and can enter the food supply. Most deaths from mushroom poisoning involve Amanita; wild mushrooms should be eaten only after expert identification.

## Radiation and Lasers

[[Radiation]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radiation)) is energy moving through space or matter as particles or electromagnetic waves. Ionizing radiation, including alpha particles, beta particles, neutrons, positrons, gamma rays, and X-rays, damages tissues by producing ions and reactive radicals that attack DNA and proteins. Nonionizing radiation is generally less toxic, but lasers can burn skin, and visible lasers can damage the retina.

## Core Equations

Health and behavioural sciences often quantify risk, dose, change over time, and population variation:

$$C(t)=C_0e^{-kt}$$

This models concentration falling exponentially over time, often used for clearance, decay, or elimination processes.

$$z=\frac{x-\mu}{\sigma}$$

A z-score tells how many standard deviations a value lies above or below the mean.

$$\text{risk}=\frac{\text{cases}}{\text{population}}$$

This ratio turns the preceding idea into a measurable comparison, with the numerator as the quantity of interest and the denominator as the scale used for comparison.

## Key Events & Developments

- 16th century: Paracelsus introduced dose and experimental study of poisons.
- 19th century: Matthieu Orfila linked toxin chemistry to biological effects.
- 1960: Turkey X disease led to discovery of aflatoxin.
- Modern toxicology: Dose-response, LD50, biotransformation, and target-organ concepts became central.

## Key People

- Paracelsus ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paracelsus)): Founder figure of dose-based toxicology.
- Matthieu Orfila ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matthieu_Orfila)): Correlated toxic chemistry and biological effects.

## Interesting Facts

- All chemicals can be toxic at sufficient dose.
- Botulinum toxin is extremely toxic but low-risk when exposure is prevented.
- DDT accumulates in food chains and can make bird eggs fragile.
- Alpha particles penetrate poorly but ionize intensely along short paths.
