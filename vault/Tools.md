## What Tools Do

Tools ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tool)) are devices that help people apply force, control motion, measure conditions, or transform materials. A knife concentrates force on a sharp edge, a hammer delivers impact, a lever multiplies force, a drill converts rotation into cutting, and a ruler makes measurement repeatable. Tools extend human capabilities, but they also require knowledge: the same tool can produce careful work or damage depending on design, skill, maintenance, and purpose.

The simplest hand tools are powered directly by the user. Machine tools use external power to cut, shape, drill, grind, or form materials with greater speed and precision. The difference is gradual rather than absolute. A hand plane, a foot-powered lathe, a computer-controlled milling machine, and a robotic production cell all use similar mechanical principles but differ in power, control, scale, and the amount of human judgment built into the process.

## Mechanical Principles

Many tools use one or more simple machines: levers, wheels and axles, pulleys, inclined planes, wedges, and screws. A wedge turns a pushing force into sideways splitting force; a screw turns rotation into linear motion; a pulley can change the direction of a pull or reduce the force needed to lift a load. These principles do not create energy, but they make a task easier by changing the size, direction, distance, or timing of a force.

Precision is another tool function. Measuring tools, jigs, fixtures, templates, gauges, and calibration standards allow parts to be made consistently and to fit together. Interchangeable parts became important in modern manufacturing because a replacement component could fit without being individually hand-filed. Precision tools also support science, medicine, construction, navigation, and digital technology by making small differences visible and repeatable.

## History of Toolmaking

Early stone tools show that toolmaking is an ancient human skill. Choppers and flakes provided cutting edges; later hand axes, scrapers, points, and burins allowed more specialized work with meat, hides, wood, bone, and plant materials. Hafting—attaching a stone or bone point to a handle—improved reach, control, and impact, turning a sharp fragment into a spear, axe, adze, or knife.

Metals changed toolmaking because copper, bronze, iron, and steel can be shaped, sharpened, hardened, and repaired in ways stone cannot. Iron and steel tools improved farming, carpentry, mining, transport, and warfare. Later industrial production made tools cheaper and more standardized, while electricity, internal combustion engines, hydraulics, electronics, and computers created machine tools capable of highly precise and repeated operations.

## Tools, Skill, and Society

Tools do not replace skill; they redistribute it. A craft worker may use a hand tool to make many minute decisions, while a machine operator may need knowledge of setup, programming, safety, materials, quality control, and maintenance. [[Automation]] can remove dangerous or repetitive tasks, but it can also reduce workers’ control or displace people whose skills are no longer demanded. Good tool design therefore considers usability, safety, repairability, access, and the conditions of work.

Digital tools extend the idea further. Software, sensors, calculators, design programs, and artificial intelligence systems help people model, measure, communicate, and make decisions. They are still tools because their value depends on the goals, data, assumptions, and judgment of their users. Learning how a tool works—including its limits—is as important as learning how to operate it.

## Key Events & Developments

- More than 2.5 million years ago: early stone tools provided durable cutting and pounding edges.
- Hafting combined handles with stone or bone components, increasing reach and mechanical advantage.
- Industrial era: machine tools and interchangeable parts made large-scale standardized production possible.

## Key People

- Early toolmakers: developed stone-working techniques that supported cutting, hunting, and craft.
- Craft workers: refined hand-tool skills through practice, apprenticeship, and material knowledge.
- Engineers: design tools and machines that apply mechanical, electrical, and digital control.

## Interesting Facts
