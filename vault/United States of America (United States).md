## Introduction

United States ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States)) is a federal republic of 50 states in North America, including the 48 contiguous states, Alaska in the far northwest of the continent, and Hawaii in the mid-Pacific. The contiguous states are bordered by Canada, the Atlantic Ocean, the Gulf of Mexico, Mexico, and the Pacific Ocean. The national capital is Washington, D.C., created as a federal district in 1790. The country is one of the largest in the world by area and one of the most influential by population, economy, military power, cultural reach, and political example.

United States ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States)) variety is one of its central facts. Its physical environments include Arctic Alaska, subtropical Florida and Hawaii, wet forests, dry deserts, high mountains, flat prairies, large rivers, and huge urban regions. Its population is also highly diverse, shaped by Indigenous peoples, descendants of enslaved Africans, European settlement, and sustained immigration from many parts of the world. The country’s economy became the largest in the world by gross national product, supported by resources, agriculture, industry, finance, technology, services, investment, and international trade.

United States ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States)) history is young compared with many older states, but its development was rapid. It was the first European colony to separate successfully from its parent country and build a national government on the principle that sovereignty rests with citizens. Its early history centred on expansion, settlement, economic growth, slavery, and civil war. In the 20th century it became a world power, and after World War II it was one of the two main Cold War superpowers before emerging as the strongest military power after the Soviet collapse.

## Land and Relief

United States ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States)) landforms are organized around a broad interior lowland framed by mountains and coastal plains. The centre of the country stretches from the ancient Canadian shield toward the Gulf of Mexico, while the Appalachian Mountains rise in the east and the Western Cordillera rises in the west. The Appalachian system is old, eroded, and generally lower than the western mountains. The Cordillera is younger, higher, more broken, and much more varied, including the Rocky Mountains, Pacific ranges, basins, plateaus, valleys, and deserts.

Appalachian Mountains run from northeastern Alabama to the Canadian border. They are the eroded remains of older mountains, with topography shaped by resistant and weaker rocks. Their main features include the Blue Ridge, the Piedmont, the Ridge and Valley country, and the Great Valley. Mount Mitchell in North Carolina is the highest Appalachian summit. The Great Valley became one of the major historic corridors through the mountain barrier, linking the Hudson region, Pennsylvania, the Shenandoah Valley, and the Cumberland Valley.

Atlantic Plain stretches along the eastern and southeastern margin of the country and continues beneath shallow coastal waters as the continental shelf. It is low, flat, young, and often swampy. Florida is an upwarped part of the plain, very low and exposed to storms. The lower Mississippi occupies a downwarped area filled with river alluvium. Chesapeake Bay and many other estuaries are drowned river valleys, while barrier islands and sandbars line much of the coast and mark the shifting boundary between land and sea.

Western Cordillera contains the Rocky Mountains, Pacific coastal ranges, and a wide interior of basins and plateaus. The Southern Rockies include high upwarped ranges, alpine meadows, glaciated peaks, and Colorado summits above 14,000 feet. The Middle Rockies include varied ranges, basins, and Yellowstone and Grand Teton landscapes. The Pacific edge includes California Coast Ranges, the Sierra Nevada, the Cascades, the Klamath Mountains, the Olympic Mountains, and important lowland troughs such as California’s Central Valley and Oregon’s Willamette Valley.

Western Intermontane Region lies between the Rockies and Pacific ranges. It includes the Colorado Plateau, Basin and Range province, and Columbia Basin. The Colorado Plateau is marked by flat-lying sedimentary rocks, mesas, buttes, coloured cliffs, and deep canyons, with the Grand Canyon as the best-known example. The Basin and Range contains north-south fault-block mountains separated by desert basins, salt flats, and alluvial plains. The Columbia Basin is shaped by volcanic and river processes.

## Drainage, Climate, Plants, and Animals

United States ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States)) drainage includes some of the world’s largest and most useful river and lake systems. In the humid East, rivers provided cheap inland transportation. In the West, many rivers were less navigable but became vital for irrigation and hydroelectric power. The Mississippi, with the Ohio and Missouri, drains much of the midcontinent. The Great Lakes-St. Lawrence system, connected to the Mississippi-Ohio system through Chicago canals and the Illinois River, forms a major inland waterway shared with Canada.

United States ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States)) waterways were also heavily polluted by industrial, agricultural, and human waste. Eastern rivers carried commerce and settlement, but large waterways became public sewers before cleanup efforts began. The Missouri was long difficult to navigate because of seasonality and sandbars, but dams, locks, and dredging opened it to barge traffic in the mid-20th century. Western rivers, especially the Colorado and Columbia systems, became central to irrigation, settlement, flood control, and power.

United States ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States)) climate is shaped by middle-latitude location, continental size, oceans, mountains, and lowlands. The contiguous states lie mostly between the tropic of Cancer and 50 degrees north, but the landmass produces strong extremes. The Atlantic and Pacific affect the margins differently, while north-south mountain chains restrict the movement of moist Pacific air and allow Arctic and tropical air masses to move across the interior. Climate controls vegetation, soils, water supply, settlement, farming, and hazards.

Humid East was the main region of early European settlement and originally contained extensive forests. Its soils were leached by rainfall, and its vegetation changed gradually with latitude and winter severity. Northern areas contained coniferous forest and thin acidic soils, while farther south mixed and broadleaf forests became dominant. The region’s forests, soils, wildlife, farms, towns, and transport corridors became foundational for early American history.

Humid Pacific Coast is narrow, mild, wet in winter, and strongly shaped by Pacific air and mountains. Western Washington, Oregon, and northern California support enormous evergreen forests, including commercially important timber. Farther south, California’s Mediterranean climate brings wet winters and dry summers. Cool marine air can create fog along the coast and temperature inversions around Los Angeles, while inland valleys can become very hot in summer.

Dry West occupies the intermontane basins and much of the western Great Plains. Water is the controlling fact. True desert is mainly in the Southwest, where sparse shrubs, annual plants, salt flats, dunes, and mineral-rich soils dominate. Much of the region is semiarid steppe with short grasses and brush. The old idea of a Great American Desert exaggerated the extent of true desert, but it reflected the difficulty eastern settlers felt when crossing or occupying the arid West.

Humid-arid transition stretches roughly between the 95th and 100th meridians from Texas to North Dakota. It is a broad zone where rainfall can alternate between surplus and deficit, making agriculture risky but potentially very productive. Native prairie grasses once dominated much of it, supported by deep fertile soils. The boundary between eastern forest and grassland was subtle and partly shaped by Indigenous burning, climate, soil, and later plowing.

United States ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States)) plant life includes northern coniferous forests, eastern hardwood forests, coastal pine barrens, southern swamps, prairie grasslands, desert shrubs, mountain conifer forests, redwoods, alpine tundra, and tropical vegetation in southern Florida and Hawaii. Human settlement deeply altered these plant regions through cutting, farming, grazing, fire, drainage, irrigation, and urbanization. The return of wild vegetation on abandoned land shows that climate and soil still exert strong long-term controls.

United States ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States)) animal life belongs mainly to the Nearctic realm, but European settlement changed it sharply. Bison, wolves, bears, beavers, otters, and many birds lost much of their range through hunting and habitat destruction. Fences, railroads, highways, plowing, chemicals, and introduced species changed animal geography. Some widespread animals, such as white-tailed deer, black bear, coyote, bobcat, beaver, muskrat, and snapping turtle, survived across broad regions, while many predators and large mammals became rare or restricted.

## Settlement Patterns and Regions

![[attachments/united_states_of_america_united_states_003.webp|420]]

*City Lights of the United States 2012*

United States ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States)) settlement transformed a landscape long occupied by Native peoples. European and later American settlement produced rapid and often destructive change, with high mobility, speculative land use, strong individualism, and transportation networks that reshaped both rural and urban areas. By the mid-20th century, rural and urban ways of life were converging: farms used urban services and technology, while suburbs blurred the line between city and countryside.

American cities grew from changing transport systems. Colonial cities such as New York, Jamestown, Charleston, Philadelphia, Newport, Savannah, and Portland were mostly ports that collected interior products and exchanged them for European, Caribbean, or African goods. Canals and roads later favoured Pittsburgh, Cincinnati, Buffalo, and St. Louis. Railroads then lifted Chicago, Atlanta, Indianapolis, Minneapolis, Fort Worth, Tacoma, and other hubs. Motor transport, air travel, and metropolitan sprawl later reshaped urban growth again.

American urbanization often produced fragmented metropolitan areas. A single social and economic urban region could include many municipalities, counties, and sometimes states. This fragmentation made planning difficult and contributed to disorderly suburbs, inner-city decline, traffic congestion, and uneven services. Some cities developed strong individuality through planned design or dramatic location, including Savannah, Washington, Salt Lake City, San Francisco, Seattle, Miami, Phoenix, and Los Angeles.

New England was a leading source of national ideas, education, religion, literature, technology, and settlement expansion from about 1780 to 1880. Its early population was relatively homogeneous, shaped by British nonconformist religion, town life, thrift, education, and communal habits. Boston became its cultural and economic centre. New England people and values spread into New York, northern New Jersey, northern Pennsylvania, the Upper Midwest, Hawaii, and Pacific missionary and commercial networks.

The South was the largest original Anglo-American culture area and long the most distinctive from national norms. It developed from British settlement, plantation agriculture, slavery, rural conservatism, diffuse settlement, and contact with Native peoples. Chesapeake society, the Carolina and Georgia lowcountry, the backcountry, and later the Deep South shared some features but were never identical. The South’s differences in economy, politics, race, dialect, food, folklore, religion, and social customs helped create the sectional conflict that led to civil war.

The Midland centred on southeastern Pennsylvania, parts of New York, New Jersey, Delaware, and Maryland. It was more polyglot than New England or the South, with English, Welsh, German, Dutch, Swedish, Finnish, Irish, Scotch-Irish, and other elements. William Penn’s colony created a prosperous agricultural and commercial society with religious pluralism and urban development. Midland influences moved through the interior and helped shape pluralistic towns, farms, churches, and industries.

The Midwest developed as a hybrid of New England, Midland, and Southern influences. Its routes converged in the upper Ohio valley, then widened into a broad interior culture region. The Upper Midwest retained more New England and northern European influence, while the Lower Midwest showed stronger Southern and Midland features. Rich farmland, towns, railroads, German and Scandinavian immigration, Protestant variety, and later industry made the region seem to many Americans like a national average.

## People

United States ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States)) population grew with exceptional speed from the 18th century onward. Immigration, internal migration, high mobility, abundant land, industrial jobs, and urban opportunity shaped the country. About 60 million immigrants arrived in the 18th and 19th centuries. Movement first ran east to west and rural to urban, then from the South to the North and Midwest, and later from older northern cities to suburbs and to the South, Southwest, and West.

United States ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States)) prosperity at the start of the 21st century coexisted with serious social problems. Many Americans enjoyed high levels of material comfort, education, and security, but poverty, crime, drug abuse, pollution, energy concerns, urban sprawl, voter apathy, AIDS, divorce, litigation, health-care problems, and unequal schools remained persistent concerns. The country’s public debates often reflected the contrast between affluence and insecurity.

European Americans include many groups whose identities changed over time. Yankees from New England carried education, Protestant religion, business, law, literature, philanthropy, and Republican politics westward. Southern whites moved through Tennessee, Kentucky, Arkansas, Missouri, Oklahoma, and Texas and remained tied to rural life and Democratic politics for a long period. Pennsylvania Germans, Amish communities, Scandinavians, Germans, Scots, Scotch-Irish, Welsh, Dutch, Italians, Poles, Jews, and many others preserved varying degrees of ethnic memory and local identity.

African Americans were brought in large numbers as enslaved people and lived mainly in the South. In 1790 enslaved and free Black people made up about one-fifth of the population. Before the Civil War, the Underground Railroad helped some escape slavery. Between 1910 and 1970, about 6.5 million Black Americans moved from the rural South to northern cities. The civil rights movement of the 1950s and 1960s attacked segregation and unequal citizenship, producing major gains in law, income, education, and politics, though deep urban poverty and discrimination continued.

Hispanic Americans are diverse rather than monolithic. Mexican Americans include descendants of people living in territory once part of Mexico and later migrants from Mexico and Central America. Puerto Ricans are U.S. citizens because of Puerto Rico’s commonwealth relationship, and migration between the island and mainland has been fluid. Cuban Americans include those who fled Fidel Castro’s revolution in 1959 and later Mariel refugees in the 1980s. Other migrants came from the Caribbean, Central America, and South America.

Asian Americans include Chinese, Japanese, Koreans, Filipinos, Asian Indians, Vietnamese, Taiwanese, Pacific Islanders, and others. Chinese labourers arrived in large numbers in the mid-19th century, especially for railroad work, but Chinese and Japanese immigrants faced severe exclusion and discrimination. Japanese Americans were interned during World War II. Later immigration and social change allowed many Asian American communities to advance economically and educationally, especially in Hawaii, California, and major metropolitan areas.

Middle Eastern Americans include Lebanese Christians, Palestinian Muslims, Armenians, Iranians, Arabs, and other groups. Early Arab immigration included Lebanese Christians on the East Coast, while later Arab and Muslim communities became prominent in the Detroit area. Armenians settled especially in California, where Iranians also concentrated later. Some newcomers retained national customs, clothing, language, and religious institutions while also joining wider American urban life.

Native Americans include many peoples rather than one simple ethnic group. Eastern groups often experienced centuries of coexistence, intermarriage, and partial assimilation, while western expansion forced many onto reservations. Federal policy shifted between assimilation and cultural preservation. The Native population rose from a low point of about 235,000 in 1900 to about 2.5 million by the early 21st century. Reservations often faced deep poverty, though casino development created wealth in some places, and intertribal organizations gave Native peoples a national voice.

American religion is highly plural because the federal government never established a national church and because immigration and individualism encouraged denominational variety. Christianity remained dominant, with Roman Catholicism as the largest single denomination and Protestant churches as the majority tradition. Native-born denominations included Disciples of Christ, Latter-day Saints, Seventh-day Adventists, Jehovah’s Witnesses, Christian Scientists, and many evangelical and Pentecostal groups. Judaism, Islam, Hinduism, Buddhism, and many other traditions added to religious diversity.

Immigration policy changed sharply over time. Mass immigration continued until after World War I. The 1924 Immigration Act created quotas based on national origins and strongly favoured northwestern Europe over southeastern Europe. The 1965 reforms abolished the old quota system and changed the flow of immigration, increasing arrivals from Asia, Latin America, the Caribbean, and the Middle East. Later policy in the 1980s and 1990s included amnesty for some undocumented migrants, higher admission limits, and refugee validation systems.

## Economy

United States ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States)) became the world’s greatest economic power in gross domestic product and one of the leaders in GDP per person. With less than 5 percent of the world’s population, it produced about one-fifth of global output. Its economy is marked by size, resilience, innovation, flexibility, resources, consumption, investment, and global trade. It is also marked by chronic trade deficits, household debt, unequal income growth, energy dependence, aging-related costs, and pressure on public budgets.

United States ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States)) government plays a limited direct role in running businesses but regulates many industries. Federal agencies oversee worker safety, air and water pollution, food and drug safety, transportation safety, fuel economy, antitrust law, pensions, securities, rail passenger service, and certain public corporations. Defense and aerospace spending give the federal government major influence over some industries. Social Security and Medicare shape public finance, retirement, health care, and labour expectations.

[[Taxation]] supplies nearly all federal revenue. Personal income tax is the largest source, corporate income tax provides a smaller share, excise taxes fall on products such as alcohol, gasoline, and tobacco, and payroll taxes fund Social Security and Medicare. Estate and gift taxes provide only a small portion. State and local governments also levy sales, income, property, and excise taxes, making taxation a federal, state, and local structure.

Labour force changed from farm and factory work toward services. By the early 21st century, services employed more than three-fourths of workers, while manufacturing and industrial trades employed less than one-fifth. Union membership peaked in the 1950s and then declined to less than 15 percent of workers, many of them public employees. The National Education Association represented teachers, while AFL-CIO divisions and the Change to Win coalition reflected changing union strategy.

[[Minerals and Rocks (Mineral)|Minerals]] and biological resources remain important. The country has petroleum fields in Alaska, California, the Gulf of Mexico, Louisiana, and Oklahoma; natural gas reserves; major coal deposits in Appalachia and Wyoming; iron ore in Minnesota and Michigan; and copper, zinc, lead, gold, silver, molybdenum, uranium, phosphates, potash, sulfur, stone, and clays. Farming uses more than two-fifths of the land, while forests, much of them privately owned, supply timber and hardwood resources.

Power generation relies mainly on coal-burning plants and nuclear power, with hydroelectricity concentrated in Pacific and Mountain states and contributing a smaller share. The country’s energy economy affects transport, industry, households, foreign policy, environmental debates, and regional development. Rising energy prices in the early 21st century increased costs for homes, businesses, transport, and government.

Manufacturing ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manufacturing)) declined in employment but remained important in output. Transportation equipment, motor vehicles, aircraft, space equipment, computers, telecommunications, software, hardware, drugs, biotechnology, health services, food products, chemicals, machinery, energy, and insurance all contributed to productivity. Services grew faster than manufacturing after the mid-20th century, but technological progress helped manufacturing output stay near one-fifth of GDP.

Finance ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Finance)) is organized around the Federal Reserve System, the Treasury, commercial banks, savings institutions, insurance companies, securities firms, and exchanges. The Federal Reserve’s Board of Governors and 12 regional banks regulate credit and influence money supply. Banking laws in the 1970s and 1980s encouraged expansion and mergers, creating large regional and national financial corporations. New York, Chicago, and other cities host important securities, commodities, futures, options, and carbon-credit exchanges.

Foreign trade is crucial because the combined value of imports and exports equals a significant share of national output. Canada, Mexico, Japan, China, and the United Kingdom are major trading partners. Exports include electrical and office machinery, chemicals, motor vehicles, airplanes, aviation parts, and scientific equipment. Imports include manufactured goods, petroleum, fuel, machinery, and transport equipment. Direct U.S. investment abroad strongly affects Canada, Mexico, China, Latin America, Europe, and Asia.

[[Transportation]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transport)) created national mobility and economic integration. The Interstate Highway System, begun in the 1950s and later named for Dwight D. Eisenhower, links most cities with at least 50,000 people and carries a large share of motor traffic. Automobile ownership is widespread, while public transit remains important in cities such as New York, Chicago, Philadelphia, and Boston. Railroads carry about one-third of intercity freight, and Amtrak took over much passenger service after private rail companies withdrew.

## Government and Society

United States ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States)) Constitution created a federal system that divided authority between national and state governments and separated power among executive, legislative, and judicial branches. It replaced Confederation government and entered force in 1789. The Constitution is the oldest written national constitution still in effect, and it is treated as a living document because interpretation changes over time. Amendments require extraordinary agreement by Congress or conventions and by the states.

Executive branch is headed by the president, who serves as commander in chief, negotiates treaties, appoints judges, ambassadors, and cabinet officials, and acts as head of state. In practice, presidential power expanded to include legislative leadership, foreign policy direction, diplomacy, party leadership, and crisis management. Cabinet departments, the Office of Management and Budget, Council of Economic Advisers, National Security Council, White House staff, independent regulatory commissions, government corporations, and executive agencies all form part of the federal executive structure.

Legislative branch is Congress, divided into the Senate and House of Representatives. Congress levies taxes, borrows money, regulates interstate commerce, declares war, disciplines members, sets procedure, and can impeach and remove officials. Revenue bills begin in the House, but both chambers must pass identical legislation. Presidents may veto bills, and Congress may override vetoes by two-thirds votes. Congressional support agencies examine federal spending and proposed legislation.

Suffrage expanded gradually. Legal barriers based on property, religion, race, gender, and age were removed over time. The Fifteenth Amendment gave former enslaved men voting rights in 1870, but Southern devices such as literacy tests, poll taxes, and grandfather clauses blocked many Black voters until the Twenty-fourth Amendment and Voting Rights Act. Women gained national suffrage through the Nineteenth Amendment in 1920. The Twenty-sixth Amendment lowered the voting age to 18 in 1971.

American elections include federal, state, and local offices, judges in some states, ballot initiatives, and referendums. The two-party system survived because the winner-take-all plurality system punishes minor parties, national parties need broad coalitions, ballot access is difficult, and campaigns are expensive. Primary elections made candidate selection more voter-centred and less controlled by party bosses, while public funding and media campaigning changed presidential politics.

Security changed after the September 11 attacks of 2001. The Department of Homeland Security consolidated more than 20 agencies and took responsibility for border and transportation security, emergency preparedness, infrastructure protection, intelligence analysis, and science and technology. The Secret Service moved under its jurisdiction. The Department of Defense oversees the Army, Navy, Marine Corps, and Air Force, while the Coast Guard falls under Homeland Security. Conscription ended in 1973, but young men must register for selective service.

Housing ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Housing)) is mostly private and owner-occupied, with many detached single-family homes. Slums and poor housing led to building codes, sanitation rules, federal housing loans, public housing, fair-housing laws, and anti-discrimination orders. Restrictive covenants once excluded racial and religious groups, but the Supreme Court made them unenforceable in 1948, and later federal action banned discrimination in federally aided housing. Racial disparities in home ownership remained large.

[[Education]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Education)) is mainly a state and local responsibility, but federal influence grew through land-grant colleges, school lunches, American Indian education, research grants, student loans, veterans’ education, desegregation enforcement, and accountability laws. Public elementary and secondary education is free and compulsory, usually from about age 7 through 16, and literacy exceeds 95 percent. The United States also has thousands of colleges and universities, including private, public, religious, research, community, and military institutions.

School integration remained a major social and political issue after legal segregation was struck down. Courts required busing in some districts to achieve integration, including the Charlotte-Mecklenburg case in 1971. Busing caused controversy because it often required long commutes and strong local resistance. The No Child Left Behind Act of 2002 increased federal involvement by requiring standards and accountability in public elementary and secondary education.

## Cultural Life

American culture is difficult to define because the country has no established art comparable to an established church. Culture is made by individuals, local communities, markets, universities, religious groups, ethnic groups, and mass media. A recurring tension runs between democratic popular culture and refined elite culture. Commercial culture sometimes reduces art to entertainment, but it also gives common experience artistic power and creates large audiences for music, film, theatre, fiction, television, sports, and visual arts.

American art often moves between high and low forms. Jazz musicians transformed popular songs into improvisational art. Choreographers such as George Balanchine, Paul Taylor, and Twyla Tharp drew on marches, tap, ballroom, and street movement. Joseph Cornell used everyday printed images in dreamlike boxes. Pop artists such as Andy Warhol, Roy Lichtenstein, and Claes Oldenburg treated commercial images, comics, soup cans, movie stars, and ordinary objects with techniques once linked to high art.

[[American Literature|American literature]] after World War II often focused on differences within the national story. Southern writers such as William Faulkner, Eudora Welty, and Flannery O’Connor explored defeat, memory, and tragedy. Jewish writers such as Saul Bellow, Bernard Malamud, and Philip Roth examined affluence, unease, exile, and identity. African American writers such as Langston Hughes, Ralph Ellison, and Toni Morrison confronted deferred promises and racial experience. Feminist writing expanded attention to women’s lives and perspectives.

Postmodern culture questioned the clean forms and utopian claims of modernism. In architecture, Robert Venturi, Denise Scott Brown, Peter Eisenman, Philip Johnson, and Michael Graves used irony, ornament, historical reference, paradox, and deliberate impurity. In painting, photography, and literature, borrowed imagery, pastiche, collage, artifice, and melancholy about authenticity became common. The line between modern and postmodern remained hard to draw because many devices overlapped.

American theatre produced a distinctive musical tradition and a powerful vernacular stage language. The musical theatre flourished in the 1940s and 1950s through works such as Guys and Dolls and later through Stephen Sondheim’s Company, Sweeney Todd, and Into the Woods. David Mamet captured a harsh, repetitive, obscene, and eloquent American speech in plays such as Glengarry Glen Ross and Speed-the-Plow.

[[Motion Pictures|Motion pictures]] became a central American art form. Hollywood commercial film was long dismissed by some critics as compromised by commerce, but French critics and later American critics recognized the artistry of directors such as John Ford, Howard Hawks, William Wyler, D.W. Griffith, and Orson Welles. Later filmmakers such as Francis Ford Coppola, Martin Scorsese, George Lucas, Spike Lee, Stephen Soderbergh, Joel and Ethan Coen, Wes Anderson, Nicole Holofcener, and Kenneth Lonergan worked across studio and independent traditions.

Television became the common atmosphere of American culture after the mid-1950s. It carried entertainment, news, sports, war, politics, reality programs, game shows, talent contests, legal drama, and national crisis into homes. Critics called it shallow and alienating, but it could not be reduced to one effect or one form. Television became more influential than any single older art form because of its reach, repetition, and ability to make shared images out of daily life.

American music created a national and global sound. Jazz moved from folk, dance, and popular roots into art music through figures such as Louis Armstrong, Duke Ellington, and later performers. Popular singers such as Billie Holiday, Doris Day, Sarah Vaughan, Peggy Lee, Joe Williams, Judy Garland, Patsy Cline, Willie Nelson, Tony Bennett, and many others formed a national soundtrack. Elvis Presley’s 1954 Sun Records recordings mixed country, rhythm and blues, and pop into rock and roll, a hybrid music spread by records, radio, television, and youth culture.

American dance developed through modern dance pioneers. Isadora Duncan helped free dance from classical ballet rules, though much of her work was in Europe. Ruth St. Denis and Ted Shawn kept dance alive in the 1920s. Martha Graham created a powerful modern dance language, while Paul Taylor and Twyla Tharp drew from popular movement, street behaviour, ballroom dance, and recorded music. Their work showed how American high art could feed on popular culture without being consumed by it.

[[Sports]] are central to American culture because they provide common myths, commercial entertainment, civic identity, and material for art. College football and basketball and professional baseball, football, basketball, and hockey bind cities, regions, and classes through shared events. Baseball, especially, became a national pastime and a source of mythmaking comparable to older epic traditions. Sports stories, statistics, heroes, scandals, rivalries, and memories form a cultural language many Americans share.

American audiences changed as television weakened older middlebrow magazines and broad educated publics. Collier’s, The Saturday Evening Post, Look, and Life declined or disappeared in old forms, while Harper’s and The Atlantic survived largely through support structures. Universities became important patrons of culture, supporting music, theatre, criticism, museums, and regional arts. This academic support decentralised culture but also raised concerns that some art was made mainly by professors for professors.

## History: Indigenous America and Colonization

Native America existed long before European arrival. The first inhabitants probably came from Asia through the Bering Strait 20,000 to 35,000 years earlier and spread through the continent. Food resources shaped regional cultures: sea mammals and fish supported coastal peoples; acorns mattered in California; bison shaped Plains life; small game, fishing, crops, and gathering supported many eastern and midwestern groups; maize was central in the Southwest. House types, clothing, tools, weapons, transport, crafts, religion, and social organization varied widely.

Native peoples influenced later American civilization through foods, herbs, crops, words, crafts, folklore, war techniques, trade, diplomacy, and intermarriage. They also suffered one of the most tragic conflicts in U.S. history as European and then American expansion took land and undermined independent societies. By the time Columbus arrived, perhaps 1.5 million American Indians lived in what became the continental United States, though estimates vary.

European expansion began with Portuguese and Spanish exploration before English colonization succeeded. Portugal developed Atlantic and African routes, reached India, and claimed Brazil. Spain conquered Caribbean, Mexican, and Peruvian empires. France explored Newfoundland and the St. Lawrence and tried colonies in Florida and Brazil. England lagged at first but later founded lasting colonies along the Atlantic coast, where private investment, religious conflict, trade, and imperial rivalry shaped settlement.

Virginia began with the Virginia Company and Jamestown. The colony struggled because many settlers expected quick profit and avoided the labour needed for survival. New charters, more capital, harsh discipline under Sir Thomas Dale, and John Rolfe’s high-grade tobacco crop gave the colony a future. Tobacco required labour and land, encouraging indentured servitude, expansion, conflict with Native peoples, and later slavery.

Maryland was founded as a proprietary colony under Lord Baltimore and his son Cecilius Calvert. It was meant to provide profit and a place where Catholics could live in peace, although Protestants outnumbered Catholics from the beginning. The colony survived with help from Virginia, developed tobacco agriculture, and experienced instability because many settlers were young male servants. After political and religious change in England, Anglicanism became established before the Calverts regained proprietary control.

New England developed through Plymouth and Massachusetts Bay. Pilgrims from Leiden founded Plymouth in 1620, suffered heavy mortality, signed the Mayflower Compact, and gradually built a self-sufficient colony. Puritans in Massachusetts Bay wanted to reform the Church of England from within, but they built a strict religious commonwealth in America. Their General Court, town life, church membership rules, education, and sense of community shaped New England institutions.

Pennsylvania began with William Penn’s grant in 1681 and became a prosperous, tolerant, and diverse colony. Penn’s frames of government struggled to balance council and assembly power, but the 1701 Charter of Privileges gave the lower house legislative authority and preserved Protestant toleration. Germans, Irish, and Scotch-Irish migrants increased population and diversity. Prosperity for European settlers, however, often required the dispossession of Native peoples despite Penn’s earlier ideals.

Carolinas and Georgia developed through proprietors, slavery, Indian wars, and regional divergence. North Carolina became a colony of small and medium farms, partly cut off by its coastline. South Carolina tied itself to the Caribbean and world markets through rice and indigo. Georgia, led by James Oglethorpe and other proprietors, began as a philanthropic and strategic project but later moved toward plantation patterns. Commerce increased across all colonies in the 18th century.

Colonial labour depended on land hunger, indentured service, slavery, and migration. Governors and proprietors used land grants to attract settlers. Many poor Europeans came as indentured servants, working for years in exchange for passage, support, and sometimes land afterward. Slavery became the great exception to the loosening of hierarchy in the New World, especially in plantation regions. The availability of land weakened some old-world constraints while strengthening racial domination.

Colonial culture emphasized practical science, religion, education, newspapers, almanacs, and local institutions. Benjamin Franklin, John Bartram, David Rittenhouse, Cadwallader Colden, Benjamin Rush, and the American Philosophical Society showed the strength of applied science. Literature in European high forms was less developed, but education, printing, public schools in New England, charity schools, academies, and colleges such as Harvard, William and Mary, Yale, Princeton, Pennsylvania, King’s College, Queen’s College, and Dartmouth spread learning.

Great Awakening revived religion in the 1730s and 1740s through preachers such as Theodore Frelinghuysen, Jonathan Edwards, Samuel Davies, and George Whitefield. It reacted against secularization and formal churches by stressing conversion, sin, grace, and emotional experience. It democratized Calvinist theology in practice, encouraged lay participation, split churches, and helped create habits of voluntary organization and popular mobilization that later influenced American politics.

Native response to European expansion varied by region and empire. Spanish missions and garrisons, French fur-trade alliances, and English agricultural settlement produced different relations. English land purchases often misunderstood Native concepts of land use and sovereignty, creating conflict. Alliances among Europeans and Native peoples shaped wars, but Britain’s victory over France reduced Indigenous diplomatic leverage. Pontiac’s resistance in 1763 showed the desire for united resistance, but unity could not overcome the pressure of settler expansion.

## History: Revolution and Early Republic

American Revolution grew from imperial costs, taxation, administration, and constitutional disagreement after Britain’s victory over France. The Proclamation of 1763 tried to limit settlement west of the Appalachians and control Indian relations. British officials believed colonists should help pay defense costs, while colonists argued that taxation without their own consent violated the rights of British subjects. Stamp Act, Townshend duties, customs enforcement, troop quartering, and the Tea Act turned legal disputes into organized resistance.

Continental Congress met in Philadelphia in 1774 after the Coercive Acts and other British actions convinced colonies that collective action was needed. Delegates debated whether rights came from common law, natural rights, colonial legislatures, or imperial membership under the king. The Congress adopted economic pressure through nonimportation, nonconsumption, and planned nonexportation. It still hoped to restore harmony, but it also created habits of intercolonial decision-making and local enforcement.

Independence ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Independence)) became likely in 1776 as fighting continued and Thomas Paine’s Common Sense spread radical arguments. Congress urged colonies to form governments and appointed a committee to draft a declaration. Thomas Jefferson wrote most of the Declaration of Independence, which grounded the new nation in natural rights, equality, and grievances against the crown. Congress voted for independence on July 2 and adopted the declaration on July 4.

Revolutionary War began as a civil conflict inside the British Empire and became international when France, Spain, and the Netherlands joined against Britain. Washington’s army lost New York but revived morale at Trenton and Princeton. Saratoga in 1777 helped bring French support. Valley Forge tested the army. The war moved through northern, middle, western, and southern campaigns until Cornwallis surrendered at Yorktown in 1781. The Treaty of Paris recognized independence in 1783.

Constitutional Convention met in 1787 to fix Confederation weaknesses but created a new national government. The Virginia Plan proposed a strong national structure, while the New Jersey Plan defended equal state authority. The Great Compromise created a bicameral Congress with representation by population in the House and equal representation in the Senate. The Constitution strengthened national power, separated powers, created an electoral college, and included compromises over slavery and representation.

Bill of Rights helped secure ratification and answer Anti-Federalist fears. The first 10 amendments, ratified in 1791, protected speech, press, assembly, religion, arms, jury trial, due process, and other liberties. Unlike Britain, the United States formally separated church and state at the federal level and guaranteed peaceable assembly and press freedom. Ratification was close in key states such as Virginia and New York, showing that the new system was contested from the beginning.

Social revolution after independence was uneven. Republican principles weakened primogeniture and encouraged more equal inheritance, but slavery remained in sharp conflict with liberty and equality. Northern states gradually ended slavery, some Virginia owners manumitted enslaved people, and free Black communities grew. In the South, slavery hardened through white-supremacist ideology, especially in South Carolina and Georgia. Religious denominations also became independent American organizations, while frontier revivals promoted emotional Protestant activism.

Federalist administration under George Washington created national authority. Alexander Hamilton proposed federal assumption of debts, payment at par, a national bank, implied powers, support for industry, and excise taxes. Jefferson and Madison opposed his fiscal program and helped form a Republican opposition. The French Revolution and war between France and Britain divided Americans. John Adams faced naval conflict with France, the XYZ Affair, Alien and Sedition Acts, and state-sovereignty replies in the Virginia and Kentucky resolutions.

Jeffersonian era brought the first party transfer of power in 1800. Jefferson bought Louisiana from Napoleon, more than doubling the country, and sent Lewis and Clark across the continent. He clashed with Chief Justice John Marshall after Marbury v. Madison established judicial review. Neutral trade suffered in the Napoleonic Wars, and Jefferson’s embargo damaged American commerce. Madison inherited tensions with Britain and declared war in 1812. The war ended with the Treaty of Ghent and Andrew Jackson’s victory at New Orleans.

Indian-American conflict worsened after independence because Native peoples now faced a resident and expanding republic rather than distant empires. Tecumseh and the Prophet tried to build unity across Native nations, but Tippecanoe, the War of 1812, Tecumseh’s death, and Jackson’s victory over the Creeks weakened resistance east of the Mississippi. After 1815, expansion, cultural confidence, and federal power pushed Native peoples into increasingly desperate positions.

## History: Expansion, Reform, and Civil War

Era of Good Feelings after 1816 mixed nationalism with conflict. The War of 1812 reduced old resentment toward Britain and encouraged westward attention. Florida was acquired in 1819, the Monroe Doctrine warned Europe against interference in the Americas in 1823, and Marshall Court decisions strengthened national power. The second Bank of the United States, internal improvements, canals, roads, tariffs, and Henry Clay’s American System reflected debates over economic development.

Market revolution changed transport, credit, industry, and politics. The Erie Canal linked the Hudson River to the Great Lakes and strengthened New York City. Other canals connected Philadelphia and Baltimore to western routes, but many failed financially. Railroads grew from the Baltimore and Ohio in 1828 to about 30,000 miles by 1860. Factories, mills, banks, state charters, speculation, panics, and regional markets made economic life more connected and unstable.

Antebellum culture became recognizably American. Longfellow, Poe, Cooper, Hawthorne, Melville, Emerson, Whitman, and others produced literature with American characters and themes. Scientific and practical works by Bowditch, Maury, Lewis and Clark, the Corps of Engineers, and Wilkes influenced navigation and natural knowledge. Webster’s dictionary, McGuffey Readers, popular humour, minstrel shows, Barnum’s entertainments, penny journalism, women’s magazines, and mass literacy created democratic culture.

Antebellum society grew rapidly through immigration, cities, farming, and reform. Irish famine migrants, German Forty-Eighters, Scandinavians, Chinese gold seekers, and utopian communities changed population and culture. Cities improved harbours, police, streets, sanitation, and welfare. Public education expanded, women gained some work and college opportunities, and reformers attacked prisons, debt, capital punishment, treatment of the insane, temperance, diet, labour exploitation, and other social problems.

Jacksonian democracy expanded white male political participation but did not erase inequality. Andrew Jackson was presented as a self-made champion of the people, though he was wealthy and socially conservative. The Democratic Party formed around his candidacy and practical organization. Reform movements flourished, but rich men still held major power, and Native Americans, enslaved people, free Black people, and women were excluded from most political rights.

Abolitionism made slavery the central moral and political issue. William Lloyd Garrison demanded immediate emancipation and denounced the Constitution for tolerating slavery. Other abolitionists, including Theodore Weld, James Birney, Gerrit Smith, Theodore Parker, Julia Ward Howe, Lewis Tappan, Salmon Chase, Lydia Maria Child, David Walker, Robert Forten, and Frederick Douglass, represented varied strategies. Many Northerners disliked abolitionists, but antislavery pressure grew as sectional conflict intensified.

Indian removal followed expansion and racial ideology. Federal policy moved Indian affairs through War and Interior departments and increasingly treated Native nations as wards. The Five Civilized Tribes, including Chickasaw, Cherokee, Creek, Choctaw, and Seminole peoples, had adopted some U.S.-style institutions, and Sequoyah created a Cherokee writing system. Yet the Indian Removal Act of 1830 led to forced relocation to Indian Territory and the Trail of Tears, while Seminole resistance continued until 1842.

Mexican-American War and western expansion deepened the slavery crisis. Opponents saw the war as a proslavery move, while Southern leaders argued slavery could not be barred from territories acquired from Mexico. Free Soilers supported the Wilmot Proviso, others supported popular sovereignty, and some wanted the Missouri Compromise line extended. The Compromise of 1850 admitted California as free, ended the slave trade in Washington, D.C., allowed territorial choice, and imposed a stronger fugitive-slave law.

Civil War ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Civil_War)) came after a decade of crisis over slavery, territory, party collapse, sectional identity, and national authority. The country had been loosely tied by parties, churches, memory, and Washington institutions, but railroads, telegraphs, newspapers, cities, markets, and corporations pulled regions into closer contact. Nativism, immigration, industrial change, and slavery all increased tension. Southern secession followed Lincoln’s election, and the Confederate States of America formed before fighting began at Fort Sumter in April 1861.

Union and Confederacy both mobilized volunteers, conscription, borrowing, paper money, railroads, and wartime administration. Lincoln refused to accept disunion while offering conciliation except on the Union’s permanence. Davis struggled with states’ rights, resources, and military pressure. The war’s military course included Bull Run, Fort Henry, Fort Donelson, Shiloh, New Orleans, the Peninsula Campaign, Antietam, Fredericksburg, Chancellorsville, Gettysburg, Vicksburg, Chattanooga, Atlanta, Sherman’s March, Petersburg, Richmond, and Appomattox.

Civil War ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Civil_War)) naval and foreign dimensions mattered. The Union blockade restricted Confederate trade with Europe. Farragut captured New Orleans and later acted at Mobile Bay. The Monitor and Merrimack battle marked a new age of ironclad naval warfare. Confederate hopes for British or French recognition failed because of Union diplomacy, Confederate military setbacks, British caution, and the political effect of emancipation. The war cost more than $15 billion and caused more than a million combined casualties.

## History: Reconstruction, Industrialization, and Reform

Reconstruction began with questions about Union restoration, emancipation, Black citizenship, and federal authority. Lincoln’s flexible plan required loyalty and acceptance of emancipation from a minority of voters. Radical Republicans wanted stronger guarantees and congressional control. Andrew Johnson, a Southern Democrat and Unionist, pardoned many Confederates, allowed rapid state reorganization, and did not require Black suffrage. Southern Black Codes treated freedpeople as inferior and pushed Congress toward civil-rights legislation.

Congressional Reconstruction produced the Fourteenth Amendment, military Reconstruction, readmission requirements, and conflict with Johnson. Congress overrode vetoes, extended the Freedmen’s Bureau, defined civil rights, and later passed the Fifteenth Amendment. Johnson’s resistance led to impeachment after conflict over the Tenure of Office Act and Secretary of War Edwin Stanton, but the Senate acquitted him by one vote. Freedpeople built families, churches, schools, and communities, while sharecropping became the dominant labour system.

Grant era saw waning Northern commitment, corruption scandals, and violent white resistance. The Fifteenth Amendment barred racial discrimination in voting, but terror, economic pressure, and fraud weakened Black political participation. Force Acts attacked the Ku Klux Klan and similar violence, yet tactics persisted through Democratic networks. The 1874 elections gave Democrats the House, and the contested 1876 election led to the final collapse of Reconstruction governments.

New South after 1877 was dominated by white Democrats, planters, and rising business interests. These Redeemer governments reduced spending on schools, health, prisons, and welfare while protecting white supremacy. Booker T. Washington’s Atlanta Compromise urged Black economic self-help, vocational education, thrift, and avoidance of direct political conflict. Southern poverty, crop-lien systems, racial violence, disfranchisement, segregation, and low public investment limited opportunity for both poor Black and poor white Southerners.

Late 19th-century immigration brought more than 9 million newcomers in the last 20 years of the century, increasingly from southern and eastern Europe after 1896. Older fears of immigrant political power, labour unrest, Catholicism, radicalism, and assimilation fed demands for restriction. The Chinese Exclusion Act of 1882 marked a major break from earlier openness and suspended Chinese labour immigration, later renewing and extending the exclusion.

The West developed through mining, cattle, farming, railroads, and conflict. Gold and silver strikes in the Comstock Lode, Black Hills, and Cripple Creek created boomtowns and sometimes ghost towns. Open-range cattle drives moved Texas cattle to railheads until fencing, settlement, and winter disasters changed the industry. Farmers, miners, railroads, irrigation projects, and federal land policies transformed plains and mountain regions, again at great cost to Native peoples.

Labour movement expanded with industry. The Knights of Labor, organized in 1869, tried to unite producers and pursued reform through education, politics, and strikes. The depression of 1873-78 and railroad conflicts increased unrest. The Haymarket Riot of 1886 damaged public sympathy for unions. The American Federation of Labor, reorganized in 1886, focused on skilled workers, craft unions, wages, hours, and conditions rather than broad social transformation.

Gilded Age politics included civil-service reform, money debates, railroad regulation, antitrust law, tariffs, and agrarian revolt. Hayes ended military Reconstruction and fought patronage. Garfield’s assassination helped produce the Pendleton Civil Service Act in 1883. The Interstate Commerce Act of 1887 regulated railroads. The Sherman Anti-Trust Act of 1890 addressed monopolies but was weak at first and sometimes used against unions. Silver, tariffs, and deflation divided regions and parties.

Populists emerged from Farmers’ Alliances in the West and South. They demanded free silver, a graduated income tax, government ownership of railroads, lower tariffs, direct election of senators, and stronger democracy. Southern white farmers often stayed Democratic because they feared splitting white political control. The Populists became a serious third-party challenge in the 1890s but struggled to unite farmers, labour, and reformers across race and region.

## History: Imperialism, Progressivism, and World War

Spanish-American War in 1898 made the United States a world power and pushed it into imperial politics. Economic growth, naval theory, Social Darwinism, missionary ideas, farmer demands for markets, and the closing of the frontier encouraged new expansion. The war was short, but its consequences were large: U.S. power reached the Caribbean and Pacific, and debates over empire, democracy, race, and national mission intensified.

Open Door policy aimed to preserve equal commercial opportunity and Chinese territorial integrity as other powers carved spheres of influence in China. Secretary of State John Hay issued Open Door notes in 1899 and 1900. The policy had limited immediate force, but later administrations treated it as a cornerstone of Far Eastern policy. Theodore Roosevelt’s mediation of the Russo-Japanese War and the Nine-Power Treaty of 1922 reflected continuing concern over China and Pacific balance.

Caribbean policy and the Panama Canal showed strategic expansion. American leaders saw a canal as vital to naval and commercial power. The United States supported Panama’s separation from Colombia and built the canal, while also asserting dominance in the Caribbean through intervention, protectorates, and dollar diplomacy. This extended older Monroe Doctrine ideas into more direct regional control.

[[World Wars (World War I)|World War I]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_War_I)) challenged U.S. neutrality. Wilson wanted mediation and peace without direct involvement, but German unrestricted submarine warfare, attacks on shipping, and the broader contest over world order led him to ask Congress for war in April 1917. Mobilization moved from voluntary cooperation to strict federal control over railroads, industry, food, fuel, labour, shipping, and propaganda. The Espionage Act suppressed opposition.

American Expeditionary Force under General John J. Pershing helped tip the balance on the Western Front by 1918. The U.S. Navy helped counter submarines, and the Army grew to about 4 million men, with more than 1.2 million reaching France by September 1918. Wilson’s Fourteen Points presented a vision of open diplomacy, self-determination, free trade, disarmament, and a League of Nations.

Versailles Treaty included the League Covenant but also compromises over reparations, territory, and Allied demands. Wilson fought for his settlement in Paris but faced Senate resistance at home. Henry Cabot Lodge and other Republicans demanded reservations, while irreconcilables opposed the League entirely. The United States never joined the League, and the election of 1920 marked a turn away from Wilsonian internationalism.

## History: Depression, New Deal, and World War II

1920s America reacted against war, radicalism, strikes, bombings, race riots, and reform. Harding promised normalcy, pardoned some wartime dissenters, and promoted probusiness policy. Hoover expanded the Commerce Department and supported voluntary cooperation. Prosperity, consumer culture, stock speculation, restrictive immigration, Prohibition, and cultural conflict marked the decade, but agriculture remained weak and financial regulation was poor.

[[Great Depression]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Depression)) began with the 1929 stock market crash and deepened into the worst unemployment crisis in U.S. history. By 1933 about one-quarter of the labour force was unemployed, salaries and industrial wages had fallen sharply, banks were overextended, agriculture was depressed, consumer debt was high, and world trade collapsed. Hoover acted more energetically than earlier presidents but failed to restore confidence, while Smoot-Hawley tariffs worsened international trade.

First New Deal began with Roosevelt’s 1933 bank holiday and emergency banking legislation. Deposits soon exceeded withdrawals, restoring confidence. Glass-Steagall separated commercial and investment banking and created deposit insurance, while the Banking Act of 1935 strengthened the Federal Reserve. Roosevelt’s Hundred Days produced relief, recovery, and reform measures for banks, securities, homeowners, agriculture, unemployed workers, industry, forests, and public works.

New Deal relief included the Federal Emergency Relief Administration, Civil Works Administration, Public Works Administration, Civilian Conservation Corps, and later large work-relief appropriations. Agricultural policy used the AAA to limit production and raise prices, while farm credit and the Rural Electrification Administration transformed rural life. Securities regulation, mortgage refinancing, labour measures, and public works expanded federal responsibility for economic security.

World War II road began with U.S. isolationism, neutrality laws, and disarmament hopes. Roosevelt’s Good Neighbor Policy improved relations with Latin America and ended claims of intervention. As Italy, Germany, and Japan expanded, neutrality became harder to maintain. The United States moved gradually from nonintervention toward support for nations resisting aggression, while Japan’s war in China and Germany’s conquest in Europe made isolation increasingly untenable.

[[World Wars (World War II)|World War II]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_War_II)) brought total mobilization after Japan attacked Pearl Harbor and Germany declared war. The United States became an arsenal, military power, and Allied leader. War production ended the Depression, expanded federal power, drew women and minorities into new jobs, accelerated migration, and strengthened science and technology. The war ended with Germany’s defeat, atomic bombing of Hiroshima and Nagasaki, Japan’s surrender, and the United States facing a new global role.

## History: Cold War and Postwar Society

Cold War ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cold_War)) began as hopes for cooperation with the Soviet Union collapsed. Truman met Stalin and Allied leaders at Potsdam, but Soviet control in eastern Europe and communist movements elsewhere increased tension. The Truman Doctrine promised aid to free peoples resisting communist pressure, first in Greece and Turkey. The Marshall Plan gave about $13 billion to western Europe and restored economic confidence, while the Berlin airlift answered the Soviet blockade.

NATO ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NATO)) was founded in 1949 as a military alliance against Soviet aggression. The National Security Act reorganized the armed forces, created the Air Force, and strengthened defense institutions. The Soviet atomic bomb in 1949 ended the U.S. monopoly and started nuclear and thermonuclear competition. Containment worked better in Europe than Asia, where China’s communist revolution pushed U.S. policy toward Japan, Taiwan, Korea, and later Indochina.

Red Scare in the late 1940s and early 1950s mixed real espionage cases with exaggerated charges of communist infiltration. Truman created a loyalty program, while Joseph McCarthy made unsupported accusations that damaged many careers. China’s fall, the Soviet atomic bomb, the Korean War, and the Rosenberg case intensified fear. McCarthy lost power after investigating the Army and being censured by the Senate in 1954.

[[Korean War]] began in 1950 when North Korea invaded South Korea. The UN Security Council, during a Soviet boycott, called for resistance. U.S.-led UN forces were nearly driven out, counterattacked, entered North Korea, and then were driven back by Chinese intervention. The line stabilized near the 38th parallel, and armistice talks lasted until 1953. The war militarized containment, increased defense spending, protected Taiwan, and expanded aid to the French in Indochina.

Eisenhower era brought peace, prosperity, and public works. The Korean armistice was signed in 1953. Eisenhower cut defense spending, controlled inflation, supported the St. Lawrence Seaway, expanded Social Security, and created the Interstate Highway System. Sputnik in 1957 raised fears of Soviet technological superiority and led to the National Defense Education Act. The U-2 incident in 1960 damaged hopes for improved relations with the Soviet Union.

Kennedy administration promised a New Frontier and a new generation of leadership. The Bay of Pigs invasion failed in 1961, and the Cuban missile crisis in 1962 brought nuclear confrontation before Soviet missiles were removed. Kennedy supported the Alliance for Progress, a nuclear test-ban treaty, tax cuts, trade expansion, medical care for the aged, and civil rights enforcement. His assassination in Dallas on November 22, 1963, brought Lyndon Johnson to the presidency.

Great Society under Johnson produced major liberal legislation. The Civil Rights Act of 1964 attacked discrimination in public accommodations, employment, and federally supported schools. The Economic Opportunity Act declared war on poverty through Job Corps, community action, training, loans, and domestic service. Medicare, Medicaid, education aid, housing, urban development, conservation, transportation, arts support, consumer protection, clean air, clean water, and regional development expanded federal social policy.

Civil rights movement reached a high point in the 1960s. The March on Washington in 1963 and Martin Luther King, Jr.’s “I Have a Dream” speech dramatized nonviolent demands. Freedom Riders, CORE, SNCC, NAACP, Freedom Summer, and voter registration campaigns faced violence. The Voting Rights Act of 1965 abolished literacy tests and authorized federal action against voter discrimination. Later Black Power activism and groups such as the Black Panther Party challenged the limits of nonviolent integrationist reform.

[[Vietnam War]] grew from Truman-era aid to French Indochina and continued support for South Vietnam after 1954. The Gulf of Tonkin Resolution in 1964 allowed Johnson to escalate. More than half a million U.S. troops were in Vietnam by 1968. The Tet Offensive was a communist military failure but a political success because it convinced many Americans the war could not be won at acceptable cost. Johnson reduced bombing, opened talks, and refused renomination.

## History: Late 20th and Early 21st Century

Nixon administration changed foreign policy through Vietnamization, detente, arms limitation, and opening to China. Nixon and Henry Kissinger sought a balance of power among the United States, Soviet Union, and China. The 1972 visits to Beijing and Moscow, SALT agreements, and Yom Kippur crisis diplomacy reshaped Cold War strategy. At home, Watergate exposed illegal political activities, abuse of power, and cover-up. Nixon resigned on August 9, 1974, after support in Congress collapsed.

Ford administration was a caretaker period marked by Nixon’s pardon, stagflation, recession, inflation, oil shock, unemployment, and conflict with Congress. Ford kept Kissinger’s foreign policy but lacked a mandate and broad base. He vetoed many bills and narrowly lost the 1976 election to Jimmy Carter, who presented himself as an outsider after Watergate.

Carter administration emphasized human rights and diplomacy. Carter improved relations with China, signed Panama Canal treaties, and brokered the Camp David Accords between Egypt and Israel. Detente with the Soviet Union weakened after Soviet actions in Africa, nuclear deployments, and the invasion of Afghanistan. The Iranian Revolution and hostage crisis crippled Carter’s leadership. Domestically, energy policy, inflation, recession, high interest rates under Paul Volcker, and weak congressional relations damaged his presidency.

Reagan administration promised smaller government, tax cuts, social spending cuts, military buildup, and economic revival. Congress approved major tax reductions in 1981, while high interest rates deepened recession before recovery came by 1984. Reagan backed Contras in Nicaragua, invaded Grenada, faced the Lebanon marine bombing, and took a hard line toward the Soviet Union before later negotiations. His popularity, lower inflation, and recovery helped him defeat Walter Mondale and Geraldine Ferraro in 1984.

George H. W. Bush administration managed the end of the Cold War and the Persian Gulf War. Bush ordered U.S. troops into Panama to arrest Manuel Noriega. After Iraq invaded Kuwait in 1990, he built a UN-backed coalition and sent about 500,000 U.S. troops to Saudi Arabia. The 1991 war quickly expelled Iraqi forces from Kuwait and showed U.S. military dominance after Soviet decline. Recession, debt, deficits, and a broken no-new-taxes promise weakened Bush before the 1992 election.

Clinton administration began amid economic weakness, crime, poverty, welfare dependency, racial tension, and health-care costs. Early failures included conflict over gay service in the military, attorney-general nominations, stimulus, campaign finance, and health-care reform led by Hillary Rodham Clinton. The “Don’t ask, don’t tell” compromise satisfied neither side. Later economic growth, stock-market gains, welfare reform, deficit reduction, balanced budgets, and budget surpluses strengthened Clinton, though scandals and impeachment marked his second term.

Clinton foreign policy included Middle East diplomacy, terrorism, and post-Cold War interventions. He brought Yitzhak Rabin and Yasir Arafat to Washington in 1993 for an agreement on limited Palestinian self-rule in Gaza and Jericho. Terrorist attacks hit the World Trade Center in 1993, U.S. embassies in Kenya and Tanzania in 1998, and the USS Cole in Yemen in 2000. The Oklahoma City bombing in 1995 showed domestic anti-government violence.

George W. Bush administration began with tax cuts, a weakening economy, an energy crisis, and narrow partisan control. The September 11 attacks killed about 3,000 people and became the central event of his presidency. The United States launched a war on terrorism, invaded Afghanistan, created the Department of Homeland Security, and later invaded Iraq. Bush also withdrew from the Anti-Ballistic Missile Treaty, rejected the Kyoto Protocol, supported Taiwan, and faced criticism from Europe and China.

Barack Obama administration began during the Great Recession. Obama defeated John McCain in 2008 and became the first African American president. The government used TARP funds, aid to automakers, Federal Reserve bond purchases, and a $787 billion stimulus to stabilize the economy. GDP returned to growth in 2009, but unemployment remained high and deficits rose. Partisanship intensified, the Tea Party movement grew, and disputes over health care, debt, taxes, Medicare, Medicaid, and spending shaped the early presidency.

Obama era also included the killing of Osama bin Laden on May 1, 2011, by U.S. special forces in Abbottabad, Pakistan. The debt-ceiling crisis of 2011 exposed deep partisan conflict over spending cuts, entitlement programs, tax increases, and federal borrowing. The crisis raised fears of default and credit downgrading, showing that the country’s political system faced severe strain even after economic stabilization.

Affordable Care Act became the central domestic achievement and controversy of Obama’s first term. The law aimed to extend insurance to millions of previously uninsured Americans, prevent denial of coverage for preexisting conditions, subsidize premiums for lower- and middle-income households, and require most people to obtain health insurance. Its passage exposed the depth of party polarization: Democrats treated it as a long-delayed expansion of social protection, while Republicans opposed its cost, mandates, and enlarged federal role. The Supreme Court later upheld the law’s individual mandate as constitutional, preserving the reform while keeping health care at the centre of national politics.

Obama foreign policy tried to shift attention from Iraq toward Afghanistan while still managing the legacies of the war on terrorism. U.S. combat operations in Iraq formally wound down, though instability there remained a major concern. In Afghanistan, Obama first increased troop commitments in response to Taliban resurgence and then faced the limits of military strategy, alliance management, and public patience. The killing of bin Laden gave the administration a symbolic victory, but it did not end broader debates over counterterrorism, secrecy, drones, military detention, and the long-term costs of post-September 11 policy.

Early 2010s politics were shaped by slow recovery, public anger, and institutional brinkmanship. The Tea Party movement pushed Republicans toward harder opposition to taxation, spending, immigration, and federal intervention, helping the party regain the House of Representatives in 2010. The Occupy Wall Street movement, in a very different register, attacked inequality, financial power, and the political influence of the wealthiest Americans. Budget fights, debt-ceiling negotiations, and a partial federal government shutdown showed how fiscal policy had become a recurring test of constitutional bargaining as well as economic management.

Obama reelection in 2012 confirmed both the durability and the limits of his coalition. He defeated Mitt Romney after a campaign focused on health care, taxation, deficits, employment, and responsibility for the recovery from the Great Recession. Democrats retained the Senate while Republicans held the House, leaving divided government in place. The result suggested that demographic change, urban and suburban voters, minority communities, and younger voters had become increasingly important to national elections, but it also left unresolved the partisan distrust that had defined the first Obama term.

## Enduring Questions in American Life

The United States ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States)) is defined by a tension between national power and local autonomy. Federalism gives the national government authority over matters such as currency, foreign relations, interstate commerce, and national defense, while states retain broad authority over education, policing, elections, property, and many social policies. This division allows regional variation but also creates conflict when national constitutional rights collide with state law or local custom.

Race and citizenship are central to the national story rather than separate subjects. Indigenous dispossession, African enslavement, immigration exclusion, segregation, and unequal access to land, housing, education, voting, and employment shaped economic and political opportunity. The Civil War amendments, civil-rights laws, court decisions, social movements, and changing immigration patterns expanded legal definitions of citizenship, yet equality under law has repeatedly required enforcement, political organization, and public argument.

The American economy grew through land, labour, resources, invention, finance, infrastructure, and international trade. Capitalism encouraged entrepreneurship and technological change, but it also produced monopoly power, financial panic, unequal wealth, insecure work, and regional decline. Labour unions, antitrust laws, banking rules, social insurance, public education, and environmental regulations developed as efforts to limit or manage those costs. Economic debates therefore often concern how much responsibility belongs to markets, employers, households, local government, states, or the federal government.

The country’s global role expanded from continental conquest to overseas intervention, world-war alliance, Cold War leadership, and post-Cold War military and economic influence. American foreign policy has alternated between caution about foreign entanglements and claims that security, trade, democracy, or human rights require active engagement abroad. Immigration, global media, multinational business, and military alliances mean that domestic and foreign policy now affect one another constantly.

American culture is similarly plural. Shared institutions such as schools, elections, sports, music, film, markets, and mass media create national reference points, yet regional, racial, religious, linguistic, and class experiences remain distinct. The country’s cultural influence comes partly from the scale of its media and economy, but its most enduring cultural forms—from jazz and blues to film, literature, technology, and sports—also grew through exchange, adaptation, and debate among many communities.

## Key Events & Developments

- United States ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States)) constitutional government began under the new Constitution in 1789 after ratification in 1788.
- Louisiana Purchase in 1803 more than doubled national territory and opened vast western expansion.
- War of 1812 ended with the Treaty of Ghent and strengthened national confidence despite military difficulties.
- Indian Removal Act of 1830 led to forced relocation and the Trail of Tears.
- Declaration of Independence was adopted on July 4, 1776, after Congress voted for independence on July 2.
- Civil War ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Civil_War)) began at Fort Sumter in April 1861 and ended with Confederate surrender in 1865.
- Reconstruction produced the Thirteenth, Fourteenth, and Fifteenth Amendments but failed to secure lasting racial equality in the South.
- Spanish-American War in 1898 pushed the country into world politics and overseas empire.
- [[World Wars (World War I)|World War I]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_War_I)) brought U.S. military intervention in 1917 and Wilson’s failed campaign for League membership.
- [[Great Depression]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Depression)) after 1929 led to Roosevelt’s New Deal and a larger federal role in economic life.
- [[World Wars (World War II)|World War II]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_War_II)) made the United States a global military, industrial, and diplomatic power.
- Cold War ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cold_War)) policy developed through the Truman Doctrine, Marshall Plan, NATO, Korean War, containment, nuclear competition, and later detente.
- Civil Rights Act of 1964 and Voting Rights Act of 1965 transformed federal civil-rights law.
- Watergate forced Richard Nixon to resign in 1974, the first resignation by a U.S. president.
- September 11 attacks in 2001 led to homeland-security reorganization and wars in Afghanistan and Iraq.
- Great Recession ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Recession)) from 2007 to 2009 shaped the transition from George W. Bush to Barack Obama.

## Key People

- Christopher Columbus ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christopher_Columbus)) reached the Caribbean in 1492, after earlier Indigenous settlement had already occupied the continent for millennia.
- John Rolfe ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Rolfe)) helped make Virginia economically viable through high-grade tobacco cultivation.
- William Penn ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Penn)) founded Pennsylvania as a proprietary colony with religious toleration for Protestants and broad settlement opportunity.
- Benjamin Franklin ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Benjamin_Franklin)) represented colonial applied science, civic organization, diplomacy, and revolutionary leadership.
- [[George Washington]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Washington)) commanded the Continental Army and became the first president under the Constitution.
- Thomas Jefferson ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Jefferson)) wrote most of the Declaration of Independence and acquired Louisiana as president.
- Alexander Hamilton ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexander_Hamilton)) built the early federal financial program through debt assumption, a national bank, and implied powers.
- John Marshall ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Marshall)) strengthened national judicial authority through Supreme Court decisions such as Marbury v. Madison.
- Andrew Jackson ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andrew_Jackson)) symbolized white male democratic politics while supporting Indian removal and strong presidential action.
- Frederick Douglass ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frederick_Douglass)) became one of the most important antislavery voices and linked abolition to Black freedom and citizenship.
- Abraham Lincoln ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abraham_Lincoln)) preserved the Union, issued emancipation policy, and led the country through the Civil War.
- Ulysses S. Grant ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ulysses_S._Grant)) became the leading Union general and later president during Reconstruction.
- Booker T. Washington ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Booker_T._Washington)) promoted vocational education and accommodation in the Atlanta Compromise.
- Theodore Roosevelt ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theodore_Roosevelt)) represented Progressive-era activism, imperial reach, naval power, and energetic presidency.
- Woodrow Wilson ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Woodrow_Wilson)) led the country into World War I and promoted the Fourteen Points and League of Nations.
- [[Franklin D. Roosevelt]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Franklin_D._Roosevelt)) led the New Deal and World War II mobilization.
- Harry S. Truman ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harry_S._Truman)) shaped containment through the Truman Doctrine, Marshall Plan, NATO, and Korean War decisions.
- Dwight D. Eisenhower ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dwight_D._Eisenhower)) ended the Korean War, supported highways, and governed during postwar prosperity.
- Martin Luther King, Jr. ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Martin_Luther_King_Jr.)) became the leading symbol of nonviolent civil-rights activism.
- Lyndon B. Johnson ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lyndon_B._Johnson)) enacted Great Society and civil-rights legislation while escalating Vietnam.
- Richard Nixon ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_Nixon)) opened relations with China, pursued detente, and resigned after Watergate.
- Ronald Reagan ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ronald_Reagan)) promoted tax cuts, military buildup, free-market conservatism, and a hard Cold War line before later negotiations.
- Barack Obama ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barack_Obama)) became the first African American president and governed during the Great Recession’s aftermath.

## Interesting Facts

- United States ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States)) includes environments from Arctic Alaska to subtropical Florida and Hawaii.
- Grand Canyon is part of the Colorado Plateau, where streams cut deep canyons into mostly flat sedimentary rocks.
- Great Lakes form the largest freshwater lake group in the world and carry enormous shipping tonnage.
- Interstate Highway System connects about nine-tenths of cities with at least 50,000 people.
- Federal Constitution is the oldest written national constitution still in effect.
- Mayflower Compact promised obedience to colony laws before Plymouth settlers landed in 1620.
- Erie Canal connected the Hudson River to the Great Lakes and helped make New York City dominant.
- Rural Electrification Administration helped raise farm electrification from about one in 10 farms in 1935 to nine in 10 by 1950.
- Hollywood film became both commercial mass entertainment and a recognized art form.
- [[Baseball]] became a national pastime and one of the central sources of American sports mythology.
