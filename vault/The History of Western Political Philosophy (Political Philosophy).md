# The History of Western Political Philosophy (Political Philosophy)

> [!abstract] Reference navigation

The source follows this tradition historically, linking changing political theories to ancient civic life, religious and legal thought, modern sovereignty, and democratic politics.

Political philosophy ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Political_philosophy)) studies the concepts, arguments, and values involved in political opinion and government. Its central problem is how to deploy or limit public power so human life can survive and improve.

Unlike political science, which is often empirical and descriptive, political philosophy is normative. It asks what government ought to do, what justice requires, when power is legitimate, and what ends political life should serve.

Political philosophy ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Political_philosophy)) reflects historical settings, social assumptions, and broader views of human nature, morality, knowledge, religion, and society.

## Antiquity and Greece

![[attachments/the_history_of_western_political_philosophy_political_philosophy_001.webp|420]]

*Sanzio 01 Plato Aristotle (cropped)*

Ancient societies such as Egypt, Mesopotamia, India, and China had laws, advice literature, imperial ethics, and social doctrines, but Western political philosophy arose most clearly in [[Greece]], where thinkers asked why government exists and what makes authority legitimate.

[[Plato and Platonism (Plato)|Plato]]'s Republic is the first elaborate European work of political philosophy. It criticizes Greek politics, moral disorder, and democratic instability while proposing rule by a trained philosophical minority.

Plato's politics rests on [[Metaphysics|metaphysics]]: a world of permanent Forms stands above ordinary experience. Justice in the state should reflect the order of the soul and the Good itself.

[[Aristotle and Aristotelianism|Aristotle]]'s Politics is more empirical. He treats political behaviour as part of ethics and biology, studies actual constitutions, and defines humans as political animals who realize their nature in the city-state.

Aristotle values law, moderation, balance, and the common good. He criticizes Plato's impracticality but shares the idea that political life should cultivate virtue and human flourishing.

## Christianity and Augustine

Christianity transformed political philosophy by dividing the earthly city from the City of God. [[Augustine]]'s City of God, written after Rome was attacked, subordinated politics to salvation.

For [[Augustine]], government exists to keep order in a fallen world marked by sin. Earthly power is necessary but limited and secondary to divine grace, predestination, and eternal destiny.

This created a lasting Western tension between church and state, spiritual and temporal authority, salvation and civic order.

## Medieval Political Thought

In the Middle Ages, Byzantine and western thinkers preserved Roman law, Christian empire, natural law, and ideas of kingship. The Code of Justinian helped transmit legal order.

In the West, thinkers such as Isidore of Sevilla held that kings rule rightly only when they do justice. Charlemagne's empire revived the idea of Christian political unity.

John of Salisbury's Policraticus was the first full speculative political work after Augustine. It reflected classical learning and medieval concern with the ideal ruler.

Thomas Aquinas gave Christian kingship and natural law their most elaborate form. Law is an ordinance of reason for the common good, and legitimate power ultimately comes through the community under God.

Aquinas follows [[Aristotle and Aristotelianism|Aristotle]] in seeing the city as the perfection of community, while adding Christian ends: rulers should promote peace, preserve life, and guide people toward virtuous life and heavenly happiness.

[[Dante]]'s De monarchia defended universal secular empire as the path to world peace and human fulfillment. He argued that imperial authority comes directly from God, not through the pope.

## Machiavelli and Secular Politics

Machiavelli secularized political philosophy by analyzing power as it operated in Renaissance Italy. He treated human beings as fickle, ambitious, fearful, and self-interested.

The ruler must combine the lion's force with the fox's cunning. Injuries should be decisive, neutrality is dangerous, and political survival may require ruthless action.

Machiavelli admired the Roman republic and civic energy, but his realism shocked readers because it detached politics from Christian moral ideals and treated effective power as a central fact.

## Hobbes, Spinoza, and Constitutionalism

Thomas Hobbes built a science of politics from motion, fear, desire, and self-preservation. He rejected transcendent natural law and argued that power creates law.

For Hobbes, the commonwealth must impose order to prevent civil war. Sovereign power is justified because without it human life is insecure and violent.

Spinoza also sought scientific political theory but defended toleration and intellectual liberty. Political power has authority only by its usefulness in protecting people and allowing reason to flourish.

Richard Hooker defended natural and human law within Anglican constitutionalism. His thought helped preserve the idea that rulers are responsible to law, God, and the commonwealth.

## Locke and Liberal Government

John Locke's Two Treatises of Government justified the Glorious Revolution and denied divine-right monarchy. Government exists to preserve life, liberty, and property.

Political authority comes from consent, and government is limited by declared laws, the public good, natural rights, and the people's ultimate power to choose representatives.

Locke's thought became a foundation of liberalism, constitutional government, separation of powers, toleration, and later American political theory.

## Burke, Vico, Montesquieu, and Rousseau

Edmund Burke defended ordered liberty, inherited institutions, and constitutional change through consent. He opposed abstract revolutionary theory and saw society as a community across generations.

Giambattista Vico introduced a historical vision of changing human consciousness through ages of gods, heroes, and humans. This challenged static legal and contractual theories.

Montesquieu's Spirit of Laws compared institutions, climates, customs, and laws, helping found sociology and anthropology. His theory of separated powers deeply influenced American and French constitution making.

[[Jean-Jacques Rousseau]] proclaimed that humans are born free but everywhere in chains. His social contract transformed older contract theory through the general will, in which legitimate law expresses the moral will of the community.

Rousseau inspired democratic, nationalist, egalitarian, welfare-state, and totalitarian interpretations, partly because his ideas moved from small civic communities into large nation-states.

## Utilitarianism and Liberal Reform

Utilitarianism, founded by Jeremy Bentham, judged law and government by the greatest happiness of the greatest number. Bentham attacked natural-law fictions and sought rational reform of law, punishment, and institutions.

[[John Stuart Mill]] developed liberal concerns about individuality, liberty, democracy, and education. He feared majority tyranny and defended free thought, discussion, and personal development.

Alexis de Tocqueville studied American democracy and warned that equality could lead either to freedom or servitude. He admired associations, local institutions, press freedom, and democratic energy while fearing mediocrity and centralized power.

T.H. Green gave liberalism an idealist foundation, arguing that the state should remove hindrances to the good life through education, health, housing, planning, and unemployment relief. His thought helped prepare the welfare state.

## Nationalism, Anarchism, and Marxism

Giuseppe Mazzini defended liberal nationalism, imagining free peoples joined in a sisterhood of nations after empires, clerical privilege, and feudal structures were overcome.

American founders such as [[Washington, D.C. (Washington)|Washington]], John Adams, and Thomas Jefferson drew on Locke and Enlightenment ideas to defend laws over men, consent, natural rights, religious toleration, press freedom, and constitutional government.

Anarchism and utopian socialism attacked centralized coercive states. William Godwin trusted reason and education; Robert Owen blamed bad institutions; Pierre-Joseph Proudhon attacked property and authority.

[[Hegel and Hegelianism (Hegel)|Hegel]] interpreted history as dialectical development of consciousness and saw the state as the divine idea on earth. His ideas deeply influenced Karl Marx and Friedrich Engels.

[[Marx and Marxism (Marxism)|Marxism]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marxism)) replaced Hegel's nation-state drama with class conflict rooted in economic production. Marx and Engels predicted capitalism's overthrow and a revolutionary transformation of society.

[[Lenin]] adapted Marxism to Russia through a disciplined revolutionary elite, dictatorship backed by armed workers, and world-revolution strategy. His authoritarian interpretation shaped Soviet communism under Stalin.

## Critical Theory, Liberal Revival, and Postmodernism

Critical theory emerged from the Frankfurt School through Max Horkheimer, Theodor Adorno, Herbert Marcuse, and later Jurgen Habermas. It analyzed capitalism, ideology, fascism, mass culture, and oppressive forms of reason.

Habermas developed communicative ethics and deliberative democracy. Political legitimacy should arise through rational, uncoerced dialogue in which the better argument prevails.

John Rawls's A Theory of Justice revived liberal political philosophy in 1971. He argued that a just society protects equal basic liberties and permits inequalities only if they benefit the least advantaged and preserve fair opportunity.

Postmodernism, influenced by Foucault, Lyotard, and Derrida, challenged grand narratives, objective values, and Enlightenment confidence in reason. It emphasized power, ideology, interpretation, and plural perspectives.

[[Feminism]], antiracism, postcolonial theory, and global justice debates expanded political philosophy beyond older national and male-centred frameworks.

## Contemporary Questions

Modern political philosophy still asks how public power can preserve life and improve its quality, but the stakes now include nuclear weapons, ecological crisis, globalization, poverty, inequality, migration, and human rights.

Thinkers such as Amartya Sen and Martha Nussbaum explore global justice and capabilities, asking what conditions every human being needs for a decent life.

Other debates concern world government, alternatives to the nation-state, just-war theory, proportional force, nuclear weapons, biological weapons, chemical weapons, democracy, identity, and global institutions.

## Key Events & Developments

- c. 1750 BCE: Hammurabi's Code expressed ancient law under divine kingship.
- 4th century BCE: Plato and Aristotle founded Western political philosophy.
- 413-426: Augustine wrote The City of God.
- 1159: John of Salisbury wrote Policraticus.
- 1265-1273: Aquinas wrote Summa theologiae.
- 1513: Machiavelli wrote The Prince.
- 1651: Hobbes published Leviathan.
- 1690: Locke published Two Treatises of Government.
- 1762: Rousseau published The Social Contract.
- 1848: Marx and Engels published The Communist Manifesto.
- 1971: Rawls published A Theory of Justice.

## Interesting Facts

- Political philosophy ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Political_philosophy)) asks what government should be for, not only how it works.
- Plato's Republic is both a utopia and a critique of Greek politics.
- Machiavelli's shocking reputation came from describing power without Christian consolation.
- Rawls revived Anglo-American political philosophy after a long logical-positivist lull.
- Global justice has become a central question because economies and risks now cross national borders.
