# Life

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> Life is an active cellular process marked by metabolism, growth, response, reproduction, heredity, and evolution. The concept links the chemical unity and immense diversity of organisms with questions about definition, origins, classification, viruses, and the biosphere's limits.

## Introduction

Life ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Life)) is living matter understood as an active process rather than a static thing. Living individuals are made of one or more cells, transform carbon-based and other compounds through metabolism, grow, respond, reproduce, and evolve through hereditary variation and natural selection.

[[Earth - Its Properties, Composition, and Structure (Earth)|Earth]] formed about 4.5 billion years ago, and fossils show life more than 3.4 billion years old. More than 99.9 percent of species that have lived are extinct, but all known life shares a deep historical, chemical, and cellular continuity.

The [[Biosphere and Concepts of Ecology (Biosphere)|biosphere]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biosphere)) is thin compared with Earth, extending from below the ocean floor to the lower atmosphere. As far as is known, life exists only on Earth, though biology also asks what life might be in principle and whether extraterrestrial life could exist.

Rhizophora apiculata appears in an illustration connected with definitions of life. Biology studies life through anatomy, ultrastructure, physiology, biochemistry, ecology, taxonomy, ethology, sociobiology, botany, microbiology, zoology, cytology, and many narrower fields.

No single definition of life is fully inclusive. Growth, metabolism, change, reproduction, resistance to disturbance, and evolution all involve transformation, which makes tidy definition difficult.

## Metabolic Definitions

Metabolic definitions describe living systems as bounded objects that exchange materials with their surroundings while maintaining general properties over time. They capture the importance of energy transformation but fail with dormant seeds, spores, and flame-like systems that grow and metabolize without being alive.

## Physiological Definitions

Physiological definitions identify life by functions such as eating, excreting, moving, growing, reproducing, and responding. The difficulty is that machines can imitate some functions and dormant organisms can temporarily lack them.

Dionaea muscipula, the Venus flytrap, illustrates active responsiveness. Its traps dramatize physiological function, yet the broader argument warns that visible activity alone cannot define life.

## Biochemical Definitions

Biochemical definitions emphasize nucleic-acid heredity and enzyme-controlled metabolism. This works better for Earth life but leaves awkward cases such as prions, viruses, and self-replicating RNA molecules that show some life-like chemistry without independent life.

## Genetic Definitions

Genetic definitions stress genes, mutation, heredity, and natural selection. Charles Darwin's explanation shows how inherited variation, differential survival, and reproduction can transform populations over time.

## Thermodynamic Definitions

Thermodynamic definitions treat life as localized organization sustained by energy flow. Living systems do not violate the second law of thermodynamics because they are open systems that maintain internal order while helping degrade gradients and spread energy.

## Autopoietic Definitions

Autopoiesis, proposed by Humberto Maturana and Francisco Varela, defines living systems as self-producing, self-maintaining, self-repairing, and self-relational. Organisms maintain identity through a continuous flow of components across a boundary made by the system itself.

On Earth, autopoietic systems require liquid water and the elements carbon, nitrogen, hydrogen, oxygen, phosphorus, and sulfur. The smallest known autopoietic system is a living bacterial cell; viruses, plasmids, prions, and organelle-like dependents require cells and therefore are not independently alive.

Larger autopoietic entities include protists, animals, plants, ecosystems, and, in the Gaia idea of James E. Lovelock, Earth life as a planet-scale system regulating atmosphere, salinity, and temperature over long periods.

The [[Biosphere and Concepts of Ecology (Biosphere)|biosphere]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biosphere)) was named by Eduard Suess and developed by Vladimir I. Vernadsky, who treated living matter as a geological force. Deep-sea vents, hot springs, rocks, caves, tundra, forests, and ocean floors revealed life in places earlier naturalists barely imagined.

Living cells share DNA ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/DNA)), RNA, proteins, enzymes, membranes, chromosomes or genetic material, and a genetic code. DNA stores hereditary information, RNA participates in coding and may be older in life's history, and proteins catalyze and structure cellular work.

The near-universal genetic code links nucleotide triplets with amino acids. Its complexity, ubiquity, and usefulness imply long evolutionary history and descent from a common genetic source.

Paramecium caudatum illustrates shared cellular structures. Cilia, flagella, microtubules, mitotic spindles, and other patterns recur across organisms, suggesting that all Earth life descends from one or a few very ancient cellular ancestors.

Metabolism continuously repairs and replaces molecules that tend to break down. Autotrophs obtain carbon from carbon dioxide using light or chemical oxidation, while heterotrophs obtain energy and carbon from preexisting organic molecules.

[[Photosynthesis]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Photosynthesis)) uses light to make ATP and organic molecules, releasing oxygen as waste, while animal respiration uses oxygen to accept electrons from food. Ecological cycles keep carbon, electrons, and energy moving through organisms and environments.

## ATP

ATP ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adenosine_triphosphate)) is the general energy currency of known life. Its phosphate bonds are stable enough to persist and accessible enough for cells to tap during movement, synthesis, growth, division, and repair.

Glucose metabolism occurs in enzyme-mediated steps, not by sudden burning. Anaerobic breakdown yields less ATP, while aerobic metabolism in mitochondria is about ten times more efficient and uses many additional reactions.

Mitochondria and chloroplasts contain their own DNA and resemble free-living bacteria in sequence patterns. This supports the explanation that they descend from bacteria that entered symbiotic partnerships with ancestral eukaryotic cells.

## Diversity

Life's cellular diversity divides broadly into prokaryotes, which lack nuclei, and eukaryotes, which have nuclei. Bacteria are prokaryotes; animals, plants, fungi, and protists are eukaryotes.

The old idea of one-celled animals or one-celled plants is obsolete. Protozoans and algae are now treated as protists, while animals and plants are multicellular embryo-forming eukaryotes.

## Multicellularity

Multicellularity evolved independently in bacteria, protists, fungi, plants, and animals. Cellular slime molds show the blurred boundary: single amoeba-like cells swarm, combine into a sluglike body, form a stalked sorocarp, and release cysts that become amoebas again.

Embryonic differentiation remains a major biological problem. Animal cells begin with nearly identical genetic information, yet later become specialized as hair, bone, liver, blood, nerve, and other tissues.

## Classification

Robert H. Whittaker's five-kingdom system raised fungi to kingdom rank and helped replace the plant-animal dichotomy. Earlier, Ernst Haeckel proposed Protista, and Herbert F. Copeland separated prokaryotes from nucleated microorganisms.

Carl Woese later proposed three domains: Archaea, Bacteria, and Eukarya, using molecular criteria such as RNA sequences. The system is influential, though RNA alone may not correlate with every other organismal feature.

## Sex

[[Sex and Sexuality (Sex)|Sex]] differs sharply across prokaryotes and eukaryotes. Prokaryotes exchange naked DNA from plasmids, viruses, cells, or environmental DNA, while eukaryotic sex requires fusion of cells or nuclei and roughly half the genes from each parent.

Some [[Protists|protists]] have many mating types, while plants and animals generally develop from embryos formed by biparental sex. Ploidy varies across eukaryotes, and uniparental reproduction can occur in protists, fungi, rotifers, whiptail lizards, and many plants.

## Viruses

[[Viruses]] and plasmids are small replicons that depend entirely on host cells. Bacteriophages can rapidly turn a bacterium into a virus factory, while plasmids usually incorporate their DNA into host-cell DNA without bursting the cell.

Life tolerates extraordinary environmental extremes. Cyanidium lives in hot sulfuric acid, some bacteria thrive in alkaline environments, tardigrades survive dehydration, halophiles use water adsorbed on salt, and vent organisms grow under high pressure and high temperature.

Deinococcus radiodurans can withstand radiation levels lethal to mammals, partly because some organisms shield themselves or repair radiation damage. Nucleic acids absorb ultraviolet light strongly near 260 nanometres, making radiation a persistent biological threat.

## Organism Size

Sequoias near 90 metres high approach mechanical limits that [[Galileo]] had already reasoned about in relation to tree stability.

Sequoiadendron giganteum illustrates the large end of life, while pleuropneumonia-like organisms and Mycoplasma genitalium illustrate the small end. Minimum free-living cells need enough genes, enzymes, membrane, and metabolism to be autopoietic.

Water, carbon, and trace elements shape [[Metabolism|metabolism]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metabolism)). Organisms concentrate rare elements for specific functions, use oxygen or alternative electron acceptors, and exploit substances ranging from formaldehyde and petroleum to ammonia, sulfide, methane, and carbon dioxide.

## Senses

Organisms perceive worlds beyond unaided human senses. Plants, insects, snakes, bats, dolphins, moths, bacteria, fish, algae, and squids respond to ultraviolet, infrared, sound, chemicals, pressure, gravity, magnetism, electricity, and light.

Sensory life extends through technology as humans build instruments that detect radiation, heat, salt, photons, wind, earthquakes, and other phenomena. Whether consciousness and choice occur outside humans remains debated, but sensing and response have existed for billions of years.

## Heritability

Heritability underlies evolution by natural selection. More organisms are produced than can survive; individuals vary; some variants leave more offspring; offspring resemble ancestors; and populations diverge through time.

All organisms today are equally evolved because all share ancient ancestors from the Archean Eon. Their differences reflect different environmental histories acting on a shared molecular basis.

## Convergence

Convergent evolution occurs when different lineages meet similar physical or ecological problems with similar solutions. Marsupial and placental wolves, streamlined marine reptiles, tuna, dolphins, seals, and repeatedly evolved eyes show this pattern.

Microenvironments such as oxygen-poor oozes, sulfide-rich soils, radioactive outcrops, boiling springs, and wet cracks in granite create ecological niches. Evolution can fill similar niches independently because physics and chemistry restrict workable designs.

The geologic record divides Earth history into Hadean, Archean, Proterozoic, and Phanerozoic eons. Fossils and chemical markers from more than three billion years ago include bacteria, cyanobacteria, stromatolites, porphyrins, isotopic carbon evidence, acritarchs, Ediacaran forms, banded-iron formations, and later Cambrian animals.

## The Origin of Life

Origin hypotheses include supernatural creation, ongoing spontaneous generation, life as coeternal with matter or arriving by panspermia, and chemical evolution on early Earth. Modern experimental work focuses mainly on progressive chemical reactions before cells.

Spontaneous generation was long accepted for worms, insects, mice, and microorganisms. Renaissance and early modern experiments gradually undermined it, but microscopic life kept the controversy alive into the 19th century.

Alexander Ross expressed the older view that living things could arise from putrefying matter. Later work by William Harvey, Francesco Redi, Lazzaro Spallanzani, and especially Louis Pasteur showed that organisms come from preexisting organisms under present conditions.

Svante Arrhenius proposed panspermia, but transport through cold, vacuum, and radiation seemed unlikely. J.B.S. Haldane and Aleksandr Oparin argued that early hydrogen-rich, oxygen-poor conditions could allow nonbiological production of organic molecules.

Stanley L. Miller and Harold C. Urey simulated early Earth conditions in 1953 using methane, ammonia, water vapour, hydrogen, and electric sparks, producing amino and hydroxy acids. Later experiments with ultraviolet light, hydrogen sulfide, formaldehyde, cyanide, and catalysts produced amino acids, sugars, bases, porphyrins, and other monomers.

The harder problem is polymerization: making long chains from monomers despite water's tendency to break them down. Sidney Fox, Clifford Matthews, John Desmond Bernal, and others explored dry heat, cyanide chemistry, condensing agents, clays, freezing, evaporation, coacervates, and concentration as possible aids.

Catalytic RNA, discovered by Thomas Cech and Sidney Altman, suggested early roles for RNA. Yet life likely required proteins and nucleic acids inside an oily boundary, with metabolism and genetic coding stabilizing each other in one surviving lineage.

The earliest living systems probably used organic molecules of one optical handedness. Once left- or right-handed molecular asymmetry was established, it maintained itself, making optical activity a likely feature of life elsewhere.

## Core Equations

Biological systems are often summarized with growth, heredity, and energy-transfer relations:

$$\frac{dN}{dt}=rN\left(1-\frac{N}{K}\right)$$

The logistic-growth equation models population growth that slows as population size N approaches carrying capacity K.

$$p^2+2pq+q^2=1$$

Hardy-Weinberg proportions describe expected genotype frequencies when allele frequencies are p and q under idealized assumptions.

$$\Delta G=\Delta G^\circ+RT\ln Q$$

This equation adjusts standard free-energy change for actual concentrations or pressures through the reaction quotient.

## Key Events & Developments

- 4.5 billion years ago: [[Earth - Its Properties, Composition, and Structure (Earth)|Earth]] formed by planetary accretion.
- More than 3.4 billion years ago: Life ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Life)) is evidenced by ancient fossils.
- 1859: Charles Darwin published On the Origin of Species.
- 1926: Vladimir I. Vernadsky published The Biosphere.

- Late 1970s and 1980s: Deep-sea vent ecosystems revealed large communities supported by chemoautotrophy.
