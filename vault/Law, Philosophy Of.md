# Law, Philosophy Of

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> Philosophy of law, or jurisprudence, investigates the nature, authority, structure, social effects, and justice of law. It combines analytical accounts of legal systems with sociological study and normative theories that evaluate law against moral and political ideals.

## Introduction

*Courtroom with legal argument in progress*

Philosophy of law ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jurisprudence)), often called jurisprudence in English-speaking countries, develops concepts and theories for understanding what law is, where its authority comes from, and how it works in society. It is not simply a branch of one philosophical school, because legal problems draw on ethics, politics, logic, social science, and other organized forms of thought.

The field can be arranged into three main branches: analytical jurisprudence ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Analytical_jurisprudence)), sociological jurisprudence, and the theory of justice ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Justice)). Analytical work studies the logical structure of legal systems, defines terms, and asks how legal norms can be understood as a coherent order.

Analytical theories range from system-building efforts such as Hans Kelsen's pure theory to narrower accounts such as John Austin's command theory. The central problem is whether a clarified assumption about law can become a general model for all legal systems or only for particular systems.

Sociological jurisprudence ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sociology_of_law)) studies law's effects on behaviour, organization, social attitudes, and institutions, and also studies how social facts shape legal rules and legal processes. It treats even ideals of justice as social facts when asking how law actually operates.

The theory of justice ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Justice)) evaluates and criticizes law according to ideals or goals. It is closely connected with ethical and political philosophy, because judgments about good law require judgments about morality, authority, equality, freedom, and social purpose.

One central question is the relation between law and morality. Legal and moral norms vary across places and periods, yet legal philosophy has repeatedly searched for unchanging standards that could judge positive law, especially through the idea of natural law.

Natural-law theories claim that human reason and social nature reveal obligations beyond human will. Yet they depend on assumptions about human nature, conscience, and reason, as seen in Thomas Aquinas's appeal to synderesis and the priority of rational and social inclinations.

Greek thought introduced powerful ideals of justice. [[Plato and Platonism (Plato)|Plato]] located justice in an ordered state guided by ideal forms and philosopher-kings, while the Stoics developed a cosmic natural law, the jus naturale, as an expression of universal reason.

Roman and Hebrew traditions added different legal images. Roman jurists made law technical and practical, while a Talmudic story pictured learned interpreters standing by human reason even against a heavenly voice, because the law had been given to humans on earth.

## Augustine

St. Augustine of Hippo joined Stoic philosophy with Judeo-Christian thought while defending Christianity against claims that it had weakened Roman power. He placed God's reason beside God's will as the origin of eternal divine law.

For [[Augustine]], natural law was divine law as humans could understand it through reason, heart, and soul. Positive law, such as the Roman law of the Christian empire, could vary by time and place but remained valid only within divine and natural limits.

This theory helped preserve the idea of government under law during the collapse of the ancient world. It later became important again during the revival of learning in the 12th and 13th centuries.

## Scholasticism

Thomas Aquinas temporarily balanced divine reason and divine will. He placed human or positive law under eternal law, treating it as a work of human reason for the common good within the limits set by natural law.

For Aquinas, unjust positive law that violated natural and eternal law was not fully law or was not binding in conscience. His view gave reason a strong role while still preserving divine order.

After Aquinas's death in 1274, voluntarist thinkers challenged this rationalist balance. John Duns Scotus argued that all law finally depends on God's unique will and the precept to love God, leaving no natural law accessible by human reason alone.

## Machiavelli

Niccolo Machiavelli moved legal and political thought toward empirical observation of power. In 1513, he studied how principalities are gained, held, and lost, and he treated state maintenance as an end that could stand apart from law or morality.

Later thinkers wrestled with the supremacy of the human lawgiver. Jean Bodin and Thomas Hobbes emphasized sovereign authority, while Hugo Grotius used natural law to restrain rulers during and after the Thirty Years' War and to reject unlimited reasons of state.

Hobbes divided natural-law thought by grounding obedience in fear, self-protection, and the need for a sovereign able to protect people from one another. After him, natural law split between ideal accounts of human sociability and harsher accounts of human danger.

By the 17th century, courts began using natural law as a test of positive law. In the United States, constitutional law was sometimes compared to natural law, allowing judges to strike down statutes that violated written limits or supposed eternal principles of justice.

Natural law later declined under historical and social explanations of law. Montesquieu tied law to environment and national conditions, Auguste Comte treated law as a positive social fact, and Charles Darwin and Herbert Spencer encouraged evolutionary models of institutions.

Immanuel Kant offered a different route through idealism. He held that moral concepts arise from a priori reason, and he imagined just law as the condition under which each person can enjoy maximum freedom from arbitrary domination by others.

Kant's supposedly a priori legal ideas still resembled natural-law thinking because they relied on transcendental principles beyond experience. Johann Fichte and later Rudolf Stammler adapted this approach toward universal ideas of just law.

Stammler sought pure forms of legal thought and an ideal community of free-willing persons. His just law required harmony between individual and common purposes, mutual respect, and participation in shared purposes.

Georg Wilhelm Friedrich Hegel's historical idealism entered jurisprudence through Josef Kohler, who tried to show values immanent in civilization. Such idealisms helped reinforce individualist and libertarian trends during the Industrial Revolution.

The 19th century brought analytical positivism, which separated law as it is from law as it ought to be. Jeremy Bentham and John Austin focused on legal concepts, logical clarity, sovereign commands, and threats behind positive law.

Historical positivism rejected universal natural-law codes and sought law in social experience. Friedrich Karl von Savigny grounded law in the Volksgeist, or people's inherited legal consciousness, while Henry Sumner Maine developed comparative historical jurisprudence.

Economic and sociological interpretations then pushed law into broader social science. Rudolf von Jhering, Otto von Gierke, Oliver Wendell Holmes, Jr., and Eugen Ehrlich helped develop views of law as a social phenomenon shaped by group life, interests, and living norms.

The 20th century revived natural-law questions without defeating positivism. Rudolf Stammler, Francois Geny, and Leon Duguit all in different ways sought principles beyond mere command, even when Duguit denied natural law and spoke instead of social solidarity as an objective rule.

Gustav Radbruch turned openly against strict positivism after Nazi barbarism. He argued that when equality and justice are constantly denied, enacted positive law loses the nature of law altogether.

Radbruch's formula made the natural-law revival explicit: extreme injustice is not merely bad law but no law in the full sense. Maurice Hauriou and Georges Renard reached a related Roman Catholic institutionalism in which families, corporations, and other institutions expressed social reality and durable ideals.

Attempts to connect natural law with existentialism, such as those by Werner Maihofer, remained unstable because existentialism stressed fateful value choices rather than discoverable principles of harmony. The same ambiguity allowed ideas such as social solidarity or Volksgeist to be misused by absolutist or racialist regimes.

Hans Kelsen's pure theory of law rejected natural law and sociology by trying to isolate legal theory from values, psychology, ethics, and social facts. Yet critics argued that his basic norm still depended on factual obedience or efficacy, so facts returned at the foundation of the system.

Legal realism challenged formal legal reasoning in the 20th century. Oliver Wendell Holmes, Jr. described law as what courts will do in fact, and American realists emphasized rule uncertainty, fact uncertainty, and the hidden premises behind judicial decisions.

By the middle of the 20th century, no single analytical, ethical, or sociological approach could claim exclusive control. Legal thought had to combine description of social facts, analysis of legal concepts, and evaluation by justice, without collapsing into simple amoralism or unsupported absolutes.

Modern sociological jurisprudence grew through Roscoe Pound in the United States and Hermann Kantorowicz in Europe. It studied law in action, legal history in social context, effective legislation, legal reasoning, and the profession through surveys, statistics, comparison, and experiment.

The future of sociological jurisprudence points toward broader social theory. Early work often pursued practical reforms, but later work increasingly asked for general laws explaining law as a social phenomenon within complex social and economic systems.

## Key Events & Developments

- 5th-century BCE: [[Plato and Platonism (Plato)|Plato]] answered legal relativism by tying justice to ideal forms and ordered rule.
- 4th century BCE: Stoic thought refined natural law as universal reason.
- 1274: after Aquinas's death, voluntarist thinkers such as Duns Scotus challenged rationalist natural law.
- 1513: Machiavelli treated political power through empirical statecraft.
- 1748: Montesquieu's Spirit of Laws linked law to social and environmental conditions.
- 20th century: natural-law revival, pure legal theory, realism, and sociological jurisprudence competed and overlapped.

## Interesting Facts

- Jurisprudence ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jurisprudence)) in English often means philosophy of law or one of its subdivisions.
- Natural law ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_law)) repeatedly returns after decline because positive law alone struggles to answer extreme injustice.
- Savigny's Volksgeist treated law as inherited social consciousness rather than as a universal code.
- Legal realism focused on what courts actually do, not only on the reasons judges state.
- Sociological jurisprudence ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sociology_of_law)) began with practical reform but moved toward general study of law in society.
