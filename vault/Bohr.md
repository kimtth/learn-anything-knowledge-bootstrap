# Bohr

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> Niels Bohr was the Danish physicist whose 1913 atomic model introduced quantized electron states and whose complementarity principle helped shape the Copenhagen interpretation of quantum mechanics. He combined foundational work on atoms, spectra, nuclei, and fission with institution-building, rescue work during Nazism, and postwar advocacy for international scientific openness.

## Introduction

Niels Bohr ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Niels_Bohr)) (1885-1962) was a Danish physicist generally regarded as one of the foremost physicists of the 20th century. He was the first to apply the quantum idea—that a system's energy is restricted to discrete values—to atomic and molecular structure, work for which he received the Nobel Prize for Physics ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize_in_Physics)) in 1922.

## Early Life and Work

![[attachments/bohr_001.webp|420]]

*Portrait of Niels Bohr*

Bohr grew up in an upper-middle-class Copenhagen family and entered the university there in 1903, earning his doctorate in 1911 on the electron theory of metals. In 1912 he married Margrethe Nørlund, his most trusted adviser for life; one of their sons, Aage Bohr, later shared the 1975 Nobel Prize.

## Atomic Model

Bohr's major early achievement, published in 1913, was an atomic model in which electrons occupy only certain allowed orbits, emitting radiation only when one jumps to a lower-energy orbit. Though at first rejected by most physicists, it explained a growing body of data, most famously the spectral lines of hydrogen.

## Institute and Research Culture

Appointed to a chair in theoretical physics in 1916, Bohr pressed for an Institute for Theoretical Physics, which opened in 1921 and insisted that experimenters were vital for testing theory. The point was proved when Georg Hevesy and Dirk Coster confirmed in 1923 that element 72 behaved as his theory required; it was named hafnium after Copenhagen.

## Copenhagen Interpretation

Bohr became central to the quantum mechanics ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_mechanics)) developed by Werner Heisenberg from 1925, whose uncertainty principle was formulated while he was Bohr's assistant. Together they shaped the Copenhagen interpretation, built on Bohr's complementarity principle ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complementarity_%28physics%29)), first presented at Como in 1927: an atomic phenomenon appears differently depending on the experiment, so that light shows itself sometimes as waves and sometimes as particles, both being needed for a full account. Albert Einstein never accepted this, objecting that "God does not play dice," and his debates with Bohr at the Solvay Conferences of 1927 and 1930 became famous.

## Nuclear Physics and War Years

In the early 1930s Bohr turned to the atomic nucleus and raised funds to build a cyclotron, and after the discovery of fission he worked with John Archibald Wheeler to explain it. From 1933 he also helped Jewish physicists flee Nazi Germany, hosting them at his institute before they moved on, often to the United States.

## Postwar Work

Believing an atomic bomb theoretically possible but impractical before the war's end, Bohr escaped occupied [[Denmark]] by boat in 1943. He campaigned for an open world, meeting Winston Churchill and Franklin Roosevelt and later writing an open letter to the [[United Nations]] in 1950, and after the war he helped found CERN and the Nordic Institute for Atomic Physics.

## Conclusion

Bohr ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Niels_Bohr)) left an unsurpassed scientific legacy and an institute still among the world's leading centres for theoretical physics, combining technical achievement with deep philosophical and political concern.

## Core Equations

Physical models often begin by relating force, energy, waves, and conserved quantities:

$$\vec{F}=m\vec{a}$$

This is Newton's second law: force is the product of mass and acceleration, so a larger mass or a faster change in motion requires a larger net force.

$$E=h\nu$$

This relation says that photon energy is proportional to frequency; higher-frequency radiation carries more energy per photon.

$$E=mc^2$$

This expresses mass-energy equivalence: mass is a form of energy, with the speed of light squared as the conversion factor.

- Element 72, hafnium, was named after Copenhagen after confirming Bohr's theory in 1923.
- The complementarity principle ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complementarity_%28physics%29)) says waves and particles can both be needed to explain one phenomenon.
