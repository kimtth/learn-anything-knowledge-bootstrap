## Introduction

Benjamin Franklin was one of the most influential American figures of the 18th century. He was a printer, writer, inventor, scientist, and diplomat, and he played a central role in the founding of the United States. Franklin helped draft the Declaration of Independence, signed it, served in France during the American Revolution, and later took part in the Constitutional Convention. He is also remembered for his contributions to the study of electricity and for the wit and clarity of his writing.

## Early life

Franklin was born in 1706 in Boston, the youngest son in a large family. He received only a limited formal education and left school at the age of ten. At twelve he became an apprentice to his brother James, a printer. This early apprenticeship shaped the rest of his life. Franklin taught himself to read widely and to write well, and he developed a strong interest in literature and public affairs.

His first major success came through the printing trade. In Philadelphia, he built a career as a printer and publisher, and his newspaper and almanac became highly successful. He also developed a broad civic vision, believing that practical improvement and public service mattered as much as personal success.

Franklin’s scientific work was especially important. He carried out experiments in electricity and made major discoveries about conductors, lightning, and electrical charge. His famous kite experiment helped establish the link between lightning and electricity. He also invented useful devices such as the lightning rod, the Franklin stove, bifocal glasses, and the glass harmonica.

At the same time, Franklin devoted himself to public service. He helped create institutions that shaped Philadelphia and colonial life, including a library, a fire company, an academy, and a hospital. He also promoted practical improvements such as street lighting, public education, and better civic organization.

## Public service and the American Revolution

Franklin moved into politics as he became more involved in colonial affairs. He served as deputy postmaster general and worked to strengthen the colonies’ position within the British Empire. He proposed a plan for colonial union at the Albany Congress, though it failed. As tensions grew with Britain, Franklin became increasingly committed to American independence.

In 1776, he was sent to France as part of the American commission. There he used his reputation as a man of science and practical wisdom to win French support for the Revolution. He secured military help, loans, and diplomatic recognition, which were crucial to American success. Franklin’s public image in France made him a powerful symbol of the new republic.

## Later years and legacy

After the Revolution, Franklin returned to the United States and played a role in shaping the new government. He helped draft America’s first confederation framework and took part in the Constitutional Convention of 1787. He remained active in public life until his death in 1790.

Franklin’s legacy is immense. He is remembered as a scientist, a writer, a statesman, and a practical reformer. His life embodied the ideal of self-improvement, public usefulness, and intellectual versatility. He became one of the most admired and influential figures in American history.
