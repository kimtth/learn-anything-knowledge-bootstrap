# Mongolia

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> Mongolia is a sparsely populated Inner Asian country of steppe, desert, mountains, pastoral peoples, and Buddhist traditions. Its history spans Genghis Khan's imperial unification, Qing control, socialist rule under Soviet influence, and the democratic and market transition after 1990.

## Introduction

![[attachments/mongolia_001.webp|420]]

*Relief map of Mongolia*

Mongolia, historically called Outer Mongolia, is a landlocked country in north-central Asia between [[Russia]] and [[China]]. It is a high plateau with long cold winters, short summers, and an average elevation of about 5,180 feet. Its landscape includes upland steppe, semidesert, desert, forested mountains, basins, and lakes. Three-fourths of the land is pasture, supporting the livestock herding for which Mongolia is known, while forests, deserts, and very limited cropland occupy the rest.

Mongolia has one of the world's lowest population densities ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Population_density)). Its historical importance is large: possible ancestors include the Huns, and in the early 13th century [[Genghis Khan]] united nomadic tribes into a state whose successors ruled a vast empire across China, Russia, Central Asia, and the Middle East. Later Mongolia came under Qing rule, declared independence in 1911, became a socialist republic under Soviet influence in 1924, and moved toward democracy and a market economy after 1990.

## Topography

![[attachments/mongolia_003.webp|420]]

*Ulaanbaatar skyline*

Mongolia's topography consists of mountain chains in the north and west, basins between and around them, and a broad upland plateau across the south and east. The country is seismically active, but earthquakes usually have limited human impact because settlement is sparse. The mountains shape drainage, vegetation, climate, transportation, and patterns of settlement.

## The Gobi Altai

![[attachments/mongolia_002.webp|420]]

*Camels in the Gobi Desert*

The Gobi Altai rises from the edge of the Gobi in southwestern Mongolia and forms a lower, more eroded continuation of the western mountain system. It helps separate desert and semidesert regions from higher mountain and basin zones. Its dry slopes and denuded hills contrast with the better-watered Khangai and northern ranges.

## The Mongolian Altai and Khangai

The Mongolian Altai ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Altai_Mountains)) in the west and southwest is the highest and longest mountain chain, reaching 14,350 feet at Khuiten Peak. The Khangai Mountains form a broad central mass with fine pastureland and peaks above 12,000 feet, while the Khentii Mountains extend northeastward into Siberia and frame the region near Ulaanbaatar. The Great Lakes region lies among the western basins, with more than 300 lakes between the Altai, Khangai, and Siberian border mountains.

## Drainage

Mongolia lies on a continental divide. Northern rivers drain toward the Arctic Ocean through systems such as the Orkhon and Selenge, while northeastern rivers flow toward the Pacific. The western and southern two-thirds contain interior drainage basins where streams end in salt lakes or vanish into desert sands. The Orkhon, about 700 miles long, is the longest river wholly within Mongolia; the Selenge flows into Russia and Lake Baikal; and the Kherlen flows east into Inner Mongolia.

## Vegetation and Animal Life

Mongolia's natural zones run from forest-steppe to steppe, semidesert, and desert, with taiga and alpine belts in higher mountains. Steppe grasslands cover more than three-fourths of the country and provide pasture for herds. Northern slopes support larch, cedar, spruce, pine, fir, birch, aspen, and poplar, while semideserts and deserts contain sparse plants, saxaul, and groves near springs. Wildlife is diverse because Mongolia lies near several zoogeographic regions and has a sparse human population.

## People

The population is ethnically concentrated around Khalkh Mongols, who make up about four-fifths of the people. Other Mongolian groups include Dorvod, Buryat, Bayad, and Dariganga. Turkic-speaking Kazakhs, Tuvans, and Tsaatans live mainly in the west, and small Russian and Chinese communities live mostly in towns. Most people speak Mongolian, and the government has increasingly recognized minority language and cultural rights. Cyrillic replaced the traditional vertical script in the 1940s, but traditional script returned to schools and public signs after the 1990s.

## Religions

Mongols originally practiced shamanism, then widely adopted Tibetan Buddhism during the Qing period. After the 1921 revolution, the socialist state attacked religious and feudal institutions. In the 1930s, monasteries were destroyed or closed, property was confiscated, monks were forced out of religious life, and many who resisted were killed. After one-party rule ended in 1990, Tibetan Buddhism revived, monasteries were rebuilt, and religious vocations returned. Buddhists, mainly of the Gelug school connected with the Dalai Lama, form the largest active religious group; Kazakh Muslims, Christians, nonreligious people, and shamanic groups also exist.

## Settlement Patterns

Settlement ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Human_settlement)) is densest in the better-watered northern basins of the Orkhon and Selenge and thinnest in deserts. Rural life remains strongly associated with the ger, or yurt, a portable felt-insulated dwelling suited to herding mobility and extreme temperatures. Socialist policy encouraged settlement around cooperatives and state farms, while post-1990 unemployment and rural hardship pushed many families toward Ulaanbaatar and other towns, creating large ger districts.

Urban growth accelerated in the 20th century. Permanent monasteries, Qing garrisons, and socialist administrative centres all shaped settlement, but modern Mongolia is most visible in towns. Ulaanbaatar is by far the largest city, with more than one million people, and it concentrates government, industry, education, culture, and services.

## Economy

At the start of the 20th century, Mongolia's economy depended mainly on subsistence herding, with wealth measured in livestock owned by nobles, officials, and monasteries. The 1921 revolution ended noble and clerical privileges, and Soviet-backed development built slaughterhouses, processing plants, factories, industrial towns, and rail links. From the 1960s, planning emphasized mineral resources, including coal at Sharyn Gol, industry at Darkhan, and copper at Erdenet.

After 1990, Mongolia shifted from a command economy to a market economy and faced severe difficulty because Soviet aid ended and debt remained large. International assistance, cashmere, mining ventures, and foreign investment helped stabilize recovery. Agriculture and herding stayed important, but mining, services, and trade became increasingly central.

## Manufacturing

Manufacturing ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manufacturing)) largely processes domestic raw materials: meat, beverages, dairy products, flour, cashmere, wool, hides, skins, furs, wood products, ger frames, furniture, cement, and other building materials. Since 2000, rolled copper sheeting, copper wire, and zinc concentrates have been added. Ulaanbaatar dominates light industry, while Erdenet, Darkhan, and Gobi industrial complexes handle heavier production.

## Finance

The financial sector was rebuilt after 1990. Under socialism it had centred on the State Bank, later Mongolbank, and the Trade and Development Bank. Mongolbank remains the central bank and regulates the tugrik. Private and international banks, insurance, mortgages, electronic banking, credit cards, ATMs, and the Ulaanbaatar stock exchange widened financial activity, though foreign interest in the exchange stayed limited.

## Trade

Since the 1980s, minerals and ore concentrates, especially copper and gold, have dominated exports, while livestock products have declined in relative value. Main imports include fuels, machinery, food, agricultural products, and vehicles. China and Russia account for much trade. Mongolia tries to diversify, but lack of sea access, dependence on Russian and Chinese transport, long distances, high costs, and international competition constrain trade. Services expanded after 1990, tourism grew modestly, and visitors were drawn to landscapes, wildlife, historical sites, religious monuments, and ger life.

## Government

The Mongolian People's Party, founded in 1920, consolidated power after the 1921 revolution. The new regime created the State Great Khural, adopted the first constitution in 1924, and proclaimed the Mongolian People's Republic. The party, later the Mongolian People's Revolutionary Party, built a Soviet-style command economy, state ownership, socialist institutions, and a one-party political system.

## Constitutional Reform

The Mongolian People's Republic followed Soviet models in political organization, economic planning, and ideology. In the 1980s, Soviet openness encouraged criticism and informal associations in Ulaanbaatar. Public pressure forced party leaders to resign in March 1990, legalized political parties, created new legislative structures, and opened elections. The 1992 constitution divided power among legislative, executive, and judicial branches, guaranteed human rights, allowed private land ownership except pastures, and created the unicameral Mongolian Great Khural.

## Justice

Justice is administered by an independent court system including the Supreme Court, provincial and capital courts, district courts, and appeals courts. The Supreme Court has criminal, civil, and administrative chambers. Judges are appointed and dismissed by the president. Elections for parliament, president, and local government occur every four years, though usually at different times. The MPP and Democratic Party dominate politics, with smaller parties including the Civil Courage Party and Mongolian Green Party.

## Security

Until the early 1990s, Mongolia's security depended on treaties with the Soviet Union and a Soviet military presence. Soviet troops left in 1992, and the constitution generally prohibits foreign troops. A 1996 security policy emphasized internationally recognized nuclear-weapons-free status. Mongolia maintains a small military, border troops, police, and peacekeeping-oriented rapid deployment forces, while cooperating with several countries in training and exercises.

## Education

Before the revolution, education was largely monastic or administrative. After 1921, Mongolia built public schools, attacked illiteracy, trained an intelligentsia, expanded elementary and secondary education, and founded Mongolian State University in 1942. Boarding schools served nomadic herders' children. Education is now compulsory for 11 years from age six, with movement toward 12 years. Universities, vocational schools, colleges, and the Academy of Sciences support higher education and research.

## Media and Cultural Life

After censorship and the state publishing monopoly ended in 1990, media and publishing expanded sharply. Newspapers such as Odriin Sonin, Zuuny Medee, and Onoodor appeared, foreign periodicals became available, and book production improved through better printing machinery and paper. Mongolian readers gained access to textbooks, scientific works, fiction, poetry, translations, encyclopaedias, atlases, and English-language books.

## History

The Mongols are a major ethnographic division of Asian peoples, tied by language and nomadic tradition. Their homeland centred on the Mongolian plateau, later divided between Inner Mongolia in China and independent Mongolia. Earlier regional peoples included Manchu-Tungus groups to the east and Xiongnu or eastern Huns and Turkic-speaking successors to the west. Later migration and conquest spread Mongols into Mongolia, southern Siberia, the Caspian region, Inner Mongolia, Xinjiang, Ningxia, Tibet, and neighbouring Chinese provinces.

## The Rise of Genghis Khan

Temuujin, later [[Genghis Khan]], grew from a difficult youth into a leader who used alliances, patronage, sworn brotherhood, and loyal companions called nokhor to overcome tribal rivals. In 1206, he was proclaimed ruler of All the Mongols. Unlike earlier nomads who attacked China too soon, he first united the people of the felt-walled tents and secured rivals across the steppe before expanding outward.

## Division of the Empire

After Genghis Khan, the empire was divided among his sons and heirs according to nomadic inheritance customs. Ogodei was confirmed as great khan in 1229. Jochi's line formed the Golden Horde, where Turkic language and Islam became dominant. Under Kublai Khan, Mongol rule in China became the Yuan dynasty, with a Chinese-style administration, foreign advisers, and a new capital at Dadu.

## Fragmentation After the Yuan

After the Yuan collapse in 1368, Mongol unity fragmented. Oirat groups rose in western Mongolia, Genghisid princes remained influential in central and eastern regions, and power depended on combining nomadic military mobility with towns, artisans, trade, and food supply. Buddhist institutions such as Erdenezuu later became important symbols of Mongolian religious and political life.

## Kublai Khan and Dadu

Kublai Khan moved the capital to Dadu and in 1260 appointed the Tibetan Phagspa Lama as state teacher, continuing a patron-priest relationship. Yuan rulers relied on foreigners, including the Polo family, to reduce dependence on Chinese bureaucrats. After Kublai's death in 1294, weak rulers followed until Ming forces drove Toghon Temur Khan from Dadu in 1368.

## The Javzandamba

The rise of Tibetan Buddhism in Mongolia centred on the Javzandamba, proclaimed Ondor Geegen or Bogd Geegen. Qing authorities later restricted the discovery of incarnations among Genghisid lineages and required later Javzandambas to be found in Tibet. This policy strengthened Tibetan clerical influence and widened the gap between religious and secular authority.

## Mongolia from 1900 to 1990

At the turn of the 20th century, Japan, Russia, China, Britain, and Tibet all shaped Mongolia's international environment. The Russo-Japanese War, British moves in Tibet, and Qing weakness affected Mongolian politics. In 1911, as revolution broke out in China, the Manchu amban was expelled, the Javzandamba became the Bogd Khan, and Mongolia declared independence. Russia recognized Mongolian autonomy rather than full independence, and a 1915 treaty left Mongolia autonomous under Chinese suzerainty.

## Social Stratification and Revolution

Pre-revolutionary Mongolia was highly stratified, with vassals of nobles and monasteries, Chinese commercial credit, taxes in kind, and widespread debt. Chinese troops occupied Niislel Khuree in 1919, but Mongolian revolutionaries formed underground groups, created the MPP in 1920, sought Bolshevik support, and organized resistance. The revolution of 1921 began the socialist era.

## Between Russia and China

Mongolia's independence was confirmed by a plebiscite in October 1945, recognized by the Republic of China in 1946, and followed by diplomatic relations with socialist states, India, and later Western countries. Mongolia joined the [[United Nations]] in 1961. The 1950s brought collectivization, Soviet-supported development, the Trans-Mongolian Railway, Comecon ties, and shifting relations with China and the Soviet Union.

## Democratic Transition

In 1990, new parties including democratic, social democratic, and green groups registered for elections. The MPRP still won heavily, but political pluralism began. The Soviet collapse, debates over party power, and constitutional reform led to the 1992 democratic constitution. Later investigations examined communist-era purges, in which about 35,000 people are thought to have died, more than half of them lamas. Many victims were later exonerated and compensated.

## Rebirth of Genghis Khan

After 1990, Mongolians openly reassessed history, religion, and national identity. Buddhist life revived, the ninth Javzandamba visited Mongolia, and the Dalai Lama made several visits despite Chinese objections. In 2006, during the 800th anniversary of Genghis Khan's founding of the Mongol state, a new monument to [[Genghis Khan]] was unveiled in Ulaanbaatar, replacing the communist-era mausoleum of Sukhbaatar and Choibalsan and symbolizing a broader historical reorientation.

## Key Events & Developments

- 1206: [[Genghis Khan]] was proclaimed ruler of All the Mongols.
- 1368: Ming forces expelled the Yuan ruler from Dadu.
- 1911: Mongolia declared independence from Qing rule.
- 1924: The Mongolian People's Republic was proclaimed.
- 1990: One-party rule ended and democratic transition began.
- 1992: A new constitution created the modern democratic state structure.

## Key People

- [[Genghis Khan]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Genghis_Khan)): United the Mongol tribes and founded the Mongol state.
- Kublai Khan ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kublai_Khan)): Yuan ruler who moved the capital to Dadu and ruled China through Mongol imperial structures.
- Javzandamba/Bogd Khan: Religious leader who became central to Mongolia's 1911 independence.

## Interesting Facts

- Pastureland covers about three-fourths of Mongolia.
- Ulaanbaatar holds a very large share of the country's population and functions.
- Gers remain widely used because they are portable, strong, warm in winter, and cool in summer.
- Mongolia ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mongolia)) is nuclear-weapons-free as a core part of its national security policy.
