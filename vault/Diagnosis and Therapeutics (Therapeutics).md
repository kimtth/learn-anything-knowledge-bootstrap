# Diagnosis and Therapeutics (Therapeutics)

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> Therapeutics concerns the treatment of disease through medicines, procedures, rehabilitation, prevention, and supportive care. It depends on diagnosis, evidence, and understanding of biological mechanisms.

## Introduction

Therapeutics is the care of the patient as a whole, not just the treatment of one disease. It aims to prevent illness, reduce pain, and help the body recover from injury or organ failure. In this broad sense, good therapeutics includes drugs, surgery, counseling, exercise, diet, and attention to mental health. A physician works best when the patient trusts the method chosen, because confidence can improve the effect of treatment and make care easier to follow.

Preventive medicine is a major part of therapeutics. It begins by finding the risks that a person has and then reducing or removing those risks before disease starts. Primary prevention tries to stop illness before it appears, such as by vaccination. Secondary prevention looks for disease early, before symptoms become clear, so that treatment can begin sooner. Tertiary prevention tries to limit harm when disease is already present. Prevention is often cheaper and more effective than waiting for serious illness and then trying to repair the damage.

## Pain

![[attachments/diagnosis_and_therapeutics_therapeutics_001.webp|420]]

*Polio physical therapy*

Pain is the most common symptom in medicine, and it often needs treatment before the exact cause is known. It is both a physical feeling and an emotional experience, so fear, sadness, loneliness, and anger can make it worse. Acute pain usually acts as a warning signal that helps the body remove the cause, while chronic pain often lasts longer and is harder to explain and treat. A patient with a low pain threshold may report pain early, while another patient may wait until the problem is already advanced. That is why pain control must be personal and not based on one fixed rule for everyone.

The best pain treatment is to remove the cause when possible. A swollen structure can sometimes be removed surgically, a muscle spasm may improve with heat, and a broken bone may need to be set in a cast. When drugs are needed, mild to moderate pain is often treated with NSAIDs such as aspirin or ibuprofen, or with acetaminophen or indomethacin. Stronger pain may need opiates, but these medicines must be used carefully because they are addictive and tightly controlled. When possible, non-drug methods such as physical therapy should be used first or alongside medicine.

## Diarrhea

Acute diarrhea often comes from food poisoning, alcohol, laxatives, or an infection with bacteria such as Escherichia coli, Salmonella, or Staphylococcus aureus. In infants it is often self-limited, so the main concern is preventing dehydration. The treatment is not only about stopping loose stools; it is also about protecting fluid balance, because loss of water and salts can become dangerous quickly, especially in small children and frail adults.

Traveler's diarrhea affects many people who visit developing regions. Simple preventive habits help, such as bottled water, hot food, peeled fruit, and avoiding dairy products, raw seafood, raw vegetables, and food left at room temperature. In more serious cases, antibiotics may be needed. These measures show that careful food and drink choices can be as important as medicine in preventing illness.

## Cough

Cough is a normal reflex that clears the airways of mucus and foreign material. It can also be triggered by irritation in the airway, the lung, the diaphragm, the ear, or the stomach. The common cold is the most common cause of acute cough, while chronic cough often comes from cigarette smoke or postnasal drainage linked to allergy. Because cough is not always a sign of disease by itself, treatment should focus on the cause and on loosening secretions when the airways are dry or blocked.

Humid air, steam, warm drinks, and soup can help, and expectorants such as guaifenesin are used to thin mucus. Antihistamines may be useful when allergy is present, but they can also dry the mucosa more and are not always helpful. Decongestants reduce secretions but may raise blood pressure and should be used carefully in people with hypertension. Codeine can suppress cough strongly, while dextromethorphan offers a safer nonnarcotic option. A productive cough should usually not be suppressed, because clearing the airway is part of the body's defense.

## Insomnia

Insomnia is difficulty falling asleep or the feeling that sleep is not refreshing. It may be short term after stress, travel, shift work, anxiety, depression, stimulant use, or pain. Older adults often sleep less deeply and wake more often, and afternoon naps can make this worse. The first treatment is good sleep hygiene: keep a regular schedule, make the room comfortable, reduce noise and light, exercise during the day, and avoid caffeine, nicotine, and alcohol. These steps are simple, but they are often the strongest long-term treatment.

When medicine is needed, doctors often choose a benzodiazepine sleep aid, but it should not be used regularly for long periods. Other nonbenzodiazepine hypnotics and sedatives may also help. Designing a therapeutic regimen means more than choosing a drug. The doctor must think about why the patient came for help, what the patient expects, and whether the plan will be realistic in daily life. A good regimen is one the patient can understand and follow, with clear explanations of benefits, side effects, and the need for follow-up. In this way the plan respects both the disease and the patient's own life.

## Diet

![[attachments/diagnosis_and_therapeutics_therapeutics_003.webp|420]]

*Treating a injured person*

Good nutrition is a preventive tool as well as a treatment tool. The body needs essential amino acids, vitamins, minerals, water, and essential fatty acids, and these nutrients must come from food because the body cannot make them all by itself. The recommended allowances are guidelines for healthy people, not absolute minimums, and needs rise during growth, pregnancy, lactation, illness, and periods of heavy activity. Protein helps maintain structure and function, carbohydrate and protein each provide 4 kilocalories per gram, and fat provides 9 kilocalories per gram. Fiber is not digested, but it helps bowel movement and may lower some disease risks by changing how food moves through the intestine.

Diet must change with age, body state, and disease. A child, a pregnant woman, an older adult, and a person with infection do not need exactly the same intake. Nutrition is not a fixed formula; it is a flexible part of health care. A balanced diet should support growth, repair tissue, and keep the body from falling into deficiency. When diet is used carefully, it can prevent disease and support other therapies instead of working against them.

## Obesity

Obesity happens when a person consumes more calories than the body uses, so the extra energy is stored as fat. Many factors influence weight, including genes, hormones, activity level, eating habits, metabolism, and stress. Because obesity is a chronic illness, short, fashionable diets are usually not enough. Real treatment needs long-term change in food intake and exercise, with a goal of making the calorie balance negative in a safe and steady way. Lasting change matters more than quick loss.

The usual plan is to reduce calories and increase activity at the same time. A modest reduction can lead to steady weight loss, and exercise helps preserve muscle while burning fat. Very-low-calorie diets may be useful for some people, but they need vitamin and mineral support. Fad diets that remove one food group often work only briefly and may be unhealthy in the long run. In some cases surgery can change digestion more permanently, but the basic treatment still depends on lifestyle change.

## Hypertension

Many people with hypertension benefit from a low-sodium diet, and this is often one of the first treatment steps. Foods with high salt content should be limited, and a no-added-salt pattern can help. Potassium-rich foods such as fruits and vegetables may also be useful, especially when diuretics are part of the treatment and may lower potassium levels. Weight control is also important, because obesity increases blood pressure and adds cardiovascular risk. Here the diet is not a small detail; it is a core part of control.

Peptic ulcer care has also changed. Older bland diets and frequent milk intake are no longer the main treatment. Today the main advice is to avoid irritating foods such as very spicy dishes and coffee, while modern drug therapy reduces stomach acid more effectively than diet alone. Helicobacter pylori is also a major cause of chronic gastritis and recurrent ulcer in many patients, and this infection needs antibiotics plus a bismuth-containing compound.

## Osteoporosis

Osteoporosis is the loss of bone density, and it is most visible in hip and vertebral fractures. It is common in older men and women, and it is especially important in postmenopausal women who have not taken estrogen. Prevention begins early, because once osteoporosis is established, only limited repair is possible. Estrogen replacement, usually with calcium, can slow bone loss if started around menopause, and in women with an intact uterus progesterone must be added to reduce the risk of endometrial cancer. Prevention is the best strategy for this disease.

Biological therapy begins with blood and blood cells. Blood transfusion became useful only after blood groups were understood and cross-matching was possible. If the wrong blood type is given, red cells may break apart and cause a fatal reaction. Type O is the universal red cell donor because it lacks A and B antigens, while type AB can receive red cells from all ABO types because it has no anti-A or anti-B antibodies. Rh status matters too, and careful matching remains a basic safety rule in transfusion medicine.

Plasma is the liquid part of blood and contains clotting factors, immunoglobulins, and other proteins. Fresh frozen plasma is used when several clotting factors are missing, such as in severe liver disease or major bleeding. Cryoprecipitate is a more concentrated product and is useful for factor VIII, von Willebrand factor, factor XIII, and fibrinogen deficiency. Specific factor concentrates can also be prepared from plasma. These products show how blood components can be separated and used precisely instead of giving whole blood when that is not needed.

Immunoglobulins are another biological treatment. Immune serum globulin from healthy donors gives passive immunity against diseases such as measles and hepatitis A, and IVIG can give immediate antibody levels without painful injections. Hyperimmune serum globulin comes from donors with high levels of a specific antibody, and Rh-immune globulin is given to Rh-negative pregnant women to prevent hemolytic disease of the newborn. Other hyperimmune products help prevent hepatitis B, tetanus, rabies, and varicella-zoster after exposure. In this way ready-made antibodies can protect people while their own immune system is weak or too slow.

Bone marrow transplantation is not a transfer of a solid organ, but it still carries a serious risk of rejection, called graft-versus-host disease. It is used mainly for leukemia, aplastic anemia, and congenital immune defects. The recipient is usually prepared with irradiation and immunosuppressive drugs, and donor-recipient matching is very important. Autologous transplants use the patient's own marrow, often collected during remission, while matched allogeneic transplants, often from a sibling, are the most common. Matching and preparation determine success.

## Hormones

Hormones are chemical messengers that set body processes in motion and regulate specific organs. They include sex hormones, thyroid hormones, insulin, adrenal cortical and pituitary hormones, and growth hormones. Estrogen supports the female reproductive system, helps develop secondary sex characteristics, and contributes to bone formation. After menopause, lower estrogen levels help explain bone loss and osteoporosis, and replacement therapy may be used to reduce that effect. Estrogen can be taken by mouth, through the skin, vaginally, or by injection, showing that the same hormone can be delivered in different ways.

Progestins combined with estrogen are used in oral contraceptives, while progestin-only methods work by thickening cervical mucus so sperm cannot pass easily. Women older than 35 who smoke have a much higher risk of stroke and heart disease, so oral contraceptives are not recommended for them. Levonorgestrel implants provide long-term birth control, and testosterone represents the androgen group. Hormonal treatment therefore includes both natural replacement and targeted control of reproduction and growth, always with attention to risk and benefit.

## Drug Interactions

Drug interactions can change how a medicine is absorbed, cleared, or made stronger by another medicine. A well-known example is quinidine reducing the clearance of digoxin, which can push digoxin to a dangerous level. Two drugs may also have additive effects, so the combination becomes toxic even if each drug is safe alone. Interaction problems often appear when several doctors are involved or when a new symptom is actually a side effect of a recently started drug. Good medication review is therefore a safety habit, not an afterthought.

The patient should tell the doctor and pharmacist about all prescription and nonprescription medicines, and one physician should ideally keep track of the full list. The Food and Drug Administration approves new drugs, watches adverse reactions, labels medicines, and reviews manufacturing methods. Before human testing, an investigational new drug must be approved, and after successful trials a new drug application is needed before marketing. In serious illnesses, accelerated approval or special permission for limited use may be possible. This is a clear example of public control serving patient safety.

## Chemotherapy

Chemotherapy means using chemical agents to kill the cause of disease without harming the patient. In a strict sense, it refers to antibiotics against bacteria, viruses, fungi, or parasites, but in common use it usually means cancer treatment. The goal is to kill tumour cells while sparing normal cells, yet that is difficult because most anticancer drugs have a narrow therapeutic window. The physician must balance effect against harm, because a treatment that causes too much suffering may not be worth the benefit. That balance is the heart of chemotherapy.

The best chance of cure is often early, when the tumour is small and localized. The larger and more spread out the tumour is, the harder it becomes to destroy. The route of administration depends on the disease stage and the site that must be reached, so oral, intravenous, intra-abdominal, and intrathecal routes may all be used. Chemotherapy works best when enough drug reaches the tumour site for long enough to have an effect, which shows why timing and delivery matter as much as the drug itself.

## Cryosurgery

Cryosurgery destroys tissue with extreme cold. Liquid nitrogen can remove warts, precancerous skin lesions, and small skin cancers, and the same idea can be used for some cataracts, brain lesions, and heart conduction problems. This method is valued because it can destroy unwanted tissue with little cutting, and in the right setting it can be a precise local treatment. It is a clear example of using physics as therapy.

Stereotaxic surgery is a closely related neurosurgical method that locates deep brain lesions with three-dimensional coordinates while the head is held still in a frame. Once the target is found, the lesion can be treated with cold, heat, chemicals, or focused radiation. The same technique can guide fine-needle biopsy through a single burr hole, and it is also used for some breast lesions found on mammography. Minimally invasive surgery then extends the same idea of smaller injury, using endoscopes through small incisions to treat sinuses, disks, fallopian tubes, shoulders, knees, the gall bladder, the appendix, and the uterus. These techniques share a common aim: less trauma with more precision.

Trauma surgery is another reminder that injury care is a major medical need. Trauma is one of the leading causes of lost potential years of life, and modern medical tools have expanded what surgeons can do in urgent settings. The point is clear: surgery is not only for planned operations but also for emergencies where fast action can save life and function. Trauma care is another field where speed and technique matter together.

## Hyperthermia

Some tumours are more sensitive than normal tissue to heat around 43 C, especially in the centre where blood flow is poor and radiation works less well. Hyperthermia can use microwaves or ultrasound to raise the tumour temperature, and it may make radiation and chemotherapy more effective. This is one form of nonionizing radiation therapy. In simple terms, heat is being used as a helper for cancer treatment.

Photodynamic therapy and extracorporeal shock wave lithotripsy are two further methods. PDT uses a light-absorbing substance that stays in tumour cells and then destroys those cells when light of the right wavelength is applied. Possible targets include tumours of the bronchus, bladder, skin, and peritoneal cavity. ESWL uses focused shock waves to break stones in the kidney or upper ureter into small pieces that can pass out of the body, with water or a water-filled device helping transmit the shock waves. Both methods show the strength of targeted nonstandard treatment when ordinary surgery is not the best answer.

## Psychotherapy

Drug therapy for emotional disorders has expanded because more effective medicines now exist for conditions that were once hard to treat. Psychotropic drugs fall into three broad types: antipsychotic drugs, antianxiety agents, and antidepressant drugs. Antipsychotic medicines such as Thorazine helped many patients with schizophrenia leave mental hospitals and live in the community again. Schizophrenia is described as a severe disorder with delusions, hallucinations, and sometimes bizarre behaviour. Choosing the best antipsychotic may depend on the patient's metabolism and on the nature of side effects, so treatment is not one-size-fits-all. It is a case where individual response shapes the plan.

Antianxiety agents are often called tranquilizers, but they do not really tranquilize people; they reduce anxiety so that patients can cope better. The group includes barbiturates, benzodiazepines, nonbenzodiazepine-nonbarbiturates, and hypnotics. Barbiturates were used first and are still important for some sedative or seizure uses, while benzodiazepines became the main drugs for acute anxiety and some sleep problems. The broader point is that drug therapy can support psychotherapy by lowering fear, improving sleep, and making daily functioning easier. Antidepressants belong to the same psychotropic family, showing that mood treatment can involve more than one class of medicine.

## Core Equations

Health and behavioural sciences often quantify risk, dose, change over time, and population variation:

$$C(t)=C_0e^{-kt}$$

This models concentration falling exponentially over time, often used for clearance, decay, or elimination processes.

$$z=\frac{x-\mu}{\sigma}$$

A z-score tells how many standard deviations a value lies above or below the mean.

$$\text{risk}=\frac{\text{cases}}{\text{population}}$$

This ratio turns the preceding idea into a measurable comparison, with the numerator as the quantity of interest and the denominator as the scale used for comparison.

## Key Events & Developments

- ancient medicine: Treatment relies on rest, diet, surgery, and simple remedies.
- 19th and 20th centuries: Drug therapy, surgery, and preventive care become more systematic.
- modern period: Therapeutics ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Therapy)) becomes increasingly evidence-based and specialized.
