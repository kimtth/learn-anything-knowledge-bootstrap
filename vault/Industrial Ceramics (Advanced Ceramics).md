# Industrial Ceramics (Advanced Ceramics)

> [!abstract] Reference navigation

Their toughness, conductivity, translucency, chemical resistance, and electromagnetic or structural performance reflect modern materials science rather than ordinary clay processing.

## Introduction

Advanced ceramics ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ceramic)) are inorganic, nonmetallic materials engineered to exhibit precisely controlled properties beyond those of traditional ceramics. Instead of being defined mainly by hardness, brittleness, low electrical conductivity, and heat processing from powders, they are designed through modern materials science to be tough, conductive, translucent, chemically resistant, or useful in demanding electromagnetic and structural applications.

The VAMAS definition of 1993 describes an advanced ceramic as a basically crystalline inorganic, nonmetallic material of rigorously controlled composition, made from highly refined or characterized raw materials to achieve specified attributes. Advanced ceramics usually lack a glassy phase, have engineered microstructures, and require careful control of grain size, porosity, second phases, fibres, whiskers, and chemical purity.

## Consolidation

Consolidation shapes powders or slurries into green bodies before firing. Traditional processes such as pressing, extrusion, and slip casting are still used, but advanced ceramics demand higher precision. A notable example is the extruded honeycomb catalyst support used in automobile catalytic converters, with hundreds of open cells per square centimetre and walls less than 0.1 millimetre thick.

Tape casting ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tape_casting)) makes thin ceramic sheets for integrated-circuit substrates, electronic packages, and multilayer capacitors. In doctor blading, a ceramic powder slurry containing solvent, binder, and additives is cast onto a smooth moving surface, spread by a knife edge to a controlled thickness, dried, and rolled for later processing. Waterfall and paper-casting methods also produce controlled ceramic layers.

## Densification

Densification bonds ceramic particles and reduces porosity, usually by sintering. Advanced ceramics often use solid-state sintering, in which matter diffuses between adjacent particles under heat and pressure, forming necks and grain boundaries without relying on a glassy liquid. Because no residual glass remains at grain boundaries, the final ceramic can retain greater strength, conductivity, or high-temperature performance.

Sintering challenges come from pores trapped inside growing grains, which are hard to remove by longer firing alone. Sintering aids therefore help control diffusion and densification. In reactive-liquid or transient-liquid sintering, additives temporarily form a liquid that speeds early sintering and is then evaporated, absorbed, or crystallized. Alumina envelopes for sodium-vapour lamps show how densification control can combine refractoriness with translucency.

[[Chemical Bonding|Chemical bonding]] and hot-processing methods broaden densification options. Reaction-bonded silicon carbide and related processes can create strong ceramics at lower shrinkage than ordinary sintering. Hot pressing and hot isostatic pressing combine heat with mechanical or gas pressure to close porosity and improve density in materials that are difficult to sinter conventionally.

## Infiltration and Film Deposition

Infiltration fills pores through reaction with, or deposition from, a liquid or vapour. Melt infiltration uses liquids; chemical vapour infiltration uses vapour phases. These methods can begin with woven carbon fibres or felts and build ceramic composites with improved toughness, thermal resistance, or structural performance. Siliconization of reaction-bonded silicon carbide is an important example.

The Lanxide process creates metal-ceramic composites by reacting molten metal with a gas at the metal-gas interface. As the composite grows, contact with the melt continues to supply reactant metal. This process has produced complex cermet shapes such as boron carbide-boron, titanium nitride-titanium, and zirconium boride-zirconium composites.

Film deposition is crucial for electromagnetic and mechanical applications. Thick films may be made by paper casting or spin coating, in which centrifugal force spreads a ceramic suspension on a rotating substrate. Thin films less than one micrometre thick can be produced by physical vapour deposition, chemical vapour deposition, laser ablation, or sputtering.

## Products

Advanced ceramic products fall into two broad groups. Electromagnetic ceramics serve electric, magnetic, and optical uses, including substrates, capacitors, sensors, insulators, conductors, and optical components. Structural ceramics serve thermomechanical uses, including wear-resistant, heat-resistant, lightweight, or chemically durable parts. Both groups depend on carefully controlled powder preparation, forming, consolidation, densification, and finishing.

## Key Events & Developments

- Materials-science processing: Advanced ceramics emerged as engineered materials with controlled composition and microstructure.
- VAMAS definition: In 1993, advanced ceramics were defined as rigorously controlled crystalline inorganic nonmetals made from refined raw materials.
- Tape casting ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tape_casting)): Doctor blading and related methods enabled ceramic films for electronic substrates and multilayer devices.
- Solid-state sintering: Glass-free densification improved high-temperature and functional performance.
- Composite processing: Infiltration and Lanxide-style reactions expanded ceramic-metal and fibre-reinforced possibilities.

## Key People

- Materials scientists and ceramic engineers: Developed the powder processing, microstructure control, sintering, infiltration, and film-deposition techniques that distinguish advanced ceramics from traditional ceramics.
