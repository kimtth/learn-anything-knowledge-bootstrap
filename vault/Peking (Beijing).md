# Peking (Beijing)

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> Beijing, formerly romanized as Peking, is China's capital and a city whose national political role extends over eight centuries. Its site, planned layout, imperial and religious architecture, industry, institutions, and long history from Yuan Dadu through modern government make it a central Chinese urban landscape.

## Introduction

Beijing ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beijing)), formerly romanized as Peking, is a province-level municipality and the capital of the People's Republic of China. Few cities have served so long as the political headquarters and cultural centre of so vast a country. Its national importance reaches back more than eight centuries, and nearly every major old building in Beijing carries historical significance for China.

More than 2,000 years ago, the area north of modern Beijing was already a military and trading centre. In 1267, under the Yuan dynasty, the city of Dadu became China's administrative capital. The Ming first renamed it Beiping, then restored it as Beijing, or Northern Capital. Except during 1928-1949, when the Nationalist capital was Nanjing, Beijing remained or resumed the central political role.

## Landscape and Site

Beijing stands at the northern apex of the North China Plain ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_China_Plain)), between the Yongding and Chaobai rivers, near routes through the Yan and Jundu mountains. This position made it a gateway between the North China Plain, the Mongolian Plateau, and Manchuria. Passes such as Juyong and Gubei gave the city military and communications importance, while the surrounding mountains form the Bay of Beijing.

## Climate

Beijing has a continental monsoon climate. Although not far from the sea, it receives little maritime moderation because air circulation is mainly from the northwest. Winters are long, cold, dry, and windy under Siberian air; summers are hot and humid, with most rain falling from June to August. Precipitation is highly variable, producing drought and flood risks.

## City Layout

Traditional Beijing was organized around a strong north-south axis running through the Imperial Palaces, Tiananmen Square, Qianmen, and old southern gates. Symmetry shaped temples, altars, markets, and gates. Streets linked city gates and formed a rectangular grid. Important buildings faced south, a practice with both climatic value and ritual meaning connected to ancestry, heaven, and earth.

Since 1949, Beijing expanded streets beyond the old walls, added government districts, industrial zones, diplomatic areas, and modern high-rise construction. The old city walls were largely removed, but street names and gate sites preserve parts of the older plan.

## Housing and Traditional Architecture

Post-1949 housing projects provided apartments for office and factory workers, especially west, north, and east of the old city. Older courtyard compounds were repaired, subdivided, or replaced by multistory apartments. Traditional Beijing houses were rectangular walled compounds arranged around courtyards, with south-facing rooms, screen walls, gates, service courts, and hierarchical family spaces.

The Forbidden City and Imperial City express Beijing's court-centred plan. The Temple of Heaven, a World Heritage site, represents the height of traditional Chinese architecture. Its square southern enclosure and semicircular northern enclosure symbolize earth and heaven. The Hall of Prayer for Good Harvests, Imperial Vault of Heaven, and Circular Mound Altar are aligned on a ceremonial axis.

## People

Beijing's population fluctuated between about 700,000 and 1.2 million when it was dynastic capital. It grew from about 725,000 after the Qing fell in 1912 to more than 2 million by 1949, as refugees and migrants arrived during warlord disorder, Japanese expansion, and civil war. After 1949, government workers, industrial development, municipal expansion, and later market reforms drove rapid growth. Most residents are Han Chinese, with Hui, Manchu, and Mongol minorities.

## Agriculture and Food Supply

Unlike many world capitals, Beijing municipality includes significant agriculture ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agriculture)). Historically, grain came by the Grand Canal and later railways; after municipal expansion, Beijing became self-sufficient in vegetables, fruit, fish, poultry, and some construction materials. Irrigated vegetable belts, hothouses, orchards, vineyards, peach farms, and mechanized farms support the city, though groundwater depletion has been a major cost.

## Industry and Tourism

*Tiananmen Square in Beijing*

Since 1949, Beijing became one of China's most industrial cities, with steel, machinery, textiles, petrochemicals, electronics, telecommunications, and high technology. Environmental concerns later curbed some industrial expansion. Tourism ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tourism)) is crucial, drawing visitors to the Forbidden City, Temple of Heaven, Great Wall, Ming tombs, Zhoukoudian, parks, historic sites, and facilities improved for the 2008 Summer Olympics.

## Transportation

Beijing is China's railway centre, with lines to Shenyang, Shanghai, Guangzhou, Hong Kong, Baotou, Taiyuan, Moscow, Pyongyang, and Ulaanbaatar. Highways radiate outward, but automobile growth has produced congestion. Bicycles and carts remain important for short distances. Capital Airport serves domestic and international routes. China's first subway opened in Beijing in 1969, and later lines expanded rapidly, especially for the 2008 Olympics.

## Government

Beijing is one of China's centrally administered municipalities, directly below the central government. Formal authority belongs to the Beijing People's Government and Municipal People's Congress, but real power lies with the local Chinese Communist Party. Districts, counties, townships, police substations, neighbourhood offices, and residents' committees handle administration, taxation, planning, security, welfare, sanitation, and family planning.

## Health and Education

Beijing has some of China's best medical services, combining traditional Chinese and Western medicine. Peking Union Medical College Hospital, founded in 1921, and many specialized hospitals support care and teaching. Education shifted after 1949 from private and missionary schools toward state control, was disrupted by the Cultural Revolution, and later recovered. Beijing now has broad primary enrollment, secondary schools, vocational institutions, and leading universities.

## Recreation

As an imperial capital, Beijing has many parks and gardens. Zhongshan Park, Bei Hai Park, Jingshan Park, the Summer Palace, and the Beijing Zoo are prominent. Zhongshan Park includes the former Altar of Earth and Harvests, while Bei Hai Park contains lakes, islands, hillocks, pavilions, and the White Pagoda. These spaces preserve ritual, imperial, and recreational traditions.

## History

The Beijing region contains some of the earliest known human remains, including Peking man at Zhoukoudian, dated roughly 770,000 to 230,000 years ago. The Yan state established its capital Ji near modern Beijing during the Warring States period. Through Qin, Han, Three Kingdoms, and later dynasties, the site remained a frontier city between Chinese states and northern nomadic peoples.

In modern times, Beijing changed rapidly after 1949. Development in the 1950s and 1960s widened streets and created functional districts, but the Great Leap Forward and Cultural Revolution disrupted plans. Economic reforms after the early 1980s accelerated new shopping centres, housing, high-tech parks, a consumer middle class, pollution, congestion, and Olympic-era infrastructure.
