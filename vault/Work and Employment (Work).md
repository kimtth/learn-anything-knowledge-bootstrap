## What Work Means

Work is purposeful activity that produces goods, services, care, knowledge, or social order. It includes paid employment but also unpaid labour such as child care, cooking, cleaning, volunteering, subsistence farming, and caring for older or disabled relatives. A society depends on both market work and unpaid work, even though only paid work is usually counted in wages, employment statistics, or national income.

Employment is a specific social relationship in which a person sells labour to an employer in return for wages or salary. Not all workers are employees: farmers, shop owners, freelancers, partners, contractors, and cooperative members may control their own work more directly. The distinction matters because employees often receive legal rights concerning pay, safety, discrimination, hours, benefits, and collective bargaining that may not apply in the same way to independent workers.

## Historical Change

The division of labour ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Division_of_labour)) grows when a community can produce enough food and resources for some people to specialize. Farming supported craftspeople, traders, administrators, soldiers, teachers, healers, builders, and religious workers. Ancient cities and states relied on complex labour systems, including free workers, family labour, taxation, corvée obligations, and slavery. Large monuments and irrigation works show that work has long required coordination as well as physical effort.

In medieval Europe, guilds organized many urban crafts through apprenticeships, journeymen, masters, quality rules, and local market privileges. Guilds could protect skills and standards, but they could also limit competition and prevent advancement. Rural work was shaped by land tenure, seasonal farming, household production, and obligations to landlords or states. Similar combinations of farming, crafts, trade, and hierarchy existed in many regions of the world.

The Industrial Revolution changed work by concentrating machines, energy, and workers in factories. Wage labour became more common as people depended on cash income rather than household production or access to land. Factory systems increased output and lowered prices, but early industrial workers often faced long hours, dangerous machinery, child labour, poor housing, and weak bargaining power. Labour movements, unions, public education, labour law, and social insurance developed partly in response.

## Work Today

Modern economies contain manufacturing, agriculture, construction, services, public administration, science, health care, education, finance, logistics, and digital work. [[Automation]] can replace repetitive physical or clerical tasks while creating demand for design, maintenance, data analysis, care, and creative work. Its effects are uneven: workers whose tasks can be standardized may face displacement, while workers with scarce skills can gain productivity and bargaining power.

Work also affects identity, health, status, and social connection. Good jobs can provide income, skill, purpose, and community; insecure or unsafe jobs can create stress and poverty even when a person is employed. Questions about fair pay, work-life balance, discrimination, surveillance, remote work, platform labour, and the right to organize are therefore not only economic issues. They concern what people owe one another in a society organized through work.

## Key Concepts

- Paid work: labour exchanged for wages, salary, or business income.
- Care work: labour that supports children, older people, disabled people, households, and communities.
- Labour productivity: output produced per unit of labour time or input.
- Collective bargaining: negotiation between workers, often through unions, and employers.
- Informal work: economic activity outside full legal, tax, or employment protection systems.

## Key People

- Craft workers: maintained specialized knowledge through apprenticeship, practice, and workshop traditions.
- Trade unionists: organized workers to bargain over pay, hours, safety, and workplace rights.
- Caregivers: perform essential paid and unpaid work that supports households and communities.

## Interesting Facts

- Unpaid care work is necessary for every economy even when it is omitted from wage and employment statistics.
- Productivity can rise without improving workers’ well-being unless gains are shared through wages, time, safety, or public services.
- Informal work may provide income where formal jobs are scarce but often lacks legal protections and social insurance.
