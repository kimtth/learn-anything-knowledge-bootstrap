## Introduction

Respiration ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cellular_respiration)) is the system by which living organisms take in oxygen and release carbon dioxide to meet energy needs. Energy is released when carbon-containing molecules are oxidized, and carbon dioxide is produced as a waste product. Respiration includes exchange between the organism and its environment and between body cells and the fluid around them.

Most animal energy ultimately comes from sunlight. Plants use sunlight and atmospheric carbon dioxide to make sugars and other compounds. Animals obtain these compounds by eating plants or other organic matter, then oxidize them to support life processes.

Respiratory needs vary by species, size, habitat, and activity. Active animals require more oxygen and produce more carbon dioxide. Changes in body-fluid chemistry affect the central nervous system, which can increase or decrease the machinery of external respiration.

Air and water create different respiratory problems. Air contains far more oxygen than air-saturated water, while water is denser, more viscous, and harder to move over respiratory surfaces. Aquatic and terrestrial animals therefore evolved different structures for gas exchange.

## Trachea

The tracheal system is characteristic of insects. It consists of branching tubes that carry oxygen directly to tissues and remove carbon dioxide, so the circulatory system is not needed for gas transport. Openings to the outside, called spiracles, are usually paired on the thorax and abdomen.

Insects regulate spiracles to reduce water loss, a major danger in dry environments. Large insects may ventilate the tracheal system with abdominal pumping movements. Some aquatic insects modify the system to exchange gases underwater.

The water beetle Dytiscus is a bubble breather. It carries an air bubble under the wing surface near the spiracles before diving. As oxygen in the bubble falls, oxygen diffuses from water into the bubble, while carbon dioxide diffuses out. Nitrogen loss makes the bubble shrink, so the beetle must eventually surface again.

## Fishes

Fish respiration is based mainly on gills. Primitive cyclostomes, including lampreys and hagfishes, have gill pouches connected internally with the pharynx and externally through slits or ducts. Their gill lamellae form rings around the margins of each sac.

Parasitic lampreys maintain water flow through gills while attached to prey by alternately expanding and contracting the gill pouches. The branchial muscles help prevent water from flowing backward into the pharynx. Hagfishes such as Myxine glutinosa draw water through a nostril into the pharynx and use velar and gill-pouch contractions to direct flow.

Fish gills must move enough water over thin respiratory surfaces to extract oxygen. Because water contains less oxygen than air, effective pumping and large surface area are essential. Gill structures also need protection from foreign material that could block exchange.

## Amphibians

[[Amphibians]] depend on water-related respiration to different degrees according to species, stage, temperature, and season. Most begin life as aquatic larvae. Larvae exchange gases through thin skin and gills, and frog tadpoles also use vascular tail fins as respiratory surfaces.

During metamorphosis, gills and tadpole tail fins usually disappear, while paired lungs develop. Amphibian lungs are simple sacs, much less internally divided than bird or mammal lungs. Even in adults, the skin often remains important for gas exchange.

Some amphibians depart from the usual pattern. The axolotl may retain external gills throughout life while also developing lungs. Lungless salamanders of the family Plethodontidae lose gills after metamorphosis but never develop adult lungs, relying entirely on skin respiration.

## Reptiles

Land life required reptiles to develop water-resistant skin, which reduced its usefulness for respiration. Most reptiles therefore depend almost entirely on lungs, though some specialized reptiles, such as sea snakes, can obtain a large share of oxygen through the skin.

Reptile lungs are more internally partitioned than amphibian lungs, giving more surface area for gas exchange. Sea turtles such as Chelonia mydas have especially complex lungs suited to high swimming metabolism, with branching airways and many gas sacs.

Reptiles use an aspiration pump to inflate the lungs. In most species, rib and body-wall muscles expand the chest and lower lung pressure, drawing air in. Crocodiles use a specialized muscle attached to the liver, and turtles use specialized body-wall and limb-girdle muscles because the shell prevents ordinary rib expansion.

## Birds

[[Birds]] require very high gas exchange because their resting oxygen consumption is high and flight raises it many times. Their lungs are small compared with mammalian lungs, but the respiratory system includes large air sacs, making total system volume about twice that of similar-sized mammals.

Bird lungs are ventilated by unidirectional airflow. Air sacs inflate and deflate in sequence like bellows, so fresh air passes through the lungs in one direction during both inspiration and expiration. The lungs themselves do not inflate and deflate much; they remain nearly constant in volume.

Gas exchange occurs between air capillaries and blood capillaries. This system is efficient because fresh air continually crosses the exchange surface. Inspiration is produced by chest and abdominal expansion, including movement of the sternum and ribs; expiration follows compression of the air sacs by skeletal muscles.

## Mammals

*Diagram of the human respiratory system with lungs and airways*

Mammalian lungs are highly subdivided to support high metabolic rates. Repeated airway branching delivers air to alveoli, the tiny sacs that form the functional gas-exchange surface. Human lungs contain about 300,000,000 alveoli with an adult surface area roughly comparable to a tennis court.

Mammals also use an aspiration pump. The diaphragm and external intercostal muscles expand the chest, lowering pressure so air flows into the lungs. The diaphragm contracts downward, enlarging the chest cavity and pressing abdominal organs downward.

Expiration is usually passive when inspiratory muscles relax and stored elastic energy in the chest wall and lungs pushes air out. During exercise or laboured breathing, internal intercostal muscles and abdominal muscles actively reduce chest volume. This flexible system supports both quiet breathing and heavy activity.

## Key Events & Developments

- Early animal evolution: Different respiratory surfaces evolved for water and air.
- Larval amphibian stages: Gills, skin, and tail fins support aquatic gas exchange before lungs develop.
- Bird evolution: Unidirectional lung ventilation and air sacs supported high oxygen demand during flight.

## Key People

- Comparative physiologists: Studied how respiration differs among insects, fishes, amphibians, reptiles, birds, and mammals.
- Respiratory researchers: Connected gas exchange, circulation, metabolism, and environmental adaptation.

## Interesting Facts

- Insect tracheae deliver oxygen directly to tissues without blood carrying the respiratory gases.
- Bird lungs have one-way airflow, unlike the tidal airflow of most lunged vertebrates.
- Human alveoli provide a gas-exchange surface roughly the size of a tennis court.
- Lungless salamanders breathe entirely across the skin as adults.

## Core Equations

Aerobic respiration reverses the broad chemistry of photosynthetic storage:

$$\mathrm{C_6H_{12}O_6 + 6O_2 \rightarrow 6CO_2 + 6H_2O + energy}$$

This summarizes aerobic respiration: glucose reacts with oxygen to release usable energy, carbon dioxide, and water.

Gas exchange is driven by pressure gradients, often summarized by diffusion proportionality:

$$J \propto \Delta P$$

The delta symbol marks a change in a quantity, so the equation tracks how a system shifts between states rather than only describing a static value.
