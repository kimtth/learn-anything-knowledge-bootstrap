# Theory of Evolution (Evolution)

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> Evolution explains the unity and diversity of life as descent with modification: populations change genetically, lineages branch, and new species arise. The source integrates fossils, biogeography, Darwinian natural selection, Mendelian genetics, population genetics, adaptation, speciation, molecular evidence, and debates about evolution and religion.

## Introduction

![[attachments/evolution_001.webp|420]]

*Portrait of Charles Darwin*

Evolution ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Evolution)) is the biological theory that living things descend from earlier forms and change through successive generations. It explains both the unity of life through common ancestry and the diversity of life through branching lineages.

Biological evolution is descent with modification ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Evolution)). Populations change genetically over time, lineages split, and new species arise. All organisms are related, from bacteria to sequoias, fungi, insects, fish, birds, mammals, and humans.

The theory of evolution is a foundation of modern biology. It connects fossils, anatomy, embryology, genetics, ecology, biogeography, molecular biology, systematics, medicine, agriculture, and the history of life.

The scale of life's variety helped make the theory necessary. Named species already number in the millions, while many more remain undescribed. Living forms range from bacteria smaller than a thousandth of a millimetre to giant sequoias, from organisms in hot springs and Antarctic ice to animals near deep-sea vents and plants high on mountains.

## Evidence from Life's Diversity

The living world shows immense diversity. Millions of species differ in size, structure, habitat, feeding, reproduction, movement, and survival strategy. Evolution explains this variety as the result of branching from common ancestors under different conditions.

Common ancestry means that humans and other mammals descend from earlier mammalian ancestors, that vertebrates share deeper aquatic ancestors, and that plants and animals ultimately trace back to ancient microorganisms.

Evolutionary theory also explains why organisms are grouped in nested patterns. Species share traits with close relatives because they inherit them from common ancestors, while new traits arise in particular lineages.

## Fossils and Human Evolution

The fossil record documents extinct organisms and transitional patterns. Fossils show that many forms once lived that are no longer present and that major groups appeared and changed through geological time.

Human evolution is known through hominin fossils. Early forms such as Sahelanthropus, Orrorin, Ardipithecus, Australopithecus, Paranthropus, Homo habilis, Homo erectus, and later Homo species show changes in bipedalism, teeth, body, tools, and brain size.

These fossils do not form a simple ladder. They show branching, side lines, extinctions, overlapping species, and gradual mosaics of traits. Bipedal walking appeared before large human-like brains.

Fossils also show deep time. Evolutionary change becomes understandable only when measured across millions and billions of years, with many extinct lineages leaving partial but meaningful records in rock.

The fossil record is incomplete because preservation is rare, but incompleteness does not make it useless. Patterns of succession, extinction, and transitional anatomy can still be tested against geological order.

## Biogeography

![[attachments/evolution_002.png|420]]

*Darwin's Galapagos finches*

Biogeography strongly supports evolution. Islands and continents have distinctive organisms not fully explained by climate or suitability alone.

Hawaii has many unique Drosophila flies and land snails because isolated colonizers entered empty ecological niches and diversified. This process is called adaptive radiation.

Continents also differ. Africa has giraffes, zebras, gorillas, and rhinoceroses, while South America has llamas, armadillos, tapirs, jaguars, and broad-nosed monkeys. These patterns reflect historical separation, ancestry, migration, and extinction.

Biogeography also explains why islands often lack whole groups found on mainlands. Large mammals may be absent not because the climate is unsuitable, but because they never reached the islands naturally.

## Darwin and Natural Selection

![[attachments/evolution_003.webp|420]]

*Archaeopteryx fossil*

Charles Darwin founded the modern theory of evolution. His voyage on HMS Beagle, fossil finds in South America, and observations in the Galapagos helped him think about species origins.

Darwin's path was not a straight professional track. He left medical study at Edinburgh, prepared at Cambridge for a clerical career, and became deeply absorbed in natural history. The Beagle voyage then gave him years of field experience collecting specimens, comparing islands and continents, and thinking about extinct and living forms together.

In 1859, Darwin published On the Origin of Species, arguing that species change and that natural selection is a major cause. In The Descent of Man, he extended evolutionary thinking to human origins.

Natural selection ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_selection)) occurs because organisms vary, some variations are heritable, more offspring are produced than can survive, and individuals with useful traits leave more descendants. Favourable variations are preserved; harmful ones tend to be removed.

Natural selection mainly explains adaptation. It does not aim at progress, and it does not directly seek new species, but evolutionary change and diversification often result when populations adapt to different environments.

Darwin's wording stressed preservation and rejection rather than conscious choice. Slight useful variations could accumulate because individuals with even small advantages were more likely to survive and reproduce, while injurious variations tended to disappear. This made adaptation a population process acting across many generations.

Selection can be directional, favouring one extreme; stabilizing, preserving an intermediate form; or disruptive, favouring different extremes. Sexual selection can favour traits that help individuals obtain mates even when those traits carry survival costs.

Darwin's theory also emphasized common descent. Similar bones in vertebrate limbs, reduced organs, embryological resemblances, and nested classification patterns make sense when organisms inherit modified structures from ancestors.

Alfred Russel Wallace independently developed the idea of natural selection. Darwin received greater credit because he presented more evidence, developed the theory in detail, and persuaded the scientific community.

## Darwinian Aftermath

The Origin caused public excitement and controversy. T.H. Huxley, known as Darwin's bulldog, defended evolution strongly in debate and writing.

Herbert Spencer popularized evolutionary slogans such as “survival of the fittest,” but his broad social and metaphysical speculations encouraged social Darwinism, a misleading extension of biological struggle to human society.

Darwin's main scientific difficulty was [[Inheritance and Succession (Inheritance)|inheritance]]. Blending inheritance would dilute useful variation, making natural selection ineffective. The solution came from Mendelian genetics.

Darwin's own pangenesis idea still assumed that tiny contributions from the body shaped offspring, and ordinary blending theories implied that differences would be halved each generation. Mendel's work mattered because discrete hereditary factors could persist through generations, allowing selection to sort variation without erasing it.

## Mendel and the Modern Synthesis

![[attachments/evolution_004.webp|420]]

*Bust of Gregor Mendel*

Gregor Mendel's pea experiments showed that traits are inherited through discrete factors, later called genes. Mendelian inheritance preserved variation instead of blending it away.

At first, mutationists and biometricians disagreed. Mutationists thought new species arose suddenly by large mutations, while biometricians emphasized small continuous variation and natural selection.

In the 1920s and 1930s, R.A. Fisher, J.B.S. Haldane, and Sewall Wright used population genetics to show that Mendelian inheritance and Darwinian selection fit together.

Theodosius Dobzhansky's Genetics and the Origin of Species helped launch the modern synthesis. It joined genetics, natural selection, variation, populations, speciation, paleontology, and systematics into a unified evolutionary theory.

## Population Genetics

Evolution can be measured as changes in allele frequencies in populations. The Hardy-Weinberg law describes conditions under which allele and genotype frequencies remain stable when no evolutionary forces disturb them.

In the simplest two-allele case, parental allele frequencies can be represented as $p$ and $q$, with genotype frequencies $p^2$, $2pq$, and $q^2$ after random mating. This model is important not because real populations are never disturbed, but because it gives a baseline for detecting mutation, selection, drift, migration, and nonrandom mating.

Forces that change populations include mutation, natural selection, genetic drift, gene flow, nonrandom mating, recombination, and chromosomal change. Mutation supplies new variation, though mutation alone usually changes frequencies slowly.

The slowness of mutation by itself matters. Even repeated mutation at one gene may alter allele frequencies only gradually, so mutation is most powerful as a continuing source of novelty for selection, drift, and recombination. Population genetics therefore separates the origin of variation from the forces that spread or remove it.

Genetic drift is random change in allele frequencies, especially in small populations. It can fix or eliminate alleles by chance, and it can make separated populations diverge even without adaptive differences.

Gene flow moves alleles between populations through migration and interbreeding. It can spread useful genes, but it can also slow divergence by mixing populations that would otherwise become different.

Selection changes gene frequencies when genotypes differ in fitness. Fitness means reproductive success, not strength alone. A trait is favoured if it helps carriers survive and reproduce in a given environment.

Overdominance occurs when heterozygotes have higher fitness than either homozygote. The sickle-cell allele is a classic example: in malarial regions, carriers can be protected from severe malaria even though homozygotes suffer sickle-cell disease.

Selection can preserve polymorphism when different forms do well under different conditions. In crowded environments, under different predators, or across varied habitats, more than one allele or visible form may remain in a population.

Population genetics made evolution mathematical without removing biology. It allowed scientists to estimate how quickly mutation, selection, drift, migration, and mating patterns can change a population.

## Adaptation and Variation

Adaptations are traits shaped by selection for functions such as feeding, movement, camouflage, mating, defense, temperature regulation, or reproduction. They are not perfect designs; they are constrained by ancestry, development, trade-offs, and chance.

Evolution also includes neutral or nearly neutral changes, especially at the molecular level. Some genetic changes spread by drift rather than because they improve adaptation.

Variation is maintained by mutation, recombination, migration, changing environments, heterozygote advantage, and balancing selection. Without variation, evolutionary change would have little material to act upon.

Adaptation must also be understood through trade-offs. A trait useful in one setting may be costly in another, and natural selection usually improves local reproductive success rather than producing ideal engineering.

Evolution also works through co-option. Structures that evolved for one function can later serve another, such as feathers, bones, enzymes, or behavioural patterns gaining new roles in changed contexts.

## Species and Speciation

The biological species concept defines species as interbreeding natural populations reproductively isolated from other such groups. Species are independent evolutionary units because genes can spread within them but not freely between them.

In practice, species boundaries can be difficult because evolution is gradual. Populations may be partly separated, poorly known, extinct, asexual, hybridizing, or changing through time.

Reproductive isolating mechanisms are prezygotic or postzygotic. Prezygotic mechanisms prevent fertilization through ecological, temporal, behavioural, mechanical, or gametic isolation. Postzygotic mechanisms reduce hybrid survival or fertility.

Ecological isolation occurs when related populations live in different habitats and rarely meet. Mosquito species may breed in brackish water, running fresh water, or stagnant fresh water, keeping their gene pools separate.

Temporal isolation occurs when reproduction happens at different times. Some orchids flower for only one day after the same weather stimulus, but each species opens after a different number of days, preventing interbreeding.

Behavioural isolation is common in animals. Songs, courtship displays, odours, colours, movements, and mating signals can attract members of the same species while failing to attract close relatives.

Speciation often begins when populations become geographically isolated and diverge genetically. It can also occur through ecological divergence, sexual selection, chromosomal change, or, in plants, polyploidy.

Allopatric speciation occurs when geography separates populations, such as mountains, water, deserts, glaciers, or distance. Over time, mutation, drift, and selection can make separated populations reproductively incompatible.

Sympatric speciation can occur without geographic separation when ecological specialization, mating preferences, host shifts, or chromosomal changes reduce gene exchange inside the same area.

Polyploidy multiplies whole chromosome sets. It is common in flowering plants and can create new species rapidly, especially when chromosome differences prevent breeding with the parent population.

Cultivated and wild examples show how broad the process can be. Bananas are triploid, potatoes tetraploid, bread wheat hexaploid, and some strawberries octaploid. Odd-numbered polyploids are often sterile because chromosomes cannot separate evenly in sex cells, but they may persist through asexual propagation or cultivation.

Polyploidy also shows that speciation is not always slow. In plants, a chromosomal change can create reproductive isolation in one or a few generations, although the new form must still survive ecologically.

## Molecular Evolution and Trees

Molecular biology gives powerful evidence for evolution. DNA, proteins, and genetic codes reveal shared ancestry and allow comparison of lineages.

Proteins such as cytochrome c show that closely related species have fewer amino acid differences than more distant species. Humans and rhesus monkeys are more similar to each other than either is to horses.

Such comparisons are useful because they can be counted. If two lineages differ at only one position in a protein while each differs more from a third lineage, the simplest tree places the first two closer together. Molecular evidence therefore turns relatedness into testable patterns of shared change.

Evolutionary trees reconstruct relationships among species or larger groups. Branching patterns show cladogenesis, or lineage splitting, while changes along branches show anagenesis, or transformation within a lineage.

Molecular data, fossils, anatomy, and biogeography can be combined to test phylogenies. When independent lines of evidence converge, confidence in evolutionary history increases.

Molecular evolution also studies rates of change. Some genes change slowly because their functions are strongly constrained, while other regions change faster and help distinguish recently separated lineages.

Comparative genomics reveals shared errors, duplicated genes, pseudogenes, transposable elements, and conserved sequences. These details are strong evidence for common ancestry because they are inherited patterns, not merely surface similarity.

## Evolution and Religion

Evolution ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Evolution)) changed humanity's self-understanding as deeply as astronomy changed Earth's place in the universe. It challenged views that species were fixed and that humans stood outside nature.

Many religious authorities accept evolution as compatible with faith. They distinguish scientific explanation of biological change from theological claims about creation, meaning, and human relation to God.

Some groups reject evolution through creationism, often based on literal readings of Genesis. In the United States, opposition to evolution teaching has been linked to movements such as Seventh-day Adventism, Pentecostalism, creation science, and later intelligent design.

The design argument, made famous by William Paley, compared the eye to a telescope and claimed that complex adaptation proves a designer. Darwin answered that complex organs can arise by many small, useful modifications.

Modern intelligent design revived claims of irreducible complexity. Evolutionary biologists respond that natural selection, mutation, co-option, duplication, gradual change, and comparative evidence can explain complex biological systems.

The debate over Genesis often turns on interpretation. Many theologians, including Augustine and later John Paul II, argued that Scripture teaches religious truth and humanity's relation to God, not technical astronomy, geology, or biology.

Public controversies over creation science and intelligent design were especially important in American education. Courts and school boards repeatedly had to decide whether proposed alternatives to evolution were science or religious doctrine.

The religious debate has never been uniform. Many Christian and Jewish authorities accepted evolution while arguing that scripture teaches religious meaning rather than technical biology, astronomy, or geology. Literalist creationism, by contrast, treated Genesis as factual natural history and became politically influential in some American school debates.

Evolutionary theory also affected [[Medicine|medicine]] and agriculture. Antibiotic resistance, pesticide resistance, viral change, crop breeding, artificial selection, and conservation biology all depend on evolutionary principles.

At larger scales, macroevolution studies extinction, adaptive radiation, long-term trends, developmental constraints, and major changes in body plans. These patterns do not replace population genetics; they extend evolutionary questions across deep time.

Paleontology adds a distinctive perspective because most species that ever existed are gone. Extinction removes lineages as well as individuals, and mass extinctions can reset ecological possibilities by opening habitats to surviving groups. Adaptive radiations after such crises show that chance, geography, and ecological opportunity can redirect life's history.

The strength of evolutionary theory lies in testing many kinds of evidence together. Fossils, genes, anatomy, embryos, geography, and observed selection repeatedly support the same branching history of life across changing environments, inherited constraints, local adaptations, population histories, genetic mechanisms, ecological pressures, extinction events, radiations, and immense spans of time.

Its explanatory power also comes from scale: the same mechanisms can be observed in present populations, modeled in genetics, and traced through fossils and genomes.

Evolution also explains homology, the presence of similar structures in different organisms because of common ancestry. The forelimbs of humans, whales, bats, horses, and birds have different functions but share underlying bone patterns inherited from earlier tetrapods. Vestigial structures, such as reduced eyes, pelvic remnants, or nonfunctional genes, also make sense as inherited features modified by changing use.

Developmental biology deepened this picture. Embryology shows that related organisms often share early developmental pathways, and evolutionary developmental biology studies how changes in regulatory genes can alter body plans, timing, and form. Major differences can arise not only from new structural genes but also from changes in when and where old genes are expressed.

The fossil record includes many transitional forms that connect larger groups. Fish-tetrapod, reptile-mammal, dinosaur-bird, horse, whale, and hominin sequences show mosaics of old and new traits. These fossils are not missing links in a single chain; they are evidence of branching populations changing through time.

Their order in rock layers also matters. A proposed evolutionary sequence must fit geological age, anatomy, and related evidence; a fossil in the wrong period would challenge the hypothesis. Instead, major transitional finds generally appear where evolutionary models predict them.

Evolutionary theory also addresses extinction. Most species that ever lived are extinct, and mass extinctions altered the direction of life's history by removing dominant groups and opening ecological opportunities for survivors. Adaptive radiations after extinction events show how contingency and opportunity shape diversity.

Human evolution is part of this broader story. Homo sapiens shares common ancestry with other primates, but human language, culture, symbolic behaviour, tool traditions, and social learning became major forces in human adaptation. Biological evolution and cultural evolution interacted rather than replacing one another.

Finally, evolution is directly observable. Antibiotic resistance, pesticide resistance, experimental evolution in microbes, changes in finch beaks, industrial melanism, and viral evolution show populations changing under selection, drift, mutation, and gene flow. These short-term examples do not by themselves show all deep history, but they demonstrate the mechanisms that also operate over geological time.

## Core Equations

Biological systems are often summarized with growth, heredity, and energy-transfer relations:

$$\frac{dN}{dt}=rN\left(1-\frac{N}{K}\right)$$

The logistic-growth equation models population growth that slows as population size N approaches carrying capacity K.

$$p^2+2pq+q^2=1$$

Hardy-Weinberg proportions describe expected genotype frequencies when allele frequencies are p and q under idealized assumptions.

$$\Delta G=\Delta G^\circ+RT\ln Q$$

This equation adjusts standard free-energy change for actual concentrations or pressures through the reaction quotient.
