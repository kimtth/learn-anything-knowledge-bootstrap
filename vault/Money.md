## Introduction

Money ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Money)) is any commodity or object accepted by general consent as a medium of economic exchange. It expresses prices and values, circulates from person to person and country to country, makes trade easier, and serves as a principal measure of wealth. Its power does not come only from physical substance; it comes from shared acceptance.

## The Nature and History of Money

Modern paper money is a social convention. A note marked 1 dollar, 10 euros, 100 yuan, or 1,000 yen has little value as paper, but it can buy goods because everyone expects others to accept it. That common expectation gives money value, but the convention can break down when governments greatly expand the quantity of money, especially during or after wars. In severe cases people turn to substitutes such as cigarettes or cognac, as in [[Germany]] after [[World Wars (World War II)|World War II]].

The basic function of money is to separate buying from selling. Barter requires a double coincidence: each trader must want exactly what the other offers. Money removes that problem because a seller can accept general purchasing power and later use it with another seller. Credit can partly do this, but credit requires information about repayment, while money avoids those costs.

Money also serves as a temporary store of purchasing power. A seller can hold money between sale and purchase, and a buyer can draw on money to make payment. This asset function explains why stable money is important. The German currency reform after 1948, paired with removal of price controls, helped replace barter with a functioning money economy and supported recovery.

Many objects have served as money, including wampum, cowries, whales' teeth, tobacco, stone disks on Yap, cattle, and cigarettes. Metals became important because they were durable, useful, and easier to standardize than many goods. Coinage allowed payment by count rather than by weight, but it also created problems such as clipping, sweating, debasement, and the pattern summarized by Gresham's law: bad money drives out good when legal exchange rates are fixed.

Metallic money developed from weighed metal into stamped coin. Coinage is traced to early Lydia under Croesus, where electrum coins carried marks certifying weight or fineness. Greek and Roman histories show different outcomes. Solon debased Athenian currency only partly in 594 BC, and the drachma remained stable for centuries. Rome repeatedly reduced metal content, especially after Nero, using money creation to cover expenses and contributing to price rises and economic strain.

Paper money began in China more than 1,000 years ago and later spread through banknotes and deposits. Early fiduciary money represented promises to pay gold or silver, but bankers learned that not all holders would redeem at once and issued more claims than metal reserves. Governments later defined legal tender, supervised coinage and notes, and issued fiat money: paper declared valuable by sovereign authority rather than by a promise to pay metal.

Fiat money often gained a bad reputation when overissued. [[France]] issued paper money in the early 18th century and assignats from 1789 to 1796; the American colonies and Continental Congress issued bills of credit. During the Napoleonic Wars and the American Civil War, governments suspended convertibility, issued paper, and later faced choices between deflation, devaluation, or return to metallic standards. Paper money also created forgery problems because production cost is far below face value.

Standards of value connected national monetary units to fixed weights of silver, gold, or both. Bimetallism often became monometallism because market ratios shifted. In Europe, silver commonly drove out gold, while Isaac Newton's mint ratio in Britain overvalued gold and encouraged an effective gold standard. The United States moved from a silver tendency under the 1792 ratio to a gold tendency after the 1834 change.

The gold standard spread after gold discoveries in California and Australia, Britain's financial dominance, and later adoption by Germany, the Latin Monetary Union, the Scandinavian Union, and the Netherlands in the 1870s. Under the international gold standard, currencies were defined by fixed weights of gold, exchange rates were held near parity by gold-shipping costs, and the price-specie-flow mechanism moved gold and money between countries until trade imbalances adjusted through prices.

The gold standard limited irresponsible monetary expansion, but that virtue also became its weakness. In an age of large governments and full-employment policy, a real gold standard restricted monetary policy. [[World Wars (World War I)|World War I]] effectively ended the old system. Attempts in the 1920s to restore prewar parities forced deflation in some countries. The restored gold exchange standard did not work automatically because central banks such as the Federal Reserve and French authorities sterilized gold inflows, spreading deflation and unemployment. Britain left gold in 1931, the United States followed in 1933, and remaining major gold countries left by 1936.

## The Bretton Woods system

![[attachments/money_001.webp|420]]

*Bretton Woods conference marker*

The Bretton Woods system was designed during [[World Wars (World War II)|World War II]] by Great Britain and the [[United States of America (United States)|United States]] and approved by more than 40 countries in July 1944. It tried to avoid both unstable floating rates and rigid deflationary adjustment under the interwar gold exchange standard. It created the International Monetary Fund, whose members paid subscriptions, maintained fixed but adjustable exchange rates, and could borrow or lend when payment deficits or surpluses arose.

The system began operating in 1947 and became fully effective after 1958. Gold remained fixed at $35 per ounce, but the world economy effectively moved onto a dollar standard. The U.S. dollar became the main reserve currency, the common unit for trade and exchange-rate expression, and the currency central banks bought and sold to maintain official rates. Over time, however, growing foreign dollar holdings made the gold commitment difficult for the United States to maintain, especially as major countries devalued or revalued in the 1960s.

## After Bretton Woods

After fixed exchange rates broke down, countries no longer had to maintain a fixed currency price against gold or other currencies. Floating exchange rates meant that exchange rates rose or fell with market demand. Appreciation made domestic goods and assets more expensive to foreigners; depreciation made them cheaper. Governments and central banks often intervened, but long-run exchange rates still depended on productivity, investment opportunities, saving, and monetary and fiscal policy.

The difference between fixed and floating systems is mainly the method of adjustment. Under fixed rates, countries adjust through costs and prices of goods and services. Under floating rates, much of the adjustment occurs through the nominal exchange rate. The same economic fundamentals still operate as long as markets for goods, services, assets, and foreign exchange remain open.

## Currency

Modern currency usually consists mainly of central-bank notes and token coins. Federal Reserve notes or Bank of England notes are not meaningful claims to gold or special assets; they are accepted because the public accepts them. Coins usually have metal value far below face value. In high-inflation countries, people may use foreign currency, especially the U.S. dollar, because it is widely recognized and carries lower information costs. By the turn of the 21st century, estimates suggested that as much as two-thirds of all dollars circulated outside the United States.

Bank deposits are also counted as money. Earlier economists often treated deposits as claims to money, but as checks and deposits became widely used in transactions, deposits themselves became part of the money holdings of the public. This broadened the idea of money from visible currency to the balances people use for payments.

## Money, Income, and Prices

Changes in the quantity of money are consistently, though not exactly, related to changes in nominal income. Velocity is not constant, but it is relatively predictable. Monetary growth affects nominal income after a lag, often around six to nine months, while effects on prices arrive later. On average, the delay between monetary growth changes and inflation changes is roughly two years.

Monetary changes affect output mainly in the short run, which may last three to five years, but in the long run the growth rate of money affects prices rather than real output. Long-run output depends on real factors such as enterprise, thrift, industrial and government structure, law, and international relations. Sustained inflation cannot occur without money growing faster than output, though the causes of monetary growth can vary.

## Conclusion

Money ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Money)) has been studied longer and more intensively than almost any other subject in economics. The field contains broad historical evidence and strong theoretical analysis. Public debate often exaggerates disagreement among specialists, but economists still differ over empirical questions, especially the stability and exact form of relations between money and other economic phenomena.
