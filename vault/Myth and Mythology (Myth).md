# Myth and Mythology (Myth)

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> Myth is a culturally authorized narrative that differs from argument, ritual, and material sacred symbols by telling rather than proving or enacting. This survey distinguishes myths from fables, folktales, legends, and parables, and follows allegorical, Romantic, comparative, folkloric, and functionalist interpretation.

## Introduction

![[attachments/myth_001.webp|420]]

*Greek mythology vase*

Myth ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Myth)) is a symbolic narrative, usually traditional and of unknown origin, that presents itself as an authoritative account of actual events, especially events connected with religious belief. Myths tell of gods, superhuman beings, origins, extraordinary circumstances, and times outside ordinary human experience. Mythology means both the study of myth and the body of myths belonging to a religious tradition.

Myths do not usually try to prove themselves or make their events plausible. Their authority comes from being accepted, not argued, and they are often easiest to identify only after another worldview has displaced them. The Greek mythos ranged from word and story to fiction, while logos came to mean reasoned, demonstrable truth. Myths differ from rituals, sacred objects, and sacred places because they narrate rather than act or embody symbolically.

## Fables

Fables ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fable)) are instructive tales, often using personified animals or natural objects, that end with explicit moral lessons about social behaviour. Unlike myths, fables present themselves as fictional or illustrative rather than sacredly true. Their settings are usually vague, while myths often name gods, heroes, places, and genealogies. A fable teaches directly; a myth reveals or authorizes a sacred order less reducible to a simple rule.

## Folktales

The relation between folktale and myth is disputed. Stith Thompson treated myth as a type of folktale dealing with sacred events at the beginning. Geoffrey S. Kirk separated myth as story with a purpose beyond entertainment from folktale as story of ordinary fears and desires. Folktale motifs, such as humble heroes, giants, witches, jealous sisters, or wicked stepmothers, also appear in myths, so classification is often partly arbitrary.

The modern idea of folktale grew from late-18th-century nationalism. European scholars argued that stories told by ordinary people preserved a nation's deep past. The Grimm brothers collected Marchen, or tales, and German legends as expressions of the Volk, shaping later folklore study.

## Legends

![[attachments/myth_002.webp|420]]

*Norse mythology manuscript*

Legends are traditional tales thought to have a historical basis, such as stories of King Arthur or Robin Hood. They are often distinguished from myths by their connection to human history rather than sacred supernatural beginnings. But the boundary is unstable: stories accepted within one's own religion may be called legends, while comparable stories from another tradition may be called myths.

## Parables

Parables illustrate a doctrine or standard of conduct. New Testament parables, Sufi tales, rabbinic interpretation, Hasidic stories, and Zen Buddhist examples clarify teachings known from elsewhere. Because a parable serves a prior lesson, it has a more subordinate function than myth and usually lacks myth's independent revelatory authority.

## Etiologic Tales

Etiologic tales explain causes: the origin of customs, natural features, divine attributes, or states of affairs. Some scholars treat them as myths, while others regard etiology as only one possible function within a myth. A tale explaining Shiva's blue neck through primordial poison is etiologic, but origin explanation alone does not define myth.

## Allegorical Interpretation

Allegorical interpretation reads mythical figures as coded ideas. Ancient commentators explained battles of gods in the Iliad as conflicts between elements: dry against wet, hot against cold, fire against water, reason against desire. Allegory creates one-to-one correspondences between mythic clothing and abstract meaning, but this can narrow myths whose meanings work on many levels.

## The Romantic Movement

The Romantic movement of the late 18th century valued emotion, folk tradition, and ancient vitality over Enlightenment rationalism. Myths and folktales were treated as living repositories of experience more powerful than elite artificial art. Johann Gottfried von Herder praised the songs and stories of the Volk and argued that ancient myths expressed natural human encounters with sea, storm, fear, and wonder.

## Herder's Travel Reflection on Myth

Herder's travel reflection argued that one understands myth best by feeling conditions like those faced by ancient people. On a ship, lightning, ocean, danger, and dependence on signs made Greek poetry seem newly natural. For him, myth arose from real existential concerns shared by ancient peoples and ordinary people of his own day.

## Comparative Study

Since Romanticism, the study of myth has been strongly comparative. Philosophical inquiry into myth requires examples across many societies, and even specialized studies must consider other traditions. Comparison made it possible to ask general questions about myth's nature, function, patterns, and relation to culture.

## The Folkloric Approach

The folkloric approach collected and classified popular stories, customs, motifs, and lower mythology. Wilhelm Mannhardt compared ancient Greek rituals with northern European agricultural customs. James Frazer, the Grimm brothers, and Stith Thompson extended this method through vast collections, classifications, and motif indexes. Folklore study often treated peasant and ancient traditions as comparable sources for myth.

## Functionalism

Functionalism asks how myth supports society rather than where it originated. Marcel Mauss called myths and religious symbols total social facts because they join religious, legal, moral, economic, aesthetic, and mythological threads. Bronislaw Malinowski and A.R. Radcliffe-Brown argued that customs, beliefs, and ideas contribute to the working of social systems. Myth, in this view, validates institutions and relationships.

## Total Social Phenomena

The idea of total social phenomena means that myth cannot be reduced to one function. A gift exchange, ritual, or sacred story may be economic, legal, religious, moral, and aesthetic at once. To understand myth, one must view it inside the concrete whole of social life.

## Structuralism

Structuralism treats myth as analogous to language. Just as language depends on meaningful oppositions, myths organize thought through contrasts such as raw/cooked, upstream/downstream, bush/village, or culture/nature. Claude Levi-Strauss argued that so-called primitive thought is logically sophisticated, using everyday categories and objects to build complex taxonomies. Structuralism searches for the underlying logic of myth across cultures.

## Formalism

Formalism studies the surface sequence of narrative action. Vladimir Propp analyzed folktales by identifying recurring actions that appear in fixed order, such as interdiction, violation, exposure of the false hero, and marriage of the hero. Walter Burkert later related recurrent Greek myth patterns to biological or cultural programs, such as puberty, defloration, pregnancy, delivery, or scapegoat expulsion.

## Explanation

Myths often explain natural or cultural facts. An Abenaki myth explains corn as the returning hair of a mysterious woman who teaches a lonely man how to burn and prepare the ground. The narrative does more than answer a causal question; its imaginative power makes the explanation memorable, emotionally credible, and educational within tradition.

## Description

Myths also describe unseen realities: the world's origin, the world's end, paradise, the underworld, or divine order. In traditional societies without separate philosophical systems, myths provide major models of instruction. Creation myths may be recited for healing, such as among the Navajo, where cosmogony helps restore a threatened personal world. Myths also renew arts, as in Norse stories of Odin winning the mead of poetic inspiration.

## Myth and Psychology

Sigmund Freud interpreted myth psychologically through dreams, repression, and family desire. In 1899, he formulated the Oedipus complex; in 1913, he treated myth as the distorted wish-dreams of peoples and linked it to a hypothetical primal horde. His anthropology has largely been rejected, but his interest in unconscious mythic mechanisms remains influential.

Carl Jung also read myth psychologically, building on ideas of archaic or prelogical mentality from Lucien Levy-Bruhl. Jungian approaches emphasize recurring images, collective structures, and psychic patterns. Critics warn against reducing myths to a single psychological theme, but psychology remains one major way of interpreting myth.

## Myth and Ritual

The relation between myth and ritual is close but varied. Older theories, especially after Frazer, often claimed ritual came first and myth explained it afterward. Modern scholars usually avoid a simple priority claim. Some myths narrate what rituals enact through action; others have no known ritual counterpart. Myths of origin often reflect dominant cultural forms: hunting, agriculture, pastoral life, initiation, harvest, or secret societies.

## Music

Myth and music are connected through origin stories, ritual performance, and structure. Greek myth says Hermes invented the lyre and gave it to Apollo; Chinese myth tells how Ling Lun found the foundation tone in a bamboo pipe. Orpheus charms even the underworld with music to recover Eurydice. Levi-Strauss compared mythic sequences to parts of a musical work.

## Animals and Plants in Myth

Animals and plants appear throughout oral and written myth because they help cultures think about boundaries: human and animal, culture and nature, gods and humans, life and death, purity and transgression.

## The Problem of Boundaries

Myths about animals and plants explore opposition, descent, mixture, transformation, identity, and similarity. Some societies stress separation between humans and animals; others imagine a primordial time when categories were fluid. The African Lele distinguished animals by lack of manners, extreme fertility, and avoidance of humans; animals crossing these boundaries could be treated as human-animals connected with sorcery or transmigration.

## Defining the Human

Since the 18th century, humans have often been defined culturally rather than biologically: as beings with language, symbols, tools, play, self-consciousness, or history. Myths map the boundaries needed for survival, ethics, territory, food, kinship, and relation to gods. Hunters, agriculturalists, pastoralists, and tuber planters all imagine animals and plants differently because their lifeways differ.

## Cosmogonies

Cosmogonic myths sometimes use animal or plant symbolism. A cosmic tree may support the world; the earth may rest on a turtle or be encircled by a serpent; rivers, lakes, and hills may arise from animal acts. Creation by sacrifice is common, with the world formed from an animal's body. Dualistic creation myths oppose creator and animal chaos-monster, while earth-diver myths use an animal helper or opponent.

The origin of [[Death|death]] is often explained by a perverted message or competing messengers. An animal may be sent to tell humans they are immortal but changes the message, or two animals may carry opposite messages and the death message arrives first. These stories connect animal action with the permanent human condition.

## Totemism

Totemism is mainly a social relationship between a human group and an animal or plant species. It may include origin myths, exogamy, taboos, and group identity, but these features vary. Claude Levi-Strauss argued in 1962 that totemism was not a primitive stage of thought but an example of using everyday natural categories to classify social experience.

## Hierarchy

Descent myths raise questions of hierarchy: whether humans are superior to, inferior to, or continuous with animals and plants. Some myths say first humans were amorphous or animal-like; others portray animals resisting human creation or primordial humans ruling nature. Such themes carry forward into wild-man stories, transformation myths, and identity traditions.

## Relationships of Mixture

Many cultures treat mixture across boundaries as dangerous because cosmic order depends on each being remaining in place. Rituals may reenact the original separation of categories, while caste systems or astrology maintain ordered distinctions. Festivals and rites of passage temporarily break boundaries, producing controlled chaos before order returns.

## Relationships of Transformation

Transformation myths describe humans becoming animals, plants, or hybrids; animals becoming humans or other animals; plants becoming animals; or the dead returning in living forms. Transformation may be caused by evil magic, divine power, ritual skin-wearing, initiation, festival reversal, or self-transcendence. Some tales end with disenchantment and restoration; others celebrate boundary-crossing as release of life.

## Soul-stuff

Soul-stuff, a term from Albertus Christiaan Kruyt, describes an impersonal reservoir of vitality circulating through humans, animals, and plants. It is especially associated with Indonesian rice cultivators but appears elsewhere. The same life-stuff may return to a creator for redistribution or enter new human, animal, or plant embryos, emphasizing continuity of life rather than personal survival.

## Death Soul and Plural Souls

Traditions of the postmortem soul describe a soul that leaves the body after death or comes into being at death. It may take animal or plant form, be guided by birds or butterflies, transmigrate, or reappear as a spirit. Other traditions teach plural souls: for example, one vegetable soul may enter paradise while an animal soul becomes a dangerous ghost. These systems classify traits across plants, animals, and humans.

## The Alter Ego, or Life Index

An alter ego or life index links a person's life to an animal, plant, or object. In Central American nagualism, a person's nagual suffers or dies when the person does, and vice versa. Some societies ritually identify a child with a tree or placenta burial, while others seek the life-linked being through visions. These beliefs make identity relational rather than isolated.

## Relationships of Similarity

Similarity myths connect humans, animals, and plants through shared attributes. A gentle person may be linked to a butterfly, a violent person to a jaguar, or a social group to an animal category. Such analogies help cultures classify character, behaviour, status, and moral qualities through the visible natural world.

## Key Events & Developments

- 1760s: James Macpherson's Ossian poems influenced Romantic views of myth.
- 1773: Herder published work linking ancient song, Volk culture, and myth.
- 1877: Mannhardt published Ancient Wood and Field Cults.
- 1899: [[Freud]] published The Interpretation of Dreams.
- 1955: Stith Thompson's motif index became a major folklore classification tool.
- 1962: Levi-Strauss reinterpreted totemism as classification.

## Interesting Facts

- Etiologic myths explain why a custom, feature, or divine attribute exists.
- Oedipus complex comes from Freud's interpretation of myth and dreams.
- Nagualism links a person's life to an animal or object alter ego.
