# The Concept And Components Of Culture (Cultural Anthropology)

> [!abstract] Reference navigation

## Introduction

![[attachments/cultural_anthropology_001.webp|420]]

*Portrait of Franz Boas*

Cultural anthropology ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cultural_anthropology)) is a major branch of anthropology that studies culture in all its aspects. It uses archaeology, ethnography, ethnology, folklore, and linguistics to describe and analyze the world's diverse peoples.

Anthropology means the science of humans, but it is one among several human sciences. It brings together disciplines that describe human beings and explain them through biological and cultural characteristics across different populations and historical periods.

Two central concepts are race and culture. Their meanings remain debated, but both became basic terms in anthropological vocabulary because anthropology began as the study of human differences.

## Scope and Origins

Cultural anthropology emerged after the Age of Discovery ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Age_of_Discovery)) brought Europeans into contact with societies outside modern Western technological civilization. Early anthropology focused on peoples labelled savage, primitive, tribal, traditional, preliterate, or prehistorical.

These labels were unsatisfactory, but they show that early anthropology studied peoples seen as most different from European and North American researchers. The distance between researcher and subject became a key feature of anthropological work.

Modern anthropologists study more than so-called primitive societies. They examine villages, cities, industrial societies, modern communities, social change, institutions, and cultural processes in many settings.

## Evolutionism

Until near the end of the 19th century, evolutionism shaped cultural anthropology. Anthropologists tried to classify societies and define universal stages through which all human groups supposedly passed.

This model imagined progress from simple to complex, homogeneous to heterogeneous, irrational to rational, savagery to barbarism to civilization. Lewis Henry Morgan expressed this view in Ancient Society in 1877.

Other evolutionists, including John F. MacLennan and Edward B. Tylor, shared similar assumptions. Cultural anthropology sought to analyze all human culture in time and space but often forced diverse histories into one linear sequence.

Evolutionism neglected discontinuity, borrowing, contact, and local meaning. Morgan and Tylor sometimes introduced diffusion, or the spread of cultural traits, which suggested that groups could borrow knowledge and skip supposed evolutionary stages.

## Marxism and Collectors

In the second half of the 19th century, Karl Marx and Friedrich Engels developed another kind of evolutionism. Their approach stressed causes of human development rather than only stages.

Marxist theory defined society by mode of production, with political, legal, and ideological superstructures based on it. Older superstructures could persist after production changed, creating historical tension.

At the same time, collectors, missionaries, administrators, and scholars gathered ethnographic materials, artifacts, life histories, and observations that later anthropologists used to build more grounded studies.

## Boas and Culture History

Franz Boas strongly redirected American cultural anthropology. He urged students such as Ruth Benedict, Alfred L. Kroeber, Margaret Mead, and Edward Sapir to study people in natural settings, gather facts and artifacts, and record observable cultural processes.

Boas became known as the founder of the American culture history school. It emphasized fieldwork, first-hand observation, cultural particularity, historical development, and resistance to sweeping evolutionary schemes.

Boas also supported a functional view in which each culture should be considered as a whole. Culture traits, patterns, and complexes intermesh like parts of a machine or organism.

By collecting life histories, Boas drew attention to the relation between culture and personality. His work encouraged anthropologists to study both shared cultural forms and individual experience.

## Mauss and Structural Approaches

![[attachments/cultural_anthropology_003.webp|420]]

*Portrait of Marcel Mauss*

Marcel Mauss influenced European sociology and anthropology, including Alfred Metraux, Claude Levi-Strauss, Bronislaw Malinowski, and A.R. Radcliffe-Brown. He helped found the Institute of Ethnology at the University of Paris.

Like Boas, Mauss studied social phenomena as systems, but he emphasized self-regulating wholes that maintain integration or adaptation. This helped shape structural and sociological approaches.

Structuralism focused on society as an indivisible social organism, more than on individual interrelations alone. Mauss also tried to connect cultural anthropology with psychology by linking culture and personality.

## Diffusionism

The American culture history school should not be confused with Austro-German diffusionism, led by Fritz Graebner and Wilhelm Schmidt. These scholars rejected classical evolutionism but still built broad theories of cultural origins.

The Kulturkreise school argued that a few ancient cultural centres or clusters gave rise to many later cultures by spreading traits. Diffusion, rather than independent evolution, was treated as the main force of human development.

British diffusionists such as Grafton Elliot Smith and William J. Perry carried this further by naming Egypt as a single source of all cultural development. This kind of grand diffusionism is now seen as pseudo-history.

## Functionalism and Structuralism

![[attachments/cultural_anthropology_002.webp|420]]

*Bronislaw Malinowski with Trobriand Islanders*

Between the world wars, some schools rejected historical reconstruction and focused on how societies worked in the present. Functionalism studied how institutions, customs, and beliefs contributed to the maintenance of social life.

Bronislaw Malinowski and A.R. Radcliffe-Brown became major figures in British social anthropology. Their approaches differed, but both emphasized fieldwork, social structure, institutions, and the functioning of cultural systems.

Structuralist approaches later associated with Claude Levi-Strauss examined deep patterns in myth, kinship, classification, and symbolic systems. They asked how human minds organize cultural meaning.

## Applied Studies

Applied anthropology uses research to help governments or organizations deal with practical problems. From an anthropologist's view, such work often helped because it examined social change in conditions close to a controlled experiment.

Applied studies deepened knowledge of economic organization, political organization, land tenure, law, and cultural change. They also gave some anthropologists the human satisfaction of aiding peoples facing Western colonial or modernizing pressures.

Yet applied work had problems. Colonial officials were not always required to follow anthropological findings, and they sometimes doubted anthropology's usefulness. Anthropologists could also be frustrated when employers wanted quick answers to problems that required broad cultural study.

## Key Events & Developments

- 1859: Marx's Critique of Political Economy helped frame historical materialist evolution.
- 1877: Morgan's Ancient Society expressed a classic evolutionary scheme.
- 1881: Tylor's Anthropology presented a major early study of humans and civilization.
- Early 20th century: Boas promoted fieldwork, culture history, and cultural particularism.
- Between world wars: Functionalism and structuralism gained influence.
