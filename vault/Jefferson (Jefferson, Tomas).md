# Jefferson (Jefferson, Tomas)

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> Thomas Jefferson was an American revolutionary, author of the Declaration of Independence, diplomat, and third president whose career linked natural rights, republican government, westward expansion, and the contradiction of slavery. His political thought and administration shaped the early United States.

## Introduction

![[attachments/jefferson_001.webp|420]]

*Portrait of Thomas Jefferson*

Thomas Jefferson, born in 1743 at Shadwell, Virginia, and dead on July 4, 1826, was the principal draftsman of the Declaration of Independence ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Declaration_of_Independence)), the first U.S. secretary of state, second vice president, and third president. He also directed the Louisiana Purchase ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Louisiana_Purchase)), supported separation of church and state, founded and designed the University of Virginia, and became the most eloquent American spokesman for individual liberty as the central meaning of the Revolution.

Jefferson's legacy is deeply paradoxical. Popular memory treats him as an icon of liberty for Americans and democratic movements abroad, but scholarship has emphasized the gap between his language of equality and his dependence on slavery. His conviction that American society should remain white made him one of the most difficult and controversial heroes in U.S. history.

## Early Years

![[attachments/jefferson_003.webp|420]]

*Monticello*

Jefferson grew up in Albemarle county, near the Blue Ridge Mountains, in a family tied to both frontier ambition and Virginia gentility. His father, Peter Jefferson, was a self-educated surveyor with an estate and 60 slaves, while his mother, Jane Randolph Jefferson, came from a leading Virginia family. After his father's death in 1757, Jefferson seemed eager to escape maternal supervision.

[[Education]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Education)) shaped Jefferson's lifelong confidence in books, mentors, and disciplined study. He entered the College of William and Mary in 1760, studied intensely, practiced violin, and learned from William Small and George Wythe. He read law with Wythe from 1762 to 1767, then built a reputation as a learned but shy lawyer handling western land claims.

Monticello and public life began together. In 1768 Jefferson decided to build his mountaintop house and enter the House of Burgesses. The two choices reflected his lifelong tension between political action and private retreat. In 1772 he married Martha Wayles Skelton, whose dowry greatly expanded his land and slave holdings.

## Declaring Independence

![[attachments/jefferson_002.webp|420]]

*Declaration of Independence painting*

A Summary View of the Rights of British America, written in 1774, made Jefferson visible beyond Virginia by arguing that the colonies were bound to Britain only by voluntary loyalty to the king. In 1775 he entered the Second Continental Congress as a tall, shy, serious advocate of independence, already carrying the contradiction of a slaveholding champion of liberty.

[[John Adams]] asked Jefferson to draft the Declaration after Congress appointed a five-person committee that included Benjamin Franklin. Jefferson later said he aimed to express the American mind rather than create new principles. Congress revised and deleted about one-fifth of the draft, but left untouched Jefferson's statement on equality, rights, consent, life, liberty, and the pursuit of happiness.

The Declaration was seen at first as a collective act of Congress, and Jefferson was not widely known as its principal author until the 1790s. After returning to Virginia in 1776, he worked on legal reforms: abolishing primogeniture and entail, proposing broad educational reform, and supporting complete separation of church and state. The religious freedom statute was not enacted until 1786.

Public disaster and private grief ended this creative phase. As governor of Virginia in 1779, Jefferson was caught unprepared by British invasion in 1780, and his flight was criticized as cowardice. In 1782, Martha died after a difficult childbirth. Jefferson vowed not to leave his family again for public service, but soon broke the vow.

## American in Paris

Jefferson served in the Continental Congress in 1782 and argued that western territories should enter the Union as equal states, not colonies. In 1784 he went to France as minister, succeeding Franklin. His diplomacy achieved little because the United States was indebted and weak, Britain dominated American trade, and France was moving toward crisis.

The Paris years reveal Jefferson's contradictions. He loved French art, architecture, wine, and food while warning Americans against European luxury. He was devoted to his daughters, Martha and Maria, but placed them in a convent and wrote sternly about female conduct. He became infatuated with Maria Cosway, and evidence also points to a sexual relationship with the enslaved Sally Hemings beginning around 1788.

The French Revolution initially seemed to Jefferson a European version of the American constitutional movement. Influenced by Marquis de Lafayette and other moderates, he expected a peaceful constitutional monarchy and failed to grasp the deep social resentments that would lead to the Reign of Terror. He left France late in 1789 just as mob violence began.

## Slavery and Racism

Notes on the State of Virginia, Jefferson's only published book, mixed travel description, science, and political reflection. It denounced slavery as a violation of Revolutionary principles and warned of violent consequences if it continued, but it also stated Jefferson's belief in biological differences between Black and white people. This made his antislavery language inseparable from deep racism.

Sally Hemings became central to later judgment of Jefferson. A public claim in 1802 accused him of fathering her children; a Hemings descendant supported the claim in 1873; and DNA evidence in 1998 showed that Jefferson was almost certainly the father of some of Hemings's children. The nature of the relationship remains morally contested because Jefferson owned Hemings.

Jefferson's early antislavery record was relatively progressive for a Virginia planter. He supported ending the slave trade, included an antislavery passage in his draft of the Declaration, proposed gradual emancipation for people born after 1800, and wanted slavery barred from western territories. Yet after 1789 he retreated from leadership, partly because emancipation combined with his racist assumption of separation seemed impossible.

Plantation life exposed the practical contradiction. Jefferson owned about 200 enslaved people at a time and more than 600 over his life. Monticello, his comforts, and his renovations depended on slave labour. He freed only five enslaved people, all from the Hemings family, and his debts eventually mortgaged enslaved people along with the rest of his property.

## Party Politics

[[George Washington]] appointed Jefferson the first secretary of state in 1789. The new Constitution was still untested, and Jefferson had missed the main debates while in France. He worried less about defining federal power than about protecting areas where government could not intrude, especially through a bill of rights.

Foreign policy divided the cabinet. Washington and [[John Adams]] wanted strict neutrality between Britain and France, Alexander Hamilton favoured commercial ties with Britain, and Jefferson favoured France because of the 1778 treaty and because he saw the French Revolution as the spirit of 1776 in Europe. He kept this pro-French view even as the Revolution turned violent.

Domestic politics produced the first American party opposition. Jefferson saw Hamilton's debt funding, state-debt assumption, and national bank as a Federalist plot to restore monarchy, aristocracy, and central power. With James Madison, he helped form the Republican opposition, even while claiming parties were dangerous factions.

The election of 1800 was bitter and personal. Federalists portrayed Jefferson as atheist, anarchic, and treasonous; Jefferson worked through intermediaries and paid journalists to attack Adams. Jefferson and Aaron Burr defeated Adams, but a constitutional flaw produced an electoral tie. The House of Representatives chose Jefferson on the 36th ballot.

## Presidency

Jefferson's presidency began amid fears that his hostility to Federalist institutions would endanger the Union. His first inaugural address in 1801 was conciliatory, claiming that Americans were all republicans and all federalists. He imagined the republic as strongest when government was limited and voluntary public opinion could release individual energies.

Early policy matched his decentralizing ideals. Jefferson reduced the army and navy, dismantled federal taxes, and tried to retire the national debt of $112 million. Prosperity helped because war between Britain and France temporarily paused, and Albert Gallatin managed Treasury policy effectively. Jefferson conducted government mainly through writing rather than speeches or cabinet debate.

The Louisiana Purchase of 1803 was Jefferson's greatest achievement and his boldest contradiction. [[Napoleon]] sold the vast territory from the Mississippi Valley toward the Rocky Mountains for $15 million. The purchase expanded debt and stretched Jefferson's constitutional principles, but he accepted it because it removed a European threat and opened a future for agrarian republicanism. He sent Meriwether Lewis to explore the new region and lands beyond.

Second-term problems exposed Jefferson's limits. He pursued state indictments against hostile Federalist newspapers despite his praise of free expression. He pressed for Burr's conviction for treason even though evidence was lacking, but John Marshall blocked him. The 1807 Embargo Act tried to force Britain and France to respect American neutrality by closing U.S. trade, but it damaged the American economy and required coercive federal enforcement. Jefferson left office in 1809 tired and defeated.

## Retirement

Retirement at Monticello remained busy rather than quiet. Jefferson wrote enormous correspondence, gardened, rode daily, hosted constant visitors, and sometimes fled to Poplar Forest for privacy. Monticello remained unfinished, while Poplar Forest and the University of Virginia became major architectural projects.

The University of Virginia embodied Jefferson's principles. He designed the academical village at Charlottesville, modelled the rotunda on the Pantheon, chose books, curriculum, faculty, and governance, and chaired the Board of Visitors. The university had no religious affiliation, no religious tests, no fixed curriculum requirements, and no president, reflecting his ideal of self-government.

[[John Adams]] and Jefferson resumed correspondence in 1812 through Benjamin Rush's mediation. Their 158 letters made them the North and South poles of the Revolution: Jefferson represented a clean break from European authority, while Adams valued continuity with representative traditions and the constitutional settlement. Their exchange clarified the Revolution's unresolved tensions.

Late slavery views marked a severe retreat from Jefferson's earlier proposals. During the Missouri crisis in 1819, he supported allowing slavery into western territories and condemned federal emancipation efforts as despotic. He still called slavery an anomaly, but he increasingly treated national antislavery action as a threat to Virginia and the Union.

Debt darkened Jefferson's final years. His inherited obligations, lavish life, renovations, and interest costs overwhelmed him. By the 1820s he owed more than $100,000. After his death, Monticello, furnishings, land, and most enslaved people were auctioned, and his daughter Martha needed charity.

Jefferson's final public words came for the 50th anniversary of independence in 1826. Too ill to attend, he wrote that the Declaration should awaken humanity to rights, science, and self-government. He died at Monticello around midday on July 4, 1826. Adams died the same day, wrongly saying, "Thomas Jefferson still lives," a phrase that proved true for Jefferson's continuing influence.

## Cabinet of President Thomas Jefferson

Jefferson's cabinet centred on figures who helped translate Republican principles into administration. Albert Gallatin at Treasury and James Madison at State dominated policy discussions, while Jefferson preferred written recommendations, revisions, and correspondence to oral debate. His cabinet style reflected both his literary gifts and his wish to make the presidency less visible.

- 1784-1789 Paris mission: Jefferson served as American minister to France.
- 1786 Religious freedom: His Virginia statute for church-state separation was enacted.
- 1789-1793 Secretary of state: Jefferson joined Washington's cabinet.
- 1800 Election crisis: Jefferson won after a House vote on the 36th ballot.
- 1803 Louisiana Purchase ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Louisiana_Purchase)): The United States bought a vast western territory from France.
- 1807 Embargo Act: Jefferson's neutrality policy damaged American trade.
- 1812-1826 Adams correspondence: Jefferson and Adams renewed their intellectual friendship.
- 1819 Missouri crisis: Jefferson retreated from earlier opposition to slavery in western territories.
- 1826 [[Death]]: Jefferson and Adams both died on July 4.

## Interesting Facts

- Jefferson stood slightly above 6 feet 2 inches and was known as a poor public speaker.
- Monticello was built on an 867-foot mountain near Shadwell.
- The Declaration's most famous 55 words were untouched by Congress during revision.
- Jefferson wrote annual presidential messages rather than delivering them in person.
- The Louisiana Purchase doubled the national domain while violating Jefferson's strict constitutional scruples.
- Monticello sometimes housed as many as 50 visitors during retirement.
- The University of Virginia had no religious affiliation and no religious requirement.
- Jefferson's debt led to the auction of Monticello and most enslaved people after his death.
