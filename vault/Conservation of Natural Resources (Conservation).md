# Conservation of Natural Resources (Conservation)

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> Conservation seeks the wise protection and sustained use of natural resources, habitats, and ecosystems. It brings ecological knowledge into decisions about land, water, forests, wildlife, and economic development.

## Introduction

Conservation ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conservation_biology)) is the study of the loss of Earth's biological diversity and the ways this loss can be prevented. Biodiversity is the variety of life in a place or on the whole planet, including its ecosystems, species, populations, and genes, so conservation seeks to protect life at every level of organization. The Millennium Ecosystem Assessment, an international effort coordinated by the United Nations Environment Programme in the early 21st century, used species as the bulk of its examples of planetary decline.

Conservation ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conservation_biology)) is called a crisis discipline because it is demanded by unusually high rates of loss, and a mission-driven one because its goal is to prevent and reverse that loss. Ecology and conservation can be compared to physiology and medicine: ecology studies how nature works, while conservation, like medicine, asks what goes wrong and how to treat it. Because the underlying cause of biodiversity loss is increasing human activity ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Human_behavior)), conservation must always involve human interactions as well as biology.

## How many species exist

Counting species is the starting point for measuring loss, yet the totals are very uncertain. About 1.5 million species have been described, but scientists estimate the true total is very roughly 10 million, with arguments ranging between 5 and 20 million. In Great Britain there are six times as many named species of fungi as flowering plants; applied worldwide to about 300,000 flowering-plant species, this predicts roughly 1.8 million fungi, though only about 70,000 have been named. One entomologist found 163 beetle species specific to a single tropical tree, and multiplying by the world's many tree species suggested tens of millions of tropical-forest insects.

## Main concerns

Extinction is the most obvious loss, but a surviving species can still lose most of its genetic diversity as local populations disappear, and ecosystems can shrink and lose their functions even when their species survive. The main concerns are extinction rates, habitat destruction, invasive species, overharvesting, pollution, fire-regime change, and the special vulnerability of small, rare, or highly localized populations.

Small populations are especially at risk because they can lose genetic variety and become easier to wipe out. Extinction is therefore a process that often begins long before the last individual disappears.

Habitat loss is the most important driver because it removes the places where species live, breed, migrate, and find food. In the fynbos scrubland at the southern tip of South Africa, 36 of about 8,500 plant species became extinct in the past century and another 618 are threatened, partly from invading Australian wattle (acacia) trees. In the wheat belt of Western Australia, only about 5 percent of the natural woodland still survives.

Invasive species are another recurring concern, especially on islands where native species evolved without predators or competitors. The American chestnut (Castanea dentata) was stripped from eastern North America after the accidental arrival of the chestnut-blight fungus Endothia parasitica in the early 20th century. European rabbits and the red fox, both introduced to [[Australia]] in the mid-19th century, helped wipe out native small mammals where foxes were not controlled.

Overharvesting, or overfishing for marine life, drives species below the level at which use is sustainable and can push them to extinction. Whales, fish, and trees and plants valued for wood or medicine are all affected. The baiji (Lipotes vexillifer), a river dolphin of the Yangtze River, was declared effectively lost after an exhaustive survey found none remaining.

## Methods and priorities

![[attachments/conservation_of_natural_resources_conservation_003.webp|420]]

*Conservation reliant species08*

Conservation ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conservation_biology)) is both analytical and practical: scientists estimate risk, identify biodiversity hotspots, protect habitats, restore damaged ecosystems, remove invasive species, and manage small populations to reduce extinction risk. Conservation is also inseparable from human politics and economics.

The methods reflect a balance between science and action. The work moves from diagnosis to intervention, showing that conservation must respond to both ecological evidence and human pressure.

Hotspot analysis is one way to set priorities. In the 1990s a team led by the British scientist Norman Myers identified 25 terrestrial hot spots where species with small ranges coincide with intense human activity. These covered about 17 million of Earth's roughly 130 million square km of ice-free land, yet held more than 133,000 of about 300,000 flowering-plant species. Only about one-eighth of their original habitat survived to the 21st century, and just two-fifths of that was protected, which is why most threatened and recently extinct species are concentrated there.

Protected areas are important but not enough by themselves. Parks and reserves must be large enough, connected enough, and managed well enough to protect species from poaching, fire change, invasive species, pollution, and climate stress.

Ex situ conservation includes seed banks, botanical gardens, zoos, captive breeding, and genetic management, used as supplements to habitat protection rather than substitutes for living ecosystems. The California condor (Gymnogyps californianus) fell from about 150 birds in the 1950s to 60 by 1970, largely from eating poisoned carcasses and lead-laced carrion; all survivors were taken into captivity in the 1980s, and after 1991 the program produced 12 to 20 fledglings a year.

Restoration ecology repairs damaged habitats by replanting native species, removing invasives, reintroducing missing species, restoring fire or water regimes, and reconnecting fragmented landscapes. Removing invaders is often slow and costly: cats on Marion Island, descended from five animals brought in 1949, were killing about 450,000 seabirds a year and took 19 years to eradicate. Protecting white rhinos (Ceratotherium simum), which numbered under 12,000 in 2001, from poaching for their horns can cost about $1,400 per square km each year.

## Whaling and overfishing

Whaling shows how poorly biodiversity has been protected even when it has clear economic value. Early whalers took slow "right whales" near shore; by 1800 they had nearly exterminated the North Atlantic northern right whale and the bowhead, and they wiped out the Atlantic gray whale. After peace in 1815, whalers poured into the Pacific; the first reached the Hawaiian Islands in 1820, and by 1846 the fleet had grown to nearly 600 ships, mostly from New England. Exploding harpoons and steamships in the late 1800s let whalers take fast species such as the blue whale and fin whale, and nearly 30,000 blue whales were killed in 1931 alone.

Overfishing is now the greatest threat to ocean biodiversity. In the United States the National Marine Fisheries Service assesses stocks within the exclusive economic zone, which extends 370 km (200 nautical miles) from shore. At the turn of the 21st century about 100 fish stocks were judged overfished, and for the major Atlantic, Pacific, and Gulf of Mexico fisheries two-thirds of stocks were overfished. The barn-door skate (Raja laevis), a large North Atlantic fish, declined every year until almost none were caught by the 1990s.

## Conclusion

Conservation ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conservation_biology)) is an urgent effort to preserve the living fabric of the planet, especially in regions where human activity and endemic biodiversity overlap.

Conservation ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conservation_biology)) is most urgent where the richest life and the strongest human pressures meet. That is why hotspots, habitat protection, and long-term management matter so much.

A larger message is that conservation is both scientific and moral. It measures extinction risk, population size, genetic variation, and habitat loss, but it also asks what obligations people have to other species and to future generations.

Extinction rates are measured against background rates from the fossil record. Modern losses are alarming because they occur much faster than ordinary evolutionary turnover and because many threatened species have small ranges or specialized habitats.

Endemism is central to conservation priority. Species found only in one island, mountain range, forest, lake, or river system can vanish globally if that local habitat is destroyed. Islands are especially vulnerable because their species often evolved without predators, competitors, or human disturbance.

Minimum viable populations are another concern. Conservationists must estimate how many individuals are needed for a population to survive demographic accidents, environmental variation, genetic drift, and inbreeding. A species can be functionally doomed even when some individuals remain alive.

Corridors and landscape planning help solve the problem of fragmentation. By linking reserves, protecting migration routes, and preserving surrounding habitat, conservation can maintain gene flow and allow species to shift ranges as conditions change.

Marine conservation is treated as a special challenge because oceans are shared, mobile, and difficult to police. Overfishing, bycatch, whaling, coral reef damage, pollution, and habitat destruction require international rules as well as local management.

The social side of conservation is equally important. Protected areas may conflict with farming, logging, mining, fishing, settlement, or Indigenous land use. Successful conservation therefore depends on law, incentives, local participation, scientific monitoring, and long-term political commitment.

The strongest practical lesson is that saving biodiversity requires acting before crisis becomes irreversible. Once habitat is gone, populations are isolated, genes are lost, and invasive species are established, recovery becomes slower, more expensive, and less certain.

Biodiversity ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biodiversity)) is used here in a broad sense. It includes the number of species, the genetic diversity within species, the variety of ecosystems, and the evolutionary processes that keep life changing. Conservation therefore cannot be reduced to saving attractive animals alone.

Genetic diversity matters because it gives populations the capacity to adapt to disease, climate change, environmental fluctuation, and new competitors. Inbreeding can reduce fertility and survival, making small populations increasingly vulnerable even before numbers become extremely low.
Species richness is unevenly distributed. Tropical forests, coral reefs, Mediterranean-type ecosystems, islands, mountains, and wetlands often contain many endemic species, but they are also exposed to agriculture, urban growth, logging, mining, roads, and climate change.

Conservation biology emerged because ordinary resource management was not enough to address extinction. It combines ecology, genetics, biogeography, demography, economics, law, and ethics. Its practical purpose is to turn scientific knowledge into timely decisions.

Conservation involves triage. Limited money, time, and political will force choices about which habitats, species, and threats receive immediate attention. Hotspots, umbrella species, keystone species, and threatened ecosystems are tools for making those choices explicit.

Conservation ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conservation_biology)) differs from simple preservation. Some ecosystems need active management: controlled burning, grazing control, invasive removal, reintroduction, water restoration, anti-poaching enforcement, or community-based resource agreements. Doing nothing can allow earlier human damage to continue.

Climate change adds another layer because protected boundaries may no longer match future habitats. Species may need connected landscapes, assisted recovery, or adaptive management as temperature, rainfall, fire, storms, and sea level change.

In sum, conservation is an emergency science of living diversity. It asks how much of Earth's biological inheritance can still be kept intact, and what practical choices must be made before extinction closes those choices permanently.

Biodiversity ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biodiversity)) is therefore tied to resilience. Diverse ecosystems can recover, adapt, and continue providing food, water regulation, pollination, soil formation, climate moderation, and cultural value. Losing diversity weakens both nature and human societies.

For that reason, conservation depends on early warning, monitoring, public support, and institutions that can protect habitats across generations. Short-term exploitation can erase options that no later policy can fully restore.

A final emphasis is practical urgency: extinction prevention works best while populations, habitats, and genetic variety still remain recoverable.

Delay narrows choices and raises ecological cost for species, ecosystems, and people.

## Core Equations

Applied biological and environmental systems use yield, growth, and resource-balance relationships:

$$Y=\frac{\text{harvested output}}{\text{area}}$$

This ratio turns the preceding idea into a measurable comparison, with the numerator as the quantity of interest and the denominator as the scale used for comparison.

$$\frac{dN}{dt}=rN\left(1-\frac{N}{K}\right)$$

The logistic-growth equation models population growth that slows as population size N approaches carrying capacity K.

$$\text{stock}_{t+1}=\text{stock}_t+\text{growth}-\text{harvest}$$

This gives a compact mathematical form for applied biological and environmental systems use yield, growth, and resource-balance relationships.

## Key Events & Developments

- 1758: Carolus Linnaeus begins the modern naming of bird species; two he described, including the dodo, were already extinct.
- 1800s: about 7,079 bird species are named, roughly 70 percent of the modern total, and 39 become extinct over the next century.
- 1949: cats are introduced to Marion Island and the ruddy duck is later introduced to Britain, both spreading to threaten native species.
- 1990s: Norman Myers and colleagues identify 25 terrestrial biodiversity hot spots.
- Early 21st century: the Millennium Ecosystem Assessment documents widespread biodiversity loss.

## Interesting Facts

- Coral reefs hold almost 100,000 species yet cover only about 0.2 percent of the ocean surface.
- The molecular clock suggests bonobos and chimpanzees split about 1 million years ago, and humans split from their line about 6 million years ago.
- Bird extinctions in the 1800s ran at about 55 per million species per year, far above the natural background rate.
- The seas cover more than two-thirds of Earth, yet only about 210,000 of 1.5 million described species are marine.
- A small population reduced to a single individual, like a rumored Loch Ness monster, has no chance of survival because of the basics of sexual reproduction.
