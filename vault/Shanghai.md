# Shanghai

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> Shanghai is China's largest urban area and a major port, commercial centre, and industrial metropolis at the Yangtze delta. Its low-lying water-linked site, treaty-port past, foreign concessions, socialist industrialization, Pudong redevelopment, migration, housing, and pollution reveal the interaction of Chinese modernization and global trade.

## Introduction

![[attachments/shanghai_001.webp|420]]

*Pudong skyline viewed from the Huangpu River*

Shanghai is a province-level municipality in east-central China and one of the world's largest seaports. It lies on the coast of the East China Sea between the mouth of the Yangtze River to the north and Hangzhou Bay to the south. The municipality includes the central city, suburbs, islands, and an agricultural hinterland, making it China's most populous urban area.

The city was one of the first Chinese ports opened to Western trade and long dominated national commerce. After 1949, it became a major industrial centre serving China's domestic needs. Its modern development has included industrial suburbs, housing complexes, public works, parks, and efforts to overcome the economic and psychological legacy of foreign domination.

## Site

Shanghai municipality borders Jiangsu to the north and west and Zhejiang to the southwest. It includes the city districts and islands near the Yangtze mouth. Chongming, the largest island, covers 489 square miles and extends more than 50 miles upstream from the river mouth.

The mainland city stands on a flat deltaic plain only about 10 to 16 feet above sea level. Canals and waterways connect it with the Lake Tai region to the west. This watery setting made Shanghai useful for shipping, but it also required constant attention to drainage, flood control, and river management.

## Climate

Shanghai has a mild maritime climate with limited seasonal contrast. The average annual temperature is about 61 F, with July averaging about 80 F and January about 37 F. Annual rainfall is about 45 inches, heaviest in June and lightest in December.

## Layout

As China's main industrial centre, Shanghai has faced serious air, water, and noise pollution. Since the 1950s, industry moved partly into suburbs, but dense population and mixed industrial-residential land use continued to cause problems. The Suzhou and Huangpu rivers have been polluted by industrial discharge, sewage, and ship waste, even though the Huangpu remains the main water source.

The city radiates from the meeting of the Suzhou and Huangpu rivers. Central Shanghai grew around concession-era districts, commercial streets, and older Chinese urban areas. Pudong, east of the Huangpu, was once an industrial slum area, but by the late 20th century it became a major finance and trade zone, with the Pudong New District founded in 1993 and the Jin Mao Tower completed in 1999.

## Downtown Shanghai

![[attachments/shanghai_002.webp|420]]

*Huxinting Teahouse at Yu Garden in Shanghai*

Before the late 1980s, downtown Shanghai still looked much like the precommunist city because suburban industrial-residential development had received priority. Many pre-World War II buildings from foreign commercial and diplomatic activity remained important landmarks.

The central district includes the former British concession, Nanjing Dong Lu as a major commercial street, and Hongkou north and east of the Suzhou River. The old Chinese city has a maze-like street plan and was once surrounded by a wall. These different districts show how Chinese, foreign, commercial, and industrial histories overlapped in the urban core.

## Housing

Since 1949, Shanghai has worked to house a growing population and reduce central overcrowding. Self-sufficient residential complexes were built with industry, agriculture, and commerce nearby. Caoyang New Village, begun in 1951, introduced state-supported housing, and later projects included Railroad Village, Post and Telegraph Village, Yangshupu, Pengpu, Zhenru, Yichuan, Rihui, and Jiangwan.

These complexes changed both city and suburb. The typical workers' housing project combined apartments with offices, workshops, clinics, nurseries, and nearby fields supplying food crops. From the 1980s, prolonged construction aimed to replace shanties, though some remained.

## Industry

Shanghai became China's leading industrial and manufacturing centre because of skilled labour, scientific research, cooperation among producers, and strong transport and supply links. Its iron and steel industry was early and important, and new Baoshan facilities from the late 1970s included Baosteel, one of the world's largest enterprises by the early 21st century.

The city also developed major machine-tool, chemical, petrochemical, textile, and light industrial sectors. Heavy industry dominated until the late 1970s, but lighter industry gained favour to reduce pollution, ease transport congestion, and respond to energy and raw-material limits.

## Commerce

Shanghai's commerce moved from bureau-managed retail trade toward more market-directed organization after reforms and the disbanding of the First Commercial Bureau in 1995. Large stores, smaller retail shops, wholesaling groups, warehousing, and distribution were reorganized into more independent business structures.

Finance and trade also remained central. Major Chinese banks managed state investment funds, while foreign and overseas Chinese banks supported foreign trade, currency exchange, and remittances. Shanghai exports manufactured goods across China and abroad, while importing food grains, fuel, construction materials, and industrial raw materials.

## Transportation

Shanghai is both a seaport and river port. The Huangpu River allows oceangoing vessels to reach the city at high tide, and specialized harbour sections developed after the early 1950s. Later terminals at Baoshan, Luojing, Waigaoqiao, and a deepwater port off Hangzhou Bay increased capacity.

The city also has dense inland waterway, rail, road, and air connections. Rail lines link Shanghai with Nanjing, Hangzhou, and Ningbo, with spurs serving industrial districts. Hongqiao Airport became mainly domestic, while Pudong International Airport, opened in 1999, grew into one of China's major international air hubs.

## Government

As a province-level municipality, Shanghai is theoretically controlled directly by the central government in Beijing. During the Cultural Revolution, the Shanghai Municipal Revolutionary Committee was created in 1967 after chaotic revolutionary seizures of power. By the mid-1970s, it was replaced by a municipal government responsible to the Shanghai People's Congress.

Public utilities expanded through drainage, sewage treatment, water supply, street lighting, refuse systems, road improvement, flood walls, and housing. Sea walls around the city were strengthened, and Shanghai became a major electric power-generating centre linked to regional transmission networks.

## Health

Shanghai's health system ranges from small clinics attached to factories, schools, stores, and offices to major teaching and research hospitals. Many hospitals teach and practice both traditional Chinese and Western medicine. During the Cultural Revolution, medical schools emphasized barefoot doctors for basic rural care.

## Education

Shanghai is China's leading centre of higher education and scientific research. Its institutions include Fudan, Jiaotong, Tongji, Huadong Shifan Daxue, technical institutes, and the Shanghai Branch of the Chinese Academy of Sciences. Since the late 1980s, market-oriented higher education became more typical than the older factory-linked work-study colleges.

## Cultural Life

Shanghai has museums, gardens, theatres, publishing houses, libraries, music schools, art schools, and professional performance groups. The Shanghai Museum holds bronzes, ceramics, and other ancient artifacts. Dashijie, founded in the 1920s, became a leading theatrical centre with opera, dance, drama, story readings, and minority-group entertainment forms.

Important cultural sites include the Yuyuan Garden ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yu_Garden)), the Temple of Confucius, Longhua Pagoda, the Industrial Exhibition Hall, and sites linked to Lu Xun. Parks and open spaces expanded after 1949, including People's Park, Huangpu Park, Hongkou Arboretum, Peace Park, Pudong Park, Longhua Park, Fuxing Park, Zhongshan Park, and Guangqi Park.

## History

![[attachments/shanghai_003.webp|420]]

*Map of Shanghai in 1871*

The Shanghai area was sparsely populated in the 5th to 7th centuries CE. During the Song dynasty, it grew from a fishing village into a port and shipping centre. A customs office existed by the 11th century, and by the end of the 13th century Shanghai was a county seat under Jiangsu.

Under the Ming dynasty, cotton became central to the surrounding economy, and by the mid-18th century more than 20,000 people worked as cotton spinners. After China's defeat by Britain in 1842 and the Treaty of Nanjing, Shanghai opened to foreign trade. British, French, American, and later Japanese concessions made it a base for commercial imperialism and transformed its agricultural economy into a trade-centred urban economy.

## Key Events & Developments

- 11th century: A customs office was established as Shanghai's port role grew.
- 13th century: Shanghai became a county seat under Jiangsu province.
- 1842: The Treaty of Nanjing opened Shanghai to unrestricted foreign trade.
- 1949: Communist victory shifted Shanghai toward domestic industrial production and urban transformation.
- 1993: Pudong New District was established.
- 1999: The Jin Mao Tower was completed and Pudong International Airport entered service.
- 2005: A deepwater port operation began off Hangzhou Bay.

## Key People

- Chen Yun ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chen_Yun)): Revolutionary leader whose former residence was combined with a history memorial hall to form a museum in 2000.
- Lu Xun ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lu_Xun)): Revolutionary writer connected with sites in Shanghai, including his tomb and former residence.
- Xu Guangqi ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Xu_Guangqi)): Ming-dynasty statesman buried in Guangqi Park.
