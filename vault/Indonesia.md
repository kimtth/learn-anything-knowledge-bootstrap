# Indonesia

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> Indonesia is a vast equatorial archipelago between the Indian and Pacific oceans, extending across major island groups from Sumatra and Java to Sulawesi, the Moluccas, and western New Guinea. Its geography, colonial history, Japanese occupation, and independence shaped a populous Southeast Asian state centered on Jakarta.

## Introduction

Indonesia ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indonesia)) is an equatorial archipelago between the Indian and Pacific oceans, stretching a distance equal to about one-eighth of Earth's circumference. Its main island groups include Sumatra, Java, southern Borneo or Kalimantan, Sulawesi, Bali and the Lesser Sundas, the Moluccas, and western New Guinea or Papua. [[Jakarta]], on Java, is the capital, and Indonesia was the most populous country in Southeast Asia and the fourth most populous in the world in the early 21st century.

The name Indonesia was used as early as 1884 and is derived from Greek elements meaning India and island. The country was formerly the Dutch or Netherlands East Indies. Japan occupied the archipelago from 1942 to 1945, Indonesian independence was declared in 1945, Dutch recognition of sovereignty came in 1949, and the western segment of New Guinea was acknowledged as Indonesian in 1969. East Timor was incorporated in 1976, voted for separation in 1999, and became fully sovereign in 2002.

## Land and Environment

Indonesia's extent is about 3,200 miles east to west and 1,100 miles north to south. It shares land borders with Malaysia on Borneo and Papua New Guinea on New Guinea. Of roughly 17,500 islands, more than 7,000 are uninhabited. Sumatra, Kalimantan, and western New Guinea make up nearly three-fourths of the land area, while Java, Sulawesi, the Moluccas, Bali, and the Lesser Sundas form much of the remainder.

Relief reflects the meeting of three major crustal units. The Sunda Shelf carries Borneo, Sumatra, Java, Bali, and the Lesser Sunda arc; the Sahul Shelf carries New Guinea and adjacent islands; and a volcanic mountain belt related to Japan and the Philippines runs between Borneo and New Guinea. Deep trenches, including the Java Trench at about 24,440 feet, mark true continental boundaries, while shallow shelf seas are geologically recent.

The major islands are physically varied. Kalimantan is mostly lowland, swamp, and forested interior mountains. Sumatra has a narrow west coast, the Barisan Mountains, inland low hills, and broad eastern alluvial lowlands. Java is dominated by volcanic mountains and fertile plains. Sulawesi, the Moluccas, and Papua have more complex mountain, trough, reef, and island-arc patterns.

Volcanoes are among Indonesia's defining features. More than 100 active volcanoes and many extinct ones run in a crescent from Sumatra and Java through Flores and north toward the Banda Sea and Sulawesi. Mount Merapi near Yogyakarta erupts frequently, Mount Kelud can unleash destructive mudflows, and Krakatau's 1883 eruption caused tsunamis, killed tens of thousands, and affected sunsets worldwide for more than a year.

Other volcanic disasters include Mount Agung's violent 1963 eruption after more than 140 years of dormancy, the eastern Java mud volcano triggered by drilling in 2006, and Mount Sinabung's 2010 eruption after more than 400 years of dormancy. Volcanism brings destruction, but volcanic ash also renews some of the archipelago's most fertile soils.

Drainage is shaped by insularity. Rivers are generally short, but the Kapuas, Barito, and Mahakam of Kalimantan are major streams, while Papua has the Baliem, Mamberamo, and Digul. Lakes include Lake Toba in Sumatra and Towuti, Matama, and Poso in Sulawesi. Surrounding seas are communication routes, ecological barriers, food sources, and areas of offshore petroleum, gas, and mineral resources.

Soils and climate vary with rock, rainfall, and altitude. Java and parts of Sumatra and Sulawesi have fertile volcanic soils, while many humid lowlands have lateritic soils vulnerable to leaching. Rainforest soils can support lush vegetation yet deteriorate quickly when cleared. The climate is equatorial and monsoonal, with high coastal temperatures, December-March rains, and a June-October dry season that becomes stronger eastward toward Australia.

Plant and animal life is exceptionally diverse. Indonesia has rainforests, mangroves, teak, rattan, orchids, durian, and Rafflesia arnoldii, one of the world's largest flowers. Wallace's Line separates Asian fauna to the west from more Australian forms to the east. Orangutans, Javan rhinoceroses, tigers, elephants, babirusa, tamarau, birds of paradise, marsupials, and the Komodo dragon show the archipelago's mixed biogeography.

## People, Language, and Religion

Unity in diversity, or Bhinneka tunggal ika, describes a population with more than 300 ethnic groups and more than twice as many languages. Western Indonesia is largely related to East Asian and Malay populations, while eastern islands include many Melanesian peoples. Arab, Indian, Chinese, European, and other contacts added further layers, especially in coastal trading regions.

Western Indonesian groups include wet-rice Javanese, Sundanese, Madurese, and Balinese communities; coastal Muslim Malays, Makassarese, and Bugis; and inland shifting-cultivation peoples such as Toraja, Batak, and Dayak groups including Kenyah, Kayan, Ngaju, and Embaloh. The Minangkabau of west-central Sumatra are noted for matrilineal descent and inheritance, while the Minahasan of northern Sulawesi are historically Christian and atypical in regional pattern.

Eastern Indonesians include Moluccan coastal and interior communities and Papuan peoples such as Asmat and Dani groups. Chinese Indonesians historically included peranakan communities of long local residence and totok communities of more recent immigration. Chinese merchants played major commercial roles in Java, Sumatra, Kalimantan, and Riau, while legal distinctions between pribumi and non-pribumi were removed in 2006.

Languages are mostly Austronesian, except for Papuan languages in western New Guinea and parts of the Moluccas. Java alone has Javanese, Sundanese, and Madurese; Sumatra, Kalimantan, Sulawesi, and eastern islands have many languages and dialects. Bahasa Indonesia, based on Malay of the Riau-Jambi literary world, became a national language because it was already a lingua franca and was not tied to a dominant ethnic hierarchy. Indonesia and Malaysia agreed on revised spelling in 1972.

Religion ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Religion)) is diverse but Islam predominates, followed by about three-fourths of the population in the source period. Christians make up about one-eighth, Hindus less than 5 percent overall but dominate Bali, and some Chinese communities practice Buddhism, Confucianism, or blended traditions. Hinduism and Buddhism entered through coastal and courtly contacts with India; Islam spread especially through South Asian Muslim traders from the 14th and 15th centuries; Christianity expanded through missions and colonial-era contacts.

Settlement ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Human_settlement)) differs sharply by region. Java, Bali, and Madura have dense wet-rice villages tied to volcanic soils, irrigation, mosques or temples, schools, markets, and mutual labour. Dayak longhouses, Balinese walled compounds, Batak houses, Minangkabau roof forms, Toraja architecture, urban kampung settlements, and cosmopolitan Jakarta show the variety of dwelling and social organization.

Demography is uneven. The population is concentrated on Java, Bali, and Madura, while outer islands are less dense. Fertility and mortality declined after the mid-20th century, and Indonesia's age structure became more balanced. Migration patterns include rural-to-urban movement, circular migration for work, and the state-sponsored transmigration of people from Java to outer islands, a program that formally ended in 2000.

## Economy and Infrastructure

![[attachments/indonesia_004.webp|420]]

*Rice terraces in East Bali, Indonesia*

Indonesia's economy has enormous resources but long played a smaller global role than its scale suggested. It exports petroleum, natural gas, rubber, coffee, cocoa, palm oil, sugar, tea, tobacco, copra, spices, seafood, timber products, textiles, machinery, and electronics. Since independence, the economy shifted from primary production toward manufacturing, trade, and services; manufacturing passed agriculture as a contributor to GDP in the early 1990s.

Economic policy changed sharply across regimes. Sukarno's Guided Economy subordinated development to political goals and produced financial chaos. After Suharto took power in the mid-1960s, stabilization, foreign investment, and five-year plans from 1969 to 1998 emphasized infrastructure and development. The 1997 Asian crisis devastated banking and business, but later reforms and the end of the IMF program in 2003 helped restore stability.

Agriculture, forestry, and fisheries remain important. Rice self-sufficiency was achieved from the mid-1980s, though later imports returned. Estate and smallholder crops include palm oil, rubber, coffee, cocoa, sugar, tea, tobacco, cloves, and copra. Fisheries produce shrimp, milkfish, scad, tuna, and mackerel. Forests of Kalimantan and Papua supply meranti, plywood, veneer, teak, and other products, but deforestation and haze became major environmental concerns.

Resources and industry include oil, gas, coal, tin, bauxite, nickel, manganese, copper, gold, hydroelectric power, and geothermal potential. Import substitution dominated early industrial policy, but the oil-revenue decline of the 1980s encouraged deregulation, exports, private investment, and international competitiveness. Textiles, batik, telecommunications, electronics, automobiles, cement, fertilizers, and consumer goods are major manufacturing fields, especially in western Java, Jakarta, Bandung, and other urban-industrial zones.

Finance and trade are centered on Bank Indonesia and the rupiah. Banking deregulation in 1983 and 1988 encouraged private banking and capital-market growth, but the 1997 crisis exposed severe weakness. The Indonesian Bank Restructuring Agency operated from 1998 to 2004. Major trade partners include Japan, the United States, Singapore, China, South Korea, Thailand, Malaysia, and Australia.

Transport and telecommunications must overcome archipelagic geography. Sea freight and air travel are crucial, roads dominate travel within islands, railways are concentrated mainly on Java, Madura, and parts of Sumatra, and ports such as Tanjung Priok, Tanjungperak, Belawan, Palembang, Semarang, Ambon, Jayapura, and Banjarmasin link regional economies. Satellites from the late 1970s, wartel telephone shops, warnet Internet shops, and rapid cell-phone adoption helped reduce isolation.

## Government and Society

The Republic of Indonesia was declared in 1945, claimed the former Dutch East Indies from Sabang to Merauke, used Yogyakarta as a provisional revolutionary capital, became the federal United States of Indonesia in 1949, and returned to a unitary republic in 1950. The 1945 constitution was restored by presidential decree and later amended during reformasi after Suharto's fall.

[[The Forms Of Government Their Historical Development (Government)|Government]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Government)) gives strong authority to the president, aided by a vice president and cabinet. Laws after 1999 limited presidents to two five-year terms, and direct presidential elections began in 2004. The People's Consultative Assembly was restructured into a bicameral system with the People's Representative Council and Regional Representative Council. Provinces include special arrangements for Jakarta, Yogyakarta, Aceh, Papua, and other regions created or reorganized through decentralization.

Justice and politics combine national institutions and local custom. The Supreme Court is the final court of appeal, the Constitutional Court was established in 2003, and the National Ombudsman Commission in 2000. Adat, or customary law, continues in civil matters, and courts are divided into general, religious, military, and administrative spheres. Elections in 1955, Guided Democracy, the 1965 crisis, New Order Golkar dominance, and post-1998 multiparty reforms shaped political life.

Security has had an unusually strong political role. The armed forces emerged from Dutch-trained, Japanese-trained, and militia groups during the independence struggle. Under Sukarno and Suharto, ABRI included army, navy, air force, and police, and the dwi-fungsi doctrine justified military participation in politics. After Suharto, the TNI separated from the police and was removed from legislative seats by 2004.

Health, housing, and education improved but remained uneven. Public health centres, village health posts, family-planning programs, traditional healers, and midwives all mattered, while dengue, cholera, HIV/AIDS, rural staffing gaps, and urban services posed difficulties. Urban kampung often lacked adequate housing, water, sanitation, and electricity. Education expanded greatly after independence; the first nine years became compulsory in the early 1990s, and major universities include Bandung Institute of Technology, University of Indonesia, Gadjah Mada, Hasanuddin, and Airlangga.

## Cultural Life

![[attachments/indonesia_002.webp|420]]

*Borobudur temple complex in Indonesia*

Indonesian culture combines local ritual speech, oral epic, Hindu-Buddhist inheritance, Islamic practice, colonial-era change, urban popular culture, and global media. Interiors of Sumatra, Kalimantan, and Papua preserve distinctive oral and ritual traditions, while Java and Bali are deeply marked by the Mahabharata, Ramayana, courts, temples, puppet theatre, dance, and gamelan.

Temple culture includes early Dieng monuments, Shailendra and Mataram achievements, Borobudur, Prambanan, and later East Javanese works such as Panataran. Borobudur, a 9th-century Mahayana Buddhist monument and UNESCO World Heritage site from 1991, has square and circular terraces, Buddha niches, relief narratives, 72 bell-shaped stupas, and a central stupa. Prambanan's Hindu temples display Ramayana reliefs and statues of Shiva, Vishnu, Brahma, and Nandi.

[[The Art of Literature (Literature)|Literature]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Literature)) includes oral didong, tekena', pantun, kentrung, macapat, randai, and modern Indonesian writing. Poedjangga Baroe in the early 1930s promoted national modern literature in Indonesian. S. Takdir Alisjahbana, Chairil Anwar, Mochtar Lubis, and Pramoedya Ananta Toer represent important modern literary currents, while journals such as Horison and Kalam continued debate.

Performing and visual arts include wayang kulit, wayang golek, wayang wong, masked dance, modern theatre, sendratari, pakarena, srimpi, seudati, kancet laki, jangger, pencak silat, gamelan, kendang, pesinden, gerong, sampe, kacapi, gondang, kroncong, and dangdut. Batik made with canting or cap, ikat and double ikat textiles, Balinese carving, Dayak decoration, Asmat woodwork, and wayang puppets show regional craft depth.

Institutions and recreation include the National Museum, Wayang Museum, Taman Mini Indonesia Indah completed in 1975, Taman Ismail Marzuki established in 1968, and the Jakarta Arts Building renovated in 1987. Football is widely popular, martial arts are common, and Indonesia's Olympic committee formed in 1946. The country entered the Olympics in 1952, won its first medal in 1988, and won badminton gold in 1992 through Alan Budi Kusuma and Susi Susanti.

## History

Prehistory begins with Homo erectus, or Java man, on Java about 1.7 million years ago. A postglacial sea-level rise about 6,000 years ago submerged land bridges and created the modern island complex. Monsoon winds, boats, timber, resins, spices, river routes, and overseas trade connected the archipelago with maritime Asia, India, China, and possibly even Madagascar in early centuries CE.

Hinduization came through Brahmans, Sanskrit culture, Shaivism, bhakti, royal cults, and the linga. Inscriptions from the 5th and 6th centuries show Indian literati or their Indonesian disciples at royal courts. Indigenous mountain, water, ancestor, and death symbolism blended with Indian religious ideas rather than being simply replaced by them.

Srivijaya, centered near Palembang, became a major maritime and Buddhist power. The Chinese pilgrim I-ching visited in 671. Old Malay inscriptions, including Talang Tuwo in 684, show royal, Buddhist, and political ambition. By 695, Srivijaya controlled the Strait of Malacca, but shifts in Chinese trade, Jambi or Malayu competition, and later regional changes weakened it by the 11th to 13th centuries.

Java's early kingdoms included the Shailendra and Mataram worlds, where Borobudur and Prambanan were built. Airlangga restored power after crisis in the early 11th century and later divided his realm. Ken Angrok defeated Kertajaya in 1222, helping found Singhasari. Kertanagara ruled from 1268 to 1292, sent a Malayu expedition in 1275, defied Kublai Khan's envoy in 1289, and fell before the Mongol expedition of 1292.

Majapahit rose under Kertarajasa and reached renown under Gajah Mada, Tribhuvana, and Hayam Wuruk, who ruled from 1350 to 1389. Prapancha's Nagarakertagama of 1365 portrayed royal divinity, sacred order, and a wide sphere of influence. Majapahit's authority later weakened, and Islamized coastal powers grew.

Islamization began gradually. A Muslim tombstone from 1082, late-13th-century Samudra-Pasai and Perlak, the 1297 Samudra-Pasai tomb, Malacca, Aceh, Ternate, Tidore, Demak, Cirebon, Japara, Gresik, and Mataram under Sultan Agung show a long process of coastal conversion, trade, court politics, and religious synthesis. Hindu-Buddhist influence persisted, especially in Java and Bali.

European expansion began with Portugal's capture of Malacca in 1511 and later Dutch and English voyages by James Lancaster, the de Houtman brothers, and Jacob van Neck. The Dutch East India Company, or VOC, received its charter in 1602. Jan Pieterszoon Coen, Anthony van Diemen, and Joan Maetsuyker helped turn commercial outposts into territorial power. The Dutch captured Malacca in 1641, Batavia became headquarters, and the VOC dissolved in 1799.

Colonial consolidation deepened in the 19th century. Herman Willem Daendels centralized Java, the British under Thomas Stamford Raffles ruled from 1811 to 1815, and Raffles published History of Java in 1817. The Java War of 1825-1830 involved Diponegoro, and the Padri War ended in 1837 with Tuanku Imam Bonjol's defeat.

The Culture System, introduced by Johannes van den Bosch in 1830, required villages to produce export crops for the state and enriched the Dutch treasury while burdening Javanese communities. Exports rose from 13 million to 74 million guldens in a decade. The Liberal Policy of 1870, the Aceh War of 1873-1908, and the Ethical Policy of 1901 marked later colonial phases.

Nationalism ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nationalism)) emerged through Budi Utomo in 1908, Sarekat Islam in 1912, the Indische Partij, socialist organizing by Henk Sneevliet, the PKI, the Bandung Study Club, the PNI under Sukarno, and the Youth Pledge of October 28, 1928. Mohammad Hatta, Sutan Sjahrir, Tan Malaka, and other leaders represented competing revolutionary, nationalist, socialist, Islamic, and constitutional strategies.

Japanese occupation in 1942-1945 weakened Dutch authority while mobilizing labour, youth, and militias. Sukarno and Hatta declared independence on August 17, 1945. The revolution included the Battle of Surabaya in 1945, Linggadjati, Renville in January 1948, the Madiun Affair of September 1948, Dutch military actions, and sovereignty transfer in December 1949, except for western New Guinea.

Constitutional democracy began with the unitary republic in 1950 and the major election of 1955, but cabinets were unstable and regional dissent grew. Sukarno introduced Guided Democracy after Hatta's resignation in 1956, announced his concept in 1957, and restored the 1945 constitution by decree on July 5, 1959. Western New Guinea, the Bandung Conference of 1955, the Fourth Asian Games of 1962, confrontation with Malaysia, and UN withdrawal in 1965 became symbols of national assertion.

The 1965 crisis began when the September 30th Movement kidnapped and murdered six generals on October 1, 1965, while A.H. Nasution escaped. Suharto took control, the army blamed the PKI, and anti-communist killings claimed from 80,000 to more than 1,000,000 lives. Suharto received extensive powers in 1966, became acting president in 1967, president in 1968, and ruled the New Order after Sukarno's fall.

The New Order ended confrontation with Malaysia, helped found ASEAN in 1967, welcomed foreign investment, stabilized inflation, promoted five-year plans, and relied on Golkar, bureaucracy, and military power. It also centralized authority, imposed Pancasila, suppressed opponents, controlled parties, intervened in East Timor in 1975-1976, faced resistance in Papua and East Timor, and became associated with corruption and Suharto-family privileges.

Reformasi followed the Asian crisis of 1997, IMF pressure, Trisakti student shootings, Jakarta riots, and Suharto's resignation on May 21, 1998. B.J. Habibie, Abdurrahman Wahid, Megawati Sukarnoputri, and Susilo Bambang Yudhoyono led the post-Suharto transition. East Timor became sovereign in 2002, direct presidential elections began in 2004, and the 2004 Aceh tsunami, 2009 Sumatra earthquake, and 2010 Mentawai tsunami and Merapi eruption tested the republic.

## Key Events & Developments

- Java man: Homo erectus lived on Java about 1.7 million years ago.
- Postglacial seas: Rising sea level about 6,000 years ago created the modern archipelago.
- Srivijaya: I-ching's 671 visit and control of the Strait of Malacca by 695 mark maritime Buddhist power.
- Central Java monuments: Borobudur and Prambanan expressed 8th-9th-century Buddhist and Hindu court culture.
- Majapahit: Hayam Wuruk's rule from 1350 to 1389 became a classical Javanese memory of greatness.
- Portuguese Malacca: The capture of Malacca in 1511 began a new European maritime phase.
- VOC charter: The Dutch East India Company was chartered in 1602.
- Culture System: The Dutch imposed export-crop obligations from 1830.
- National awakening: Budi Utomo in 1908 and the Youth Pledge in 1928 helped form Indonesian nationalism.
- Independence ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Independence)): Indonesia declared independence on August 17, 1945, and sovereignty was recognized in 1949.
- Guided Democracy: Sukarno restored the 1945 constitution in 1959.
- New Order: Suharto rose after the 1965 crisis and became president in 1968.
- Reformasi: Suharto resigned on May 21, 1998, after economic crisis and unrest.
- Direct elections: Indonesia held its first direct presidential election in 2004.
