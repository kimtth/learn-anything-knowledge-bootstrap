# Lincoln

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> Abraham Lincoln was the sixteenth U.S. president who led the Union through the Civil War and made emancipation central to its outcome. His defense of self-government, political skill, and language of national purpose shaped both the preservation of the Union and the abolition of slavery.

## Introduction

![[attachments/lincoln_001_mw.webp|420]]

*Portrait of Abraham Lincoln in 1863*

Abraham Lincoln, born near Hodgenville, Kentucky, on February 12, 1809, became the 16th president of the United States and led the country through the Civil War. His presidency preserved the Union and turned the war into a struggle that also destroyed slavery.

Lincoln's appeal rests on a rare mixture of humble origins, dramatic death, plain humanity, political skill, and democratic language. For him, the Union mattered because it tested whether self-government could survive, and later debate has examined both his role as emancipator and the limits of his racial views.

## Self-Government

The ideal of self-government gave Lincoln's career a meaning beyond ordinary national politics. The United States, as he understood it, was a practical experiment in government by the people, and the Civil War threatened that experiment before the world.

## Public Image

The public image of Lincoln grew through photographs, memorials, speeches, and stories of his life. The Lincoln Memorial in [[Washington, D.C. (Washington)|Washington, D.C.]], dedicated on May 30, 1922, reflected the lasting national memory of his work and death.

## Life

Lincoln was born into frontier poverty. His father, Thomas Lincoln, moved the family from Kentucky to Indiana in 1816 after land-title trouble, and the young Lincoln remembered hard work, flooding, wild animals, and the death of his mother, Nancy Hanks Lincoln, in 1818.

His stepmother, Sarah Bush Johnston Lincoln, brought affection and order to the household. Lincoln had little formal schooling, but he borrowed books, taught himself grammar and mathematics, and developed the reading habits that helped him move from farm labour to law, politics, and national leadership.

## Frontier Upbringing

Lincoln's early homes in Kentucky and Indiana shaped his dislike of frontier violence and his respect for practical work. He helped clear fields and tend crops, but hunting and fishing never attracted him.

## Childhood Homes

The backwoods cabin near Hodgenville and the later farm at Knob Creek became central to Lincoln's remembered childhood. These places made his rise seem extraordinary because his early surroundings offered almost nothing to encourage ambition.

## Early Career and Law

In New Salem, Illinois, Lincoln worked as storekeeper, postmaster, and surveyor. During the 1832 Black Hawk War, he enlisted and was elected captain; soon afterward he turned from local jobs toward the law, passed the bar in 1836, and began legal practice.

As a prairie lawyer, Lincoln moved to Springfield in 1837, joined partnerships with John T. Stuart, Stephen T. Logan, and later William H. Herndon, and earned a strong reputation for fairness, shrewdness, and honesty. Railroad work, including cases for the Illinois Central and the Rock Island Bridge, made his practice more profitable and more prominent.

## Springfield and the Circuit

Springfield gave Lincoln a larger legal and political world than New Salem. Circuit travel across Illinois taught him local opinion, sharpened his storytelling and argument, and placed him among lawyers, judges, merchants, railroad men, and voters.

## Religion

Lincoln's religious life moved from early skepticism toward a deep sense of Providence. He never joined a church, yet he knew the Bible well, read Shakespeare with feeling, believed events might carry divine meaning, and during the Civil War increasingly described himself as an instrument within God's larger purpose.

## Entering Politics

As a young politician, Lincoln entered the Whig tradition of Henry Clay and Daniel Webster. He supported a national bank, protective tariff, and internal improvements, especially because Illinois and the West needed roads, canals, railroads, and federal encouragement for economic growth.

He opposed slavery but was not an abolitionist. In 1837, after the murder of antislavery editor Elijah Lovejoy, Lincoln protested resolutions that defended slavery, calling slavery unjust and bad policy while also warning that abolitionist agitation might worsen the problem.

The 1854 Kansas-Nebraska Act and the collapse of the Whigs pushed Lincoln toward the Republican Party. He challenged Stephen A. Douglas for the Senate in 1858, arguing that Congress must block slavery from the territories, and his “house divided” warning made the slavery question a national test of whether the country would become all free or all slave.

## Election and Secession

Lincoln won the Republican nomination in 1860 after skillful preparation at Chicago and then managed a campaign built on party unity. With Democrats divided among several candidates, he won the electoral college decisively despite receiving no Deep South votes and only about forty percent of the national popular vote.

Before his inauguration, South Carolina seceded, followed by six more states. Lincoln rejected any compromise that would divide the territories between slavery and freedom because such a principle would encourage further slave expansion, even while he accepted noninterference with slavery where it already existed.

## The First Inaugural Address

In the March 4, 1861 inaugural address, Lincoln declared that federal property would be held, occupied, and possessed, but that force would not be used against the South unless necessary. The message placed responsibility for conflict on secessionists while still appealing for sectional peace.

## The Fort Sumter Crisis

The Fort Sumter crisis forced Lincoln's first great presidential decision. He delayed while advisers disagreed, then prepared relief for Sumter and Fort Pickens, notifying South Carolina that only provisions would be sent unless the effort was resisted.

## War Begins

Confederate batteries fired on Fort Sumter on April 12, 1861, before the relief expedition arrived. Lincoln then called for volunteers and proclaimed a blockade of Southern ports, turning secession into open war while insisting that the Confederacy had begun the conflict of arms.

## War Strategy

Lincoln's early war strategy rejected Winfield Scott's passive Anaconda plan as too slow. After defeat at Bull Run on July 21, 1861, Lincoln argued that Union armies had to use superior numbers by threatening Confederate forces at several points at the same time, while the blockade tightened pressure by sea.

## War Leadership

Lincoln's war leadership was experimental and practical. He claimed no fixed policy, adjusted to events, tested generals and command systems, and used failure as evidence for changing direction rather than as a reason to stop acting.

## Finding a General

From 1861 to 1864, Lincoln tried George B. McClellan, John Pope, Ambrose E. Burnside, Joseph Hooker, and George Gordon Meade in eastern command. He became frustrated with delays and with generals who aimed at cities or territory rather than the destruction of Robert E. Lee's army.

Lincoln finally found the coordinated command he needed in Ulysses S. Grant, whose Vicksburg campaign won Lincoln's admiration. In March 1864, Grant became lieutenant general and general in chief, working with commanders such as William T. Sherman, Philip Sheridan, and George H. Thomas under Lincoln's overall direction.

## Wartime Photography

The war years also fixed Lincoln's public face through Mathew Brady's photography. Images of the president belonged to the same public record as his military decisions, speeches, and evolving reputation.

## Union Before Abolition

Lincoln hated slavery but first placed Union preservation above immediate abolition. He revoked early military emancipation orders by John C. Fremont and David Hunter, hesitated over confiscation laws, and told Horace Greeley on August 22, 1862 that his paramount object was to save the Union.

## Emancipation

Lincoln first preferred gradual, compensated emancipation by state action, with federal aid and colonization abroad, but border states would not adopt the plan and many African American leaders rejected colonization. He then issued the preliminary Emancipation Proclamation on September 22, 1862, and the final proclamation on January 1, 1863.

The Emancipation Proclamation ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emancipation_Proclamation)) applied only to areas under Confederate control and directly or indirectly freed fewer than 200,000 enslaved people during the war. Its larger meaning was symbolic and political: freedom joined reunion as a war aim, European liberal opinion moved closer to the Union, and the need for the Thirteenth Amendment became clear.

Lincoln helped place the antislavery amendment in the 1864 Republican platform and then used persuasion and patronage to secure the two-thirds congressional vote during the lame-duck session. His title as the Great Emancipator rests not only on the proclamation but also on this constitutional campaign and on his wartime leadership.

## Civil Liberties

Civil liberties became one of Lincoln's hardest wartime problems. He authorized arrests, banished Clement L. Vallandigham inside Confederate lines, and allowed temporary newspaper suppression, while arguing that limited sacrifices of constitutional procedure could be necessary to preserve the Constitution itself.

Lincoln also warned commanders to use arrests and press restrictions only when speech or assemblies caused direct military injury. Critics still saw danger in suspended habeas corpus, but his general practice remained cautious compared with the fears and provocations of civil war.

## Reconstruction and Reelection

Within the Republican Party, Lincoln balanced conservatives and Radicals while supporting major Whig-Republican economic measures such as tariff protection, a national banking system, and internal improvements. Reconstruction exposed sharper conflict: his ten percent plan allowed new governments when a small loyal share of voters took an oath, while Radicals demanded a majority oath in the Wade-Davis Bill.

Lincoln won reelection in 1864 against George B. McClellan with fifty-five percent of the popular vote. He required any serious peace proposal to include restoration of the whole Union and abandonment of slavery, and at Hampton Roads on February 3, 1865 he offered liberal pardons but no peace without reunion.

## The Assassination

Lincoln's last public speech on April 11, 1865 still treated Reconstruction as flexible because each Southern state had different conditions. He supported education for freedpeople and suggested immediate voting rights for some African Americans, especially the very intelligent and those who had fought for the Union.

Soon after the effective end of the Civil War, John Wilkes Booth fatally shot Lincoln at Ford's Theatre. His death on Good Friday gave many mourners a religious frame for national grief, and Edwin M. Stanton was remembered as saying that Lincoln now belonged to the ages.

## Reputation

Lincoln's reputation was shaped by memory, argument, and biography. William H. Herndon resisted saintly portrayals and emphasized Lincoln's rise from poor origins, his supposed love for Ann Rutledge, and the darker sides of family life, while other interpreters saw him as martyr, liberator, nationalist, or dangerous centralizer.

## The Gettysburg Address

Lincoln's central language returned again and again to government by the people. From the Springfield Lyceum speech of 1838 to the Gettysburg Address, he refined the idea that the Civil War tested whether a constitutional republic could survive domestic rebellion.

At Gettysburg, he connected the soldiers' sacrifice with a “new birth of freedom” and with the survival of government of, by, and for the people. The speech became the clearest expression of his democratic faith and of the war's meaning.

## Cabinet and Administration

Lincoln's administration depended on strong and sometimes rival figures, including William H. Seward, Edwin M. Stanton, and military leaders who shaped policy and command. The cabinet and high command formed part of a larger wartime system for concentrating national resources.

## Key Events & Developments

- 1809: Abraham Lincoln was born near Hodgenville, Kentucky.
- 1836: Lincoln passed the bar and entered the [[Legal Profession|legal profession]] in Illinois.
- 1858: The Lincoln-Douglas debates made slavery in the territories a national issue.
- 1860: Lincoln won the presidency as the Republican candidate.
- April 12, 1861: Confederate fire on Fort Sumter opened the Civil War.
- January 1, 1863: The Emancipation Proclamation ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emancipation_Proclamation)) added freedom to reunion as a Union war aim.
- 1864: Lincoln won reelection and pressed the Thirteenth Amendment forward.
- April 15, 1865: Lincoln died after being shot by John Wilkes Booth.

## Key People

- Abraham Lincoln ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abraham_Lincoln)): President who preserved the Union, led the Civil War effort, and helped destroy slavery.
- Mary Todd Lincoln ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mary_Todd_Lincoln)): Lincoln's wife, from a prominent Kentucky family, who shared a difficult private life marked by family loss.
- Stephen A. Douglas ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stephen_A._Douglas)): Democratic rival in the 1858 debates over slavery and popular sovereignty.
- Ulysses S. Grant ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ulysses_S._Grant)): General whose Vicksburg success led Lincoln to give him command of all federal armies.
- Frederick Douglass ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frederick_Douglass)): Former slave and abolitionist who respected Lincoln's plain treatment of African American visitors.
- William H. Herndon ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_H._Herndon)): Law partner whose memories strongly influenced later interpretations of Lincoln.
- John Wilkes Booth ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Wilkes_Booth)): Assassin who killed Lincoln at Ford's Theatre.

## Interesting Facts

- Honest Abe, the Rail-Splitter, and the Great Emancipator were all names associated with Lincoln.
- Lincoln received no more than about forty percent of the national popular vote in 1860 but won the electoral college clearly.
- The Emancipation Proclamation ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emancipation_Proclamation)) did not cover loyal slave states or federally occupied Confederate areas.
- Lincoln admitted to Grant after Vicksburg that his own military expectation had been wrong.
- The Second Inaugural Address expressed Reconstruction in the spirit of “malice toward none” and “charity for all.”
