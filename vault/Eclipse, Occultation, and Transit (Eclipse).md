# Eclipse, Occultation, and Transit (Eclipse)

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> An eclipse occurs when one celestial body moves into another's shadow or blocks its light. Eclipses, occultations, and transits provide observable evidence about orbital motion, sizes, and atmospheres.

## Introduction

An eclipse is the complete or partial hiding of one celestial body by another. It happens when three objects in space line up. From Earth, a solar eclipse happens when the Moon comes between [[Earth - Its Properties, Composition, and Structure (Earth)|Earth]] and the Sun. A lunar eclipse happens when the Moon moves into Earth's shadow. The same basic idea also applies to moons and spacecraft near other planets, because they can move into a planet's shadow or pass behind the planet as seen from Earth.

## Phenomena observed during eclipses

Eclipses are not only alignments but also observable physical events. During lunar eclipses ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lunar_eclipse)), the Moon changes colour and brightness because sunlight is filtered and bent by Earth's atmosphere before reaching the Moon. During solar eclipses ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solar_eclipse)), observers may see changing daylight, Baily's beads, the chromosphere, prominences, the corona, shadow bands, and sudden changes in temperature and animal behaviour.

## Lunar eclipse phenomena

*Geometry of a lunar eclipse showing sunlight, Earth, Moon, umbra, and penumbra*

During a lunar eclipse ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lunar_eclipse)), the full Moon may enter Earth's shadow. The Moon moves in its orbit from west to east, so for an observer facing south the darkening usually starts at the Moon's left edge. In a favourable total eclipse, the Moon takes about two hours to pass through the umbra ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Umbra%2C_penumbra_and_antumbra)), the darkest part of Earth's shadow.

## Solar eclipse phenomena

A total solar eclipse is visible only from a narrow path on Earth, sometimes only about 150 km, or 90 miles, wide. The first contact is the moment when the invisible dark disk of the Moon first touches the Sun's disk. The partial phase then begins as a small notch appears on the western rim of the Sun, and the Sun slowly becomes a crescent.

Solar observation requires special methods because the uneclipsed or partly eclipsed Sun can injure the eye. Astronomers and observers use safe filters, projection, photography, spectroscopy, and space-based instruments to study eclipse phases. Totality is uniquely valuable because the Moon blocks the bright photosphere, briefly revealing the corona, prominences, and faint structures that are otherwise overwhelmed by sunlight.

## The geometry of eclipses, occultations, and transits

The [[Geometry|geometry]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geometry)) begins with shadows and apparent size. A solar eclipse occurs when the Moon comes between Earth and the Sun and its shadow sweeps across Earth. A lunar eclipse occurs when the Moon moves through Earth's shadow. An occultation occurs when the nearer or apparently larger body hides another object, and a transit occurs when a smaller-looking body crosses the face of a larger one.

## Eclipses of the Sun

For an observer inside the Moon's umbra ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Umbra%2C_penumbra_and_antumbra)), the Sun is completely covered and the solar eclipse is total. For an observer in the penumbra, the Moon covers only part of the Sun, so the eclipse is partial. Because the umbral cone is narrow at Earth's distance, the path of totality crosses only a thin strip of land or sea, while the partial eclipse is visible over a much wider region.

## Eclipses of the Moon

A lunar eclipse ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lunar_eclipse)) can occur only at full Moon, because Earth's shadow points away from the Sun and the Moon must be opposite the Sun from Earth. Unlike a solar eclipse, a lunar eclipse looks much the same from every place on Earth where the Moon is above the horizon.

## Eclipses, occultations, and transits of satellites and other objects
The four large Galilean satellites of Jupiter show how the same geometry applies outside the Earth-Moon-Sun system. The three inner moons, Io, Europa, and Ganymede, enter Jupiter's shadow at each revolution, while Callisto is not eclipsed every time. Because these bodies are large enough, several minutes can pass between first contact with the shadow and totality.

## The frequency of solar and lunar eclipses

Solar eclipses happen two to five times a year, with five being exceptional. five last occurred in 1935 and will not occur again until 2206. For Earth as a whole, the average number of total solar eclipses in a century is 66, but any one place on Earth may experience no more than one total solar eclipse in three to four centuries.

## Cycles of eclipses

The basic lunar cycle for eclipses is the synodic month, the time from one new Moon to the next, which is about 29.530589 days. Solar eclipses occur at new Moon, and lunar eclipses occur at full Moon. But eclipses do not happen every month because the Moon's orbital plane is tilted about 5 degrees to the ecliptic, the plane of Earth's orbit around the Sun.

## Prediction and calculation of solar and lunar eclipses

Eclipse prediction has two tasks: finding when an eclipse will occur and finding where and when it will be visible. this by imagining an observer at Earth's centre watching the Sun and Moon projected on the celestial sphere. The Sun moves slowly along the ecliptic in one year, while the Moon moves along its own path once per lunar month.

## Eclipse research activities

Eclipses have been important tools for scientific discovery. Total solar eclipses reveal the Sun's faint outer atmosphere, while lunar eclipses reveal how the Moon's surface responds when solar heating stops. Occultations and transits also allow scientists to measure sizes, atmospheres, orbital motions, and even distances.

## Solar research

During totality, the Moon blocks the Sun's bright visible disk and reveals the corona, the faint outer solar atmosphere. Just before the corona appears, observers may see the chromosphere, a thin red layer in the lower solar atmosphere. The corona is about a million times fainter than the solar disk, so it is normally invisible in daylight.

## Discovery of helium

In 1868, during an eclipse whose path crossed [[India]], the French astronomer Pierre Janssen observed a bright yellow line in the spectrum of a solar prominence. Its wavelength was slightly shorter than the known yellow sodium line. Janssen reported this to the British astronomer Joseph Norman Lockyer, who had missed the eclipse.

## Support for the general theory of relativity

After Albert Einstein published the general theory of relativity in 1916, astronomers looked for tests of its predictions. One prediction involved redshifts in solar absorption lines, but early attempts failed. Another predicted that starlight passing near the Sun would be bent by the Sun's gravity, with a deflection of 1.75 arc seconds for light just grazing the Sun's edge.

## Temperature of the corona

Around 1930, the German astronomer Walter Grotrian studied spectra of the solar corona from a total eclipse. He found that coronal light had the same colour distribution as photospheric light but lacked the absorption lines of the photosphere. He argued that the coronal light was photospheric light scattered by free electrons moving very fast, which meant the corona had to be very hot.

## Lunar research

Lunar eclipses help scientists study the lunar soil. When sunlight suddenly stops, infrared and radio radiation from the Moon fade more slowly than visible light because they come from below the surface. By measuring these changes, researchers learn how deeply different kinds of radiation penetrate the soil and how well the soil conducts heat.

## Transits of Mercury and Venus

A transit ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astronomical_transit)) of Mercury or Venus occurs when the planet is at inferior conjunction, between Earth and the Sun, and also near a node of its inclined orbit. Otherwise the planet passes above or below the Sun. This requirement parallels the Moon's need to be near a node for an eclipse.

## Occultations

The Moon occults every object in a 10-degree-wide belt centred on the ecliptic over about nine years. Early astronomers mainly observed lunar occultations to improve the Moon's orbit. With large telescopes and fast electronics, these events became tools for measuring stellar angular diameters, detecting dust around stars, and studying other details.

## Eclipsing binary stars

More than half the stars in the Milky Way Galaxy are estimated to belong to double or multiple systems. If a binary star system is seen edge-on from Earth, each star may eclipse the other once per orbit. Such a pair is an eclipsing binary, also called an eclipsing variable star because its total light changes periodically.

## Eclipses in history

In ancient and medieval times, eclipses of the Sun and Moon were often recorded as omens, usually of disaster. More than 1,000 individual eclipse records survive from the ancient and medieval world. Most ancient observations come from [[China]], Babylonia, and [[Greece]], while no ancient eclipse records appear to have survived from Egypt or India.

## Literary and historical references

Small partial solar eclipses were often missed by casual observers because the Sun remains very bright unless a large part of its disk is covered. Astronomers in Babylonia, [[China]], and Arab lands recorded smaller eclipses more systematically because they could predict roughly when to look. Arab observers sometimes reduced glare by viewing the Sun reflected in water, and the Roman writer Seneca said pitch was used in his time.

## Chinese

Chinese astronomical tradition was said to reach back before 2000 BCE, but the earliest surviving astronomical relics come much later. The Anyang oracle bones from the later Shang dynasty, about 1600-1046 BCE, record eclipses of both the Sun and Moon. One divination text asks whether a solar eclipse in the evening was good or bad.

## Assyrian

The Assyrian Chronicle names annual officials and records that in the year corresponding to 763-762 BCE, during the month Siwan, the Sun was eclipsed while there was revolt in the citadel. This must refer to the eclipse of June 15, 763 BCE, the only large eclipse visible in Assyria over many years.

## Babylonian

For a long time, Ptolemy's Almagest was the only known source for Babylonian eclipse observations. Ptolemy cited lunar eclipses as early as 721 BCE, but the dates and details had been edited, probably by Hipparchus, using the Egyptian 365-day calendar and hours rather than the original units.

## Jewish

The Jewish historian Flavius Josephus says in Antiquities of the Jews that King Herod died in spring soon after a lunar eclipse. Calculations show that the only spring lunar eclipses visible in Israel between 17 BCE and 3 CE were on March 23, 5 BCE, and March 13, 4 BCE.

## Greek

The Greek poet Archilochus, writing in the 7th century BCE, describes Zeus making night out of noon and hiding the bright sunlight. This is a clear reference to a total solar eclipse and identifies the most likely event as the eclipse of April 6, 648 BCE, which was total in the Aegean during Archilochus's lifetime.

## Roman

Roman sources contain fewer eclipse references than Greek sources, and some reported events never happened. For example, Dio Cassius said a total solar eclipse occurred at the funeral of Julia Agrippina, mother of Nero, but calculation shows that no such eclipse occurred.

## Medieval European

After the Classical age, European eclipse records became rare for several centuries. They became more common again after about 800 CE, especially in monastic chronicles. Hydatius, bishop of Chaves in Portugal, was unusual because he recorded five eclipses of the Sun and Moon between 447 and 464 CE, using Olympiads to date them.

## Medieval Islamic

Medieval Islamic chroniclers, like Christian chroniclers, recorded many vivid eclipse descriptions, usually with exact dates in the lunar calendar. The [[Baghdad]] annalist Ibn al-Jawzi, writing about a century later, described the total solar eclipse of June 20, 1061, when darkness came two hours after daybreak and birds were said to fall while flying.

## Uses of eclipses for chronological purposes

Eclipses help chronology because many ancient dating systems were not continuous. Years might be named by a king's reign or by annual officials, so historians need fixed points that connect those systems to BCE or CE years. A dated eclipse can supply such a fixed point because eclipse dates can be calculated exactly.

## Uses of eclipses for astronomical purposes

Ancient and medieval eclipse observations are highly valuable for studying long-term changes in the length of the day. Early investigators such as Edmond Halley used eclipse records to infer that the Moon's motion seemed to be accelerating. In 1939, Harold Spencer Jones showed that only part of this acceleration was real; the rest came from measuring time by Earth's nonuniform rotation.

## Core Equations

Physical models often begin by relating force, energy, waves, and conserved quantities:

$$\vec{F}=m\vec{a}$$

This is Newton's second law: force is the product of mass and acceleration, so a larger mass or a faster change in motion requires a larger net force.

$$E=h\nu$$

This relation says that photon energy is proportional to frequency; higher-frequency radiation carries more energy per photon.

$$E=mc^2$$

This expresses mass-energy equivalence: mass is a form of energy, with the speed of light squared as the conversion factor.

## Key Events & Developments

- July 17, 709 BCE: The Chunqiu records a total solar eclipse at Qufu, the earliest known total solar eclipse record from any civilization.
- June 15, 763 BCE: The Assyrian Chronicle records a major solar eclipse, providing the earliest reliable dated eclipse observation.
- August 27, 413 BCE: A lunar eclipse delays the Athenian withdrawal from Syracuse and helps lead to the destruction of their army and fleet.
- April 15, 136 BCE: Babylonian astronomers record a total solar eclipse in enough detail to help study changes in Earth's rotation.
- 1868: Pierre Janssen and Joseph Norman Lockyer connect a solar spectral line with the new element helium.
- May 29, 1919: Eclipse expeditions to Sobral and Principe support [[Einstein]]'s prediction that the Sun bends starlight.
- 1930: Bernard Lyot invents the coronagraph, making artificial solar eclipses possible for coronal study.
- March 10, 1977: A stellar occultation by Uranus reveals its previously unknown ring system.

## Key People

- Francis Baily ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Francis_Baily)): Drew attention to Baily's beads, the bright points seen just before and after solar totality.
- Ole Romer ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ole_Romer)): Used eclipses of Jupiter's moons in 1676 to explain evidence for the finite speed of light.
- Pierre Janssen ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pierre_Janssen)): Observed the yellow spectral line during the 1868 eclipse that led to the identification of helium.
- Joseph Norman Lockyer ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph_Norman_Lockyer)): Named helium after observing the same solar spectral line outside eclipse conditions.
- Arthur Eddington ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arthur_Eddington)): Led the Principe eclipse expedition in 1919 that supported general relativity.
- Walter Grotrian ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walter_Grotrian)) and Bengt Edlen ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bengt_Edlen)): Connected coronal spectral lines with very hot ionized elements and helped show that the corona reaches million-kelvin temperatures.
- Cyril Hazard ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cyril_Hazard)) and Maarten Schmidt ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maarten_Schmidt)): Used lunar occultation and spectroscopy to identify 3C 273 as the first known quasar.
- al-Biruni ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/al-Biruni)): Used eclipse observations for longitude measurement and later provided data useful for studying Earth's rotation.

## Interesting Facts

- A total solar eclipse ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solar_eclipse)) may be visible from a path only about 150 km wide, while a lunar eclipse can be seen from anywhere on Earth where the Moon is above the horizon.
- The reddish colour of a totally eclipsed Moon comes from sunlight filtered and bent through Earth's atmosphere.
- Baily's beads are caused by sunlight shining through depressions along the Moon's edge.
- The maximum total phase of a lunar eclipse ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lunar_eclipse)) can last about an hour and three-quarters, while solar totality can last only about seven and a half minutes at most.
- The saros ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saros_%28astronomy%29)) cycle lasts 223 synodic months, or 6,585.321124 days, and was known to the ancient Babylonians.
- Transits of Venus helped 18th- and 19th-century astronomers measure the astronomical unit.
- A lunar occultation helped locate 3C 273, the first known quasar.
- Ancient eclipse timings show that [[Earth - Its Properties, Composition, and Structure (Earth)|Earth]]'s rotation has changed enough to shift calculated ancient eclipse paths by thousands of kilometres if the changing day length is ignored.
