## Introduction

Antoine-Laurent Lavoisier, born in [[Paris]] on Aug. 26, 1743 and executed there on May 8, 1794, was a leading French chemist of the 18th-century chemical revolution. He developed an experimental theory of oxygen's chemical reactivity, helped create modern chemical naming, and also worked as a financier and public administrator before the revolutionary terror.

Lavoisier came from a wealthy bourgeois family, studied at College Mazarin, completed legal training, and entered the Order of Barristers. Instead of practicing law, he pursued science and entered the Academy of Sciences in 1768.

The chemistry he entered lacked firm conceptual order. Many thinkers still used the four old elements of earth, air, fire, and water ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Classical_element)), while chemists tried to analyze compounds such as salts into simpler substances. This uncertainty made air, combustion, calcination, and composition central problems for Lavoisier.

Pneumatic chemistry ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pneumatic_chemistry)) prepared the way for Lavoisier's work. Stephen Hales showed in the 1720s that air could lose elasticity when fixed in solids and liquids, and Joseph Black showed in the 1750s that fixed air, later called carbon dioxide, differed chemically from common air.

Lavoisier treated conservation of mass ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conservation_of_mass)) as a basic law of chemical reaction. He believed matter was neither created nor destroyed, and he used careful weighing to make chemistry more like experimental physics, even though proving universal conservation was difficult.

Lavoisier treated conservation of mass ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conservation_of_mass)) as a law of reaction and used weighing to raise chemistry toward the standards of experimental physics. This method challenged Georg Ernst Stahl's phlogiston theory, which explained combustion, respiration, and calcination by a fiery substance.

Lavoisier's administrative life was as important as his laboratory life. He bought a share in the General Farm, collected taxes for the royal government, married Marie Anne Paulze, and relied on her as translator, laboratory assistant, illustrator, and scientific hostess.

From 1775 to 1792, Lavoisier directed the French Gunpowder Administration and helped make France self-sufficient in gunpowder. He also studied agriculture, advised on finance and banking, and worked on the commission that helped create the metric system.

His oxygen theory of combustion grew slowly from experiments in the early 1770s to broad acceptance by 1785. Weight gains in metal calxes and weight losses when calxes returned to metals led him to argue that air, not fire, was fixed and released in these reactions.

Discoveries by Joseph Priestley and other British pneumatic chemists helped Lavoisier identify oxygen's role. His strength lay less in isolated discoveries than in quantification, demonstration, and a program to rebuild chemistry as a rigorous science.

By 1787, Lavoisier and three colleagues published a new chemical nomenclature, whose basic principles still underlie modern naming. In 1789, his Traite elementaire de chimie explained how chemists should investigate, organize, and explain substances.

The French Revolution first seemed to Lavoisier like a chance to rationalize politics and economy, but radicalization turned against the old regime's financiers and privileged administrators. He stayed in France, defended the Academy of Sciences, and was imprisoned with other Tax Farmers.

In May 1794, Lavoisier, his father-in-law, and 26 other Tax Farmers were guillotined. Joseph-Louis Lagrange summed up the loss by saying that a head cut off in an instant might not be replaced in a hundred years.
