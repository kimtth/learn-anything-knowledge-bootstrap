# American Literature

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> American literature is writing in English produced in the United States, shaped by colonial origins, independence, regional experience, social conflict, and changing ideas of national identity. Its major traditions include Romanticism, realism, naturalism, modernism, and diverse later voices.

## Introduction

American literature ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_literature)) is the body of written work produced in the English language in the United States, and like every national literature it was shaped by the history of the country that created it. For almost a century and a half, America was only a scattering of colonies along the eastern seaboard of North America, from which a few hardy settlers ventured westward. After a successful rebellion against the mother country, these colonies became the United States, a single nation that by the end of the 19th century reached south to the Gulf of Mexico, north to the 49th parallel, and west to the Pacific, taking its place among the world powers.

The growth of the nation, together with the rise of science and industry and deep changes in ways of thinking and feeling, molded a literature of poetry, drama, fiction, and criticism that ran from the early 17th century to the late 20th. John Smith, a soldier of fortune, is credited with initiating American writing through books such as *A True Relation of ... Virginia* (1608) and *The Generall Historie of Virginia, New England, and the Summer Isles* (1624), works that glorified their author but were openly meant to advertise colonizing opportunities to Englishmen.

## Colonial and Early National Writing

The earliest colonial literature ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_literature)) was naturally written by Englishmen who thought and wrote as such, and each colony in time produced its own promotional, religious, and historical writing. The new spirit of independence emerged after the Revolution, when authors were urged to create a literature that was genuinely native rather than imitative of British models, a demand that grew stronger after the War of 1812.

A transitional figure was Charles Brockden Brown, who nationalized the Gothic thriller in novels such as *Wieland* (1798), *Arthur Mervyn* (1799–1800), and *Edgar Huntly* (1799), bending European terror conventions to American settings and psychology. His example helped prepare the ground for the more confident generation of writers who would dominate the first half of the 19th century.

## Early Nineteenth-Century Literature

Four authors of real stature answered the call for a native literature and launched a great half century of development. William Cullen Bryant, a New Englander, drew attention in 1817 with his poem "Thanatopsis"; influenced first by 18th-century English poets and later by Wordsworth and the Romantics, he wrote nature lyrics of the New England scene before a long career as the liberal editor of *The Evening Post*.

Washington Irving, a New Yorker, satirized Manhattan life in the *Salmagundi* papers (1807–08) and the burlesque *A History of New York* (1809), then introduced a Romantic note in *The Sketch Book* (1819–20) and *Bracebridge Hall* (1822), becoming the first American writer to win unreserved respect from British critics. James Fenimore Cooper won even wider fame, following the pattern of Sir Walter Scott's Waverley novels in his Leatherstocking tales of the frontier (begun in 1823).

Regional fiction also flourished, with John Pendleton Kennedy portraying plantation life in *Swallow Barn* (1832) and William Gilmore Simms writing historical novels of the frontier and his native South Carolina, such as *The Yemassee* (1835). Around the same time Edgar Allan Poe developed the short tale of terror and ratiocination and a theory of poetry that would prove far more influential in Europe and later America than among his immediate contemporaries.

## American Renaissance

The writers who rose to prominence in the 1830s and worked until about the end of the Civil War — the humorists, the classic New Englanders, Herman Melville, and Walt Whitman — worked in a new spirit shaped partly by the broadening democratic feeling that triumphed with Andrew Jackson's inauguration in 1829. In this Romantic period of emphasis on native scenes and characters, they poured much of America into their books.

Two groups of humorists were especially vivid between 1830 and 1867. Down-east Yankee characters created by Seba Smith, James Russell Lowell, and Benjamin P. Shillaber used commonsense argument to comment on politics and society, while in the old Southwest writers such as Davy Crockett, Augustus Baldwin Longstreet, Johnson J. Hooper, Thomas Bangs Thorpe, and George Washington Harris drew lively pictures of the ebullient frontier and the common man celebrated by Jacksonian democracy.

The polished opposite of this earthy humor was found among the New England Brahmins, a group of aristocratic, foreign-cultured Harvard professors. Henry Wadsworth Longfellow adapted European storytelling to narrative poems on American history; Oliver Wendell Holmes brought urbanity and wit to his *Breakfast-Table* series (1858–91); and James Russell Lowell put his homeland into verse, reaching noble expression in the "Harvard Commemoration Ode" (1865).

## The Transcendentalists

The village of Concord, Massachusetts, was home to another important New England group, whose way had been prepared by the rise of Unitarianism as a replacement for Calvinism early in the 19th century. Ralph Waldo Emerson, the most famous of the Concord philosophers, began as a Unitarian minister but became a Transcendentalist who trusted insights beyond logic and experience for the deepest truths, expounding his beliefs in *Essays* (1841–44), *Representative Men* (1850), and *English Traits* (1856).

His associate Henry David Thoreau was closer to the earthy and practical, a dry Yankee humorist and learned naturalist whose *A Week on the Concord and Merrimack Rivers* (1849) and *Walden* (1854) recorded his belief that modern people should simplify their demands. In "Civil Disobedience" (1849) he set out his anarchistic view that an unjust government should be resisted by letting one's life be "a counter friction to stop the machine." Minor Transcendentalists included Bronson Alcott, George Ripley, and Margaret Fuller, who edited the movement's chief magazine, *The Dial*, and was important in the feminist movement.

The reforming impulse of the age fed a literature of social change, especially over slavery. William Lloyd Garrison founded the abolitionist weekly *The Liberator* (1831–65); John Greenleaf Whittier wrote emotional poems for the cause; and Harriet Beecher Stowe dramatized the plight of the enslaved in *Uncle Tom's Cabin* (1852). A parallel achievement came from historians such as George Bancroft, John Lothrop Motley, and especially Francis Parkman, who combined scholarly method with dramatic narration in his accounts of the Anglo-French contest for the frontier and his own travels in *The Oregon Trail* (1849).

## Hawthorne, Melville, and Whitman

*Portrait of Walt Whitman*

History also shaped the fiction of Nathaniel Hawthorne, the leading New England fictionist of the period, whose masterpiece *The Scarlet Letter* (1850) and works such as *The House of the Seven Gables* (1851) and *The Marble Faun* (1860) probed deep psychological and ethical problems behind their colonial and foreign settings. His friend and neighbor Herman Melville, who said a whaling ship was his "Yale College and his Harvard," began with sea narratives such as *Typee* (1846) and *Omoo* (1847) before his reading in philosophy and in Hawthorne's symbolism transformed his aims.

That transformation produced *Mardi* (1849) and then the richly symbolic *Moby Dick* (1851), a complex but brilliantly integrated work, after which only stories such as "Benito Cereno," the novel *Pierre* (1852), and the late novella *Billy Budd* showed flashes of the same genius. Walt Whitman, an ardent singer of Manhattan and democratic life, broke with conventional verse in *Leaves of Grass*, whose free-flowing lines and frank celebration of the self and the body marked a wholly new direction for American poetry.

## Realism and Naturalism

![[attachments/american_literature_002.webp|420]]

*Portrait of Mark Twain*

After the Civil War a wave of local-colour fiction portrayed nearly every region of the country, including Bret Harte's California mining camps in *The Luck of Roaring Camp* (1870), Edward Eggleston's Indiana in *The Hoosier Schoolmaster* (1871), and the New England of Sarah Orne Jewett and Mary E. Wilkins Freeman, with George W. Cable, Joel Chandler Harris, and Mary Noailles Murfree mapping other corners. Touched by nostalgia and sentiment, these stories were transitional to realism because they treated common folk, dialect, and mores sympathetically.

Mark Twain (Samuel Langhorne Clemens) grew out of this tradition of comedians and local colorists, beginning with travel books such as *The Innocents Abroad* (1869) and *Roughing It* (1872) before his masterpieces of boyhood and the Mississippi. The leading theorist of realism was William Dean Howells, who in *A Modern Instance* (1882) and *The Rise of Silas Lapham* (1885), and after reading Tolstoy in later novels such as *A Hazard of New Fortunes* (1890), made his commonplace characters vehicles for commentary on society, government, and economics.

Naturalism pushed realism toward a harsher, more deterministic view. Hamlin Garland stressed the grim details of farm life in *Main-Travelled Roads* (1891); Theodore Dreiser, treating daring subjects in *Sister Carrie* (1900) and *An American Tragedy* (1925), showed characters driven by chemical compulsion and defeated by stronger rivals; and Stephen Crane in *Maggie* (1893) and *The Red Badge of Courage* (1895), with Frank Norris in *McTeague* (1899) and *The Octopus* (1901), made vivid impressionistic scenes of human beings overwhelmed by environment. Both Crane and Norris died young, their experiments foreshadowing the 20th-century novel.

## Henry James

In the fiction of Henry James, born in New York but later an expatriate in England, the novel took a different path. He agreed that fiction should reproduce reality but held that reality was "twice translated," first through the author's experience of it and then through his unique depiction, so that the artist's delicate task mattered as much as the subject. His essay *The Art of Fiction* (1884) and the prefaces to his collected works form an important body of discussion of fictional craft.

His doctrines found concrete form in novels such as *What Maisie Knew* (1897), *The Wings of the Dove* (1902), *The Ambassadors* (1903), and *The Golden Bowl* (1904). The earliest were "international" novels in which conflict grew from contrasts between Americans and Europeans, while in the later books James turned increasingly to the inner psychological processes of his characters and to a subtle rendering of their limited perceptions and emotions.

## Critics and Poets of the Gilded Age

A large body of literature of social revolt flourished between the Civil War and 1914, attacking the growing power of business and the corruption of government. Henry Adams's *Democracy* (1880) exposed political corruption, Edward Bellamy's *Looking Backward* (1888) imagined a collectivist utopia in the year 2000, and Upton Sinclair's *The Jungle* (1906) urged socialism, while poets such as Edwin Markham and William Vaughn Moody and the journalist "muckrakers" — including Ida M. Tarbell and Lincoln Steffens — pressed their criticisms in song and exposé.

The most literate of these attacks was *The Education of Henry Adams* (printed 1906; published 1918), in which Henry Adams, a scion of an ancient New England family and author of *Mont-Saint-Michel and Chartres* (1904), used a quiet, ironic style to record his baffled, lifelong hunt for order and faith. The later 19th century was otherwise a poor period for poetry, yet two figures endured: the Southern musician-poet Sidney Lanier, who used musical rhythms in songs such as "The Marshes of Glynn" (1878), and the reclusive New Englander Emily Dickinson, whose cryptic, slant-rhymed lyrics, first published in 1890 after her death, would deeply influence later poets.

## The New Poetry

Early 20th-century poetry ranged from traditional verse to radical experiment. Two New England poets won both critical and popular acclaim without much technical experiment: Edwin Arlington Robinson, who took three Pulitzer Prizes in the 1920s, and Robert Frost, who shaped common speech into traditional form in *A Boy's Will* (1913) and *North of Boston* (1914) while commenting on the tragic side of life. Modern poetry took form in little magazines, above all *Poetry: A Magazine of Verse*, founded by Harriet Monroe in Chicago in 1912.

The Midwest produced Vachel Lindsay, whose chanting odes suited oral performance; Carl Sandburg, who wrote of prairies and cities in Whitmanesque free verse; and Edgar Lee Masters, whose *Spoon River Anthology* (1915) gave bitter free-verse monologues to village dead. Edna St. Vincent Millay and Sara Teasdale wrote frank personal lyrics, Amy Lowell experimented with the image, and the black poets James Weldon Johnson, Langston Hughes, and Countee Cullen used older forms to confront racism, with Hughes's colloquial language proving especially lasting.

The most powerful new influences were two expatriates. Ezra Pound, who lived outside the United States after 1908, profoundly shaped 20th-century writing both as a poet of *The Cantos* (begun 1926) and as a patron of other writers, while T.S. Eliot, who became a British subject in 1927, transformed poetry with *The Waste Land* and related work. Opposed to Eliot's influence were romantic and visionary poets including Hart Crane, whose *The Bridge* (1930) aimed at a Whitmanesque epic, the lush Wallace Stevens of *Harmonium* (1923), and William Carlos Williams, who filled experimental lyrics with the mundane details of American life.

## Modern Fiction

The little magazines and the ferociously funny critic H.L. Mencken, who edited *The Smart Set* and *American Mercury*, helped launch a new fiction in revolt against gentility and small-town narrowness. Its most distinguished early voice was Sherwood Anderson, whose *Winesburg, Ohio* (1919) collected stories of villagers crippled by phobias and suppressions, and by 1920 a new school had arrived with F. Scott Fitzgerald's *This Side of Paradise* and Sinclair Lewis's *Main Street*.

Novels of the 1920s were often lyrical, personal, and steeped in the disillusionment of the postwar generation, while those of the 1930s leaned toward radical social criticism in response to the Great Depression. F. Scott Fitzgerald traced moral disintegration from *This Side of Paradise* (1920) to his spare masterpiece *The Great Gatsby* (1925), and a wave of proletarian and protest fiction followed, including John Dos Passos's *U.S.A.* trilogy, with its "camera eye" and "newsreel" devices, and Nathanael West's black-comic *The Day of the Locust* (1939).

Social protest also gave force to Richard Wright, whose *Native Son* (1940) and *Black Boy* (1945) confronted the plight of American blacks with Dostoyevskian intensity, and to Zora Neale Hurston, whose anthropological training shaped the feminist novel *Their Eyes Were Watching God* (1937). A more lyric, symbolic strain ran alongside the naturalists in the work of Willa Cather, from *My Ántonia* (1918) to *Death Comes for the Archbishop* (1927), and Katherine Anne Porter.

## Hemingway, Faulkner, and Steinbeck

Three writers showed a shift away from pure disillusionment. Ernest Hemingway captured the existential loss of the Lost Generation in *The Sun Also Rises* (1926) and *A Farewell to Arms* (1929), then moved toward collective hope in *For Whom the Bell Tolls* (1940) and recovered his form in *The Old Man and the Sea* (1952); his stripped-down prose, learned partly from Gertrude Stein, deeply marked later writers.

His great rival as stylist and mythmaker, William Faulkner, wrote in a baroque manner shaped by James Joyce, combining stream-of-consciousness technique with the rich social history of the mythical Yoknapatawpha County in *The Sound and the Fury* (1929), *As I Lay Dying* (1930), and *Light in August* (1932). His work, dominated by a sense of guilt reaching back to the Civil War, traced the decay of Southern families and, in later books such as *Go Down, Moses* (1942), the troubled role of race.

John Steinbeck drew on California outcasts in *Tortilla Flat* (1935) and *Of Mice and Men* (1937) and on the social struggles of Depression migrant workers in *In Dubious Battle* (1936) and his flawed masterpiece *The Grapes of Wrath* (1939), the almost biblical story of the Joad family's migration from the Oklahoma Dust Bowl to California and their discovery of the need for collective action among the poor.

## Literary Criticism

Looking back, many historians judged the first half of the 20th century especially notable for its literary criticism, which became a powerful influence on writing itself. The period opened with a battle between the New Humanists, such as Irving Babbitt and Paul Elmer More, who defended older moral values and won an echo in T.S. Eliot, and their pugnacious opponent H.L. Mencken, whose *Prejudices* (1919–27) demanded the unvarnished truth about life and helped free American literature from a moralistic framework.

In an age of social change, critics also weighed literature against society and politics. Van Wyck Brooks attacked American philistinism in *The Ordeal of Mark Twain* (1920) and later wove a cultural tapestry of American writers, while Vernon L. Parrington reread the tradition through Jeffersonian democracy in *Main Currents in American Thought* (1927–30), and growing Marxian influence shaped critics from Granville Hicks to the New York intellectuals around *Partisan Review*, including Lionel Trilling.

A balance of aesthetic and moral concern marked critics such as Edmund Wilson and Kenneth Burke, all influenced by Eliot's close attention to literary language. That influence culminated in the New Criticism, whose practitioners — many of them conservative poet-critics such as John Crowe Ransom, Yvor Winters, and Cleanth Brooks, author of *The Well Wrought Urn* (1947) — rewrote literary history and, despite later charges of formalism, greatly advanced the close understanding of poetry.

## After World War II

The critic Malcolm Cowley called the years between the wars a "second flowering" of American writing, and major figures kept publishing after 1945: Faulkner, Hemingway, and Steinbeck wrote on, important poetry appeared, and Eugene O'Neill's finest play, *Long Day's Journey into Night*, was staged posthumously in 1956. A gradual changing of the guard brought a new generation of different ethnic, regional, and social character, including children of immigrants, African Americans, and, with the rise of feminism, women in a new voice.

Two groups of novelists responded to the technological horror of the war, the realists such as Norman Mailer of *The Naked and the Dead* (1948) and, increasingly, the writers of black humor and absurdist fable, including Kurt Vonnegut, Jr., whose *Slaughterhouse-Five* (1969) described the firebombing of Dresden. Drawing on Vladimir Nabokov and Jorge Luis Borges, a self-conscious metafiction emerged in John Barth, Donald Barthelme, and Thomas Pynchon, whose conspiratorial novels such as *Gravity's Rainbow* (1973) used paranoia and entropy as structuring ideas.

A strong line of writers kept faith with Social Realism on more personal terms, above all Saul Bellow, whose *The Adventures of Augie March* (1953) and *Herzog* (1964) proclaimed the necessity of being human, and fellow Jewish writers Bernard Malamud, Philip Roth, and the Yiddish storyteller Isaac Bashevis Singer, who won the Nobel Prize in 1978. Southern fiction inherited Faulkner's legacy through Eudora Welty, Flannery O'Connor, and Carson McCullers, while African American writing moved beyond protest in James Baldwin's *Go Tell It on the Mountain* (1953), Ralph Ellison's *Invisible Man* (1952), and the fiction of Toni Morrison, awarded the Nobel Prize in 1993 for novels such as *Beloved* (1987).

## Postwar Poetry and Drama

Postwar poetry was abundant but had no single dominant figure. The formal Modernism of the late 1940s, led by Robert Lowell in *Lord Weary's Castle* (1946) and including Theodore Roethke and Richard Wilbur, gave way after the mid-1950s to open forms and colloquial styles. The Black Mountain poets such as Charles Olson treated the poem as process, and Allen Ginsberg's *Howl* (1956) energized the Beat movement, while Lowell's own *Life Studies* (1959) inspired the confessional intensity of Anne Sexton, Sylvia Plath, and John Berryman, and Robert Bly's circle pursued the surreal "deep image."

In drama, two playwrights rose to reputations comparable to O'Neill's. Arthur Miller defended a modern, democratic concept of tragedy in *Death of a Salesman* (1949) and used the Salem witch trials to attack McCarthyism in *The Crucible* (1953), while Tennessee Williams brought passionate lyricism and a tragic Southern vision to *A Streetcar Named Desire* (1947) and *Cat on a Hot Tin Roof* (1955). In 1962 Edward Albee secured his reputation with the absurdist power of *Who's Afraid of Virginia Woolf?*, and later decades brought Jewish family drama, the work of women playwrights such as Paula Vogel, and the surreal pageants of Suzan-Lori Parks.

## Multicultural and Contemporary Writing

The loosening of immigration restrictions in the mid-1960s set the stage for a rich multicultural literature. New Jewish voices included E.L. Doctorow, who mingled history and fiction in *Ragtime* (1975), and Cynthia Ozick; Native American identity was explored by N. Scott Momaday's Pulitzer-winning *House Made of Dawn* (1969) and Louise Erdrich's *Love Medicine* (1984); and Mexican American experience appeared in Sandra Cisneros's *The House on Mango Street* (1983).

Assimilated immigrant writers brought a double cultural understanding, among them the Cuban American Oscar Hijuelos, the Antigua-born Jamaica Kincaid, and the Dominican-born Junot Díaz, whose *The Brief Wondrous Life of Oscar Wao* (2007) won a Pulitzer Prize. Chinese American writing found a voice in Maxine Hong Kingston's *The Woman Warrior* (1976), and Asian American fiction continued through Gish Jen, Chang-rae Lee, and Ha Jin, whose *Waiting* (1999) won the National Book Award.

The 1990s saw rich autobiography, such as Frank McCourt's *Angela's Ashes* (1996), alongside huge, ambitious novels including David Foster Wallace's *Infinite Jest* (1996), Jonathan Franzen's *The Corrections* (2001), and Don DeLillo's Cold War epic *Underworld* (1997), works that mark a convergence of Social Realism and Pynchonesque invention. Thomas Pynchon himself returned with the sprawling historical novel *Mason & Dixon* (1997).

## Theory

In academic criticism, difficult theorists displaced close readers after the 1960s. European poststructuralists such as Michel Foucault, Roland Barthes, and Jacques Derrida found a welcome in a skeptical American climate, and the Yale collection *Deconstruction and Criticism* (1979) made Paul de Man and J. Hillis Miller leading exponents of deconstruction.

Other critics resisted or redirected this turn. Harold Bloom built a Freudian theory of literary influence in *The Anxiety of Influence* (1973) and later defended the Western tradition in *The Western Canon* (1994), while philosophers such as Richard Rorty found a native parallel in Emerson and the pragmatists. A more political historicism returned through Stephen Greenblatt's work on Shakespeare and Edward Said's *Orientalism* (1978), which directed attention to the effects of colonialism on the arts and society.

## Key Events & Developments

- 1608–1624: John Smith's promotional histories of Virginia and New England mark the start of American writing.
- 1817: Bryant's "Thanatopsis" helps open a great half century of native literature.
- 1829: Andrew Jackson's inauguration spreads the democratic spirit behind the American Renaissance.
- 1850–1855: Hawthorne's *The Scarlet Letter*, Melville's *Moby Dick*, and Whitman's *Leaves of Grass* appear in close succession.
- 1865–1914: Realism, naturalism, and a "literature of social revolt" follow the Civil War.
- 1912: Harriet Monroe founds *Poetry* magazine, a seedbed of modern verse.
- 1920s–1930s: A second flowering brings Fitzgerald, Hemingway, Faulkner, and Depression-era protest fiction.
- After 1945: Metafiction, multicultural writing, confessional and Beat poetry, and literary theory reshape the field.

## Key People

- Walt Whitman ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walt_Whitman)): Pioneer of free verse and the democratic American self in *Leaves of Grass*.

## Interesting Facts

- Herman Melville called a whaling ship his "Yale College and his Harvard," reflecting his lack of formal schooling.
- Emily Dickinson published almost nothing in her lifetime; her first collection appeared in 1890, four years after her death.
- Edward Bellamy's utopia *Looking Backward* (1888) imagined a collectivist society achieved by the year 2000.
- Modern American drama ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drama)) and poetry both grew out of "little" theatres and "little" magazines rather than established institutions.
- Henry Adams had *The Education of Henry Adams* printed privately in 1906; it was published for a wide audience only in 1918.
