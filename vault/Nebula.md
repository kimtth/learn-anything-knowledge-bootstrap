## Introduction

Nebulae ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nebula)) are tenuous clouds of gas and dust in interstellar space. The term once meant any diffuse-looking object beyond the solar system, which mixed two unrelated categories: galaxies, formerly called extragalactic nebulae, and true galactic nebulae made of interstellar gas and dust within a galaxy. Modern usage generally reserves nebula for the interstellar medium.

In a spiral galaxy ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spiral_galaxy)), the interstellar medium is only about 3 to 5 percent of total mass, but in spiral arms it may reach about 20 percent. About 1 percent of that medium is dust, which absorbs and scatters radiation. Gas is extremely clumpy across scales from the whole Milky Way to the Earth-Sun distance. Dense molecular clouds can be as cold as 10 K and contain about 1,000 hydrogen molecules per cubic centimetre, while hot gas between clouds may reach 10 million K at only 0.001 ion per cubic centimetre.

The Cat's Eye Nebula ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cat%27s_Eye_Nebula)) represents one of the visually striking objects once grouped under the broad nebula category. Such objects helped astronomers distinguish types of diffuse celestial bodies as telescopes, photography, spectroscopy, and later radio and millimetre observations improved. The modern classification of nebulae depends on physical composition, emission, absorption, temperature, density, and relation to stars.

## Historical Observation

Early observers discovered bright nebulae while searching for comets. Nicolas-Claude Fabri de Peiresc recorded the Orion Nebula, and in 1656 Christiaan Huygens described its bright inner region and showed that its inner star was a compact quadruple system. Charles Messier compiled his famous 1781 catalog of 103 extended objects to prevent confusion with comets; many Messier objects are clusters or galaxies, but some are true nebulae such as the Trifid Nebula, M20.

## The Development of Nebular Catalogs

The great nebular catalogs expanded during the 19th century. William Herschel, aided by Caroline Herschel, compiled about 2,500 clusters, nebulae, and galaxies from 1786 to 1802. John Herschel added southern and northern objects, and J.L. Dreyer's New General Catalogue of 1888 listed 7,840 nebulae, galaxies, and clusters. Later Index Catalogues added 5,386 objects. Many bright galaxies and nebulae still carry NGC or IC numbers.

## Turbulence

All types of nebulae show turbulence, chaotic gas motion that transfers kinetic and magnetic energy from large scales to small scales, where it becomes heat. Turbulence affects nebular pressure support and energy balance, though its full physics remains difficult. It is observed through broadened emission or absorption lines in spectra. Thermal motion broadens lines according to temperature and atomic mass, but observed line widths require additional bulk chaotic motion shared by atoms of different masses.

The energy that maintains nebular turbulence must be continually supplied because turbulent cascades would otherwise dissipate into heat. Strong stellar winds, supernova explosions, expanding ionized regions, magnetic fields, and large-scale interstellar flows can inject motion into nebular gas. Spectral line shifts also reveal whether gas is moving toward or away from the observer through the Doppler effect.

## Key Events & Developments

- 1656: Christiaan Huygens described the bright inner region of the Orion Nebula.
- 1781: Charles Messier published a catalog of 103 nebulous objects.
- 1786-1802: William Herschel and Caroline Herschel compiled major nebular catalogs.
- 1888: J.L. Dreyer published the New General Catalogue.
- 1895 and 1908: Index Catalogues supplemented the NGC.
