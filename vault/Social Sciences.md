# Social Sciences

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> The social sciences study human behaviour, institutions, cultures, economies, and political life through disciplines such as anthropology, sociology, psychology, economics, and political science. The source traces their formation from classical and Enlightenment thought through democratic and industrial transformations that made society a distinct object of empirical inquiry and reform.

## Introduction

![[attachments/social_sciences_001.webp|420]]

*Portrait of Adam Smith*

Social sciences ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_science)) are disciplines that study human behaviour in its social and cultural forms. They include anthropology, sociology, social psychology, political science, and economics. Social and economic geography and parts of education are often included, while history stands near the boundary between the humanities and social sciences.

Since the 1950s, the term behavioral sciences ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Behavioural_sciences)) has also been used for these fields. Supporters of that term wanted to connect social inquiry more closely with sciences that study human behaviour, such as physical anthropology and physiological psychology. In practice, the two terms can be treated as overlapping.

As separate disciplines, the social sciences belong mainly to the 19th century. Their deeper roots, however, reach back to Greek and Roman thought about human nature, morality, the state, and social order. The recovery of classical rationalism in the Renaissance and the Age of Reason helped prepare the intellectual conditions for later scientific study of society.

## Classical, Medieval, and Enlightenment Backgrounds

The Greeks supplied the rationalist habit of asking general questions about human beings and society. Their influence returned strongly in the Renaissance, when thinkers commented on Plato, Aristotle, and other classical authors. This classical inheritance encouraged reflection on politics, ethics, law, and community, but it could also delay empirical study because ancient authority remained powerful.

Another major influence was [[Descartes and Cartesianism (Cartesianism)|Cartesianism]]. The method of Rene Descartes encouraged deductive, geometrical reasoning about human beings and society. For a long time, many social philosophers preferred rational construction over concrete historical or empirical investigation.

The Enlightenment ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Age_of_Enlightenment)) added reforming energy. Many writers wanted not only to understand society but to improve it by reason, criticism, education, and sometimes revolution. Humanitarianism, literacy, rising living standards, and the belief that poverty and oppression were not inevitable all helped link social inquiry with reform.

Enlightenment thought also developed key ideas of [[Social Structure and Change (Social Structure)|social structure]] and developmental change. Thinkers such as Condorcet, Rousseau, and Adam Smith helped imagine society as an ordered system whose institutions and stages could be studied. These ideas later fed the 19th-century theory of social evolution.

## Democratic and Industrial Revolutions

The modern social sciences were shaped by the French Revolution and the Industrial Revolution. Together they weakened old orders based on kinship, land, social class, religion, local community, monarchy, and inherited authority. They released new conflicts around status, wealth, class, citizenship, and political power.

The vocabulary of the new age shows the change. Words such as industry, democracy, class, ideology, rationalism, humanitarian, masses, proletariat, bureaucracy, capitalism, liberal, conservative, and crisis became central to 19th-century thought. Social science grew from efforts to understand these new realities.

One theme was the condition of labour. Writers saw masses of people removed from village, guild, parish, and family into factories and cities. Economics became known as the dismal science because thinkers from David Ricardo to Karl Marx doubted that capitalism would easily improve labouring life.

Another theme was the transformation of property. Wealth became less tied to visible land and more tied to factories, finance, speculation, and industrial capital. This made possible new concentrations of wealth and new forms of economic power over politics and culture.

Urbanization also became central. Earlier writers often saw the city as civilization and refinement, but 19th-century social thinkers stressed anonymity, broken families, alienation, mass life, and disrupted values. Sociology especially studied the contrast between rural community and urban, individualistic society.

Technology and the factory system raised moral and psychological concerns. Thinkers as different as Thomas Carlyle, Karl Marx, and Alexis de Tocqueville worried that mechanization and specialized labour could dehumanize workers. Factory work, long hours, and separation from home became major subjects of social thought and reform.

The expansion of political masses was equally important. Wider voting rights made larger populations aware of themselves as participants in politics. Tocqueville's Democracy in America treated democracy not only as an American topic but as a global historical force, with both liberating promise and danger for individual freedom.

## Ideologies and Science of Society

![[attachments/social_sciences_002.webp|420]]

*Portrait of Auguste Comte*

The reactions to democratic and industrial change produced liberal, conservative, and radical ideologies. Liberals generally welcomed democracy, capitalism, industry, and individualism. Conservatives, from Edmund Burke through later writers, valued tradition, authority, and social order threatened by revolution and industrialism.

Radicals accepted democracy but opposed capitalism. They sought deeper social transformation, often through people's sovereignty, social ownership, or planning. These ideological divisions affected almost every social scientist of the 19th century.

At the same time, many thinkers believed that a true science of society was possible. Auguste Comte coined the word sociology and imagined it as the highest science, doing for social humanity what biology did for living organisms. Humanitarianism and science reinforced each other because knowledge of society seemed a path to reform.

[[Theory of Evolution (Evolution)|Evolution]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Evolution)) also became a guiding idea. Social scientists tried to explain societies through stages, development, and progress. This evolutionary language influenced sociology, anthropology, economics, political science, law, and history, even when later scholars rejected simple unilinear schemes.

## Economics

[[Economics]] was the first social science to gain the status of a distinct discipline. The Physiocrats and [[Adam Smith]] argued that wealth, prices, rents, interest, and wages had their own mechanisms. In the 19th century this supported political economy and the doctrine of laissez-faire.

Classical economists such as David Ricardo, [[John Stuart Mill]], Nassau William Senior, Frederic Bastiat, Jean-Baptiste Say, and later Carl Menger's Austrian school treated economic processes as partly self-regulating. They often argued that government and society should avoid unnecessary interference.

Other economists challenged this view. German historical economists such as Wilhelm Roscher and Karl Knies rejected timeless universal laws and emphasized the developmental character of capitalism. Socialists also rejected laissez-faire and argued for social ownership, planning, and the overcoming of competition and conflict.

## Political Science

Political science developed from long-standing inquiry into the state, represented by thinkers such as Machiavelli, Hobbes, Locke, and Rousseau. In the 19th century, democratic revolution made sovereignty the discipline's central issue. Political scientists studied who held power and by what right.

Many writers treated the sovereign state as a timeless and universal reality, much as classical economists treated capitalism. Others dissented historically. Sir Henry Maine, Numa Fustel de Coulanges, and Otto von Gierke argued that state and sovereignty developed historically rather than emerging simply from a universal social contract.

## Sociology

![[attachments/social_sciences_003.webp|420]]

*Portrait of Max Weber*

Sociology arose as an attempt to study society as a whole. Auguste Comte and Herbert Spencer imagined one comprehensive science of society, concerned with civilization, institutions, order, and evolutionary change. They divided sociology into statics, the study of order, and dynamics, the study of change.

Not all sociologists followed this grand evolutionary model. Other approaches studied local communities, urban life, statistics, social bonds, and the contrast between traditional and modern forms of association. Figures such as Emile Durkheim, Ferdinand Tonnies, Georg Simmel, Max Weber, Adolphe Quetelet, Charles H. Cooley, and Robert E. Park helped define the field's range.

Sociology became especially concerned with the social bond. Themes such as alienation, anomie, identity crisis, estrangement, loss of community, and the search for community connected sociology with philosophy and literature. The individual separated from inherited norms became a central problem of modern social inquiry.

## Twentieth-Century Influences

[[Marx and Marxism (Marxism)|Marxism]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marxism)) deeply influenced 20th-century social science. For many people, Marx's ideas as transmitted by Lenin had moral and almost religious force. Even where Communism had little direct political power, Marxism shaped work on class, capitalism, ideology, exploitation, and historical conflict.

Freudian and existential themes also mattered. The concern with the lonely, anxious, or divided individual linked social science with Soren Kierkegaard's stress on subjective existence and with later psychology. Ideas of alienation and identity became important in sociology, social psychology, and political science.

## Developmentalism

Developmentalism continued the older interest in social evolution but changed form. Early 20th-century scholars criticized the 19th-century idea that all societies move through one universal sequence of stages. Anthropology especially showed that actual cultural change did not fit simple unilinear models.

After the late 1940s and 1950s, developmental ideas returned through studies of new nations, economic growth, and political and social change. These studies compared traditional and modern ways and examined how Western impact changed other parts of the world. They still used ideas of stages and cumulative change, but with more attention to concrete mechanisms.

## Interactionism

Interactionism ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interactionism)) became another major 20th-century concept. Social interaction means that relations between people or groups are reciprocal, symbolic, and mediated by meanings. The presence of another person changes how one defines the outside world and the self.

From this view, identity develops through interaction beginning in infancy. The first sense of another person, such as the mother, helps form the child's sense of self. Socialization continues through later contacts, changing roles, behaviour, expectations, and the meanings people attach to the world.

## Future of the Social Sciences

The future of the social sciences depends partly on their relation to organized society, especially government, industry, and other centres of authority. Funding for research creates opportunities but also raises questions about scientific independence and objectivity.

The social sciences have always been close to public concerns: reform, administration, education, economy, war, welfare, and development. Their challenge is to study society with disciplined methods while remaining aware that their subjects, sponsors, and consequences are themselves part of social life.

## Key Events & Developments

- Ancient Greece and Rome: Rational inquiry into humans, state, and morality supplied deep roots for social thought.
- 17th-18th centuries: The Age of Reason and Enlightenment connected social inquiry with reason, reform, and humanitarianism.
- Late 18th and early 19th centuries: Democratic and industrial revolutions created the main problems that modern social sciences studied.
- 19th century: Economics, political science, sociology, anthropology, and related fields became separate disciplines.
- 1950s: The term behavioral sciences became widely used for many social-science fields.
- Late 1940s-1950s: Development studies revived broad interest in growth and modernization.

- Charles H. Cooley ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_Horton_Cooley)) and Robert E. Park ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_E._Park)): Important American sociologists of interaction and urban life.
- Soren Kierkegaard ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/S%C3%B8ren_Kierkegaard)): Influenced later concern with subjective life, anxiety, and the isolated individual.

## Interesting Facts

- History is treated as marginal between the humanities and social sciences because its methods and insights belong to both.
- [[Economics]] became a separate social science before the others.
- Urbanization changed the city from a symbol of civilization into a major problem of alienation and disrupted values.
- Interactionism ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interactionism)) sees the self as something formed through relationships, not as an isolated starting point.
- Funding can strengthen social research while also raising questions about independence.
