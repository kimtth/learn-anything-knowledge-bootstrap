# Political Parties and Interest Groups (Political Party)

## Introduction

![[attachments/political_party_001.webp|420]]

*Political party convention logo*

A political party ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Political_party)) is a group organized to acquire and exercise political power. Modern parties developed in the 19th century in Europe and the United States alongside electoral and parliamentary systems. The word party later came to cover all organized groups seeking power, whether through elections, revolution, or control of a state.

Before modern parties, political competition often took place among cliques and factions around nobles, princes, wealthy merchants, bankers, industrialists, or other influential people. Early parliamentary regimes did not immediately end this narrow politics. They often replaced aristocratic factions with elite groups tied to new social forces.

During the 19th century, parties based on mass support began to grow in Europe and America. In the 20th century, political parties spread across the world. In developing countries, some large parties rested partly on ethnic, tribal, or religious ties, and some were partly military as well as political.

Parties are not automatically democratic. Political parties ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Political_party)) first grew inside liberal democracy, but dictatorships also used them for undemocratic purposes. Socialist and communist parties in Europe could operate in multiparty regimes or serve as the sole party in a dictatorship.

## Origins and Early Party Forms

![[attachments/political_party_002.webp|420]]

*Ballot election printing*

Early parties grew from older social divisions. Cadre parties were built around notables, local elites, and parliamentary leaders rather than mass membership. Conservative parties often reflected aristocratic interests, while liberal parties expressed the political and economic interests of the bourgeoisie.

Liberal ideology developed earlier than modern mass parties. It drew on ideas associated with John Locke and later French thinkers, especially formal equality before law and opposition to aristocratic privilege. Conservative ideology was often tied to hierarchy, religion, and social order, especially in Roman Catholic countries where clerical parties appeared.

European conservative and liberal parties of the 19th century were usually focused on elections and parliament. They did not normally organize violence directly. Their local units supported candidates with money, influence, and personal networks, while leaders used the army or police only once in office.

## American Party Machines

The early [[United States of America (United States)|United States]] party structure also began with local notables and weak national coordination. State organizations became more effective than national ones, but after the Civil War some local party organizations became machines that controlled cities, counties, or states.

A party machine ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Political_machine)) could dominate elections by controlling public offices, jobs, licenses, police protection, courts, and local finances. The boss and precinct captains secured votes by offering rewards and services. This made the machine a development of cadre politics, but with stronger local discipline and material incentives.

Such party machines showed how decentralized parties could become powerful without a rigid national ideology. They also revealed a recurring problem in party history: organization can connect voters to government, but it can also concentrate power in the hands of local leaders who distribute benefits for loyalty.

## Labour and Mass-Based Parties

The early British Labour Party formed an intermediate type between cadre and mass-based parties. It was supported by trade unions and left-wing intellectuals. At first, individuals usually joined through affiliated bodies rather than directly, so the party represented organized working-class interests through unions and local labour committees.

After 1918, Labour allowed direct membership, though affiliated membership remained very important for much of the 20th century. The party's union link later became limited; at the 1987 annual conference, union delegates were capped at 50 percent. This history shows how a party could combine parliamentary politics, class organization, and affiliated social movements.

Mass-based parties differed from cadre parties because they tried to organize large numbers of ordinary followers, not just influential supporters. Their goal was not only to win elections but also to educate, mobilize, and discipline members. Socialist parties were major examples, and some nonsocialist parties later copied their methods.

Large mass parties required hierarchy and administration. This created oligarchical tendencies, because leaders and officials who managed the apparatus gained continuing influence. Socialist parties tried to answer this by electing officers and delegates at local, regional, and national levels.

## Communist and Fascist Party Models

![[attachments/political_party_003.webp|420]]

*Communist party congress poster*

Communist parties first came from socialist parties, but after 1924 the Comintern pushed them toward the Soviet model. They became mass parties limited to people committed to Marxism-Leninism, and they organized workplace cells rather than only local territorial branches.

For communist parties, ideology was central. The party aimed to indoctrinate members, discipline action, and guide the state. In single-party communist systems, the party hierarchy often held real power over the formal state hierarchy, and the first secretary became the most important political figure.

Fascist parties also sought mass mobilization, but their teaching was authoritarian and elitist. They claimed society should be led by the most capable elite under a supreme leader. Their organization resembled a military pyramid, with uniforms, ranks, orders, salutes, marches, and obedience.

Fascist party structure was linked to the idea that organized minorities could seize power by force. Fascist parties appeared in many western European countries between the world wars, but only in Italy and Germany did they come to power. Even there, their role differed: the German Nazi Party had far greater influence than the weaker fascist parties in Italy, Spain, or Portugal.

## Legal and Revolutionary Methods

Political parties may seek power legally or through revolution. Some parties combine election work with underground activity, especially when regimes restrict political competition. In the 19th century, liberal parties sometimes used conspiratorial methods in Italy, Austria, Germany, Poland, and Russia, while in Britain and France they often worked through the ballot box.

Revolutionary methods vary. Clandestine plots may seize centres of power in monarchies or dictatorships. Terrorist or disruptive action may try to mobilize citizens and expose government weakness. General strikes, rural guerrilla war, and urban guerrilla action have also been used, though modern police and military techniques made some forms harder.

Most parties, however, compete legally through elections. Their main tasks are propaganda, candidate selection, and campaign finance. A party label helps voters identify a candidate's general position. This label may matter more than individual promises because it places candidates inside a recognizable political family.

## Candidate Selection and Campaign Finance

Parties also select candidates. In some systems, committees and party leaders control nomination. In others, members vote through party procedures, and in the United States primaries partly limit the power of party committees. Candidate selection is one of the main places where party organization controls access to public office.

Campaign finance also separates party types. Cadre parties often rely on wealthy individuals and corporations. Mass-based parties try to collect smaller regular payments from many members. Some countries regulate campaign spending or provide public funds, though laws may be difficult to enforce.

State aid to parties began with campaign expenses and sometimes developed into support for general party finances. France, Sweden, and Finland show forms of public financial participation. Money therefore affects not only campaigns but also the kind of organization a party can maintain.

## Participation in Power and Opposition

Once a party wins office, its control over elected officials varies. In flexible parties, legislators retain much personal freedom in debates, government participation, and votes. In rigid parties, discipline is strong and members are expected to vote together.

The [[United States of America (United States)|United States]] shows a flexible pattern, while the [[United Kingdom]] depends on rigid parties because a government can fall if it loses a parliamentary majority. This makes discipline crucial for party unity, cabinet survival, and opposition strategy.

Opposition parties also work differently in flexible and rigid systems. In a rigid party system, the opposition can offer a clear alternative government, as in Britain's shadow cabinet. In flexible systems, weak discipline makes it harder for opposition parties to counterbalance the governing side in a unified way.

Parties therefore provide a communication channel between government, opposition, legislators, and the public. Even when they are imperfect, they organize both support and criticism. They help translate political disagreement into forms that can be debated, voted on, and administered.

## Oligarchy and Representation

Democratic parties contain a tension. Parties represent citizens, but they also create leadership circles that tend to preserve their own power. In cadre parties, powerful committees and influential leaders may manipulate members. In mass parties, elected leaders can still use the apparatus to stay in office.

This is the problem of party oligarchy. Democratic systems may rest on competition among rival political elites rather than direct rule by all citizens. Yet these elites remain more open than hereditary aristocracies because ambitious people can enter party politics and compete for leadership.

This party hierarchy is treated as a necessary evil. Modern democracy in large societies cannot easily function without parties, but parties always risk becoming large, slow, disciplined, and self-protecting organizations. Representation and organization therefore remain linked to hierarchy.

## Party Systems

Party systems are usually divided into two-party, multiparty, and single-party systems. The distinction is not only numerical. Two-party and multiparty systems organize political conflict in pluralistic societies. Single-party systems usually operate where genuine conflict with the party line is not tolerated.

The number of parties is shaped by history, social divisions, and electoral rules. Proportional representation tends to encourage multiparty systems because small parties can win seats. First-past-the-post voting tends to produce two-party systems because small parties may win many votes but few constituencies. Two-round systems often produce multiparty systems moderated by alliances.

Multiparty systems often require coalitions. If parties are flexible and undisciplined, coalitions may be weak and unstable. If parties are rigid and disciplined, two durable alliances can form inside a multiparty framework, producing a bipolarized system similar in some ways to a two-party system.

## The American and British Two-Party Systems

The [[United States of America (United States)|United States]] has long had a two-party system, from Federalists and Anti-Federalists to Republicans and Democrats. Third-party movements appeared often but failed to replace the two major parties. Presidential elections helped reinforce this structure by requiring broad national organizations and simplified voter choices.

American parties are flexible and decentralized. They are less tied than European parties to fixed class or ideological movements. They can be understood as two varieties of a broad liberal party, each containing opinions from right to left, though party voting became more partisan from about the first decade of the 21st century.

The British two-party system works differently. Britain moved from Conservative-Liberal competition before 1914 to Conservative-Labour competition after 1935, with an intermediate phase from 1920 to 1935. Its parties are rigid in parliamentary voting, so government and opposition are clearer and more disciplined.

This two-party system works only when both major parties accept democratic rules. If a fascist and communist party faced each other without shared democratic commitments, the winner might suppress the opponent. The British system is stable, but it can also frustrate innovation by forcing change through established party structures.

## Single-Party Systems

Single-party systems have taken communist, fascist, and developing-country forms. In the communist model, the party claims to lead the working class and allied groups during the transition from capitalism to socialism. The party is meant to guide the state and society, not merely compete for office.

Late in the 20th century, the communist model changed as power shifted toward elected state institutions in some countries. Reformers criticized party domination of government and the economy. Constitutional changes and the fall of communism in the former Soviet Union and eastern Europe opened the way to competitive parties.

The fascist single-party model was different. Fascist parties often had a policing or military role, but they did not always dominate the state. In Germany, Adolf Hitler's dictatorship depended heavily on the SS and Gestapo as well as the Nazi Party. In Italy, Spain, and Portugal, the party was often less central than the dictator, army, or state apparatus.

Developing-country single parties were diverse. Some were nationalist, some claimed socialism, and some rested on personal loyalty, tribal ties, or weak organization. Leaders such as Kemal Ataturk and Gamal Abdel Nasser tried to use parties to support modernizing regimes, though the party often remained secondary to army, leader, or state.

## Future of Political Parties

Claims that political parties are in decline have a long history and often reflect hostility to parties, but there are real causes of frustration. Large parties can make members feel powerless, and established organizations are difficult to reform.

Creating new parties strong enough to matter is also very hard. This produces impatience with party systems, especially when older parties seem burdened by entrenched interests and routines. Yet modern democracy in a large industrial society is hard to imagine without parties.

Political parties ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Political_party)) remain both inside and outside democracy. They organize competition for power, channel representation, discipline governments, structure opposition, and give voters recognizable choices. Their weaknesses are serious, but their disappearance would leave modern representative government without its main instrument.

## Key Events & Developments

- the 19th century: Modern political parties developed in Europe and the United States with parliamentary and electoral systems.
- 1918: The British Labour Party adopted direct membership while keeping strong affiliated union links.
- 1924: The Comintern pushed communist parties toward the Soviet model of disciplined mass organization.
- 1935: Britain entered the Conservative-Labour two-party alignment that replaced the older Conservative-Liberal competition.
- the late 20th century: Communist systems changed as party monopoly weakened and multiparty competition emerged in eastern Europe and the former Soviet Union.

## Interesting Facts

- Party machines could control not only elections but also jobs, courts, police protection, and local finances.
- Proportional representation usually helps small parties, while first-past-the-post voting tends to favour two major parties.
- Rigid parties can make parliamentary government stable, but they can also reduce individual freedom for legislators.
- Single-party systems can preserve a dictatorship beyond the life of one dominant leader.
- [[Democracy]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Democracy)) and political parties are almost inseparable in modern large societies.
