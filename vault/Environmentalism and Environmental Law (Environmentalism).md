# Environmentalism and Environmental Law (Environmentalism)

## Introduction

Environmentalism ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Environmentalism)) is a political and ethical movement that seeks to protect the natural environment by changing harmful human activities, reorganizing social and economic life, and reconsidering humanity's relationship with nature. It argues that nonhuman living things, and often the natural environment as a whole, deserve moral attention when people make political, economic, and social decisions.

The movement is not only about pollution control or resource management. Environmental thought asks whether nature matters only because humans benefit from it, or whether animals, species, ecosystems, and the biosphere have value beyond human use. This moral question shapes disagreements about law, government power, economic planning, technology, population, energy, and conservation.

The anthropocentric, or human-centred, tradition judges environmental harm mainly by its effects on human health, recreation, welfare, and quality of life. In this view, duties to protect the environment come indirectly from duties to present and future human beings. Nonhuman creatures and species tend to have instrumental value because they serve human interests.

The biocentric, or life-centred, tradition gives moral importance to living things and natural systems more directly. Similar contrasts appear in the terms shallow ecology and deep ecology, or technocentrism and ecocentrism. Critics of anthropocentrism argue that it treats nature too mechanically and can become a form of human chauvinism.

## Apocalyptic Environmentalism

From the 1960s onward, apocalyptic or survivalist environmentalism warned that industrial civilization was approaching ecological limits. Books such as Rachel Carson's Silent Spring ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silent_Spring)) in 1962, Paul Ehrlich's The Population Bomb in 1968, Donella H. Meadows' The Limits to Growth ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Limits_to_Growth)) in 1972, and Edward Goldsmith's Blueprint for Survival ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A_Blueprint_for_Survival)) in 1972 argued that pollution, population growth, and resource use threatened planetary stability.

This survivalist literature sometimes led to calls for stronger central control over environmentally damaging activities. Robert Heilbroner's An Inquiry into the Human Prospect in 1974 expressed the hard version of this view, arguing that human survival might require limiting human freedom. Critics such as Julian Simon and Herman Kahn later answered that humans could invent substitutes for scarce resources and adapt through ingenuity.

## Emancipatory Environmentalism

Beginning in the 1970s, many environmentalists looked for practical alternatives to both ecological pessimism and coercive survivalist politics. Emancipatory environmentalism promoted recycling, alternative energy, decentralized planning, democratic economic decisions, and changes in major sectors such as agriculture and energy.

One form, human-welfare ecology, aimed to improve human life through a clean and safe environment and linked environmental protection with distributive justice. This approach reflected a postmaterialist shift in advanced industrial societies, where many citizens began to place greater value on quality of life than on traditional measures of economic growth alone.

Some emancipatory environmentalists emphasized small-scale production systems that fit more closely with surrounding ecosystems. Barry Commoner and Ernst Friedrich Schumacher argued for productive processes that worked with nature rather than against it, using organic and renewable resources instead of synthetic products such as plastics and chemical fertilizers. They also supported renewable energy, public transportation, and energy efficiency.

## Ecofeminism

Ecofeminism ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ecofeminism)) connects the domination of nature with the oppression of women. Ecofeminists argue that political theories and social practices have often treated both women and nature as objects to be owned, used, or controlled.

Ecofeminism seeks an environmentally sound and socially just society in which women have a central role. Cultural ecofeminists tend to see women's relationship with nature as rooted in reproductive and nurturing roles. Social ecofeminists accept that the relationship is immediate and important, but explain it mainly through social hierarchies that confine women to the private sphere.

## History of the Environmental Movement

Concern about environmental harm is ancient, but early concern did not usually become organized public activism. Air and water pollution troubled communities at least in Roman times, epidemic disease in Europe between the late 14th century and mid-16th century was associated with pollution, and soil conservation was practiced in China, India, and Peru about 2,000 years ago.

The modern environmental movement arose mainly in the late 19th century. In Europe it focused on protecting the countryside, while in the United States it focused strongly on wilderness preservation and on the health effects of pollution during the Industrial Revolution. Early environmentalists often rejected the liberal belief that free markets could solve all social problems, arguing instead that government had to protect environmental goods.

## 20th Century Activism

During the 20th century, environmentalism expanded from local conservation and pollution concerns into a global movement. Public fear of pesticides, industrial contamination, population pressure, nuclear risk, urban pollution, and resource depletion made environmental protection a major political issue.

The movement also diversified intellectually and organizationally. Green politics, deep ecology, social ecology, ecofeminism, human-welfare ecology, and conservation campaigns offered different answers to the same central problem: how modern societies can live within ecological limits without abandoning justice, freedom, and human well-being.

## Core Equations

Applied biological and environmental systems use yield, growth, and resource-balance relationships:

$$Y=\frac{\text{harvested output}}{\text{area}}$$

This ratio turns the preceding idea into a measurable comparison, with the numerator as the quantity of interest and the denominator as the scale used for comparison.

$$\frac{dN}{dt}=rN\left(1-\frac{N}{K}\right)$$

The logistic-growth equation models population growth that slows as population size N approaches carrying capacity K.

$$\text{stock}_{t+1}=\text{stock}_t+\text{growth}-\text{harvest}$$

## Key Events & Developments

- 2,000 years ago: Soil conservation was practiced in China, India, and Peru, showing early awareness of human dependence on land care.
- Late 14th to mid-16th centuries: Pollution was associated with epidemic disease in Europe.
- Late 19th century: The contemporary environmental movement emerged from countryside protection, wilderness preservation, and concern over industrial pollution.
- 1962: Rachel Carson's Silent Spring ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silent_Spring)) helped launch modern environmental activism by warning about pesticide damage.
- 1968-1974: Survivalist works such as The Population Bomb, The Limits to Growth ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Limits_to_Growth)), Blueprint for Survival ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A_Blueprint_for_Survival)), and An Inquiry into the Human Prospect framed environmental crisis as a threat to human survival.
- 1970s: Emancipatory environmentalism promoted recycling, alternative energy, decentralization, stewardship, and more democratic planning.

## Key People

- Rachel Carson ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rachel_Carson)): Marine biologist and author of Silent Spring ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silent_Spring)), which became a central warning against pesticide harm.
- Paul Ehrlich ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_Ehrlich)): Author of The Population Bomb, associated with survivalist warnings about population pressure.
- Donella H. Meadows ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Donella_H._Meadows)): Coauthor associated with The Limits to Growth ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Limits_to_Growth)) and arguments about planetary limits.
- Edward Goldsmith ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edward_Goldsmith)): Author of Blueprint for Survival ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A_Blueprint_for_Survival)), a major survivalist environmental work.
- Robert Heilbroner ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Heilbroner)): Political economist whose An Inquiry into the Human Prospect argued that survival might require strong limits on freedom.
- Julian Simon ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Julian_Simon)) and Herman Kahn ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Herman_Kahn)): Critics of survivalist pessimism who emphasized human inventiveness and resource substitution.
- Barry Commoner ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barry_Commoner)): Ecologist who promoted ecological interdependence, renewable resources, and production that works with nature.
- Ernst Friedrich Schumacher ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ernst_Friedrich_Schumacher)): Economist associated with small-scale, human-centred, and ecologically integrated economic thinking.

## Interesting Facts

- The terms shallow ecology and deep ecology mark a dispute over whether environmentalism should protect nature mainly for human benefit or treat nature as valuable in itself.
- Anthropocentrism makes environmental duties indirect duties to humans, while biocentrism gives direct moral standing to living things or natural systems.
- Ecofeminism ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ecofeminism)) treats hierarchy itself as an environmental issue because domination of people and domination of nature are seen as connected.
- Emancipatory environmentalism differs from apocalyptic environmentalism by seeking practical, democratic, and positive reforms instead of mainly warning about collapse.
