# The Study of History (Historiography)

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> Historiography is both the writing of history and the critical study of how history is written. The source explains how historians work from traces such as documents, objects, memories, images, statistics, and landscapes, combining narrative with source criticism, interpretation, evidence testing, and reflection on historical knowledge.

## Introduction

![[attachments/historiography_001.webp|420]]

*Bust of Herodotus*

Historiography ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Historiography)) means both the writing of history and the study of how history has been written. It includes critical examination of sources, selection of evidence, interpretation, narrative, explanation, and reflection on the purposes of historical knowledge.

Modern historians usually aim to reconstruct human activity ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Human_behavior)) in the past and understand it more deeply. This ideal is recent. Before the late 18th and early 19th centuries, history rarely stood at the centre of education or claimed to interpret human life as a whole.

History grew from older narrative traditions: myth, legend, epic, genealogy, ritual memory, and stories of ancestors, heroes, gods, and sacred animals. Unlike myth, history claims that its persons and events really existed, though it can know them only through surviving traces.

That claim makes evidence the central problem of historiography. Historians work from remains rather than the past itself: documents, inscriptions, objects, memories, images, buildings, statistics, and landscapes. The craft therefore combines narrative with criticism, because every source must be dated, placed, compared, interpreted, and tested against what else survives.

## Ancient China and Hebrew Traditions

![[attachments/historiography_002.webp|420]]

*Portrait of Sima Qian*

Chinese historiography developed a strong annalistic and moral tradition. Early historians were linked to temple archives and later to the state bureaucracy. History preserved examples of virtue, failure, institutions, dynastic change, and the Mandate of Heaven.

Sima Qian's Shiji gave Chinese historiography a foundational model. It organized China's past from mythical times to the Han dynasty and used documents, categories, biographies, and eyewitness questioning where possible.

Chinese historians valued objectivity, though they rarely explained source choices. Liu Zhiji's Shitong became an early major work of historical criticism, urging skepticism, wide source knowledge, and evidence-based argument.

The Hebrew Bible shaped Western historical thought through covenant, divine purpose, prophecy, genealogy, law, kingship, exile, punishment, and restoration. It treated history as a moral drama under God's will.

These two traditions show different early answers to historical meaning. Chinese writing often made rulers and officials responsible to inherited moral order, while Hebrew writing made collective fate intelligible through covenant and divine judgment. Both gave history an ethical weight that later secular historians often retained even when they changed the explanation.

## Greek Historiography

Herodotus is often called the father of history. His account of the Greco-Persian Wars combined inquiry, travel, ethnography, memory, marvels, and narrative explanation. He wanted to preserve great deeds and explain the causes of conflict.

Thucydides created a more severe model. In his history of the Peloponnesian War, he tested eyewitness reports, criticized romance, analyzed power, fear, interest, rhetoric, plague, civil conflict, and the collapse of Athenian judgment.

Thucydides aimed at a useful history that would last because human nature would produce similar situations again. His work became a model for political analysis, realism, source criticism, and the search for recurring patterns.

His method also exposed a permanent tension in historical writing. A historian may seek exact evidence, but speeches, motives, fear, ambition, and collective panic cannot be weighed like objects. Thucydides solved this by combining disciplined inquiry with analytical reconstruction, making political history a study of judgment under pressure.

## Roman Historiography

Roman historiography inherited Greek models but gave them a Roman political and moral tone. It asked how Rome rose, how its institutions worked, and how power corrupted public life.

Polybius explained Rome's rapid conquest through mixed government, public spirit, discipline, and fortune. Sallust emphasized corruption and moral decline. Livy memorialized Rome's early virtues as the republic gave way to empire.

Tacitus wrote sharp accounts of imperial power, fear, character, and loss of liberty. His focus on emperors, courts, and crisis made him a pioneer of psychological political history.

Suetonius and Plutarch brought history close to biography. Their lives of rulers and great figures made character, anecdote, moral example, and comparison central to historical understanding.

## Christian and Medieval Historiography

![[attachments/historiography_003.webp|420]]

*Bede manuscript page*

Christian history absorbed the Hebrew biblical view that events formed part of divine providence. Early Christian historians had to explain persecution, empire, conversion, and the place of Rome in God's plan.

Eusebius interpreted the Roman Empire as the setting prepared for Christianity's spread. [[Augustine]], after the sack of Rome, separated the earthly city from the heavenly city and gave medieval historiography a powerful providential framework.

Medieval chronicles often listed events year by year: wars, deaths, harvests, portents, rulers, saints, disasters, and marvels. They often lacked causal explanation but preserved essential material for later historians.

Later medieval chronicles grew richer. Matthew Paris, for example, gathered information across Europe from a monastic base. Chronicle writing, saints' lives, royal histories, and local records created large archives of memory.

The apparent simplicity of chronicles can be misleading. Their year-by-year form often records what a community considered worth remembering: famine, prodigies, battles, rulers, holy persons, and signs of divine favour or anger. Even without explicit causes, these lists reveal medieval assumptions about order, warning, time, and providence.

## Islamic Historiography

![[attachments/historiography_004.webp|420]]

*Statue of Ibn Khaldun*

Islamic historical writing reached high sophistication in Ibn Khaldun's Muqaddimah. He discussed method, culture, economics, geography, public finance, population, state, religion, politics, and the social setting of knowledge.

Ibn Khaldun criticized surface history and credulous tradition. He wanted historians to seek inner causes and explain how Bedouin solidarity, dynasty, wealth, luxury, decay, and civilization formed recurring patterns.

## Renaissance Humanism

Renaissance historians rediscovered antiquity and developed a sharper sense of distance between past and present. Petrarch admired Roman writers and felt that his own age had broken with the Middle Ages.

Humanists used philology to test documents. Lorenzo Valla proved the Donation of Constantine was not authentic by showing that its Latin belonged to a later age. This was a major victory for source criticism.

Flavio Biondo helped found archaeology and the idea of the Middle Ages as a historical period. Leonardo Bruni used classical models to write civic history focused on Florence, politics, republican virtue, and narrative rise.

Machiavelli treated history as political instruction, using ancient examples to guide statesmen. Guicciardini was more skeptical about simple lessons, insisting that small differences in circumstances change outcomes.

Giorgio Vasari's artist biographies organized art history into stages of progress. Renaissance biography, antiquarianism, civic history, and philology all expanded historical method.

## Reformation and Early Modern Scholarship

The Reformation turned church history into a field of conflict. Protestants and Catholics searched documents, edited sources, and wrote huge works defending rival interpretations of Christian history.

The Magdeburg Centuries attacked papal history; Caesar Baronius's Annales Ecclesiastici answered from the Catholic side. The controversy was partisan, but it stimulated publication and criticism of medieval sources.

Paolo Sarpi's History of the Council of Trent joined research, irony, political insight, and literary force. It showed how religious controversy could produce major historical writing.

French legal humanists used historical philology to study Roman and French law. Guillaume Bude, Francois Hotman, Francois Baudouin, Jean Bodin, and Etienne Pasquier connected legal history, national history, source criticism, and method.

The Bollandists and Jean Mabillon advanced documentary criticism. Mabillon's De re diplomatica studied handwriting, parchment, ink, seals, and comparison of charters, founding diplomatics as a tool for authenticating documents.

This scholarship made historical truth depend on material details as well as literary judgment. A charter could be exposed by its script, seal, formula, parchment, vocabulary, or mismatch with known practice. The same habit of comparing form, language, and context later became basic to archival history, paleography, textual editing, and the study of forgery.

## Enlightenment Historiography

The Enlightenment brought science ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Science)), skepticism, wider readership, and hopes for general laws of society. It also raised doubts about the reliability of tradition, forgeries, and inherited authority.

David Hume wrote with sober empiricism, while Edward Gibbon's Decline and Fall of the Roman Empire joined erudition, wit, skepticism, and broad narrative power. Gibbon explained Rome's fall as the effect of overextended greatness and internal decay.

Enlightenment historians could lack sympathy for some cultures and periods, but they expanded the historical world and made history a public literary form read by many educated people.

## Romantic Historiography

Romantic thinkers challenged Enlightenment universalism by emphasizing culture ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Culture)), language, imagination, nation, spirit, and difference. Herder argued that each people had its own inner world expressed through language, poetry, religion, and custom.

Vico argued that humans can know the world of nations because humans made it. He described ages of gods, heroes, and men and treated primitive thought as poetic rather than merely foolish.

[[Hegel and Hegelianism (Hegel)|Hegel]] saw world history as Spirit developing freedom through time. His philosophy influenced many historians, including those who rejected his abstractions, because it made history a meaningful process of development.

Jules Michelet represented Romantic historical imagination. He wanted to resurrect the past, especially the people of France, through documents, empathy, national feeling, and vivid narrative.

## Professional History and Ranke

The 19th century made history an academic profession. Universities, archives, seminars, journals, source editions, and specialized training created modern historical scholarship.

Leopold von Ranke became the symbol of critical professional history. He stressed firsthand sources, archives, memoirs, diaries, reports, eyewitness narratives, and the aim of showing the past as it actually was.

Ranke's method shaped historical training, but later thinkers such as Droysen argued that historians never give an unmediated account. Facts require interpretation, discourse, purpose, and awareness of relevance.

The professional seminar made this debate practical. Students learned to handle manuscripts, compare editions, check footnotes, and argue from primary sources. At the same time, the opening of state archives pushed many historians toward diplomatic and political history, because government papers were newly available and seemed to promise secure knowledge.

## Social, Scientific, and Annales History

By the late 19th and early 20th centuries, some historians wanted more scientific history. They criticized state-centred political narrative and turned toward society, economy, culture, collective psychology, and long-term structures.

Auguste Comte proposed laws of society and helped inspire sociology. Henri Berr called for interdisciplinary synthesis. Emile Durkheim showed how social science could influence historical explanation.

Marc Bloch and Lucien Febvre helped found the Annales movement, which emphasized economy, society, geography, mentalities, long duration, interdisciplinary work, and ordinary attitudes as much as events and rulers.

The Annales approach changed scale. Instead of treating a battle, ministry, or ruler as the natural unit, historians could study climate, prices, fields, trade routes, disease, family structure, maps, rituals, and habits of belief over centuries. This did not eliminate narrative, but it made slow collective change as historically important as dramatic events.

## American and Marxist Historiography

In the United States, professional history developed late in the 19th century. The American Historical Association, Ph.D. programs, and journals created trained historians who challenged earlier amateur national histories.

Frederick Jackson Turner's frontier thesis argued that westward expansion shaped American democracy and character. Progressive historians such as Charles Beard, Carl Becker, and James Harvey Robinson called for new history tied to democracy, social interests, and rational reform.

Marxist historiography treated class conflict, labour, property, capitalism, production, and material conditions as central forces. Marx adapted Hegelian historical change but grounded it in economic and social relations.

Its influence reached far beyond explicitly Marxist writers. Labour history, peasant studies, slavery studies, imperial history, and economic history all absorbed questions about production, exploitation, ownership, ideology, and resistance. Critics objected to reductionism, but the field could no longer treat politics and ideas as detached from social power.

## Modern Fields and Debates

Modern historiography broadened into diplomatic history, economic history, social history, cultural history, art history, intellectual history, women's history, psychohistory, history of science, world history, and postcolonial and non-Western historiographies.

Economic history used prices, wages, cost-of-living indexes, industrialization, trade, and quantitative evidence. Some historians used cliometrics, while others warned that mathematical models can miss human complexity.

Quantitative history changed what counted as historical evidence. Serial data on harvests, prices, wages, migration, population, and family structure allowed historians to study groups that rarely left memoirs or political papers. But the method also raised problems of scale, because numerical regularities can explain broad patterns while still missing motives, meanings, and local experience.

Diplomatic history remained important because [[War|war]], alliances, treaties, archives, and national policy shaped the modern world. Debates over the origins of World War I, World War II, the Cold War, Korea, and Vietnam showed how evidence and ideology could collide.

Art history developed its own methods for attribution, style, iconography, patronage, and meaning. Questions about forgeries, schools, motifs, visual symbols, and modern objects made art history another branch of historical interpretation.

Social and cultural history studied mentalites, belief, memory, ritual, family, class, gender, witchcraft, popular culture, everyday life, symbols, and microhistory. Clifford Geertz's thick description influenced historians who read social acts as meaningful texts.

Microhistory showed how a small case could open large questions. A village, trial, miller, heresy accusation, family dispute, or ritual could reveal social hierarchy, language, belief, legal culture, and conflict. This approach did not reject large-scale history; it used close reading to test whether broad categories actually fit lived experience.

Women's history changed the field by asking how gender, family, work, law, education, reproduction, politics, and public memory had hidden or distorted half of human experience. It also showed how new questions can create new archives.

Intellectual history and the history of science examined ideas, texts, concepts, paradigms, censorship, discovery, and argument. They asked not only what thinkers meant, but how communities decide what counts as knowledge.

World history and world-systems theory examined global connections, trade, empire, slavery, capitalism, core and periphery, dependency, modernization, and cross-cultural exchange. These approaches widened history beyond national frames.

The newer fields also changed scale. Historians could move from a single village, court record, image, or family to oceans, empires, labour systems, migration routes, and planetary exchanges. This made history less only a story of rulers and wars.

They also changed evidence. Prices, maps, bones, buildings, paintings, films, oral memories, census tables, rituals, songs, trial records, and material objects could all become sources when historians asked suitable questions.

Postmodern and poststructuralist debates questioned [[Language|language]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Language)), representation, narrative, ideology, and the historian's authority. Even historians who reject radical skepticism now pay closer attention to rhetoric, memory, trauma, and standpoint.

Thus historical method remains both disciplined and changeable, tied to evidence but renewed by every serious question historians learn to ask about time, memory, society, and power.

Modern historical writing also depends on source communities. Government archives, church records, notarial files, newspapers, business papers, oral testimony, archaeological finds, art objects, and digital datasets all preserve different kinds of past activity. Each kind of evidence has limits: official records may hide ordinary people, oral memories may reshape events, statistics may flatten experience, and images may need their own rules of interpretation.

The growth of specialization made history more powerful but also more fragmented. Diplomatic historians learned foreign-office archives and treaty systems; economic historians used prices, wages, trade figures, and models; social historians reconstructed households, villages, and labour; cultural historians interpreted symbols and rituals; intellectual historians followed concepts through texts and institutions. The field therefore became a family of methods rather than one single narrative craft.

World and postcolonial approaches challenged national history as the default frame. They asked how empire, migration, slavery, trade, disease, missions, capitalism, ecology, and war connected distant societies. These approaches also criticized older Eurocentric stories of progress and insisted that Chinese, Islamic, African, Indigenous, Indian, and Latin American traditions have their own historical logics and archives.

Digital and environmental history further widened the record. Digitized newspapers, census data, maps, climate records, satellite images, and network analysis allow historians to ask questions at scales impossible for earlier scholarship. Yet the same old discipline remains necessary: data still has provenance, silences, categories, and distortions that must be criticized.

Debates about objectivity did not disappear. Ranke's ideal of the past as it actually was remains influential as a discipline of evidence, but later historians emphasized that questions, categories, language, and moral concerns shape what is noticed. Good historiography therefore combines source criticism, imagination, comparison, explanation, and self-awareness about the historian's own position.

The same balance applies to public history. Museums, monuments, films, schoolbooks, anniversaries, and digital archives carry historical claims to broad audiences, often with political consequences. Historiography therefore studies not only professional scholarship but also the ways societies choose what to commemorate, repair, forget, or argue about in public memory.

The study of history is finally a study of time and change. It explains how institutions form and decay, how memories survive or are suppressed, how communities give meaning to suffering and achievement, and how later generations reinterpret earlier ones. That is why historiography includes both technical criticism of documents and broad reflection on why human beings need accounts of the past.

## Key Events & Developments

- 5th century BCE: Herodotus and Thucydides established major Greek models of inquiry and critical political history.
- 1st century BCE-2nd century CE: Roman historians such as Livy, Tacitus, Suetonius, and Plutarch shaped political, moral, and biographical history.
- 413-426/427: [[Augustine]]'s City of God gave Christian historiography a major providential framework.
- 14th-16th centuries: Renaissance humanists developed philology, antiquarianism, civic history, and document criticism.
- 1681: Jean Mabillon's De re diplomatica founded diplomatics.
- 18th-19th centuries: Enlightenment, Romanticism, archives, universities, and Ranke created modern professional history.
- 1929: the Annales journal launched a major shift toward social, economic, and cultural history.

## Interesting Facts

- History began from human storytelling, but modern historiography depends on disciplined doubt about stories.
- Forgery detection played a major role in historical method, from Valla's attack on the Donation of Constantine to Mabillon's charter analysis.
- Archives changed the profession: once state papers and official records opened, historical research became more specialized and source-based.
- Historiography ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Historiography)) keeps changing because historians ask new questions about power, gender, class, culture, memory, language, ecology, empire, and global connection.
