# Drugs and Drug Action (Drug)

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> Drugs are substances used to diagnose, prevent, treat, or alter bodily processes. Their actions depend on dosage, absorption, metabolism, targets, benefits, and adverse effects.

## Introduction

Drugs are chemical substances that change how living things work, including the microbes that cause disease. In medicine, pharmacology studies the action, properties, uses, and dangers of drugs, so doctors can weigh benefit against risk in a careful way. Modern treatment relies on both natural and synthetic drugs, and also on some therapeutic proteins made by genetic engineering. Drug use is powerful because the body depends on chemical messages between cells, so a drug can alter a large system by changing only one part of that network. 

## Mechanisms

A drug usually works only if it meets a target molecule in a cell and forms a molecular interaction with it. Most of these links are loose and reversible, which lets the drug act for a limited time, but some drugs make stronger bonds that can last much longer. The main targets fall into receptors, functional macromolecules such as enzymes and transporters, and membrane lipids. This shows that drug action is not one single process but a set of different chemical and biological mechanisms.

## Receptors

![[attachments/drugs_drug_action_001.png|420]]

*Schematic diagram of a GABAA receptor*

Receptors are proteins that normally receive the body's own chemical messengers, such as hormones and neurotransmitters. When a drug binds to a receptor, it can start a chain of physiological changes in the cell. Pharmacology separates binding from activation: the first step forms the drug-receptor complex, and the second step produces the actual response. Affinity describes how strongly a drug binds, while efficacy describes how well it can trigger an effect.

## Absorption

After a drug is taken by mouth, it usually enters the bloodstream through the stomach and intestine over a period of one to six hours. Food in the gut, particle size, and acidity can all slow or speed this process. Other routes work faster: intravenous use can act within seconds, injections under the skin or into muscle often act within minutes, and inhaled gases or vapours can also work very quickly. The best route depends on how urgent the treatment is and how quickly the drug must begin to act.

## Distribution

Once a drug is absorbed, the bloodstream carries it to the target site and also to organs that may change or remove it, especially the liver and kidneys. Many drugs attach to plasma proteins, and this binding can hold most of the drug in an inactive form. That bound part acts as a reservoir, which helps keep drug levels in the blood more stable over time. Protein binding therefore lowers immediate potency but can also support a more even concentration.

## Metabolism

Metabolism changes a drug so that its activity can stop or so that the body can remove it more easily. The liver is the main organ for this process, and enzymes on the endoplasmic reticulum carry out many of the reactions. Often the new product is less active than the original drug, but in some cases it is equally active or even more active. Some toxic effects also come from metabolites rather than from the parent drug, which makes metabolism both a protective process and a source of risk. 

## Elimination

![[attachments/drugs_drug_action_002.png|420]]

*Diagram of nephron cells in the kidney*

The kidneys remove most drugs from the body, although gases and volatile agents can also leave through the lungs. Small amounts may pass into sweat, saliva, and breast milk. Many drugs are first metabolized, but some leave the body unchanged. In the kidneys, plasma is filtered in the glomeruli, the drug enters the renal tubule, and then it may be reabsorbed or passed out in urine. The rate of elimination depends on protein binding and on urinary acidity, which can change how easily the drug is reabsorbed.

## Time course of drug action

The amount of drug in the blood rises after dosing, reaches a peak, and then falls as the body absorbs, changes, and removes it. For oral drugs, the curve has an absorption phase and an elimination phase. The half-life of the drug helps determine how often doses should be given, because the goal is usually to keep the blood level within a useful range for as long as needed. A long half-life allows wider spacing, while a short half-life usually requires more frequent doses. 

## Types of drugs

Drugs can be grouped by use, structure, or mechanism of action, and each system gives a different view of the same medicine. These classes are helpful because they let physicians choose a replacement drug when a patient has a bad reaction to one agent. They also help researchers look for new medicines with better structure and stronger effects. Disease and organ systems offer a broad way to organize the many kinds of drugs used in medicine.

## Antimicrobial drugs

Antimicrobial drugs are used to prevent or treat infections caused by bacteria, fungi, viruses, protozoa, and helminths. They may be synthetic chemicals, products made by microorganisms, or compounds derived from plants. Many of them work against a narrow group of organisms and do not harm human cells directly, which is why they can be so useful. Some antimicrobial agents do cause human toxicity, but they are still used when their chemotherapeutic index is favourable.

## Central nervous system drugs

Drugs acting on the central nervous system are often used to change sensation, behaviour, or mental state. General anesthetics can produce temporary loss of consciousness so surgery can happen without pain, while local anesthetics block sensation in one area by stopping nerve conduction near the injection site. Other drugs change brain neurotransmitter systems and can strongly affect mood and behaviour. These medicines are important because the brain is a major site of control over experience and action.

## Cardiovascular drugs

Cardiovascular drugs act on the heart and blood vessels, so they are widely used in conditions such as hypertension and atherosclerosis. Some drugs change the heart rate, some change the force of contraction, and some change the rhythm of the heartbeat. Others act on blood vessels, causing them to narrow or relax, which can raise or lower blood pressure. These medicines are common because diseases of the heart and vessels are common, and the circulatory system needs constant control.

## Drugs affecting blood

Some drugs act directly on the blood by changing the clotting process. They may activate or inhibit enzymes that form thrombi, the clots that develop after vessel injury or disease in the vessel wall. A thrombus can block blood flow, and when part of it breaks away it becomes an embolus that may travel and cause a heart attack or stroke. This makes blood drugs crucial in preventing dangerous occlusion events.

## Endocrine system drugs

The endocrine and nervous systems work together as the body's two main communication systems. Hormones carry signals through the blood, while nerves send faster electrical and chemical messages, and the two systems depend on one another. Because hormones regulate growth, metabolism, and reproduction, drugs that copy or block them can have very strong effects. Long-term use of some hormones can also reduce the body's own hormone production, sometimes in a lasting way. 

## Renal system drugs

Renal drugs change the way the kidneys handle water and solutes, so they often alter body fluid volume. The kidney filters blood in the glomeruli, then reabsorbs most of the filtrate in different parts of the nephron, including the proximal tubule, loop of Henle, distal tubule, and collecting duct. Because the kidneys normally reclaim almost all of the filtered fluid, small changes in transport can have large effects on urine output. This makes the kidney a powerful site for fluid control.

## Dermatologic drugs

The skin is usually a strong barrier, so most drugs do not pass through it quickly. A few exceptions exist, and penetration can also be improved by special vehicles or enhancers. Absorption increases when the drug is lipid-soluble, when the skin is hydrated, or when the outer layer is damaged by disease, abrasion, or burns. Because of these factors, transdermal drug use depends on the state of the skin as much as on the medicine itself. 

## Drugs affecting muscle

Drugs that affect muscle can act on smooth muscle, which is found in internal organs and contracts without voluntary control. Smooth muscle often responds by changing the level of intracellular calcium, although messenger systems such as cAMP and cGMP are also important. Many drugs and hormones can influence these contractions, including adrenoceptor agonists, muscarinic agonists, nitrates, and calcium channel blockers. The main idea is that muscle tone can be altered by changing the cell's internal signals.

## Autonomic nervous system drugs

The autonomic nervous system controls involuntary body functions, including gland activity, internal organs, the heart, and blood vessels. It has two branches, sympathetic and parasympathetic, which support fight-or-flight responses or rest and energy conservation. Drug research in this area grew from early discoveries about acetylcholine and norepinephrine as neurotransmitters. These findings gave pharmacology a clearer picture of how nerves control [[Organ and Tissue Transplants (Transplant)|organ]] function.

## Anticancer drugs

Anticancer drugs are used against malignant disease and include alkylating agents, antimetabolites, natural products, hormones, and other chemicals that stop cancer cells from copying themselves. Some act by blocking hormones that tumors need for growth, while others work by attacking specific pathways in cancer biology. Tamoxifen, for example, competes with estrogen in breast tissue, and other drugs use knowledge of cell signalling to stop tumour growth. These medicines are aimed at a disease that grows by breaking the normal rules of [[Cells - Their Structures and Functions|cell]] control.

## Immunosuppressants

Immunosuppressants block the immune response and are most often used after organ transplantation or in autoimmune disease. They help stop the body from attacking a transplanted organ or from damaging its own tissues. Common types include calcineurin inhibitors, glucocorticoids, and monoclonal or polyclonal antibodies. The purpose is to reduce harmful immunity while keeping enough defence for the patient to remain safe.

## Core Equations

Health and behavioural sciences often quantify risk, dose, change over time, and population variation:

$$C(t)=C_0e^{-kt}$$

This models concentration falling exponentially over time, often used for clearance, decay, or elimination processes.

$$z=\frac{x-\mu}{\sigma}$$

A z-score tells how many standard deviations a value lies above or below the mean.

$$\text{risk}=\frac{\text{cases}}{\text{population}}$$

This ratio turns the preceding idea into a measurable comparison, with the numerator as the quantity of interest and the denominator as the scale used for comparison.

## Key Events & Developments

- ancient medicine: Drugs are drawn from plants, minerals, and animal products for healing and ritual.
- 19th century: Pharmacology becomes a scientific discipline and drug action is studied experimentally.
- 20th century: Synthetic drugs, antibiotics, and tighter regulation transform medical practice.
