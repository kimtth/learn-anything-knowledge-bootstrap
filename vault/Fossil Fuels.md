## Introduction

![[attachments/fossil_fuels_002.webp|420]]

*Oil refinery processing petroleum fuels*

Fossil fuels ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fossil_fuel)) are energy-rich materials formed from ancient organic matter and altered within Earth over geological time. The main fuels are coal, petroleum, and natural gas; related resources include oil shale, tar sands, bitumen, and heavy oil. They store chemical energy in carbon-hydrogen bonds. When these bonds react with oxygen during combustion, the reaction releases heat that can warm buildings, turn turbines, power engines, or generate electricity.

The rise of fossil fuels transformed modern society because they provide concentrated, transportable energy. Coal powered the early industrial revolution, steamships, railways, furnaces, and electricity generation. Petroleum became central to cars, aviation, shipping, petrochemicals, and military mobility. Natural gas became important for heating, electricity, fertilizer production, and industrial heat. Their advantages—high energy density, established infrastructure, and dispatchable power—also created long-term dependence on finite resources and carbon-intensive systems.

## How Fossil Fuels Form

[[Photosynthesis]] is the ultimate origin of most fossil fuel energy. Plants, algae, and microorganisms use sunlight to combine carbon dioxide and water into organic matter. Normally, dead organisms decay and return carbon to the atmosphere or ocean. Fossil fuels form only when unusually large amounts of organic material are buried faster than they decompose, then altered by pressure, temperature, and time.

Coal usually begins in wetlands where plant remains accumulate as peat. Burial removes the peat from oxygen and gradually drives out water and volatile compounds. With increasing heat and pressure, it becomes lignite, then bituminous coal, and in some settings anthracite. Petroleum and natural gas commonly form from microscopic marine organisms buried in sedimentary basins. Heat changes their organic material into liquid and gaseous hydrocarbons, which may migrate through porous rocks and collect beneath impermeable cap rocks.

## Coal

Coal is a solid fuel whose properties vary with carbon content, moisture, ash, sulfur, and geological rank. Lignite is relatively young, moist, and lower in energy density. Bituminous coal has higher carbon content and has been widely used in power plants and steelmaking. Anthracite is harder, shinier, and generally higher in carbon. Coal can be mined from surface deposits or from underground seams, then crushed, washed, transported, and burned or converted into coke for iron and steel production.

Coal has historically been inexpensive and abundant in several regions, but its combustion produces substantial carbon dioxide and, unless controlled, pollutants such as sulfur dioxide, nitrogen oxides, particulate matter, and mercury. Pollution-control equipment can reduce some local pollutants, but it does not remove the basic climate effect of releasing carbon that was stored underground for millions of years. Mining also affects land, water, workers, and nearby communities.

## Petroleum

Petroleum, often called crude oil, is a mixture of hydrocarbons found in underground reservoirs. Wells bring crude oil to the surface, where refineries separate and transform it through distillation, cracking, reforming, and blending. Refining produces fuels such as gasoline, diesel, jet fuel, marine fuel, and liquefied petroleum gas, as well as feedstocks for plastics, solvents, lubricants, synthetic fibres, fertilizers, pharmaceuticals, and many other chemicals.

Oil is especially useful in transport because liquid fuels store much energy in a small mass and can be pumped, stored, and refuelled quickly. This advantage made petroleum central to cars, trucks, aircraft, ships, and farm machinery. It also makes oil markets globally important: disruption in producing regions, shipping routes, refineries, or pipelines can affect prices, inflation, trade balances, and government policy far away from the original event.

## Natural Gas

Natural gas consists mainly of methane, often with other hydrocarbons and impurities. It may occur with petroleum or in separate gas reservoirs. It can be delivered through pipelines, cooled into liquefied natural gas for ship transport, or used near the production site. Gas burns more cleanly at the point of use than coal or oil in many applications and can respond quickly to changing electricity demand, which made gas-fired turbines important in modern power systems.

Natural gas is still a fossil fuel. Burning methane produces carbon dioxide, and leaks during extraction, processing, and transport matter because methane is a potent greenhouse gas. Its climate value therefore depends on replacing dirtier fuels while controlling leakage and eventually reducing overall dependence on fossil carbon. Gas also links energy to agriculture because ammonia fertilizer is commonly made from natural-gas-derived hydrogen.

## Extraction, Reserves, and Markets

A resource is the total known or suspected amount of a material, whereas a reserve is the portion that can be extracted economically under current conditions. The difference matters because geology alone does not decide supply. Prices, technology, labour, regulations, transport, financing, environmental rules, and political stability determine whether a deposit can be developed. Improved drilling, seismic imaging, horizontal wells, offshore platforms, and recovery methods can turn previously inaccessible material into an economic reserve.

Extraction also has limits. A field may contain a large total quantity of oil or gas but yield only a portion before pressure falls or production becomes too costly. Coal seams can be too thin, deep, fractured, or contaminated to mine safely and profitably. Unconventional resources such as tar sands and shale can enlarge supply, but they generally require more energy, water, processing, investment, or land disturbance than easily produced conventional deposits.

## Climate, Pollution, and Energy Transition

The central environmental issue is carbon dioxide. Fossil fuels move carbon from long-term geological storage into the active atmosphere-ocean system. Carbon dioxide accumulates because natural sinks remove it more slowly than modern society emits it. This strengthens the greenhouse effect and contributes to global warming, sea-level rise, changing rainfall, heat extremes, ocean acidification, and ecological disruption.

Reducing fossil-fuel dependence involves several linked changes: using less energy for the same service through efficiency, electrifying vehicles and heating where possible, generating electricity from low-carbon sources, improving grids and storage, reducing methane leakage, and developing alternatives for difficult sectors such as steel, cement, shipping, and aviation. The transition is challenging because energy systems include long-lived equipment, jobs, tax revenue, trade relationships, and unequal access to reliable power. A durable solution must address both emissions and the social need for affordable energy.

## Key Concepts

- Hydrocarbon: a compound made mainly of hydrogen and carbon.
- Combustion: the reaction of a fuel with oxygen that releases heat.
- Refining: separating and converting crude oil into useful fuels and chemical feedstocks.
- Reserve: the part of a resource that can be extracted economically with current conditions and technology.
- Greenhouse gas: a gas that absorbs heat radiation and contributes to atmospheric warming.

## Key Events & Developments

- 18th and 19th centuries: coal-powered steam engines helped drive industrialization and railway growth.
- 20th century: petroleum became central to road transport, aviation, petrochemicals, and global trade.
- 21st century: climate policy and renewable technologies increased pressure to reduce fossil-carbon emissions.

## Key People

- James Watt: engineer whose improved steam engine increased the usefulness of coal-powered machinery.
- Svante Arrhenius: early scientist who examined how carbon dioxide can affect Earth’s temperature.
- Energy engineers: design the extraction, refining, transport, and replacement of fossil-fuel systems.

## Interesting Facts

- Natural gas is mainly methane, which can warm the climate strongly if it leaks before combustion.
- Petrochemicals make many products besides fuels, including plastics, solvents, medicines, and fertilizers.
- Reserves can change when technology, prices, regulation, or demand changes.
