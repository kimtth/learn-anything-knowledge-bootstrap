# Arithmetic

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> Arithmetic studies numbers and elementary operations such as addition, subtraction, multiplication, division, powers, and roots. It begins with counting and repeatedly extends number systems—from natural numbers to integers and rational numbers—to make operations and relations such as fractions, divisors, and greatest common divisors intelligible.

## Introduction

Arithmetic ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arithmetic)), from the Greek arithmos ("number"), is the branch of mathematics that studies numbers, the relations among them, and the operations of addition, subtraction, multiplication, division, raising to powers, and extracting roots. The term usually refers to these elementary aspects together with measurement and numerical computation, though its meaning has not been fixed: Carl Friedrich Gauss, in Disquisitiones Arithmeticae of 1801, used it more broadly to include advanced [[Number Theory|number theory]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Number_theory)). Arithmetic begins with counting: finding how many objects are in a set yields the natural numbers ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_number)) (1, 2, 3, ...), and counting an empty set gives 0, which with the naturals forms the whole numbers. Two sets whose elements can be matched one to one are called equivalent, an idea basic to modern foundations and to the "new math" introduced in primary schools in the 1960s.

## Integers

![[attachments/arithmetic_002.webp|420]]

*Portrait of Carl Friedrich Gauss*

Subtraction ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Subtraction)) is defined as the inverse of addition: the difference a − b is the solution x of the equation b + x = a. Within the natural numbers such a difference does not always exist, so the system must be extended. Including the solution of 1 + x = 0—that is, the number −1—and all products of the form −1 × n produces the integers, in which the positive integers are the same as the natural numbers and the newly added ones are the negative integers ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Negative_number)). The basic laws of addition and multiplication then carry over and apply more generally to these differences.

## Exponents

Just as a repeated sum is written as a multiple, a repeated product a × a × ⋯ × a of k factors is written aᵏ, where k is the exponent and a the base. As long as integers are only added and multiplied, the results stay integers, but division changes this: quotients, or fractions, include numbers of a new kind, the rationals, that are not integers. This illustrates a recurring habit of mathematics, in which simple ideas rooted in concrete acts like counting take on new meanings far beyond their original definition—the same pattern that later yields the irrational numbers. The idea also forces clarification of expressions such as a⁰ or a raised to a fractional power, which at first seem meaningless.

## Divisors and Rational Numbers

![[attachments/arithmetic_001.webp|420]]

*Euclidean algorithm diagram*

A central idea in the theory of divisors is the greatest common divisor, found efficiently by the Euclidean algorithm ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euclidean_algorithm)) of repeated division. For 544 and 119, the steps 544 = 4 × 119 + 68, 119 = 1 × 68 + 51, 68 = 1 × 51 + 17, and 51 = 3 × 17 show that the GCD is 17. A fraction n/d is built from the fractional unit 1/d, defined by d × 1/d = 1; here d is the denominator, which names the fractional unit, and n the numerator, which counts how many such units are taken. A positive fraction is proper when n < d and improper otherwise, and because numerator and denominator may both be multiplied by the same integer without changing the value, every fraction can be reduced to lowest terms as a quotient of two relatively prime integers. The integers and fractions together form the rational numbers ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rational_number)), and fractions are added or subtracted by first rewriting them with a common denominator.

## Core Equations

Arithmetic progressions and geometric progressions capture two basic patterns of number growth:

$$a_n=a_1+(n-1)d$$

An arithmetic sequence adds the same difference d each step, so the nth term is found by adding that difference n-1 times.

$$a_n=a_1r^{n-1}$$

A geometric sequence multiplies by the same ratio r each step, so growth or decay compounds across terms.

## Key Events & Developments

- Counting leads to natural numbers ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_number)) and then whole numbers including 0.
- Subtraction motivates extension from naturals to integers.
- Division motivates extension from integers to rational numbers ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rational_number)).
- The Euclidean algorithm ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euclidean_algorithm)) gives a systematic method for GCD.

## Key People

- Carl Friedrich Gauss ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carl_Friedrich_Gauss)): Used the term arithmetic in a wider sense in Disquisitiones Arithmeticae (1801).

## Interesting Facts

- A fraction’s value stays the same when both terms are multiplied by the same integer.
- Arithmetic repeatedly grows by extending number systems when operations require it.
- The Euclidean algorithm ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euclidean_algorithm)) finds GCD(544, 119) = 17 in just four division steps.
