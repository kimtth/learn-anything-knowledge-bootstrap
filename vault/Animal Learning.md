# Animal Learning

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> Animal learning is the modification of behaviour by individual experience. It includes habituation, sensitization, associative learning, navigation, imprinting, and abstraction, all constrained by development, perception, motivation, and evolutionary history.

## Introduction

Animal learning ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Animal_cognition)) is the alteration of behaviour through individual experience. This simple formula immediately raises difficult questions, because not every behavioural change counts as learning. Animals may change because of fatigue, sensory adaptation, motivation, hormonal state, or maturation, and learning must be separated from these other causes of change. There is probably no single, perfectly uniform kind of learning. The learning of a food signal, the learning of a route home, the acquisition of birdsong, and the abstraction of number or pattern all differ greatly. Many species instead live by fixed routines—solitary insects, for instance, lay eggs on a particular plant or captured prey, hatch and feed, recognize mates by a set of fixed signs, provision their own eggs, and die before the next generation hatches, repeating the same successful sequence unchanged for generations. For that reason, the subject is explored through several distinct forms and mechanisms rather than through one narrow definition.

## Learning and Other Sources of Change

Many animals follow highly regular patterns of behaviour that seem almost unchanged from one generation to the next, which raises the question of how to tell when a change in behaviour is due to learning rather than to other processes. Two especially important alternatives are [[Human Motivation (Motivation)|motivation]] and maturation.

An animal may behave differently because hunger, thirst, sexual state, or fear has changed, even though it has learned nothing new. Likewise, behaviour may shift as the body develops: male puppies at first urinate by squatting like females, and only the onset of sexual maturity produces the adult pattern of cocking a hind leg. Because both motivational and maturational shifts often arise from changes in hormonal state—cyclical in motivation, one-directional in maturation—learning should be identified only when relevant experience has modified behaviour in a way these other factors cannot explain.

## Nonassociative Learning

Some simple forms of learning do not depend on building associations between two events. The two main examples are habituation and sensitization, treated as opposite tendencies.

In habituation, repeated presentation of a stimulus leads to a decline in response. In a classic example, the snail Helix albolabris moving along a wooden surface withdraws into its shell when the experimenter taps, but with continued regular tapping its withdrawal grows briefer until the stimulus has no detectable effect. Observed in animals from single-celled protozoans to humans, habituation is not mere sensory adaptation or muscular fatigue, because the response can be restored by almost any change in conditions.

In sensitization, repeated exposure to a significant stimulus lowers the threshold for a response, so that even another stimulus can now trigger it. The marine worm Nereis: a worm fed at regular intervals becomes more likely make exploratory, food-seeking movements toward any novel stimulus such as a change in illumination, whereas one given mild electric shocks becomes more likely to withdraw. Sensitization is therefore a simple way for experience to heighten responsiveness.

## Associative Learning

![[attachments/animal_learning_002.webp|420]]

*Operant conditioning chamber used in animal learning experiments*

Although nonassociative learning receives the most emphasis here, associative learning is a major domain of the subject. In associative learning, an animal comes to connect one event, object, or action with another. This includes the broad territory commonly discussed under conditioning and instrumental problem solving.

The importance of associative learning lies in its flexibility. By linking signals with consequences, animals can adjust to environments that vary in time and place. This is one of the central ways experience expands behavioural repertoire beyond fixed inherited patterns.
Reinforcement, avoidance, and trial-and-error problem solving are also ways animals learn which actions lead to food, safety, or other outcomes. A cat that runs to its dish at the sound of the cupboard opening, or a rat that masters a laboratory maze, illustrates how a once-neutral signal or action becomes linked to a consequence. These cases show learning as active adjustment, not only passive response to repeated stimuli.

## Navigation and Spatial Learning

![[attachments/animal_learning_001.webp|420]]

*Homing pigeon used in navigation learning studies*

One important domain of learning is [[Navigation|navigation]]. A Manx shearwater carried by airplane from its breeding site on Skokholm, off south Wales, to Boston returned within 13 days across a direct distance of 3,050 miles, implying an average speed above 20 mph; an albatross flew 4,120 miles from the Philippines back to Midway Island in 32 days. Such feats show that experience is used together with built-in orientation systems.

No single cue solves all navigation problems. Salmon identify their home stream by smell, a sense useless in the open ocean; diurnal birds steer by the position of the Sun, nocturnal migrants by the pattern of the stars, and some long-range migrants appear sensitive to the Earth's magnetic field. Homing pigeons are trained by release from sites progressively farther from the loft. Learning therefore interacts with inherited sensory capacities to produce highly effective spatial behaviour.

## Imprinting

Special attention goes to imprinting in precocial young—such as ducks, geese, and guinea pigs—which can walk within minutes or hours of birth and so risk wandering from the parent, unlike altricial young (songbirds, rats, cats, primates) born helpless. Though first studied by the Englishman Douglas Spalding in the 19th century, the phenomenon is credited mainly to Konrad Lorenz, who found that a duckling or gosling follows the first conspicuous moving object it sees within the first few days; in nature this is the mother, but Lorenz himself, a model of another species, or a bright red ball served equally well.

Imprinting is important because it combines features of learning and timing. Like birdsong learning, it depends on experience, yet that experience must occur during a limited sensitive period in the first days after hatching, and its effects may not appear until much later—shaping the bird's social attachments and even its choice of mate as an adult. Imprinting shows that learning is often tightly constrained by development.

Filial imprinting is adaptive precisely because a precocial chick that strays risks death; by binding it to the first large moving object, the mechanism normally guarantees attachment to the true parent while remaining open to error in the artificial conditions of the laboratory.

## Concept Learning and Abstraction

More complex learning involves animals responding to an abstract relation or property rather than a single cue. Birds such as ravens, rooks, and jackdaws have solved counting tasks in which the actual stimuli vary from trial to trial, and chimpanzees have been trained to label pictures with the numeral for the number of objects shown. A rat can learn one response after a signal lasting two seconds and a different response after five seconds, responding to duration rather than to the physical identity of the signal.

Counting ability may not be accidental: the cue that tells a female bird to stop laying is thought to involve the number of eggs already in the nest. Similarly, pigeons shown hundreds of colour photographs of natural scenes can learn to peck only at those containing a tree or part of a tree, even though the exact pictures differ from trial to trial. These results suggest that some animals can abstract a shared property from many different examples rather than simply memorizing one fixed stimulus.

## Learning, Biology, and Constraint

A broad lesson is that learning cannot be understood as a single unlimited general process. It is shaped by the biology of the species, by the kinds of problems animals face, and by the sensory and motor systems available to them. Thus, what an animal can learn and how it learns are influenced by its evolutionary history.

Learning is therefore linked to behavioral ecology and comparative psychology. It is adaptive, but not infinitely plastic. Species differ in what they attend to, when they can learn, and what kinds of associations or concepts they are especially prepared to acquire.

## Conclusion

Animal learning ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Animal_cognition)) is a varied set of ways in which experience modifies behaviour. These range from simple nonassociative changes such as habituation and sensitization to more elaborate phenomena such as navigation, imprinting, and concept learning. Learning is thus neither a trivial label for all change nor a single uniform mechanism.

Instead, learning appears as one major component of animal adaptation. It works together with motivation, maturation, perception, and inherited structure to help animals deal flexibly with their surroundings. 's overall message is that to understand learning, one must study both the process itself and the biological context in which it evolved.

## Key Events & Developments

- 19th century: Experimental work on learning and imprinting begins to define the field.
- 1930s to 1950s: Studies of habituation, sensitization, and [[Navigation|navigation]] show that learning is broader than simple conditioning.
- 20th century: Konrad Lorenz and other researchers demonstrate sensitive periods and species-specific constraints on learning.
- Late 20th century: Work on abstract discrimination expands the study of concept learning.
