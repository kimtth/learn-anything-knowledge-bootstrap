## Introduction

Napoleon Bonaparte, born Napoleone Buonaparte, was a French general, first consul from 1799 to 1804, and emperor of the French from 1804 to 1814/1815. He reorganized military training and administration, sponsored the Napoleonic Code ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Napoleonic_Code)), reshaped education, and concluded a lasting Concordat with the papacy. His reforms deeply marked France and western Europe, while his military ambition brought both extraordinary power and eventual catastrophe.

## Death and Reputation

Napoleon died on St. Helena on May 5, 1821, after exile imposed by the British. By then his reputation was already dividing Europe between hostile accounts of tyranny and a growing heroic legend. His final years, writings, illness, and burial helped transform the defeated emperor into a symbol of glory, order, national power, and tragic isolation.

## The Emperor

*Portrait of Napoleon Bonaparte in military uniform*

Napoleon was born on Corsica soon after the island passed from Genoa to France. His father Carlo Buonaparte came from minor Tuscan nobility and first supported Pasquale Paoli's Corsican resistance before accepting French rule. Napoleon was educated at Autun, Brienne, and the Paris military academy. His father's death in 1785 left the family poor, and Napoleon took on family responsibility before age 16.

Although Corsican by birth and temperament, Napoleon became a man of the 18th century, shaped by Enlightenment reading, military education, ambition, and revolutionary opportunity. He entered the French army as an artillery officer and rose during the Revolution because talent and success could now overcome birth.

## The Directory

Napoleon's decisive political rise began in 1795, when he helped suppress the royalist uprising against the National Convention on 13 Vendemiaire. The Directory rewarded him with command of the Army of the Interior and then the Army of Italy. He also met and married Josephine de Beauharnais in March 1796, just before leaving for the Italian campaign.

In Italy, Napoleon turned an ill-fed and ill-equipped army into a victorious force. He defeated Austrian and Sardinian armies, forced Sardinia to peace, occupied Milan, created the Cisalpine Republic, defeated repeated Austrian relief attempts, captured Mantua, and advanced toward Vienna. The Treaty of Campo Formio in 1797 confirmed his prestige, even though the Directory disliked parts of his settlement.

## Marriage to Josephine

Josephine, widow of Alexandre de Beauharnais, became Napoleon's wife as his career opened into politics and conquest. Their marriage connected him with Parisian society, though it later became politically fragile because it produced no heir. Her son Eugene de Beauharnais later served Napoleon as viceroy of Italy.

## Command and Propaganda

Napoleon's first Italian proclamation promised honour, glory, and wealth to soldiers who were badly fed and badly supplied. It shows his gift for morale and propaganda: he linked hardship to opportunity, conquest, and destiny. Fast movement, concentration of force, and political exploitation of victory became marks of his command.

## The Battle of Arcole

The fighting around Arcole in November 1796 became part of the Napoleonic image of personal courage and battlefield drama. Later art turned such moments into symbols of heroic leadership, even when the military reality was more complex than the legend.

## The Egyptian Expedition

In 1798, Napoleon led an expedition to [[Egypt]] after judging an invasion of Britain impractical. Malta and Alexandria fell quickly, but Horatio Nelson destroyed the French fleet at the Battle of the Nile, trapping the army. Napoleon introduced Western administration and technical measures in Egypt, then invaded Syria in 1799 but failed at Acre and retreated. He returned to France as the Directory weakened.

## The Consulate

The coup of 18 Brumaire in 1799 brought Napoleon to power as first consul. The constitution of Year VIII disguised dictatorship beneath republican forms. Napoleon promised order, peace, and consolidation of revolutionary gains, but he distrusted popular sovereignty and parliamentary debate. He preferred centralized authority, capable administrators, public opinion management, and military backing.

## Reforms of the Consulate

Under the Consulate, administration, finance, education, church relations, and law were reorganized. Tax collection moved to special officials, the franc stabilized, and the Banque de France was created. Secondary education received a semimilitary structure, the university faculties were restored, and the Concordat of 1801 reconciled the church with the Revolution while preserving the lay state and sale of church lands.

## The Napoleonic Code

The Napoleonic Code ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Napoleonic_Code)), promulgated on March 21, 1804, preserved individual liberty, equality before law, freedom of work and conscience, property rights, and the secular state. It also strengthened employers, protected landed property, maintained divorce, and gave women limited legal rights. Napoleon also maintained conscription, promotion by merit, Saint-Cyr, and a militarized Ecole Polytechnique.

## The Imperial Wars

Napoleon's wars after becoming emperor expanded French dominance but exposed the limits of conquest. Victories led to the Treaty of Tilsit in 1807, where Alexander I of Russia and Napoleon divided influence, creating the Grand Duchy of Warsaw and confirming French power in western Europe. Yet the war against Britain continued.

## Continental System

The Continental System tried to defeat Britain through economic warfare by closing European ports to British trade. It showed Napoleon's strategic problem: he could win land battles but could not easily break British sea power, finance, commerce, and global reach. The blockade hurt merchants, consumers, neutral states, and allies, and enforcement pushed Napoleon deeper into coercion. Resistance in Iberia and tension with Russia made the system a cause of imperial overextension rather than a substitute for victory.

## Remaking the Map of Europe

Napoleon remade Europe's political map through satellite kingdoms, family rulers, treaties, and client states: Westphalia under Jerome Bonaparte, Spain under Joseph Bonaparte, Italy under Eugene, Naples under Joachim Murat, and allied structures such as the Confederation of the Rhine and Grand Duchy of Warsaw. These changes simplified Europe but ignored national boundaries and helped awaken German, Italian, and Spanish resistance.

## The Russian Campaign

The Russian campaign of 1812 destroyed Napoleon's power. His Grand Army of about 600,000 crossed the Nemen, but Russian retreat, scorched earth, Borodino, the burning of Moscow, Alexander's refusal to negotiate, and winter retreat ruined the army. After the Berezina crossing, fewer than 10,000 fit soldiers remained with the main force, encouraging Prussia, Austria, Italy, and France itself to turn against him.

## Exile on St. Helena

After final defeat, Napoleon reached St. Helena on October 15, 1815, with companions including Henri-Gratien Bertrand, Charles de Montholon, Gaspard Gourgaud, and Emmanuel Las Cases. At Longwood he lived by routine, read, dictated memories, learned some English, and increasingly withdrew because he refused movement under British escort. Governor Hudson Lowe worsened relations by strict enforcement of regulations.

## Isolation and Decline

Napoleon's unhappiness on St. Helena came from isolation, inactivity, conflict with Lowe, separation from Marie-Louise, and lack of news about his son, the duke of Reichstadt. His health declined from 1817, probably through stomach disease. Poisoning theories remain unproven and are not accepted as conclusive by many scholars.

## Final Illness and Death

By spring 1821, Napoleon was bedridden. In April he dictated a will asking that his ashes rest on the banks of the Seine among the French people. He died on May 5 and was buried simply in Rupert Valley, wearing the uniform of the Chasseurs de la Garde and the gray overcoat associated with Marengo.

## The Napoleonic Legend

After Napoleon's fall, hostile royalist writings attacked him, but admirers quickly built the Napoleonic legend. Lord Byron, Heinrich Heine, Stendhal, memoirists, engravings, and exile narratives shaped the image of a tragic hero. The July Monarchy restored his statue to the Vendome Column in 1833, and in 1840 his remains were returned to Paris and placed at the Invalides.

## Return to the Invalides

The return of Napoleon's body to the Invalides turned memory into state ceremony. His coffin passed through the Arc de Triomphe, and burial beneath the dome made him a national monument. Louis-Napoleon later exploited the legend to win the presidency in 1848, stage the coup of 1851, and become emperor in 1852.

## Historical Assessment

The dome of the Invalides, designed by Jules Hardouin-Mansart, became the architectural setting for Napoleon's posthumous cult. Later judgments became more balanced after the fall of the Second Empire and the experience of 20th-century dictatorships. Napoleon could be criticized for war and ambition, but he also appeared as an Enlightenment despot who respected law, talent, administration, and religious tolerance.
