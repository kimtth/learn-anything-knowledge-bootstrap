## Introduction

![[attachments/sri_lanka_001.webp|420]]

*Relief map of Sri Lanka*

Sri Lanka, formerly Ceylon, is an island country in the Indian Ocean separated from India by the Palk Strait. Its location near India and along Indian Ocean sea routes exposed it to cultural influences from South Asia, the Middle East, Southeast Asia, Europe, and East Asia. Greeks called it Taprobane, Arabs called it Serendib, and Europeans called it Ceylon until the official name Sri Lanka was adopted in 1972.

Sri Lankan civilization has roots traceable to the 6th century BCE. Two defining features are the preservation of Theravada Buddhism ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theravada)) and the development of a sophisticated irrigation system in the drier parts of the island. Hinduism and Islam also enriched the island's culture.

Sri Lanka became independent in 1948 after nearly 150 years of British rule and joined the United Nations seven years later. Colombo remains the executive and judicial capital, while Sri Jayewardenepura Kotte is the legislative capital. The country is divided into nine provinces and 25 districts.

## Relief

Sri Lanka's land is dominated by the Central Highlands in the south-central region. These highlands contain plateaus, ridges, escarpments, basins, and valleys. Pidurutalagala, Kirigalpotta, and Adam's Peak are among the highest mountains, and World's End is a dramatic near-vertical escarpment.

The highlands are surrounded by plains that cover about five-sixths of the island. These plains are not completely flat; they include ridges, valleys, rocky buttes, mounds, sandy beaches, spits, lagoons, bays, cliffs, and offshore islets. Geologically, the island is a southern extension of peninsular India and is mostly hard crystalline rock.

## Drainage

The island has about 100 rivers, most of them seasonal wet-season rivulets. Twelve major rivers carry about three-fourths of the country's annual discharge. Most flow outward from the Central Highlands to the sea.

The Mahaweli River, 208 miles long, is the exception to the radial pattern and is Sri Lanka's longest river. Because part of its catchment lies in the Wet Zone, it has a larger and less seasonal flow than many Dry Zone rivers, making it very important for irrigation.

## Soils and Climate

Sri Lankan [[Soils|soils]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soil)) reflect climate, rock, and terrain. Red-yellow podzolic soils dominate the Wet Zone, reddish brown earths are common in the Dry Zone, and reddish brown latosolic soils or immature brown loams occur in the Central Highlands. Alluvial soils line lower rivers, and sandy regosols occur along coasts.

The island has a tropical climate with high lowland temperatures throughout the year. Rainfall is the main seasonal difference. The Wet Zone in the southwest and highlands receives heavy, well-distributed rain, especially from the southwest monsoon. The Dry Zone receives less rain, much of it from the northeast monsoon, and droughts longer than three months are common.

## Plant and Animal Life

Natural vegetation ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vegetation)) covers about one-third of Sri Lanka. The Wet Zone has tropical wet evergreen forests in the lowlands and submontane and montane forests in the highlands. The Dry Zone has dry evergreen and moist deciduous forest, grading into drought-tolerant shrubs in the driest areas.

Sri Lanka's animals include species shared with India and many island forms. Habitats have been reduced by forest clearing, farming, plantations, and settlement. Protected areas preserve elephants, birds, reptiles, and other wildlife, but population pressure and conflict have affected conservation.

## Resources and Economy

![[attachments/sri_lanka_003.webp|420]]

*Tea plantation in Haputale, Sri Lanka*

Sri Lanka has mineral resources such as gemstones, graphite, ilmenite, iron ore, limestone, quartz, mica, industrial clays, salt, titanium, monazite, and zircon. The only known fossil fuel is low-grade peat along the west coast.

Rice production is the most important activity of peasant agriculture. Since independence, paddy output increased through Dry Zone colonization schemes, the Mahaweli Development Program, irrigation, and yield-raising technology. Peasant agriculture also diversified and became more commercial.

Plantation agriculture remains important but has declined relatively. Tea is the leading plantation crop and grows in the Wet Zone, especially at higher elevations that produce high-quality black tea. Rubber grows mainly in the interior Wet Zone, and coconut grows in the western coastal hinterland. Land reforms in 1972-75 transferred much tea and rubber acreage to the state and contributed to productivity problems.

Forestry and fishing are smaller economic sectors. Forest clearing for settlement and agriculture was rapid between 1956 and 1981. Fisheries have strong potential, especially on the north and northwest coasts, but technology limits development. Fishing remains important for coastal communities.

## Industry, Finance, Trade, and Transport

Mining includes gem extraction, graphite, beach sands, kaolin, apatite, quartz, clay, and salt. Gem mining produces sapphire, ruby, topaz, and semiprecious stones for foreign markets. Graphite, ilmenite, and monazite contribute modest export earnings.

Before the late 1970s, manufacturing was dominated by large state enterprises producing cement, steel, ceramics, oils, paper, leather, tires, textiles, sugar, and liquor. Liberalization after 1977 privatized some enterprises and encouraged foreign investment through incentives and investment zones. New industries created jobs and rivaled tea in gross export earnings, though many depended on imported inputs and repatriated profits.

The Central Bank of Sri Lanka controls currency and banking. Until liberalization, the Bank of Ceylon and People's Bank dominated commercial banking. After 1979, private and foreign banks expanded, along with insurance, wholesale trade, and a small stock market in Colombo.

Sri Lanka's trade ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trade)) shifted as plantation exports became relatively less dominant and industrial goods gained importance. The country still suffers trade deficits, especially with industrialized East Asian countries. Road and rail carry most internal transport. Colombo is the major port and airline base, while Trincomalee and Galle handle some international cargo.

## Government and Social Conditions

Sri Lanka has had a representative democratic system since independence in 1948. The 1978 constitution created an executive president elected nationally, with a cabinet drawn from parliament. Parliament has more than 200 members elected through proportional representation.

The main parties since independence have been the United National Party and the Sri Lanka Freedom Party, often with smaller coalition partners. Provincial councils, municipal councils, urban councils, and local authorities share administration. The judiciary is constitutionally protected, with the Supreme Court at the top.

Education is free from primary through university levels in many fields. Literacy exceeds 85 percent, one of the highest rates among developing countries. Higher education has an academic bias and produces many graduates suited mainly to limited white-collar employment, contributing to educated youth frustration.

Government health services are free and widely distributed through hospitals and dispensaries. Preventive health, family planning, and Western medical technology improved health conditions. Ayurveda and other indigenous medical traditions remain important and often have more practitioners than Western medicine.

## Cultural Life

Sri Lanka is culturally diverse and strongly shaped by religion ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Religion)). Buddhism, Hinduism, Islam, and Christianity influence festivals, art, law, education, social life, and identity. Ancient cities and religious centres such as Anuradhapura, Sigiriya, Polonnaruwa, Kandy, Kataragama, and Adam's Peak attract pilgrims and tourists.

Sports and recreation include cricket, volleyball, football, bicycle racing, water sports, and pilgrimages to cultural sites. Media operate in Sinhala, Tamil, and English. Government controls radio, television, and some newspapers, while private newspapers have significant freedom, though censorship can be imposed under security law.

## History

![[attachments/sri_lanka_002.webp|420]]

*Sigiriya rock fortress in Sri Lanka*

Sri Lanka has a continuous record of settlement for more than two millennia. Its civilization was shaped by [[India]] but developed its own identity because of island separation, maritime trade, Theravada Buddhism, irrigation, and the Sinhala language. Buddhism declined in India but flourished among the Sinhalese in Sri Lanka.

The island's position on maritime trade routes brought Greek, Roman, Persian, Armenian, Arab, Malay, Chinese, and later European contacts. Portuguese, Dutch, and British colonial powers fought for control of the shores because of commercial and strategic value.

## Early and Medieval Developments

The early civilization of Sri Lanka centred on irrigation, Buddhism, kingship, and Dry Zone agriculture. Great ancient capitals such as Anuradhapura and Polonnaruwa depended on hydraulic systems that supported farming, monastic endowments, and political power.

Later political centres became smaller, more defensive, and more isolated. [[Cities]] lost some of their earlier economic role and became more military in character. Buddhist monasteries lost revenue as irrigation systems and agriculture changed, while Hindu influence and Tamil connections grew through ruling-class marriage and Brahman migration.

## The Portuguese in Sri Lanka (1505-1658)

The Portuguese arrived in the Indian Ocean with armed ships in the early 16th century. In 1505, Lourenco de Almeida reached Colombo and made contact with the king of Kotte. In 1518 the Portuguese built a fort at Colombo and received trading concessions.

Portuguese influence deepened through internal conflict in Kotte. Bhuvanaika Bahu relied on Portuguese help against Mayadunne of Sitawake, while his successor Dharmapala became increasingly dependent on Portuguese support. Portuguese control grew through trade, fortification, alliance, and conversion politics.

## Dutch Rule

Dutch rule centred on Colombo, Galle, and Jaffna. The Dutch governor and council controlled administration, while divisions were subdivided into provinces and districts using traditional forms. Local chiefs loyal to the Dutch held subordinate offices.

The Dutch period integrated Sri Lanka more deeply into the world economy. Cinnamon, betel, gemstones, pearls, coconut products, elephants, tobacco, and coffee became important. The Dutch East India Company used taxes, labour, and trade to increase revenue.

Dutch law affected society. Roman-Dutch law spread in the southwest and south, supporting private property rights and changing family practices toward monogamy. The Thesawalamai customs of Jaffna Tamils were codified in 1707.

The Dutch were Calvinist and tried to suppress Catholicism and spread the Reformed church. Yet many Catholics remained loyal, and Buddhism revived in Kandy and the south. The first printing press in 1734 helped produce Christian texts in Sinhala and Tamil.

## British Ceylon (1796-1900)

The British took Sri Lanka from the Dutch in 1796 during the French Revolutionary wars. In 1802 Ceylon became a crown colony. Britain first thought occupation temporary, but the island's strategic position made permanent control attractive.

Kandy remained independent at first. A British attempt to conquer it in 1803 failed, but internal divisions later allowed British intervention. With support from dissatisfied Kandyan chiefs, Britain took over the kingdom in 1815 and promised to preserve Kandyan rights and customs.

British rule reshaped economy and society. Plantation agriculture, especially coffee and later tea, became central. Roads, railways, export production, labour migration from India, English education, and administrative centralization tied the island more closely to the British Empire and world markets.

## Independent Ceylon (1948-1971)

Ceylon became independent on February 4, 1948, with a bicameral legislature, prime minister, cabinet, and governor-general representing the British monarch. The UNP dominated early government, but the political system mainly represented English-educated Westernized elites.

Economic difficulties soon grew. Tea, rubber, and coconuts supplied most foreign exchange, but population growth, consumer imports, falling export prices, rising food costs, and educated unemployment strained the economy. Traditionalist and revivalist movements challenged elite cultural values.

S.W.R.D. Bandaranaike and Sinhalese nationalism changed politics. Language policy alienated Tamils, education reforms angered Christian groups, and cultural reforms divided Sinhalese factions. Bandaranaike was assassinated in 1959, and his widow Sirimavo Bandaranaike became the world's first woman prime minister in 1960.

Sirimavo Bandaranaike expanded state control, nationalized many schools, supported local industry, and later returned to power in 1970 with Marxist allies. Land reform, nationalization, and social-equality measures followed, but economic problems continued. Educated youth discontent helped produce the failed JVP rebellion of 1971.

## The Republic of Sri Lanka

The 1972 constitution made Ceylon the Republic of Sri Lanka while keeping the Commonwealth link. It created a unicameral legislature, replaced the governor-general with a president, gave Buddhism the foremost place, and recognized Sinhalese as the official language.

In 1977, J.R. Jayawardene's UNP defeated the SLFP. The 1978 constitution created the Democratic Socialist Republic of Sri Lanka and gave the president executive power as head of state and government. Jayawardene opened the economy toward private enterprise and foreign capital.

Ethnic conflict intensified. Tamil insurgency grew in the north and east and in Tamil Nadu support networks. Organized militant groups challenged the state, and the Liberation Tigers of Tamil Eelam became the dominant armed movement.

## Civil War and Recent History

Ranasinghe Premadasa became president in 1989 after Jayawardene and negotiated the withdrawal of the Indian Peace Keeping Force. Fighting with Tamil insurgents resumed under the Sri Lankan army. Premadasa was assassinated in 1993 by a suicide bomber allegedly linked to the LTTE.

Chandrika Kumaratunga, daughter of S.W.R.D. and Sirimavo Bandaranaike, became president in 1994 and survived an assassination attempt blamed on the LTTE in 1999. A cease-fire was negotiated in 2002, but violence resumed within a few years.

The 2004 tsunami devastated northern, eastern, and southern coastal areas, killing tens of thousands and damaging infrastructure. Recovery was faster in the south and east than in the north because conflict continued there.

Mahinda Rajapakse was elected president in 2005. In 2008 the government abandoned the cease-fire, and fighting intensified. Government forces captured LTTE strongholds in 2009, killed LTTE leaders including Vellupillai Prabhakaran, and ended the organization as a military force. Civil-war deaths since the early 1980s were estimated at 70,000 to 80,000, with many more displaced.

Postwar politics remained shaped by military victory, displacement, ethnic reconciliation, security power, economic rebuilding, and debates about accountability. Rajapakse won a second term in 2010 against former army commander Sarath Fonseka, who was later arrested.
