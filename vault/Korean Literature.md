# Korean Literature

## Introduction

Korean literature ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Korean_literature)) includes works by Koreans first written in Classical Chinese, then in transcription systems using Chinese characters, and finally in Hangul. Korea had a spoken language for millennia but no national alphabet until Hangul was created in the mid-15th century, so early literary activity depended heavily on Chinese writing.

Writing systems ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Writing_system)) shaped literary history. Educated Koreans spoke Korean but wrote Chinese, and from the 7th century systems such as idu, hyangch'al, and kugyol used Chinese characters to represent Korean sounds, particles, endings, or grammatical elements. Even after Hangul, much pre-20th-century literature remained in Chinese.

## Poetry

Traditional Korean poetry has four major forms: hyangga, pyolgok or changga, sijo, and kasa. Because Korean poetry was originally sung, its forms preserve melodic origins and often use alternating groups of three or four syllables.

Hyangga ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyangga)) are the oldest form, written in hyangch'al from Unified Silla into early Koryo. They usually had four, eight, or 10 lines, and many surviving examples are Buddhist. Pyolgok, or changga, flourished in middle and late Koryo, often using refrains and frank language about love.

Sijo became the longest-lasting and most popular form, especially in Choson. It suited moral reflection, political loyalty, nature, emotion, and later wider social participation. Kasa developed as extended verse that could be lyrical, didactic, travel-based, religious, domestic, or social in subject.

## Prose

Korean prose includes narratives, fiction, and literary miscellany. Myths, legends, and folktales were preserved especially in Koryo compilations such as Samguk sagi and Samguk yusa. These works recorded foundation myths, royal legends, Buddhist saints, miracles, monasteries, stupas, bells, animals, supernatural beings, moral tales, and cleverness stories.

Korean fiction can be classified by language, length, and social viewpoint. Works were written in Chinese or Korean, ranged from short single volumes to long multi-volume narratives, and reflected yangban, commoner, or mixed perspectives. Many fictional works expanded older narratives by adding characters, incidents, and moral or social meaning.

## History

![[attachments/korean_literature_003.webp|420]]

*Korean book fair*

Early Korean literature grew from Old Stone Age performance that joined dance, music, and words. Festival songs and myths served political, religious, and agricultural functions. Bronze Age foundation myths, including the story of Tangun, praised rulers as heaven-linked founders who overcame hardship.

Three Kingdoms literature moved from oral to written culture. Historical records and monumental inscriptions in Chinese helped consolidate states. Confucianism and Buddhism deepened themes, while songs such as Hwangjo ka, legends, and Silla hyangga show both lyric feeling and ritual culture.

Unified Silla strengthened literature in Chinese through literati who valued Confucian learning. Sol Ch'ong wrote Hwawanggye, a flower allegory satirizing kingship, and Ch'oe Ch'i-Won, trained in Tang China, advanced Korean poetry and prose in Chinese. Legends of this period increasingly centred on human protagonists rather than only mythic heroes.

Early Koryo carried forward Silla and Tang traditions while creating new forms. Hyangga declined, but pyolgok, Buddhist writing, historical compilations, and aristocratic Chinese poetry flourished. The great records Samguk sagi and Samguk yusa preserved older myths and became foundational sources for later literature.

## Early Choson: 1392-1598

Early Choson literature reflected the founding of a new Confucian state. Some writers praised the new dynasty, while loyalists to Koryo wrote poems of withdrawal, regret, and fidelity. Under King Sejong, Hangul made vernacular literature possible and became the decisive development in Korean literary history.

Hangul literature began with works such as Yongbi och'on ka, praising the Yi dynasty founders, and Worin ch'ongang chigok, a Buddhist narrative poem. Akchang accompanied court music, while instructional books and Buddhist translations showed that Korean could serve serious literary, religious, and educational purposes.

Early Choson poetry and prose also included kyonggi-style poems, sijo, kasa, humorous Chinese tales, and fictional narratives. Kim Sisup's Kumo shinhwa, using dream meetings and spirit journeys, is treated as the first Korean fictional narrative.

## Later Choson: 1598-1894

Later Choson literature was shaped by the Japanese invasion of 1592 and later Manchu invasion. War diaries, sijo, dream-journey records, and court writings expressed destruction, loyalty, humiliation, and reflection. Chinese-language literature split between writers challenging old norms and scholar-bureaucrats trying to restore them.

Sirhak writing turned toward contemporary reality. Pak Chi-Won used lively prose and social criticism, while Chong Yak-Yong sought a folk-song flavour in verse. Wihangin writers, including middle and lower-status functionaries and village residents, expanded poetry about Korean customs and everyday life.

Sijo and kasa widened socially in the 18th and 19th centuries. Professional singers and wihangin compiled sijo collections, while kasa became longer and more varied, covering travel, manners, religion, family life, women writers, and domestic feeling.

Fiction and p'ansori grew with popular readership. Chinese narratives such as Kuun mong and Yangban chon offered dream structures and social criticism, while vernacular fiction began with Hong Kil-dong chon. P'ansori transformed oral performance into stories about everyday life, satire, loyalty, filial piety, and female virtue, later reappearing as fiction.

## Transitional Literature: 1894-1910

Transitional literature followed the reforms of 1894. New schools, learned societies, enlightenment movements, vernacular newspapers, the Korean Language Institute, and grammar study helped prepare modern literature while older habits remained strong.

Ch'angga and sinsosol were the first new forms. Ch'angga grew from hymns and patriotic songs, denouncing corruption and supporting independence and modernization. Sinsosol, or the new novel, kept moral patterns of virtue and vice but moved toward prose, realistic settings, spoken language, science, study abroad, and social reform.

## Modern Literature: 1910 To The End Of The 20th Century

![[attachments/korean_literature_002.webp|420]]

*Poem monument of Kim Yeong-nang Gangjin South Korea*

Modern Korean literature was launched by Ch'oe Namson and Yi Kwangsu. Ch'oe's 1908 poem Hae egeso pada ege used new punctuation, uneven stanzas, and youthful reform energy. Yi's Mujong, published in 1917, became the first modern Korean novel through its everyday prose style and reformist themes.

Western influence grew after 1919. Translations of Verlaine, Mallarme, and other Symbolists introduced suggestion, free verse, melancholy, and indirection. Naturalist fiction in the 1920s, led by Yom Sangsop, explored awakened individuality, psychological analysis, documentary detail, poverty, disillusionment, and social disharmony.

Poetry of the 1920s and 1930s included Buddhist contemplation, folk lyricism, resistance, and modernist experiment. Han Yongun wrote meditative poems about occupation, Kim Sowol used simple direct folk language, Chong Chiyong became a major modern poet, and Yi Yuksa and Yun Tongju wrote sorrowful resistance poetry without violent hatred.

Proletarian and modernist currents competed under Japanese rule. The Korean Artist Proletariat Federation treated literature as socialist propaganda, while other writers resisted reducing literature to journalism or politics. After KAPF was dissolved in 1935, fiction turned toward nature, sex, mysticism, shamanism, alienation, and defeated characters.

Japanese censorship intensified after annexation and suppressed Korean-language writing in the early 1940s. Liberation in 1945 revived left-right literary conflict. By 1948, many hard-left writers were in North Korea, while South Korean literature flowered in many poetic directions.

Postwar literature was dominated by national division. Fiction after the Korean War treated the 38th parallel as a symbol of Korea's suffering, human division, and alienation. Writers explored war trauma, lost generations, self-deception, boredom, damaged goodness, and the pressure of modern society on freedom and individuality.

Late-20th-century writing included poets and novelists seeking individual voices. So Chongju explored Korean language from sensual to spiritual registers, Pak Tujin joined Christian and national feeling, Pak Kyongni's T'oji became a major roman-fleuve, and writers such as Hwang Tonggyu and Yun Hunggil used personal experience to express broader Korean predicaments.

Modern drama began in 1908 and developed through small theatre groups such as T'oworhoe and the Theatrical Arts Research Society. Experimental theatre staged Western plays and encouraged original works, but weak institutional support, few strong playwrights and actors, and limited audiences constrained dramatic growth.

## Key Events & Developments

- 4th century CE: Korean scholars were writing poetry in Classical Chinese.
- 7th century: Idu helped represent Korean through Chinese characters.
- 1443: Hangul was created under Sejong.
- 1445-1447: Yongbi och'on ka became an early Hangul literary work.
- 1592: Japanese invasion reshaped later Choson literature.
- 1894: Reforms began the transition toward modern literature.
- 1917: Yi Kwangsu's Mujong became the first modern Korean novel.
- 1945: Liberation transformed Korean literary politics.
