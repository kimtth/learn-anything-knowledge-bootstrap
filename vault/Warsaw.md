## Introduction

Warsaw, Polish Warszawa, is the capital of [[Poland]] and of Mazowieckie wojewodztwo. It lies in east-central Poland on the Vistula River. Warsaw is notable among European capitals less for age or beauty than for indestructibility. It has repeatedly risen from destruction, especially after the failed Warsaw Uprising ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Warsaw_Uprising)) of 1944, when Adolf Hitler ordered the city razed.

In 1945, returning Varsovians rebuilt Warsaw and restored its role as Poland's political, social, economic, scientific, and cultural centre. Many historic streets, churches, palaces, and buildings were reconstructed according to their earlier forms. The city's emblem is a mermaid with sword and shield, and its motto, Contemnit procellas, means “It defies the storms.”

## Landscape and City Layout

Warsaw stands about 240 miles southeast of Gdansk, in the middle of the Warsaw Plain. The Vistula River divides the city into right- and left-bank sections. The climate is moderate and cool, with frequent weather changes, a July average of about 66 F, a January average near 26 F, and snow cover for roughly 50 to 64 days each year.

The city grew from about half a square mile in the 17th century to a much larger modern capital after World War II. Its districts preserve older local names such as Srodmiescie, Zoliborz, Wola, Ochota, Mokotow, Praga-Poudnie, and Praga-Ponoc. Postwar planning placed industry and warehouses on outskirts or between housing zones, expanded parks, widened streets, and rebuilt the Old and New towns.

The Old Town became a UNESCO World Heritage site in 1980. Its Gothic St. John's Cathedral, Barbican, Old Town Market Square, Baroque churches, Royal Castle, and palaces at Lazienkowski Park and Wilanow show the restored historical city. Modern monuments also carry political meaning, from the Warsaw Ghetto Uprising memorial to later recognition of the Home Army and removal of Soviet-era symbols.

## People, Economy, and Government

![[attachments/warsaw_002.webp|420]]

*Warszawa - synagoga z Gwoźdźca 2*

Before World War II, Warsaw was multinational. Poles, Jews, Germans, and Russians lived there, and early in the 20th century the Yiddish-speaking Jewish community made up almost half the population. Nazi occupation virtually annihilated Warsaw's Jews, while the intelligentsia, administrative class, and working class were also devastated. After the war, refugees, peasants, and communist officials' families repopulated the city, which became overwhelmingly Polish and Roman Catholic.

Under communism, state-owned and cooperative sectors dominated employment, and manufacturing was central. Electrical engineering, metallurgy, machine production, automobiles, chemicals, printing, textiles, clothing, and food industries were long important. After the collapse of communism in 1989, services, banking, insurance, private firms, and foreign companies grew quickly. The Warsaw Stock Exchange reopened in 1991.

Warsaw is Poland's main rail, road, and air hub. Expressways cross the city, trams share main streets, an underground railway was begun in the 1990s, and Warsaw Frederic Chopin Airport serves domestic and international routes. As capital, Warsaw contains central state institutions, the presidential residence, and the Sejm. Its elected City Council and city president govern the municipality, with local elected bodies in its subdivisions.

## Education, Culture, and History

![[attachments/warsaw_001.webp|420]]

*View from the Palace of Culture and Science in Warsaw*

Warsaw is a major educational and cultural centre. The Polish Academy of Sciences, Technical University of Warsaw, University of Warsaw, University Library, and National Library anchor research and learning. The National Museum, Zacheta National Gallery of Art, theatres, radio, television, the National Philharmonic Orchestra, National Opera, and the Warsaw Autumn festival make the city central to Polish culture.

The city's origins are obscure, but settlements such as Stare Brodno, Kamion, and Jazdow preceded medieval Warsaw. By the 15th century, Warsaw was capital of the duchy of Mazovia. It entered the kingdom of Poland in 1526, hosted the Sejm from 1569, and became the royal capital when Sigismund III Vasa moved his court from Krakow in 1611.

Warsaw flourished in the Enlightenment under Stanisaw II August Poniatowski, hosted the constitution of May 3, 1791, and suffered after the partitions of Poland. It became a capital again under Napoleon I's duchy of Warsaw, then passed under Russian domination. Insurrections in 1831 and 1863 failed, but industry, railways, education, and culture continued to grow.

World War II brought catastrophic destruction. The Nazi siege of 1939 killed thousands. The Warsaw Ghetto Uprising ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Warsaw_Ghetto_Uprising)) of 1943 was the last heroic act of Jewish resistance before annihilation. In 1944, the Home Army rose against German forces, but the uprising was crushed and the city destroyed. Between 1939 and 1944, an estimated 600,000 to 800,000 Varsovians died. In 1945, Poland made Warsaw its capital again and began reconstruction.

## Key Events & Developments

- 10th-11th centuries: Stare Brodno existed as an early trading settlement.
- 1526: Warsaw and Mazovia were incorporated into the kingdom of Poland.
- 1569: The Sejm began meeting in Warsaw.
- 1611: Sigismund III Vasa moved the royal court from Krakow to Warsaw.
- 1791: The May 3 Constitution was proclaimed in Warsaw.
- 1831 and 1863: Polish insurrections against Russian rule failed.
- 1939: Nazi Germany besieged Warsaw.
- 1943: The Warsaw Ghetto Uprising ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Warsaw_Ghetto_Uprising)) resisted Nazi annihilation.
- 1944: The Warsaw Uprising ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Warsaw_Uprising)) was crushed, and the city was systematically destroyed.
- 1945: Warsaw was restored as Poland's capital and reconstruction began.
- 1989: The fall of communism began rapid economic transformation.
- 1991: The Warsaw Stock Exchange reopened.
