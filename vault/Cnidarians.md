# Cnidarians

> [!abstract] Reference navigation

## Introduction

Cnidarians are mostly marine animals such as corals, jellyfish, sea anemones, hydras, and Portuguese men-of-war. They make up a distinctive phylum defined by radial symmetry, two-cell-layer body organization, nematocysts, and life cycles that often alternate between polyp and medusa forms.

They are ecologically important, especially in reef building, and have long interested both biology and human use.

The phylum includes more than 9,000 living species, and cnidarians occur in nearly all marine environments as well as some freshwater habitats. They are most abundant and diverse in tropical waters, where coral skeletons can form reefs and atolls, including the Great Barrier Reef.

Cnidarians range from solitary to colonial forms and from freshwater hydras to deep-sea jellyfish and reef-building corals. Their biology spans locomotion, feeding, reproduction, symbiosis, and varied relationships with algae, fish, crustaceans, and other organisms.

Their bodies are organized around a simple coelenteron, or central cavity, with one opening that serves as mouth. Most polyps are sedentary, while medusae swim by jet propulsion or drift with currents. All cnidarians are carnivorous, using cnidae and toxins to capture prey, although many also depend on symbiotic algae or mucus feeding.

Reproduction is varied. Sexual reproduction usually occurs in the medusa phase, while asexual budding or division can occur in polyps and colonial forms. When polyps remain attached, they form colonies in which individual members may specialize for feeding, defense, reproduction, or floating.

Symbiosis is a major theme in cnidarian biology. Reef-forming corals depend heavily on zooxanthellae, and some sea anemones live with hermit crabs, true crabs, nudibranchs, or clown fish. These associations can provide food, transport, protection, or habitat for both partners.

## Evolution and classification

Competing views on cnidarian evolution lead into a detailed classification of the living classes and orders. Anthozoa, Cubozoa, Hydrozoa, and Scyphozoa are treated as the core modern classes, with subclass and order-level distinctions based on form, skeleton, and life history.

The origin of the phylum is difficult to resolve because early soft-bodied forms fossilized poorly and preservable skeletons appeared later. Some theories treat the medusa as ancestral, others the polyp, and another view links anthozoans to a simplified bilaterian ancestor. Ediacaran fossils have often been interpreted as cnidarian-like, but Cambrian evidence is less abundant.

## Importance

Cnidarians matter to humans as food, building material, jewelry, research organisms, and sometimes as dangerous stinging animals. They have both economic uses and medical significance through their toxins.

Their effects on humans are mixed. Jellyfish are eaten in parts of Asia, sea anemones are eaten in some regions, coral skeletons can be used for building or cement, and precious corals are made into jewelry. At the same time, stings from Physalia, some sea anemones, and especially box jellyfish can cause severe pain or death.

## Conclusion

Cnidarians are an ancient, diverse, and biologically distinctive phylum whose life cycles, stinging cells, and symbioses have made them important in ecology and science.
