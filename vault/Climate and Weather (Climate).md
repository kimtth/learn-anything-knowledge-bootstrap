# Climate and Weather (Climate)

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> Climate describes the long-term pattern of atmospheric conditions in a region, including temperature, precipitation, winds, and seasonal variability. It results from solar energy, circulation, oceans, landforms, and human influence.

## Introduction

Climate is the long-term pattern of atmospheric conditions in a given place, not simply average weather. It is the combined experience of temperature, humidity, precipitation, pressure, wind, solar energy, and their variability over many years. The word comes from the ancient Greek klima, meaning an inclination or slope, as of the Sun's rays, and a latitude zone of the Earth; in its older English form, clime, it sometimes included the natural vegetation as well. Because one year, decade, or century can differ from another, climate is time-dependent, and climatic values should always state the years they refer to.

The physical causes of climate range from radiation and the atmosphere to oceans, monsoons, upper-level winds, and human influences.

## Major controls on climate

![[attachments/climate_and_weather_climate_002.webp|420]]

*Diagram of global atmospheric circulation cells and prevailing winds*

Latitude, continentality, ocean currents, topography, atmospheric composition, and seasonal circulation patterns all shape regional climates. Humidity, precipitation, pressure, wind, and cloud formation are also central climatic elements.

Solar radiation ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solar_irradiance)) is the basic energy source. Because Earth is spherical and tilted, sunlight is distributed unevenly by latitude and season. The tropics receive more direct radiation, while polar regions receive less and experience extreme seasonal contrasts; poleward of 66.5° N and S the length of day ranges from zero at the winter solstice to 24 hours at the summer solstice, whereas the Equator keeps a constant 12-hour day. On average 46 percent of the radiant energy reaching the top of the atmosphere is absorbed by Earth's surface, but this varies with surface type: water surfaces reflect only 4 to 10 percent and absorb efficiently, while snow reflects 40 to 80 percent and absorbs poorly.

The atmosphere redistributes energy through pressure systems, winds, clouds, water vapour, and storms. About 23 percent of incoming solar radiation is absorbed within the atmosphere, mainly by water vapour and low clouds and by ozone in the stratosphere, where ozone shields the surface from harmful ultraviolet light and produces a temperature maximum at an altitude of about 50 kilometres (30 miles). Temperature differences produce circulation cells, trade winds, westerlies, polar easterlies, jet streams, cyclones, and anticyclones. These systems make climate dynamic rather than fixed.

Water vapour links climate to the hydrologic cycle. Evaporation, condensation, cloud formation, precipitation, snow cover, and humidity determine whether regions become rainforest, grassland, desert, monsoon country, or ice-covered terrain. Vapour capacity is set by temperature: at 30 °C (86 °F) water molecules may fill about 4 percent of the air's volume, but where the air is colder than -40 °C less than one-fifth of one percent of the molecules can be water. Latent heat released in storms also powers atmospheric circulation.

[[Oceans]] store and transport heat. About 90 percent of the ocean's radiation budget is used for evaporation, so ocean temperatures change slowly and create long lag effects useful in forecasting: stronger Atlantic trade winds drive more warmth into the Gulf Stream about six months later, and anomalies at the Gulf Stream–Labrador Current boundary near the Grand Banks produce persistent weather changes over the British Isles and northern Europe, while the Kuroshio (Japan) Current has similar effects in the Pacific. Currents such as western boundary currents and cold upwelling systems affect coastal climates, fog, fisheries, rainfall, and storm tracks. Ocean heat capacity also delays seasonal change and moderates maritime climates.

Topography shapes climate through altitude, rain shadow, slope exposure, and mountain barriers. Rising air cools and produces precipitation on windward slopes, while leeward regions may become dry. Elevation can create cold climates even in low latitudes.

Continentality explains why interiors often have hotter summers, colder winters, and lower humidity than coasts. Land heats and cools faster than water because it has a much lower effective heat capacity; the greatest temperature swings occur over dry, sandy soils, which conduct heat poorly and hold no moisture for evaporation, whereas water surfaces have by far the largest heat capacity and spread solar heating to depths of several metres. Distance from the sea therefore strongly affects annual temperature range.

## Climate variability
Climate changes over many time scales, from daily and seasonal effects to multi-year oscillations such as El Nino and La Nina and to longer historical changes. Ocean-atmosphere interaction, especially in the tropics and midlatitudes, is a major source of variability.

El Nino and La Nina are coupled ocean-atmosphere events in the tropical Pacific. El Nino is Spanish for "The Child" and was named by Peruvian fishermen who noticed its onset around Christmas; recurring at irregular intervals of two to seven years, it sends warm surface water eastward to replace the cold upwelling of the Peru Current off Peru and Ecuador. These events alter sea-surface temperature, pressure patterns, rainfall, drought, fisheries, tropical storms, and weather far beyond the Pacific through atmospheric teleconnections.

Monsoons show how seasonal heating of land and ocean can reverse winds and rainfall. South Asian, East Asian, African, Australian, and American monsoon systems support agriculture but also create risks of flood, drought, and seasonal dependence.

Longer changes are linked to volcanic eruptions, solar variation, orbital cycles, ice sheets, greenhouse gases, and feedbacks involving snow, sea ice, clouds, vegetation, and oceans. These mechanisms help explain past ice ages and warm periods.

## Climate and life

Climate is both a condition for life and a product of life. The biosphere, human land use, and greenhouse-gas changes all shape the atmosphere and alter regional and global climatic conditions.

Vegetation ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vegetation)) both responds to climate and modifies it. Forests, grasslands, deserts, wetlands, crops, and cities affect albedo, evapotranspiration, carbon storage, roughness, and local humidity. Land clearing can therefore alter regional temperature and rainfall.

Human activity changes climate through greenhouse gases, aerosols, deforestation, irrigation, urbanization, and industrial emissions. Climate science connects to social risk because climate governs water supply, agriculture, disease range, energy demand, transport, and settlement.

Climate classification is useful because it links atmospheric patterns to landscapes and life zones. Tropical, dry, temperate, continental, polar, mountain, and monsoon climates are not just labels; they summarize interactions among heat, moisture, circulation, and ecology.

The larger point is that climate is a system of exchanges among Sun, atmosphere, ocean, land, ice, and living things. Weather is the short-term expression of that system, while climate is its long-term pattern and memory.

## Atmospheric Structure and Circulation

The vertical structure of the [[Atmosphere|atmosphere]] matters greatly. Temperature, pressure, density, humidity, and stability change with height, shaping clouds, storms, inversions, mountain weather, and upper-air flow. The troposphere is where most weather occurs, while higher layers still affect radiation and circulation.

Pressure gradients, the Coriolis effect, friction, and temperature contrasts help explain wind. Air does not simply move from hot to cold; it circulates through rotating systems that produce trade winds, westerlies, polar flows, monsoon reversals, cyclones, and jet streams.

Jet streams are important because they steer storms, separate air masses, and shift with season and thermal contrast. Their position affects cold outbreaks, heat waves, frontal systems, rainfall belts, and aviation routes.

## Moisture, Storms, and Regional Climates

Precipitation is a process involving uplift, cooling, condensation nuclei, cloud droplets, ice crystals, and atmospheric instability. Rain, snow, hail, sleet, fog, drizzle, and thunderstorms each reflect different combinations of temperature and vertical motion.

Cyclones and frontal systems dominate many midlatitude climates. Warm and cold fronts organize clouds, precipitation, wind shifts, and temperature changes. Tropical cyclones draw energy from warm seas and can reshape coastal climates through wind, storm surge, and rainfall extremes.

Regional climates emerge from repeated patterns. Deserts often form under subtropical high pressure, cold currents, rain shadows, or continental interiors. Tropical rainforests form where convection and moisture are persistent. Mediterranean climates reflect seasonal shifts between dry subtropical highs and wetter winter westerlies.

## Climate Classification and Change

![[attachments/climate_and_weather_climate_001.webp|420]]

*World map of present Koppen-Geiger climate classification zones*

Climate classification systems simplify complexity by grouping places according to temperature, precipitation, seasonality, and vegetation. The widely used Koppen scheme reached a notable edition in 1939, and after World War II the World Meteorological Organization standardized observation; even precipitation is classified in fine detail, with seven recognized types of snow crystal. Such classifications are useful for agriculture, ecology, settlement, and comparison, but they cannot capture every local effect.

Paleoclimate evidence from ice cores, sediments, fossils, tree rings, and landforms shows that climate has changed repeatedly. Glacial and interglacial cycles, abrupt shifts, drought periods, and warm intervals demonstrate that climate is naturally variable over long time scales.

Modern climate change is different because human emissions alter the greenhouse balance at a rapid pace. Carbon dioxide, methane, nitrous oxide, land-use change, aerosols, and feedbacks involving ice, water vapour, vegetation, and clouds affect temperature and precipitation patterns.

Climate also connects to human systems through crops, water resources, health, energy, cities, transportation, disaster risk, and international policy. Climate is therefore both a physical system and a condition under which societies organize survival and prosperity.

## Human and Ecological Implications

Urban climates show how human surfaces and emissions modify local conditions. Heat islands, reduced ventilation, altered runoff, air pollution, and changed humidity make cities distinct climatic environments.

Agriculture ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agriculture)) depends on frost-free seasons, rainfall timing, soil moisture, growing degree days, and extreme events. Shifts in climate can change crop zones, pest ranges, irrigation demand, and food security.

The broad lesson is that climate cannot be understood from one factor alone. It is produced by interacting controls, and changes in one part of the system can travel through oceans, air, ice, ecosystems, and human economies.

## Ocean-Ice Feedbacks and Climate Records

Sea ice ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sea_ice)), snow cover, glaciers, and ice sheets affect climate through albedo, freshwater storage, sea level, and atmospheric circulation. When ice expands, more sunlight is reflected; when it shrinks, darker ocean or land absorbs more heat. This feedback can amplify change.

Thermohaline circulation links temperature, salinity, density, and deep-ocean movement. It helps redistribute heat globally and connects polar processes with tropical and midlatitude climates. Changes in ocean circulation can therefore affect rainfall, storm tracks, and regional temperatures.

Clouds act as complex feedbacks. Clouds can reflect sunlight and cool the surface, but they can also trap outgoing radiation. Their effect depends on height, thickness, droplet size, season, and location.

## Climate Hazards, Measurement, and Society

Drought, flood, heat waves, cold waves, blizzards, hurricanes, monsoon failure, and severe storms show that climate is tied to hazard. Long-term averages matter, but extremes often determine human vulnerability.

Water supply is especially climate-sensitive. Snowpack, river flow, groundwater recharge, evaporation, and seasonal rainfall determine irrigation, hydropower, drinking water, ecosystems, and urban planning. Climate variability can therefore become economic and political stress.

Climatology depends on observation and measurement. Weather stations, balloons, satellites, ocean buoys, radar, reanalysis, and computer models allow scientists to describe climate patterns and test explanations. Climate knowledge depends on long records and global comparison.

Climate models simplify physical processes but are essential for understanding interaction among radiation, circulation, oceans, ice, land, and greenhouse gases. They are used to analyze past climates, seasonal patterns, and possible future change.

The final emphasis is that climate is both stable enough to define regions and variable enough to challenge them. Human societies build agriculture, architecture, infrastructure, and culture around expected conditions, but climate variability and change can unsettle those expectations.

Climate records also depend on scale. A local thermometer, a regional drought history, a satellite map, and an ice-core series each reveals a different part of the system. Climatology must connect local observation with global circulation.

Adaptation and risk management follow from this knowledge. Irrigation, reservoirs, crop choice, building design, coastal protection, heat planning, and disaster warning all depend on understanding both average climate and extremes.

A final lesson is that climate is not background scenery. It is an active condition of ecology and civilization, shaping where people live, what they grow, what hazards they face, and how they interpret environmental change.

Air masses are another link between weather and climate. Tropical, polar, maritime, and continental air masses carry different temperature and moisture traits. Their boundaries create fronts, storms, fog, precipitation, and sharp seasonal changes.

Cloud formation matters because clouds mark invisible atmospheric processes. Cooling by uplift, convergence, convection, or contact with cold surfaces can produce condensation. Cloud type then signals stability, moisture, vertical motion, and possible weather change.

Monsoon climates are examples of seasonal circulation on a large scale. They depend on land-sea heating contrasts, pressure reversal, moisture transport, and topography. Their success or failure affects crops, water supply, floods, and famine risk.

Arid climates show another side of circulation. Deserts can form under descending subtropical air, along cold ocean currents, behind mountain barriers, or deep inside continents. Dryness is therefore a result of circulation and geography, not simply heat.

Highland climates compress climate zones into vertical distance. Elevation lowers temperature, changes precipitation, creates snow and glacier zones, and affects vegetation belts. Mountains also interrupt winds and divide regions into wet and dry sides.

The treatment of climate change ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Climate_change)) connects past and present. Natural causes explain ancient shifts, but modern greenhouse-gas forcing creates new risks because it is rapid and interacts with human settlement, agriculture, coasts, and infrastructure.

The subject is therefore much broader than a definition of climate. It covers atmospheric physics, water, radiation, ocean coupling, landforms, ecosystems, hazards, observation, prediction, and human alteration of the Earth system.

Bioclimatic regions are linked to vegetation and soils. Climate helps decide where forests, savannas, deserts, tundra, grasslands, wetlands, and mountain belts develop, while those surfaces feed back into moisture, carbon, and energy exchange.

Seasonality matters as much as annual averages. Two places with the same yearly rainfall can have very different climates if one receives rain all year and the other receives it in a short monsoon or winter season. Temperature range and frost timing likewise affect agriculture and ecology.

Microclimate shows that climate also exists at small scales. A city street, forest floor, valley bottom, slope, coast, or irrigated field can have conditions different from the wider region because of shade, wind, surface material, moisture, and exposure.

The breadth of evidence means climate must be followed from physical causes to lived effects. Climate ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Climate)) governs water, ecosystems, hazards, food, health, buildings, and long-term environmental planning.
Climate boundaries are rarely sharp. Transitional zones, mountain gradients, coastlines, urban areas, and seasonal shifts create mixtures where one classification blends into another. This matters because people often live and farm in those boundary regions.

Climate variability also affects memory and planning. A region may be described by normal averages, but drought sequences, wet decades, rare freezes, exceptional heat, and storm clusters can matter more to agriculture, water systems, forests, and cities than the average alone.

Weather and climate are best seen together. Weather supplies the events people experience; climate supplies the pattern that makes those events likely, seasonal, risky, or exceptional.

## Oceans, Ice, and Feedbacks

![[attachments/climate_and_weather_climate_003.webp|420]]

*World map diagram of thermohaline circulation*

Ocean-atmosphere coupling carries major weight. Sea-surface temperature affects evaporation, pressure, convection, clouds, and rainfall, while winds drive currents, upwelling, and heat redistribution. This two-way exchange explains why ocean anomalies can reorganize weather on distant continents.

Sea ice ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sea_ice)) and snow cover create strong feedbacks because bright surfaces reflect sunlight. When ice melts, darker ocean or land absorbs more heat, encouraging further warming. This makes polar regions sensitive indicators and amplifiers of climate change.

Glaciers and ice sheets store water and record past climate. Their advance and retreat affect sea level, river flow, landscapes, and evidence for former climates. Ice cores preserve trapped gases and chemical signals that help reconstruct atmospheric history.

## Measurement and Prediction

Modern climatology depends on observation networks: weather stations, ships, balloons, aircraft, satellites, radar, ocean buoys, and reanalysis data. These records allow scientists to describe normals, extremes, trends, and variability.

Climate models translate physical laws into simulations of radiation, circulation, clouds, oceans, ice, land surfaces, and greenhouse gases. They are imperfect, but they are essential for testing mechanisms and projecting possible futures.

Weather forecasting differs from climate prediction. Weather forecasts track specific short-term states; climate projections estimate statistical tendencies under physical forcing. Confusing those levels leads to misunderstanding.

Climate uncertainty is managed through evidence, comparison, and probability rather than ignored. Scientists compare models with observations, run ensembles, test sensitivity to greenhouse gases and aerosols, and separate internal variability from forced change. The result is not a single guaranteed future but a range of plausible outcomes that can guide water management, agriculture, infrastructure, public health, insurance, and disaster planning.

## Climate, Hazards, and Society

Climate shapes hazards such as drought, flood, heat wave, cold wave, wildfire, tropical cyclone, blizzard, and storm surge. Societies respond through agriculture, reservoirs, building codes, insurance, migration, emergency planning, and international agreements.

Human influence makes climate a public problem as well as a scientific subject. Greenhouse warming, urbanization, land-use change, aerosols, and water management all show that societies now participate directly in climatic processes.

In sum, climate is physical, biological, and human at once. It is measured in averages and extremes, explained through circulation and energy balance, and experienced through crops, cities, ecosystems, storms, and risk.

## Conclusion

Climate is a dynamic system made from the interaction of sun, air, oceans, land, and life, with major implications for weather, ecology, and human society.
