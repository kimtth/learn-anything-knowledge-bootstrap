## Introduction

Chinese literature is the body of works written in Chinese, including poetry, prose, drama, and fiction. It has an uninterrupted history of more than 3,000 years and an unusual continuity of written language despite shifts in pronunciation, dialect, and political rule.

Because the Chinese script is nonphonetic, foreign conquerors generally had to adopt it for official use rather than replace it. This helped preserve literary continuity across dynastic change and made Chinese literature one of the most durable traditions in world civilization.

## Origins

![[attachments/chinese_literature_003.webp|420]]

*The dragon, image, and demon; or, The three religions of China- Confucianism, Buddhism, and Taoism, giving...*

The earliest surviving texts are Shang oracle-bone and bronze inscriptions, dating from the last three centuries of the dynasty and recording divinations at the royal capital. They are not literature in the full sense, but their vocabulary of about 3,400 characters, nearly 2,000 of them reliably deciphered, is the direct ancestor of the modern script. Frequent words for dance, music, drum, and chimes show that songs were already sung with music, though those songs are now lost.

Early Chinese writing ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Written_Chinese)) was tied to divination, ritual memory, and political authority. No great epic mythology survives, and Han scholars later reworked ancient texts so heavily that much cannot be trusted. Even before full literary genres developed, inscriptions show a culture in which written records preserved questions to ancestors, royal acts, and bronze dedications, making the beginning of literature inseparable from the beginning of recorded civilization.

## Poetry

![[attachments/chinese_literature_001.webp|420]]

*Li Bai depicted walking and composing poetry*

Chinese poetry begins with the Shijing, the first anthology of Chinese verse, given definitive form around the time of [[Confucius and Confucianism (Confucianism)|Confucius]] (551-479 BCE) and containing 305 temple, court, and folk songs. It is the third of the Five Classics, or Wujing, of Confucian literature. The poems are often lyrical, direct, and musical. Under the Han, the fu genre of rhymed prose flourished, with nearly 1,000 pieces by masters such as Mei Sheng and Sima Xiangru, and in 125 BCE the government reactivated the Yuefu, or Music Bureau, to collect folk songs and ballads such as The Orphan.

Tang poetry is the classical high point. More than 2,200 Tang poets are preserved, with over 48,900 poems, in refined forms like the eight-line lushi (regulated verse) and the four-line jueju. Leading figures include Wang Wei, the father of monochrome landscape painting, Li Bai, Du Fu, and Bai Juyi. Song poetry made the ci form its greatest genre; Li Qingzhao is considered China's greatest woman poet, while the patriot Lu You wrote some 20,000 poems, of which over 9,000 survive.

## Prose

Early prose includes the Shujing and Yijing, followed by the sayings of Laozi and [[Confucius and Confucianism (Confucianism)|Confucius]]. During the Han, prose became a major literary art, above all in historical writing: the Shiji (Historical Records) by Sima Qian took 18 years, runs to 130 chapters and more than 520,000 words, and set the pattern for later dynastic histories, while Ban Gu's Hanshu reached more than 800,000 words. The literary language, wenyan, rose from the Han onward and remained influential for more than two millennia.

In later periods, prose moves through the individualistic writing of the Six Dynasties to the Tang reform led by Han Yu and Liu Zongyuan against the rigid pianwen style. The guwen revival was renewed in the 11th century by Ouyang Xiu and Su Dongpo, helped by printing (invented in the 8th century) and public schools spread from 1044. Alongside elite prose, vernacular storytelling and the huaben tale prepared the way for the long fiction of the Yuan and Ming.

## Drama and fiction

Drama flowered under the Yuan, when between 1234 and 1368 more than 1,700 musical plays were written and 105 dramatists recorded, though only about 167 survive and their musical scores are lost. The leading dramatist was Guan Hanqing, author of some 60 plays including Dou'e yuan (Injustice Suffered by Dou'e), and the related sanqu song form. In fiction, the great classical novels such as Romance of the Three Kingdoms, The Water Margin, and Journey to the West grew from long narrative traditions attributed loosely to writers like Luo Guanzhong.

Vernacular literature is especially important because it opened literary culture beyond elite classical prose. Storytellers, drama, popular fiction, and printed editions helped create works that appealed to broader audiences while still drawing on history, legend, religion, and moral debate.

## Ming, Qing, and modern literature

![[attachments/chinese_literature_002.webp|420]]

*Page from the Jimao manuscript of Dream of the Red Chamber*

The Ming period was antiquarian and conventional in much elite writing, enforced by the schematic baguwen (eight-legged essay) used in the examinations from 1487 to 1901, though drama and fiction remained vigorous. After the first Opium War (1839-42 ) forced open the port cities, translators such as Yen Fu and Lin Shu, who rendered more than 170 Western novels into classical Chinese, brought new models. The May Fourth Movement then transformed Chinese literature by promoting the vernacular as a national literary language.

The modern reform began when Hu Shih published his Tentative Proposal for Literary Reform in January 1917 in the magazine New Youth, supported by its editor Chen Duxiu; a student protest on May 4, 1919 against the Paris Peace Conference gave the movement its name. Lu Xun, whose The Diary of a Madman appeared in 1918, is the central early modern writer, and then follows later revolutionary, socialist, and post-1949 developments, including the reshaping of literature under communist rule and the reemergence of diversity after the Cultural Revolution.

Modern literature became a struggle over language and nation. Writers rejected the dominance of classical wenyan because they wanted literature to speak to a modern public. Vernacular prose, translation, social criticism, revolutionary writing, and later post-Mao experimentation all show literature responding to political crisis and cultural renewal.

## Conclusion

Chinese literature is a long, continuous literary civilization whose forms repeatedly adapted to political change, while remaining tied to the written language, classical learning, and the tension between elite and popular expression.

## Key Events & Developments

- Shang and Zhou eras: Oracle inscriptions and early canonical texts appear.
- Han dynasty: Poetry, historical prose, and the literary language develop.
- Tang dynasty: Classical poetry reaches its peak.
- Song dynasty: Prose reform and ci poetry mature.
- Yuan dynasty: Drama and vernacular fiction flourish.
- Ming and Qing: Antiquarianism dominates elite literature.
- 1917-1919: The vernacular literary reform and May Fourth Movement reshape modern literature.
