# Pasteur

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> Louis Pasteur was a French chemist and microbiologist whose work on molecular asymmetry, fermentation, spontaneous generation, pasteurization, silkworm disease, and vaccination transformed medicine and industry. His rabies research and Pasteur Institute made laboratory methods central to modern germ theory and public health.

## Life and Importance

![[attachments/pasteur_001.webp|420]]

*Portrait of Louis Pasteur*

Louis Pasteur, born December 27, 1822, in Dole, France, and died September 28, 1895, in Saint-Cloud, was a French chemist and microbiologist and one of the founders of medical microbiology. He studied molecular asymmetry, showed that microorganisms cause fermentation and disease, developed pasteurization, aided the wine, beer, and silk industries, and created vaccines against anthrax and rabies.

Pasteur's father, Jean-Joseph Pasteur, was a tanner and decorated Napoleonic veteran, helping shape Pasteur's patriotism. Louis was an average early student but talented in drawing. He earned arts and science degrees at Besancon, entered the Ecole Normale Superieure ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C3%89cole_normale_sup%C3%A9rieure_%28Paris%29)) in 1843, studied with Jean-Baptiste-Andre Dumas, earned a doctorate in 1847, and married Marie Laurent in 1849.

## Molecular Asymmetry

Pasteur's first major research concerned molecular asymmetry. Investigating tartrates and paratartrates, he found that optically inactive paratartrate crystals were a balanced mixture of right- and left-handed forms. By manually separating the crystals, he showed that molecular structure could explain optical activity. This work helped found stereochemistry and convinced him that asymmetry was fundamental to living matter.

## Fermentation and the Pasteur Effect

Pasteur showed that specific microorganisms cause fermentation. His studies of butyric acid fermentation led to the discovery that passing oxygen through fermenting fluid could halt the process, now called the Pasteur effect. He introduced the terms aerobic and anaerobic for organisms living with or without oxygen and linked putrefaction to specific anaerobic germs.

## Pasteurization and Spontaneous Generation

![[attachments/pasteur_002.webp|420]]

*Swan-neck flask experiment*

In 1863, at Napoleon III's request, Pasteur studied wine contamination and showed it was caused by microbes. Heating wine to 50-60 C prevented spoilage, creating pasteurization, later widely applied to milk and other foods. He also disproved spontaneous generation through experiments showing that boiled broth remained sterile unless reexposed to contaminated air, strengthening bacteriology and aseptic technique.

## Silkworms and Disease

In the 1860s, Pasteur tackled France's silkworm crisis at Dumas's request. Though initially ignorant of silkworms, he identified disease-causing organisms and developed methods to preserve healthy eggs and prevent contamination. His success saved the silk industry and deepened his understanding of infectious disease.

## Vaccines

Pasteur's vaccine work began with chicken cholera in 1879, when weakened cultures lost pathogenicity but still protected chickens. He then investigated anthrax, confirming the disease role of the bacillus isolated by Robert Koch. In 1881, at Pouilly-le-Fort, he publicly vaccinated farm animals against anthrax with great success.

## Rabies and the Pasteur Institute

Pasteur developed a rabies vaccine by treating infectious material until it lost virulence. On July 6, 1885, he vaccinated Joseph Meister, a nine-year-old bitten by a rabid dog. The success brought worldwide fame and helped launch preventive medicine. An international campaign funded the Pasteur Institute, inaugurated in Paris on November 14, 1888.

## Implications and Legacy

Pasteur argued that science and its applications were inseparable. His work showed that virulence is variable rather than fixed, helping explain attenuation, epidemics, and the emergence of infectious diseases. He recognized that microbes harmless in one host might become dangerous after passage through another. This idea remains important for understanding new diseases.
