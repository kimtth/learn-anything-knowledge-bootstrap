# Encyclopaedias and Dictionaries (Dictionary)

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> A dictionary is a reference work that records words and may explain their meanings, pronunciation, grammar, origins, spellings, and usage. Its forms include general, historical, specialized, and bilingual works built around the study and organization of language.

## Introduction

A dictionary is a reference book that lists words—usually in alphabetical order ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alphabetical_order)) for Western languages—and gives their meanings. Beyond defining words, it may record pronunciation, grammatical forms and functions, etymologies, syntactic peculiarities, variant spellings, and antonyms. Many dictionaries also include quotations that illustrate how a word is used, and these can be dated to show the earliest known uses in particular senses. The term itself derives from the Latin dictio, "the act of speaking," and dictionarius, "a collection of words."

Several related kinds of word list have their own names. A short list, often placed at the back of a book, is a glossary. A list that indexes a limited body of writing, pointing to each passage, is a concordance; in theory a strong dictionary could be assembled by merging many concordances. A list made only of geographic names is a gazetteer. The word lexicon means a wordbook but also, among linguists, the body of separable structural units that make up a language, so a preliterate culture has a lexicon long before its words are written down. The making of a dictionary is lexicography, while lexicology is the branch of linguistics that develops the theories lexicographers apply.

### The Earliest Word Lists

Dictionary-making began with bilingual rather than monolingual works. Its roots lie in the early Middle Ages practice of writing interlinear glosses—explanations of difficult words—into manuscripts. Collecting these glosses at the back of a manuscript, and then gathering several such glossaries together, produced the first reference lists; some survive from the 7th and 8th centuries and preserve the earliest recorded English forms. The first bilingual glossary to reach print was a French-English vocabulary for travelers, issued by William Caxton in 1480.

A succession of interlingual works followed: a Latin-English vocabulary by the grammarian John Stanbridge in 1496, and the more substantial English-Latin Promptorium parvulorum printed in 1499 and attributed to Geoffrey the Grammarian, who composed it around 1440. John Palsgrave produced an English-French dictionary in 1530, while Richard Huloet's Abecedarium Anglo-Latinum of 1552 held more English words than any earlier work. Other landmarks included John Withals' widely reprinted school dictionary of 1556, John Baret's trilingual Alveary of 1573, and the first rhyming dictionary by Peter Levens in 1570. These interlingual books carried a far larger stock of English words than the first all-English dictionaries.

### Early English Dictionaries

The first dictionaries explaining English in English grew from religious and educational needs. The desire that ordinary readers should understand Scripture led William Tyndale to add a table of difficult words to his Pentateuch in 1530. Spelling reformers and schoolmasters pushed the idea further: in 1582 Richard Mulcaster listed about 8,000 words without definitions, and in 1596 Edmund Coote's The English Schoolmaster supplied a table of roughly 1,400 words sorted by etymology, which became the direct model for the first English dictionary.

### From 1604 to 1828

In 1604 Robert Cawdrey issued in London the first purely English dictionary as a separate book, A Table Alphabetical, expanding Coote's table to explain "hard usual English words." A tradition of hard-word books followed: John Bullokar's English Expositor (entered 1610, printed 1616), and in 1623 Henry Cockeram's work, the first to carry the word "dictionary" in its title. Thomas Blount's Glossographia of 1656 advanced method by collecting troublesome words from his own reading and often citing sources, material later borrowed by Edward Phillips. In 1702 John Kersey the Younger became the first professional lexicographer.

The age also sought to "fix" the language, a task that in England fell to Samuel Johnson, who signed a booksellers' contract on June 18, 1746, and published his Dictionary in 1755. It held about 43,500 carefully chosen words and was crowned by 118,000 illustrative quotations; Johnson divided the transitive verb *take* into 113 senses and the intransitive into 21 more. Four editions appeared in his lifetime, the carefully revised fourth in 1773. Pronouncing dictionaries followed from 1757 onward, including those of John Walker in 1791. In the United States, the first compiled dictionary was Samuel Johnson, Jr.'s school work of 1798, and Noah Webster crowned his career with An American Dictionary of the English Language in 1828 at age 70.

### Since 1828

Webster was overtaken in philology almost as his masterwork appeared, because German scholars such as Franz Bopp and Rasmus Rask had built a rigorous comparative philology. As early as 1812 Franz Passow urged that quotations be arranged chronologically to show each word's history. The Brothers Grimm applied these ideas in the Deutsches Wörterbuch, begun in 1838, printed from 1852, and not finished until 1960, while John Jamieson's Scottish dictionary of 1808 broke ground by quoting humble sources.

The historical method culminated in the Oxford English Dictionary. After Chenevix Trench's paper of 1857, the Philological Society planned a new dictionary on historical principles, and in 1879 James A.H. Murray became editor, supported by volunteer readers who supplied some 5,000,000 quotation slips. The work was completed in 1928 across more than 15,500 pages, reissued as the OED in 1933, and published in a second edition of 20 volumes in 1989.

### American Lexicography

In mid-19th-century America a "war of the dictionaries" pitted supporters of Noah Webster against those of Joseph Emerson Worcester, partly a commercial struggle for the schools. Worcester issued a strong Universal and Critical Dictionary in 1846, and his climactic edition of 1860 gave him an edge, but the aggressive G. & C. Merriam Co. answered with their famous "unabridged" of 1864.

## Kinds of Dictionaries

### General-Purpose Dictionaries

Every dictionary, even a so-called general-purpose one, is compiled for a particular set of users, and the word "authoritative" is ambiguous—it can mean sound scholarship or a demand for prescriptive compliance. Most linguists hold that a dictionary that goes beyond recording the actual state of the language becomes a bad dictionary. Familiar abridged or college-size works date to the 18th century, but were revitalized in the 1930s when Edward L. Thorndike built school dictionaries around word and meaning counts.

This new mode reached college level with Clarence L. Barnhart's American College Dictionary in 1947. The Merriam-Webster New International of 1909 was reedited in 1934 and then superseded by the Third New International of 1961, edited by Philip Babcock Gove, whose record of present-day usage—including its note on "ain't"—provoked a storm of criticism. In reaction, The American Heritage Dictionary of 1969 added usage notes drawn from a panel of 104 mostly conservative members.

### Scholarly Dictionaries

Specialized works serve etymology, pronunciation, meaning-grouping, and usage. Etymological scholarship is represented by Pokorny's Indo-European dictionary of 1959 and by Eric Partridge's Origins of 1958. The pronouncing dictionary was best shown by Daniel Jones's English Pronouncing Dictionary of 1917, which described "Received Pronunciation," and by Kenyon and Knott's American work of 1944. The conceptual dictionary, arranging words by meaning, began with Bishop John Wilkins in 1668 and reached practical form in Peter Mark Roget's Thesaurus of 1852. Usage dictionaries grew from 18th-century error lists, the most loyal following belonging to H.W. Fowler's Dictionary of Modern English Usage of 1926, reedited by Sir Ernest Gowers in 1965.

## Features and Problems

### Spelling

Dictionaries have helped fix the conventions of English spelling. Samuel Johnson is often credited, though he changed little from predecessors; he even printed "smoak" early in his book before settling on "smoke." Noah Webster introduced simplifications now standard in American English, and American dictionaries routinely label distinctive British forms such as "centre," "honour," "gaol," and "tyre." The OED is exceptional in listing early variant spellings, showing that a common word like "good" was once written in more than a dozen ways. Because uniformity is now prized, dictionaries are used as authoritarian style manuals for spelling and hyphenation.

### Pronunciation

Dictionaries respond to actual usage more readily in pronunciation than in spelling. Webster's Third went to remarkable lengths, listing 132 ways to pronounce "a fortiori," and modern works mark pronunciation as it occurs in continuous speech rather than in isolation. A trend toward "democratization" recognizes common forms such as a reduced "government." Because the alphabet poorly records English sounds, dictionaries adopt extra symbols; Clarence L. Barnhart introduced the schwa into the American College Dictionary in 1947, while the International Phonetic Alphabet remains too abstruse for general readers.

### Etymology

Supplying etymologies forces hard choices about how far to reach into prehistory and how much to speculate. The scholar Yakov Malkiel proposed that words follow "trajectories" linking changes in form and meaning. A basic distinction separates the native stock from loanwords, of which English has so many that the language has been called "hypertrophied." James A.H. Murray described four stages of a borrowed word's citizenship—the casual, the alien, the denizen, and the natural.

## Foreign-Language Dictionaries

The great national traditions of dictionary-making reach across every major language. French readers were served by the Petit Larousse of 1959 and Paul Robert's analytical dictionary of 1960–64, while Italian lexicography ran from the Crusca Academy's Vocabolario of 1612 to Salvatore Battaglia's dictionary modeled on the OED. Standard works included Liddell and Scott's Greek-English Lexicon of 1843 for Ancient Greek and Hans Wehr's Dictionary of Modern Written Arabic of 1961.

## Key Events & Developments

- 1604: Robert Cawdrey issues the first purely English dictionary as a separate work.
- 1755: Samuel Johnson publishes his Dictionary with 118,000 illustrative quotations.
- 1828: Noah Webster publishes An American Dictionary of the English Language.
- 1879–1928: James A.H. Murray and his successors edit the Oxford English Dictionary to completion.

## Key People

- Samuel Johnson ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samuel_Johnson)): Produced the dominant English dictionary of 1755, prized for its quotations and definitions.
- Noah Webster ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noah_Webster)): Champion of American spellings and vocabulary, author of the 1828 American Dictionary.
- James A.H. Murray ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_A.H._Murray)): Chief editor who shaped the Oxford English Dictionary on historical principles.
