# Sacred Rites and Ceremonies (Ceremonial Object)

> [!abstract] Reference navigation

## Introduction

Ceremonial objects are objects used in ritual or religious ceremony. Across cultures, they are usually both practical and symbolic. They help establish or maintain communication between the sacred realm and the profane world of time, space, and cause and effect.

Such objects may mediate human contact with divine power ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Omnipotence)), as in a shaman's drum, or may serve as the means by which a god or supernatural being relates to humans. They can also compel, invite, protect against, or symbolize sacred action. In royal and communal ceremonies, they may help show that a ruler or chief has divine status.

Examples include cult statues ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cult_image)), lingams associated with Shiva under royal names in Hindu-influenced Southeast Asia, Buddhist body-of-glory statues from Cambodia, and religious dance masks in Tibet and Japanese Buddhist sects. The object can function as tool, sign, presence, or sacred embodiment.

## Varieties

*Chimu libation vessel used as a ritual object*

Ritual objects vary greatly in material, form, and function. A strict inventory limited only to religion would be incomplete because similar objects appear in solemn public ceremonies such as consecrations, enthronements, and coronations. In Hindu, Buddhist, and Christian cultures, such secular ceremonies may still be tied to divine order.

One major category consists of icons and symbols, representations of deities or sacred beings. They may be statues, painted images, figurative forms, or abstract signs. In Tantrism, for example, the sacred syllable Om can be more sacred than an anthropomorphic image because it carries cosmological and divine meaning.

Images placed in temples, shrines, chapels, or domestic altars may be treated as embodiments of the deities they represent. Household cult objects may include prosperity and fertility divinities, mother goddesses, saints, ancestor tablets, relics, and household gods. Materials range from clay and terra-cotta to metals, stone, wood, and precious substances.

## Sound and Musical Objects

Many ceremonial objects create sacred sound. Gongs, bells, cymbals, sistra, drums, and bull-roarers may summon spirits, mark ritual rhythm, frighten evil beings, or open contact with the divine. Sound objects often combine sensory force with symbolic meaning.

The sistrum, used in Egypt for Isis and Hathor and later elsewhere, has a handle and frame with metal rods and disks that make a sharp ringing sound. It was considered especially sacred and could be carried to the temple by high-ranking women. Cymbals were used in Hellenistic mystery religions and in the Temple of Jerusalem.

The Indian ghanta or Tibetan dril-bu is a handbell shaken during prayers to attract beneficent spirits and frighten evil ones. The shaman's drum among Buryat, Sakha, Altaic Turkic, and Inuit peoples helps the shaman ascend symbolically to the sacred realm. The bull-roarer, used in Australia, Africa, the Americas, and Sumatra, makes a humming sound that calls to the divine world and suggests a celestial link.

## Figures

Statues of human and animal figures are among the clearest objects representing divine order. In image-using religions, gods may appear as human, animal, or mixed forms. Egyptian, Greek, and Indian images often follow strict aesthetic, iconometric, and iconographic rules so the statue conforms to the divine model.

Hindu vahana animals such as elephants, lions, horses, bulls, and birds serve as sacred mounts for gods. Religious masks used in dances in Nepal, Tibet, Japan, and other societies can represent beneficent or dangerous sacred powers and may be worshipped like statues.

Some figure practices are close to magic. Prehistoric and later customs could include skulls in anthropomorphic statues to intensify sacred or magical force. Christian relic veneration shows a related logic, where bodily remains of holy persons become objects of contact with sacred power.

## Plants and Plant Representations

Plants and trees are often sacred objects. A tree may be a god's dwelling or may itself be worshipped as divine. In early Indian Buddhism, trees could represent the divine order or mark sacred events and places. Plant symbols can express fertility, life, growth, cosmic order, and connection between earth and heaven.

Representations of [[Plants|plants]] may appear in architecture, ritual implements, painting, sculpture, and sacred ornament. Their meaning depends on tradition: a tree can be axis, shelter, ancestor, deity, or sign of spiritual awakening.

## Relics

Relics ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Relic)) are remains of saints, founders, or holy persons and are often objects of worship or veneration. They may include skulls, hands, fingers, feet, teeth, hair, fingernails, garments, or fragments of clothing. Relic veneration is widespread, and reliquaries are made to protect and display such remains.

Reliquaries vary in size, form, and material. They may be portable for processions and pilgrimages or fixed in sacred places. Wood, bone, ivory, quartz, glass, semiprecious stones, gold, silver, bronze, and copper may be used, often with enamel, chasing, and precious stones.

In Tibetan and Tantric contexts, bones of holy persons may become ritual instruments, including flutes, horns, drums, or skull cups. In many Asian regions, human relics may be replaced by sacred writings placed inside bronze or stucco statues, making the written words a sacred presence.

## Prayer and Meditation Objects

Objects used in prayer ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prayer)) and meditation include rosaries, chaplets, prayer wheels, candles, lamps, flags, and inscribed objects. Rosaries are widespread in Hinduism, Buddhism, Islam, Roman Catholicism, Eastern Orthodoxy, and Judaism, though not in Shinto.

Brahmanic and Buddhist rosaries often have 108 beads, made from basil, lotus seeds, small bones, or disks of human bone. Japanese Buddhist rosaries may have 112 wooden beads, Islamic rosaries 99 amber beads, and Christian rosaries 150 beads divided into decades with a cross. Their main function is mnemonic, helping count prayers or meditations.

The Tibetan prayer wheel is a rotating cylinder containing prayers written on paper, cloth, or parchment. It may be turned by hand, water, or electricity. The idea of permanent prayer also appears in church candles, Buddhist ever-burning lamps, and Tibetan prayer flags.

## Conclusion

Ceremonial objects have been important in everyday rites and in solemn communal ceremonies from prehistoric times onward. Their construction, forms, dimensions, and styles were often codified, and some were treated as symbolic or actual manifestations of deity.

Over time, some sacred objects may lose their immediate sacred force and survive mainly as formal, artistic, or cultural objects. Even then, their shapes and uses preserve traces of earlier relationships between human communities and the sacred realm.

## Key Events & Developments

- Prehistoric times: Ritual objects already linked magic, religion, and communal ceremony.
- After 850 BC: Egyptian bronze sistra show the long ritual history of sacred sound objects.
- 4th-6th century: Stucco statues in Afghanistan could contain sacred-text substitutes for relics.
- 7th century: Cambodian Buddhist body-of-glory statues show royal and divine symbolism.

## Key People

- Shamans ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shamans)): Use drums and other mediatory objects to contact sacred realms.
- High-ranking ritual women: In some Egyptian cults, carried sacred sistra to temples.
- Saints and religious founders: Their remains became relics and centres of veneration.

## Interesting Facts

- Om can be treated as more sacred than a human-form image in some Tantric contexts.
- Prayer wheels can be powered by hand, water, or electricity.
- Reliquaries may be shaped like churches, towers, hands, feet, busts, or sarcophagi.
- Bull-roarers use sound and movement to symbolize contact with the divine world.
