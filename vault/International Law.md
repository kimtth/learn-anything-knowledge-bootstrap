# International Law

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> International law is the decentralized body of rules, principles, institutions, and practices governing relations among sovereign states and other international actors. Its modern scope reaches from war and peace to diplomacy, human rights, trade, environmental protection, space, organizations, and individual responsibility.

## Introduction

![[attachments/international_law_001.webp|420]]

*International Court of Justice at the Peace Palace*

International law ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_law)), also called public international law or the law of nations, is the body of rules, norms, standards, principles, practices, and procedures that govern relations among sovereign states and other recognized international actors. The term was coined by Jeremy Bentham, but the field has expanded far beyond his state-centered definition.

Modern international law covers war, peace, diplomacy, human rights, trade, economic relations, environmental protection, space law, international organizations, and individual responsibility. It is distinct from nonbinding international comity and from private international law, which concerns domestic-law conflicts involving foreign elements. Its system is decentralized and horizontal, so rules are made through consent, practice, institutions, courts, and shared legal concepts rather than through a single world legislature.

## Development

Early international law drew on natural law, theology, Roman law, and state practice. Francisco Suarez linked it to natural law, while Alberico Gentili helped create a secular law-of-war tradition in 1598. Hugo Grotius organized the subject into a comprehensive system in 1625, especially through ideas about the law of war and peace and freedom of the high seas.

Later theorists divided between naturalists, such as Samuel von Pufendorf, who stressed the law of nature, and positivists, such as Richard Zouche and Cornelis van Bynkershoek, who emphasized actual state practice. Over time the field shifted from justifying war toward regulating peace, interstate relations, and cooperation.

## Sources of Law

Article 38 of the International Court of Justice statute identifies treaties, custom, and general principles as central sources of international law. Treaties are written agreements, called conventions, pacts, charters, covenants, or agreements, in which parties consent to be bound. They may be bilateral or multilateral and may create organizations, regulate conduct, or function as general legislation.

Major treaties include the 1945 United Nations Charter, the 1948 Genocide Convention, the 1949 Geneva Conventions, the 1959 Antarctic Treaty, the 1961 Vienna Convention on Diplomatic Relations, the 1982 Law of the Sea treaty, and the 1998 Rome Statute of the International Criminal Court. States usually consent by signature, ratification, accession, or exchange of instruments, and reservations may qualify obligations when compatible with a treaty's object and purpose.

Customary international law arises from general state practice accepted as law. Its material element is repeated, consistent, and sufficiently general conduct; its legal element is opinio juris, the belief that the conduct is required by law. Once a custom forms, it binds states generally, except for a state that persistently objected while the custom was emerging.

General principles and soft law supplement treaties and custom. General legal principles fill gaps, while recommendations, guidelines, declarations, codes, and standards may influence conduct even when not strictly binding. Peremptory norms, or jus cogens, stand above ordinary rules and include widely accepted prohibitions against genocide, slavery, piracy, and aggression.

## Statehood, Recognition, and Territory

Statehood combines facts and law. The 1933 Montevideo Convention listed permanent population, defined territory, government, and capacity for international relations as criteria. Effective government matters, but recognition has occurred even during civil war or territorial instability, as with the Congo in 1960, Angola in 1975, and Bosnia and Herzegovina and Croatia in 1992.

Recognition is the political and legal acknowledgment of a status such as statehood, sovereignty, or government authority. Declaratory theory treats recognition as acceptance of an existing factual situation, while constitutive theory treats recognition as helping create the state. Premature recognition of secession can violate the principle of nonintervention.

Territory defines the spatial reach of state sovereignty. States may acquire territory by cession, occupation of terra nullius, or prescription, but not simply by force under the United Nations Charter. When new states emerge from former administrative units, uti possidetis favors existing internal borders to preserve stability. Coastal states also hold maritime zones governed by sea-law conventions and later law-of-the-sea rules.

## Jurisdiction and Immunity

Jurisdiction is a state's power to regulate people, property, and events. The territorial principle gives primary authority over crimes within a state's territory. The nationality principle reaches nationals abroad, the passive personality principle reaches crimes abroad affecting nationals, the protective principle reaches foreign conduct threatening state interests, and universality allows all states to prosecute crimes such as piracy, slavery, war crimes, crimes against peace, and crimes against humanity.

Immunity rules limit jurisdiction in special contexts. Diplomatic and consular officials have immunities that vary by rank and function under the Vienna conventions. International organizations, diplomatic missions, archives, international judges, and visiting armed forces may also enjoy protection from local jurisdiction under treaties or agreements.

## Disputes and Special Regimes

Peaceful dispute settlement includes negotiation, good offices, mediation, inquiry, conciliation, arbitration, judicial settlement, and action by regional or global organizations. No single method takes automatic priority, and the decentralized legal order often relies on consent and institutional practice.

[[Antarctica]] is governed by the 1959 Antarctic Treaty, which prevents militarization and suspends territorial claims while creating an open-ended regime. Later agreements protect the Antarctic environment.

[[Environmentalism and Environmental Law (Environmental Law)|Environmental law]] developed because proving state responsibility for transboundary harm can be difficult. Cooperative agreements use emission limits, consultation, notification, environmental-impact assessment, monitoring, and treaty regimes. Major instruments include the 1972 Stockholm Declaration, the 1992 Rio Declaration, the 1985 Ozone Layer convention, the 1992 Biodiversity Convention, the 1992 Climate Change convention, and the 1997 Kyoto Protocol.

## Individuals and Nonstate Actors

Individuals became increasingly important subjects of international law during the 20th century. The 1948 Universal Declaration of Human Rights led to treaties on genocide, racial discrimination, civil and political rights, economic and social rights, discrimination against women, torture, and children's rights. Regional systems in Europe, the Americas, and Africa added courts or commissions.

Individual responsibility also expanded. After the 1945 Nurnberg Charter and the prosecution of Nazi war criminals, individuals could be directly liable under international law. Modern international criminal law developed around genocide, war crimes, crimes against humanity, and related offenses.

## Key Events & Developments

- 1598 Gentili's law-of-war work: Helped secularize international legal thought.
- 1625 Grotius's synthesis: Organized the law of war and peace into a lasting framework.
- 1933 Montevideo Convention: Stated classic criteria of statehood.
- 1945 United Nations Charter: Became a constitutional treaty for the postwar order.
- 1948 Universal Declaration of Human Rights: Anchored modern human-rights law.
- 1959 Antarctic Treaty: Demilitarized Antarctica and suspended claims.
- 1982 Law of the Sea treaty: Consolidated rules on maritime spaces.
- 1998 Rome Statute: Established the International Criminal Court.

## Interesting Facts

- International law ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_law)) is legal rather than merely moral, but human-rights rules show strong ethical influence.
- Treaties can bind more than 150 parties, making them resemble general legislation.
- Custom can bind states that never signed a treaty.
- Jus cogens rules cannot be overridden by ordinary treaty agreement.
- Recognition is political, but it can strongly support a claim to statehood.
- [[Antarctica]] is one of the clearest examples of a territorial regime built by treaty.
