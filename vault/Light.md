# Light

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> Light is visible electromagnetic radiation whose wave and photon descriptions explain optical phenomena, astronomical observation, photosynthesis, and technologies from lasers to fiber optics. Its study developed from ancient optics through electromagnetic theory and quantum mechanics.

## Introduction

![[attachments/light_001.png|420]]

*Visible spectrum diagram*

Light ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Light)) is electromagnetic radiation detectable by the human eye, within the much wider electromagnetic spectrum. Visible wavelengths run from about 700 nanometres for red to about 400 nanometres for violet, while neighbouring infrared and ultraviolet regions are often called light as well.

The speed of light ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Speed_of_light)) in vacuum is exactly 299,792,458 metres per second. Light warms Earth, drives weather, sustains photosynthesis, carries nearly all astronomical information from the universe, and underlies technologies such as lasers, holography, fibre optics, microscopes, telescopes, and optical communications.

No single classical picture fully answers what light is. Classical electromagnetism ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Classical_electromagnetism)) treats light as coupled electric and magnetic waves, while quantum theory describes light in discrete packets called photons; quantum mechanics shows that light has complementary wave-like and particle-like aspects.

Ancient theorists debated vision and rays. Pythagoras, Empedocles, Epicurus, Euclid, and Ptolemy developed ideas about visual rays, reflection, straight-line propagation, and refraction before Islamic scholars improved geometrical optics.

The Islamic optical tradition included al-Kindi and especially Ibn al-Haytham, whose [[Principles of Optics (Optics)|Optics]] explained vision as passive reception of reflected light and studied mirrors and the eye. His Latin influence reached Roger Bacon and later European optics.

Early modern instruments sharpened optical theory. Hans and Zacharias Jansen are linked with compound microscopes, Hans Lippershey with the telescope, [[Galileo]] with astronomical telescopic discoveries, Johannes Kepler with lens focusing, Willebrord Snell with refraction, Pierre de Fermat with least time, and Francesco Grimaldi with diffraction.

## Wave Versus Particle Theories

Rene Descartes described light as a pressure wave transmitted through an elastic medium, while Robert Hooke treated light as rapid vibration and Christiaan Huygens developed a detailed wave theory. Isaac Newton instead argued in Opticks that light consists of corpuscles.

Newton's authority supported particle theory through much of the 18th century, but 19th-century interference, diffraction, electromagnetic theory, and later quantum mechanics transformed the debate. Light ultimately required both wave and photon descriptions, each valid in its own regime.

Geometrical optics treats light as rays and works well when objects and apertures are much larger than light's wavelength. Rays travel straight in uniform media, reflect from surfaces, refract at boundaries, focus through lenses, and explain cameras, microscopes, telescopes, mirrors, shadows, and everyday images.

Reflection follows the law of reflection: angle of incidence equals angle of reflection. Smooth surfaces produce mirrorlike images, while rough surfaces scatter rays diffusely so illuminated surfaces can be seen from many positions.

Snell's law describes refraction through boundaries between transparent media: refracted angle depends on the indices of refraction. The index is the ratio of the speed of light in vacuum to its speed in the medium, so transparent media slow light and bend rays.

## Total Internal Reflection

![[attachments/light_002.webp|420]]

*Light refraction through a prism*

Total internal reflection occurs when light in a denser medium strikes a boundary with a lower-index medium above the critical angle. This lets optical fibres pipe light over long distances and makes fibre-optic communications and endoscopes possible.

## Dispersion

Dispersion occurs because the index of refraction depends on wavelength. Newton's prism experiments showed that white light contains a spectrum of colours, and chromatic dispersion explains colour separation, chromatic aberration, rainbows, mirages, halos, and the green flash.

Geometrical optics fails when dimensions approach the wavelength of visible light. Then wave behaviour appears through interference, diffraction, and polarization, requiring models beyond simple rays.

## Interference

![[attachments/light_003.webp|420]]

*Double-slit interference diagram*

Interference follows from wave superposition. When waves overlap, their disturbances add; crests aligned with crests make constructive interference, while crests aligned with troughs make destructive interference.

Thomas Young's double-slit experiment showed that light forms interference fringes. Bright bands occur where path differences equal whole wavelengths, dark bands where path differences equal half-integer wavelengths, proving light has wave-like properties.

Interference requires coherence for stable patterns. Ordinary light often changes phase randomly, while laser light is nearly monochromatic and coherent, making interference effects easier to observe.

Thin-film interference explains colours in soap bubbles, oil films, iridescent feathers, beetle shells, pearls, abalone, and opals. It also supports antireflection coatings, wavelength filters, laser mirrors, telescope mirrors, and interferometric measurement.

## Diffraction

Diffraction ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diffraction)) is the spreading and fringe formation of waves around obstacles or through apertures. Francesco Grimaldi observed it in the 17th century, and Augustin-Jean Fresnel built a systematic theory by combining Huygens's wavelets with Young's interference.

## Single-Slit Diffraction

Single-slit diffraction follows from Christiaan Huygens's principle that every point on a wavefront can act as a source of spherical wavelets. A narrow aperture makes transmitted light spread into the geometrical shadow.

## Diffraction and Wavelength Scale

Diffraction ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diffraction)) becomes important when obstacles or apertures are comparable in size to wavelength. Visible light's short wavelengths make diffraction subtle, while sound waves with metre-scale wavelengths easily bend around everyday objects.

Fresnel's theory predicted Poisson's spot, a bright point at the centre of a circular shadow; Francois Arago confirmed it experimentally. Diffraction also limits telescope and microscope resolution through finite aperture size and Rayleigh's criterion.

Atmospheric diffraction produces coronas around the Moon, glories around an observer's shadow on clouds, and supernumerary rainbow arcs. Pointillism and colour displays also rely on the eye's limited diffraction-based resolution.

The Doppler effect shifts light from approaching sources toward higher frequency and from receding sources toward lower frequency. Astronomical redshifts provide key evidence for cosmic expansion and help measure stellar, galactic, and radar-detected motion.

## Electromagnetic Theory of Light

James Clerk Maxwell unified electricity, magnetism, and optics by showing that changing electric and magnetic fields can propagate as waves. His equations predicted a wave speed matching the measured speed of light.

[[Maxwell]] concluded that light, radiant heat, and related radiation are electromagnetic disturbances propagated through the electromagnetic field. Heinrich Hertz confirmed electromagnetic waves experimentally in 1888 and showed that radio waves share light-like properties.

The electromagnetic spectrum has no theoretical upper or lower limit and is divided historically by production, detection, wavelength, and use. Visible light is only one narrow part of this continuous spectrum.

Classical electromagnetic waves come from accelerating electric charges. Radio antennas illustrate the mechanism, but infrared, visible, and ultraviolet emission from atoms and molecules usually requires quantum explanation.

## Measuring the Speed of Light

Armand-Hippolyte-Louis Fizeau made the first successful terrestrial speed-of-light measurement in 1849 using a rotating toothed wheel and a distant mirror. Jean-Bernard-Leon Foucault improved the accuracy a year later.

Foucault showed that light travels more slowly in water than in air, contradicting Newton's corpuscular prediction and supporting the wave view. This also confirmed that refractive index measures the ratio of vacuum speed to speed in a medium.

## The Michelson-Morley Experiment

A.A. Michelson refined speed measurements and, with Edward Morley, tested whether Earth's motion through a luminiferous ether changed measured light speed. The famous Michelson-Morley experiment found no such difference.

Albert Einstein reconciled the null result in 1905 by postulating that the speed of light is the same in all reference frames. The ether became unnecessary, and light speed became the universe's limiting signal speed and a constant in $E=mc^2$.

In 1983, the speed of light was fixed exactly as 299,792,458 metres per second. The metre became the distance light travels in 1/299,792,458 of a second.

## Polarization

Polarization reveals that light waves are transverse, with oscillations perpendicular to propagation. Erasmus Bartholin discovered double refraction in calcite, and Thomas Young and Augustin-Jean Fresnel connected such effects to transverse waves.

## Polarization States

An electromagnetic wave has electric and magnetic fields perpendicular to each other and to the direction of travel. Light can be linearly, circularly, elliptically, partially, or randomly polarized.

Common sources such as the Sun, flames, incandescent lights, and fluorescent lights emit mostly unpolarized light. Polarization can arise from reflection, scattering, synchrotron radiation, birefringent materials, optically active materials, and polaroid filters.

Edwin Land's polaroid absorbs one electric-field orientation, while Etienne-Louis Malus and David Brewster explained polarization by reflection. Polarizing sunglasses remove horizontally polarized glare from roads and water.

Light transports energy ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Energy)). Solar radiation sets Earth's energy balance, drives weather, powers photosynthesis, and is stored indirectly in fossil fuels; irradiance measures energy flow per unit area.

Light also transports momentum ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Momentum)) and exerts radiation pressure. Though small for ordinary sources, radiation pressure helps balance stellar gravity, shapes comet tails, and enables laser cooling, optical molasses, and optical tweezers.

Interactions of light with matter include reflection, refraction, scattering, absorption, and transmission. Atomic electrons oscillate under the fields of incoming light and reradiate, with interference among reradiated waves determining whether light passes through glass or scatters in air.

Lord Rayleigh's scattering law explains the blue sky and red sunsets because shorter wavelengths scatter more strongly. Nonlinear optics appears at high intensities, especially with lasers, producing effects such as second harmonic generation in crystals.

## Photons

Max Planck solved blackbody radiation in 1900 by proposing discrete energy packets with $E=hf$. Albert Einstein gave the idea physical meaning in 1905, arguing that electromagnetic radiation itself consists of quanta later called photons.

The photoelectric effect showed that electron emission depends on light frequency and material, not merely intensity. Robert Millikan verified Einstein's frequency-energy prediction in 1916, and Arthur Compton later confirmed photon momentum through X-ray scattering.

Visible photons have energies from about 1.8 eV for red to 3.1 eV for violet. Photons power photosynthesis, photovoltaic systems, photochemical reactions, photomultiplier tubes, photoconductive detectors, and ultraviolet ionization or molecular dissociation.

Einstein's relativity also links photons with gravity. General relativity predicts gravitational deflection of light, blueshift toward massive objects, and redshift away from them, even though photons have zero rest mass.

Quantum mechanics resolved wave-particle duality by replacing classical trajectories with wave functions and probabilities. Louis de Broglie, Clinton Davisson, Lester Germer, Werner Heisenberg, Erwin Schrodinger, and Max Born helped establish this framework.

In weak double-slit experiments, single photons arrive as pointlike detections yet gradually build an interference pattern. The pattern gives the probability of photon arrival, not a classical path through one slit or the other.

Richard Feynman called two-slit interference the central quantum mystery. Quantum mechanics rejects a purely classical wave or particle account and treats those pictures as complementary approximations.

Quantum optics studies quantum interactions of light and matter, including single-photon interference, measurement, entanglement, nonlocality, quantum cryptography, and quantum computing. Better sources and detectors since the early 1980s expanded the field.

Emission and absorption of light reflect atomic energy levels. Niels Bohr's 1913 hydrogen model explained spectral regularities by allowing electrons only in stationary states and making photons carry energy differences between states.

Spontaneous emission occurs when an excited atom emits a photon probabilistically and falls to a lower state. [[Einstein]] also predicted stimulated emission, where matching photons induce emission of identical photons, leading eventually to the laser.

## Quantum Electrodynamics

Richard Feynman, Julian S. Schwinger, and Tomonaga Shin'ichiro are credited with the fully developed theory of quantum electrodynamics. QED quantizes electromagnetic fields, treats photons as field excitations, and describes how photons mediate electromagnetic interactions.

QED explains processes such as photon emission, virtual-photon exchange between charges, pair creation, and electron-positron annihilation into high-energy photons. Its precision and success made it a prototype for later field theories.

## Core Equations

Light is described by wave and photon relations:

$$c=\lambda\nu$$

This wave relation connects speed, wavelength, and frequency; for light in vacuum, the speed is fixed, so shorter wavelengths have higher frequencies.

$$E=h\nu$$

This relation says that photon energy is proportional to frequency; higher-frequency radiation carries more energy per photon.

$$n_1\sin\theta_1=n_2\sin\theta_2$$

This gives a compact mathematical form for light is described by wave and photon relations.

## Interesting Facts

- Visible light is only a narrow band in the electromagnetic spectrum.
- Rainbows require refraction, reflection, and dispersion inside water droplets.
- Soap-bubble colours come from thin-film interference.
- Poisson's spot is a bright point predicted by wave theory at the centre of a shadow.
- Blue sky results because air molecules scatter high-frequency visible light more strongly.
- Single photons arrive as particles but build interference patterns over many detections.
