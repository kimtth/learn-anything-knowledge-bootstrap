# Printmaking

## Introduction

Printmaking ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Printmaking)) is an art form in which images are produced, usually on paper but sometimes on fabric, parchment, plastic, or another support, by techniques of multiplication supervised by or carried out by the artist. Fine prints are considered original works of art even though they exist in multiple impressions.

A fine print ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fine_print)) differs from a commercial reproduction because the artist intends from the start to make an etching, woodcut, lithograph, or other graphic work. The technique is not a neutral copying device; each method changes the image through its tools, materials, pressure, ink, surface, and printing process.

Originality in printmaking is therefore not the same as uniqueness. A print is original if the artist conceived and worked it as a print. The number of impressions affects commercial value, but not originality. Later debates over photographic and mechanically produced prints made artist participation and disclosure important.

Modern printmaking often uses edition information. Buyers may need to know the edition size, proof status, who made the plate or block, who printed it, and how directly the artist participated. Older prints sometimes recorded roles with Latin abbreviations such as del., imp., and sculp.

## Processes and Categories

Printmaking methods are commonly divided into relief, intaglio, and surface processes. Surface processes include planographic methods such as lithography and stencil methods such as screen printing. Artists often combine processes to gain mixed effects.

In relief printing, the nonprinting parts are cut or etched away, leaving the design raised. Ink sits on the raised surface and transfers to paper. Woodcut and linocut are familiar forms, but metal, plastic, and built-up surfaces can also be used.

In intaglio printing, the image is below the plate surface. Ink is rubbed into incised lines or grooves, the surface is wiped clean, and damp paper is forced into the recesses under heavy pressure. Engraving, etching, drypoint, mezzotint, and aquatint are major intaglio methods.

## Woodcut and Rubbing

![[attachments/printmaking_001.webp|420]]

*Woodcut printmaking image*

Woodcut is the earliest known relief-printing method, appearing in East Asia in the 8th century and in the West in the early 15th century. The artist cuts away the surface around the design, using knives for fine detail and gouges for larger areas.

Woodcut can produce bold lines and large areas, but texture and tone are also possible through scraping, scratching, hammering, gouge marks, and the natural grain of wood. Old masters preferred fine-grained hardwoods, while modern artists often use coarse softwoods as part of the design.

Rubbing may be the earliest print form. A paper is placed over an incised or carved surface and rubbed with heelball or ink. Rubbings were made from Indian tombstones, Chinese calligraphy, Japanese fish, museum bas-reliefs, colonial gravestones, and European brass plaques.

## Engraving, Drypoint, and Mezzotint

Engraving cuts a design into metal with a burin. The deeper the burin enters the plate, the wider the line. Engraving produces sharp, clean, intense lines, but the tool moves by pushing forward while the other hand turns the plate.

Drypoint scratches lines into metal with a steel or diamond point. Its printing character comes from the burr raised beside the line, which holds ink and prints as a soft, velvety mark. Drypoint plates are fragile because pressure and wiping flatten the burr.

Mezzotint begins by roughening the whole plate so it prints rich black, then working back toward light with scrapers and burnishers. It was used heavily for portraits and painting reproductions in the 18th and 19th centuries. Because the burr layer is delicate, printing must be gentle.

## Etching and Aquatint

![[attachments/printmaking_002.webp|420]]

*Etching plate trays*

Etching uses acid to bite lines or textures into a metal plate. The plate is covered with acid-resistant ground, the design is scratched into the ground, and acid cuts the exposed metal. Copper, zinc, aluminum, and magnesium can be used with different mordants.

Etching is not merely technical; the acid bite, ground, paper, wiping, and plate material all affect expression. Hard-ground etching uses a waxy coating that allows drawn lines to be bitten into the plate after exposure to acid.

Aquatint creates tonal areas. Rosin powder is dusted and fused onto the plate, leaving tiny openings through which acid bites small cavities. Different tones are made by staged biting and stopping out. Aquatint was often combined with line etching and became central to tonal printmaking.

## Lithography and Newer Methods

Lithography depends on the repulsion of water and grease. A greasy crayon or tusche drawing on stone or metal accepts oily ink and repels water, while blank areas retain water and reject ink. It can reproduce the artist's drawing with directness and subtlety.

Cliche-verre combines drawing and photography. The artist scratches a design through an opaque coating on glass, then exposes photosensitized paper through it to create a strong black-and-white print. Camille Corot made notable examples.

Collagraphy is an additive process in which materials such as sand, sawdust, tarlatan, lace, crushed paper, or walnut shells are glued to a support and sealed. It is mainly intaglio but can combine with relief printing. Cellocut, plaster print, and process-print methods also expanded modern printmaking.

## Process Prints and Commercial Methods

Linecut is a simple photoreproductive relief process for black-and-white line drawings. Halftone plates reproduce tonal variation by breaking the image into dots. Rotogravure controls tone through cells of equal size but different depth.

Offset lithography applies lithographic principles to mass production. The image is transferred from plate to rubber blanket and then to paper. Though commercial, such methods were also used by artists, especially as the boundary between fine art and process reproduction changed in the 20th century.

## Early European Printmaking

Early German woodcuts of the 15th century were often religious single figures, then became more complex with architecture, landscape, and decorative borders. Metal dotted prints and intaglio engraving appeared in the same century.

German engraving developed through figures such as the Master of the Playing Cards, Master of the Banderoles, and Master E.S. Their work led toward the great printmakers of the 16th century. The earliest dated intaglio engraving is The Flagellation from 1446.

In [[Italy]], 15th-century printmaking centred on Florence, Venice, and Milan. It included playing cards, book illustrations, mythology, ornament, religious subjects, and portraits. Antonio Pollaiuolo's Battle of the Nudes is a major early engraving.

The Netherlands and Burgundy produced important woodcut book illustrations and engravers such as the Master of the Death of Mary and the Master of the Amsterdam Cabinet, who made rare, painterly, expressive drypoints.

## Durer and the 16th Century

Albrecht Durer was the master of 16th-century German graphic art. He raised engraving to a height it never reached again and also made woodcuts, etchings, and drypoints. His prints treated religion, history, mythology, folklore, and portraiture with Renaissance intellect and technical brilliance.

Other German printmakers included Hans Baldung, Lucas Cranach the Elder, Albrecht Altdorfer, Augustin Hirschvogel, and Hanns Lautensack. Landscape printmaking became increasingly important, and chiaroscuro woodcuts used multiple blocks for tonal effects.

In the Netherlands, Lucas van Leyden rivalled Durer in engraving. Hendrik Goltzius later used an extraordinary variety of cuts and textures. In Italy, Marcantonio Raimondi became technically brilliant but helped turn engraving toward reproduction by organizing work after Raphael.

By the late 16th century, engraving declined as original art while print publishing expanded. Maps, religious pictures, portraits, and illustrations were in high demand during an era of travel, discovery, and religious upheaval.

## The 17th and 18th Centuries

In the 17th century, etching became the leading creative medium because engraving was increasingly commercial and reproductive. Artists valued etching's freedom and painterly flexibility. Jacques Callot, Claude Lorrain, Jose de Ribera, Salvator Rosa, and Guido Reni shaped early European etching.

Rembrandt van Rijn fully explored etching's expressive possibilities and left a major graphic oeuvre. Hercules Seghers experimented with colour intaglio, tinted supports, hand colouring, and unorthodox etched lines. In Flanders, Rubens organized a reproductive workshop, while Anthony Van Dyck etched portrait heads for the Iconography.

In [[Italy]], 18th-century printmaking included Tiepolo's luminous etchings, Canaletto's Venetian views, and Piranesi's architectural imagination. Piranesi's Imaginary Prisons became one of the most powerful print series of the century.

In England, William Hogarth created a national satirical school and helped prompt the first engraving copyright act in 1735. Thomas Rowlandson, William Blake, and Thomas Bewick expanded satire, visionary relief etching, colour printing, and wood engraving.

Francisco de Goya became Spain's first great printmaker and one of the greatest in Europe. Los caprichos, The Disasters of War, La tauromaquia, and Los disparates used etching and aquatint to attack folly, violence, persecution, and cruelty.

## Japan and the 19th Century

Japanese ukiyo-e colour woodcut developed actor portraits, theatre prints, women, landscapes, and multi-panel formats. Torii Kiyonobu, Okumura Masanobu, Suzuki Harunobu, Torii Kiyonaga, Kitagawa Utamaro, Hokusai, and Hiroshige shaped its history.

Hokusai produced an enormous body of drawings and prints, including Thirty-six Views of Mount Fuji and Hokusai manga. Hiroshige became a master of landscape, especially through Fifty-three Stations of the Tokaido. Japanese colour woodcuts later transformed European art.

In [[France]], 19th-century printmaking included Ingres, Delacroix, Gericault, Barbizon landscape etchers, Millet, Daumier, Meryon, Bresdin, Redon, Manet, Degas, Pissarro, Gauguin, and Toulouse-Lautrec. Lithography, etching, cliche-verre, monotype, and woodcut all gained new artistic roles.

## The 20th Century

[[Photography (Photography, History of)|Photography]] made reproductive printmaking obsolete and returned printmaking to creative artists. The experimental attitude of Impressionism accelerated, and the first half of the 20th century became a period of rapid stylistic change.

Pablo Picasso became the monumental figure of modern graphic art, making more than 1,000 prints in many techniques. Braque, Matisse, Rouault, Villon, Chagall, Miro, Max Ernst, Dali, Derain, Dubuffet, Giacometti, Leger, Masson, and others expanded modern French and international printmaking.

German Expressionism made printmaking central again, especially woodcut and drypoint. Lovis Corinth, Kathe Kollwitz, Ernst Ludwig Kirchner, Emil Nolde, Max Beckmann, George Grosz, Ernst Barlach, Erich Heckel, Oskar Kokoschka, Karl Schmidt-Rottluff, and later Georg Baselitz used graphic media for psychological, social, and spiritual force.

## Key Events & Developments

- 8th century: Woodcut appeared in East Asia.
- 1446: The Flagellation became the earliest dated intaglio engraving.
- 1735: William Hogarth helped secure an engraving copyright act.
- 1799: Goya published Los caprichos.
- 1814-1878: Hokusai manga volumes recorded Japanese life and culture.
- 20th century: Photography ended the dominance of reproductive printmaking.
