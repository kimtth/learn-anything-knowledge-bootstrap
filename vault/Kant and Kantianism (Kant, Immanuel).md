# Kant and Kantianism (Kant, Immanuel)

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> Immanuel Kant was a German philosopher who transformed epistemology, ethics, aesthetics, metaphysics, and idealism by examining the conditions and limits of human knowledge and moral action. His critical philosophy engaged rationalism, empiricism, Newtonian science, and Enlightenment moral thought.

## Introduction

Immanuel Kant, born on April 22, 1724 in Konigsberg, Prussia, and dead there on February 12, 1804, was a German philosopher whose work reshaped epistemology, ethics, aesthetics, metaphysics, and later idealism. He joined the major currents of rationalism, associated with Rene Descartes, and empiricism, associated with Francis Bacon, while also responding to Newtonian science, Enlightenment thought, and moral concerns raised by Jean-Jacques Rousseau.

Kant's early life stayed almost entirely within Konigsberg. His parents were Pietist Lutherans, and the moral seriousness of that religious setting shaped him deeply. A pastor helped him receive schooling, and at age eight he entered a Latin school, where he developed a lasting love for classical literature, especially Lucretius. In 1740 he entered the University of Konigsberg as a theology student, but mathematics and physics soon became his strongest interests.

Early scientific work began under the influence of Newtonian physics and rationalist philosophy. Kant first wrote on the problem of living forces and criticized parts of Leibnizian physics. After his father's death in 1746, he left the university without a degree and worked as a family tutor, gaining social polish and limited travel outside Konigsberg.

## Tutor and Privatdozent

Kant's academic career resumed in 1755, when he completed his degree and became a Privatdozent, or university lecturer. His early dissertations showed interests in fire, physical monads, metaphysics, and the principle of sufficient reason. He taught mathematics and physics first, then expanded into logic, metaphysics, moral philosophy, physical geography, fortifications, and even fireworks.

The nebular hypothesis appeared in his Universal Natural History and Theory of the Heavens of 1755. Kant proposed that the Sun and planets condensed from a single gaseous cloud, an idea later independently advanced by Laplace and remembered as the Kant-Laplace hypothesis. His scientific writing also treated race, winds, earthquakes, and the general theory of the heavens.

## Early Metaphysics

Kant's early metaphysics still moved within the world of Leibniz, Wolff, and Baumgarten, but he was already testing its limits. His Physical Monadology compared Newtonian scientific method with German rationalist metaphysics. His New Elucidation analyzed sufficient reason, though he remained cautious and had not yet made the decisive break with Leibnizian assumptions.

As a lecturer, Kant became popular because his spoken style was vivid, humorous, and filled with examples from literature, travel, science, and philosophy. He twice failed to obtain a professorship at Konigsberg and refused posts elsewhere, preferring to remain in his home city. His intellectual range and teaching reputation continued to grow during the 15 years of this period.

## Critic of Leibnizian rationalism

During the 1760s, Kant became increasingly critical of Leibnizian rationalism. In the Inquiry into the Distinctness of the Fundamental Principles of Natural Theology and Morals, he argued that philosophy cannot imitate mathematics. Mathematics begins with clear definitions and constructed concepts, while philosophy must analyze concepts already given in confused or incomplete form.

Kant's criticism also attacked the reduction of real opposition to logical contradiction. Physical forces and causal relations could not be explained simply as formal logical relations. Rousseau's moral philosophy reinforced Kant's belief that a purely synthetic rationalist system was empty, while Newton taught him that sound thought must respect experience and real relations in nature.

The Inaugural Dissertation of 1770 marked a decisive turn. Kant distinguished sensibility from understanding and argued that space and time are not objective properties of things but pure forms of human intuition. Sensible objects are known as appearances, while things in themselves cannot be directly intuited. This prepared the way for his critical philosophy.

## The Critique of Pure Reason

The Critique of Pure Reason ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Critique_of_Pure_Reason)), published in 1781 and revised in 1787, was the result of roughly 10 years of reflection. Kant believed its doctrine was true but worried that its exposition was difficult, and early readers often misunderstood it. He therefore wrote the Prolegomena in 1783 to clarify what any future scientific metaphysics would require.

The first Critique asks how knowledge is possible and where its limits lie. It accepts that knowledge needs experience, but it also argues that the mind contributes necessary forms and concepts. Space and time structure sensibility; categories such as causality structure understanding. Metaphysics becomes legitimate only when it respects these conditions and does not claim direct knowledge of things beyond possible experience.

The book's difficulty partly reflects Kant's university context. He wrote within the technical handbook style of Wolff and Baumgarten, using rigid divisions such as Analytic, Dialectic, and Methodology. Even so, the work revolutionized philosophy by changing the question from what reality is in itself to how objects can be known by finite human minds.

## The Critique of Practical Reason

Kant's ethics rejects the idea that his criticism of speculative metaphysics destroys morality or religion. The Critique of Practical Reason of 1788 and the earlier Groundwork of the Metaphysics of Morals of 1785 explain pure moral principles. The later Metaphysics of Morals of 1797 applies them to law, politics, virtues, and vices.

Moral philosophy parallels the first Critique. As knowledge depends on the relation between sense and understanding, morality depends on the relation between inclination and reason. Moral action is not grounded in desire, advantage, or feeling but in rational obligation. Kant also connected moral reason with belief in freedom, God, and a future life as practical, not speculative, necessities.

## The Critique of Judgment

The Critique of Judgment ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Critique_of_Judgment)), published in 1790, was not part of Kant's original plan but became a bridge between nature and freedom. Its first part studies aesthetic judgment. A judgment of beauty claims general validity, but it is not cognitive proof; it rests on a felt harmony between imagination and understanding during free contemplation.

The second part studies teleological judgment in nature. Organisms seem to have parts that are both means and ends for each other, so mechanical explanation alone seems insufficient. Yet treating nature as literally designed by a supernatural maker would overstep the limits set in the first Critique. Kant therefore treated teleological language as necessary for reflection on living beings, not as theoretical knowledge of supersensible design.

## Last years

Kant's critical philosophy rapidly spread through German-speaking universities, and Konigsberg became a destination for young philosophers. Kant lived by strict habits, and his daily walk was so regular that people were said to set clocks by it. From 1790 his health declined, though he continued writing clarifications, replies, and new works.

Religion Within the Limits of Reason Alone, published in 1793, brought Kant into conflict with Prussian authorities, who thought it too rationalistic and hostile to orthodox Christianity. He agreed not to lecture or write further on religion under King Frederick William II, but after the king's death in 1797 he regarded the promise as no longer binding. Kant died in Konigsberg on February 12, 1804.

## Major Works

Kant's pre-critical writings include works on living forces, universal natural history, physical monadology, optimism, negative quantities, natural theology and morals, the beautiful and sublime, dreams and metaphysics, space, and human races. These writings show his movement from scientific and rationalist problems toward the critical turn.

Kant's critical and post-critical writings include the three Critiques, the Prolegomena, Groundwork of the Metaphysics of Morals, Metaphysical Foundations of Natural Science, Religion Within the Limits of Reason Alone, Perpetual Peace, The Metaphysics of Morals, The Conflict of the Faculties, Anthropology from a Pragmatic Point of View, Physical Geography, Logic, and Education. Together they made Kant central to modern philosophy.

## Key Events & Developments

- 1724: Immanuel Kant was born in Konigsberg, Prussia.
- 1740: Kant entered the University of Konigsberg as a theology student.
- 1755: Kant became a Privatdozent and published Universal Natural History and Theory of the Heavens.
- 1770: The Inaugural Dissertation introduced his mature distinction between sensibility and understanding.
- 1781: The Critique of Pure Reason ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Critique_of_Pure_Reason)) began the period of the three Critiques.
- 1788: The Critique of Practical Reason presented Kant's mature ethical doctrine.
- 1790: The Critique of Judgment ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Critique_of_Judgment)) linked aesthetics and teleology with the critical system.
- 1804: Kant died in Konigsberg.

## Key People

- Immanuel Kant ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Immanuel_Kant)): German philosopher who transformed modern epistemology, ethics, aesthetics, and metaphysics.
- Rene Descartes ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ren%C3%A9_Descartes)): Rationalist thinker whose stress on reason formed part of the background Kant inherited.
- [[Francis bacon|Francis Bacon]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Francis_Bacon)): Empiricist figure whose stress on experience formed another background to Kant's synthesis.
- Isaac Newton ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isaac_Newton)): Scientist whose physics shaped Kant's scientific and philosophical development.
- [[Jean-Jacques Rousseau]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jean-Jacques_Rousseau)): Moral thinker whose work strengthened Kant's concern with the moral order.
- Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gottfried_Wilhelm_Leibniz)): Rationalist philosopher whose system became a major target of Kant's criticism.
- Christian Wolff: Systematizer of rationalist philosophy influential in the German university setting Kant challenged.
- Alexander Gottlieb Baumgarten ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexander_Gottlieb_Baumgarten)): Author of a metaphysics handbook that shaped the academic style Kant inherited.

## Interesting Facts

- Kant spent his entire life in or near Konigsberg and made his farthest travels only about 60 miles from the city.
- The Kant-Laplace hypothesis grew from Kant's proposal that the solar system condensed from a single gaseous cloud.
- Kant's daily walk was said to be so regular that people set their clocks by it.
- The Critique of Pure Reason ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Critique_of_Pure_Reason)) appeared in two editions, and debates about their differences continued among interpreters.
