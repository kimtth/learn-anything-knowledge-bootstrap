# Human Emotion (Emotion)

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> Emotion is a personally significant experience joining consciousness, bodily sensation, and behavior, ranging from brief fear or joy to lasting grief, love, resentment, and remorse. Its varied forms shape survival, relationships, morality, creativity, perception, and mental health.

## Introduction

Emotion ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emotion)) is a complex experience of consciousness, bodily sensation, and behaviour that reflects the personal significance of a thing, event, or state of affairs. It may be brief or lasting, visible or hidden, general or directed toward a particular object, socially appropriate or inappropriate, and sometimes even socially required. [[Aristotle and Aristotelianism|Aristotle]] described emotions as feelings that change judgment and are accompanied by pleasure or pain.

The range of human emotions ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emotion)) is wide. Fear and panic are related but not identical, because panic may be irrational while intelligent fear can depend on information, such as fear of nuclear war. Love, resentment, grief, embarrassment, anger, joy, depression, remorse, and pity vary in duration, intensity, expression, and importance. Some emotions become lasting features of personality, while others pass almost unnoticed.

Emotions matter because they support survival, mental health, relationships, morality, creativity, curiosity, aesthetic experience, perception, and memory. Love ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Love)), respect, and compassion are essential to personal relations, while emotions can motivate both moral and immoral action. What people notice and remember often depends on what they care about, so emotion helps shape how the world is interpreted.

Emotions can also malfunction. Uncontrolled or dysfunctional emotional problems can damage personal well-being and social relationships, making emotional pathology a lasting social concern. The same general system that supports adaptation can become harmful when fear, anger, depression, resentment, or anxiety becomes excessive, misplaced, or persistent.

Emotions have been studied by [[Biological Sciences (Biology)|biology]], psychology, neuroscience, psychiatry, anthropology, sociology, business, advertising, and communications. These perspectives should be treated as complementary, because emotion has several structures at once: bodily processes, expressive behaviour, cognitive appraisal, social meaning, and neural pathways.

The bodily structure of emotion includes changes in heart rate, breathing, skin response, muscles, posture, hormones, and facial expression. Some traditions ask whether bodily change causes emotion or whether emotion causes bodily change, but modern approaches often treat bodily sensation and conscious appraisal as interacting parts of one process.

Expression is another structure of emotion. Facial expressions, gesture, voice, posture, and action tendencies communicate feelings to others and help coordinate social life. Some emotional expressions seem widely recognizable, but cultures also teach rules about when, how strongly, and toward whom emotions should be displayed.

Cognitive structure involves appraisal, or the interpretation of what an event means for a person's goals, values, safety, pride, relationships, or future. Anger, fear, shame, pity, envy, and gratitude depend not only on bodily arousal but also on judgments about harm, danger, responsibility, status, loss, or benefit. This explains why the same event can produce different emotions in different people.

Social and cultural structures shape emotional life. Some emotions depend on social norms, moral expectations, language, roles, and institutions. Remorse after a crime and grief at a funeral may be socially expected, while pride, shame, jealousy, or compassion can be shaped by local ideas about duty, honor, family, gender, religion, and community.

## Neural and brain structures

The core of emotional brain activity lies in the limbic forebrain, including the thalamus, hypothalamus, reticular formation, and amygdala, all below the cerebral cortex. The hypothalamus is linked to pleasure and misery, and the reticular formation may be linked to depression. The amygdala is especially important in fear, evaluation, and emotional judgment.

Joseph E. LeDoux showed that fear from sound can move through a rapid pathway from the auditory system to the thalamus and then to the dorsal amygdala. This thalamo-amygdala pathway produces quick, minimal, automatic evaluation. It helps explain why infants and young children respond emotionally to pain, and why adults may have preferences or judgments before they consciously know their reasons.

Emotion can also be activated through a slower pathway from the thalamus to the neocortex and then into evaluative processing. This cortical route supports cognitive appraisal and conscious interpretation of events. Together, the subcortical and cortical routes show why emotion can be both immediate and reflective, automatic and reason-shaped.

The two hemispheres of the brain seem to contribute differently to emotional processes. The right hemisphere may be better at distinguishing emotional expressions and may be more involved in negative emotions, while the left hemisphere may be more involved in positive emotions. Anxiety, anger, and depression are associated with increased activity in the amygdala and right prefrontal cortex, while positive moods show more activity in the left prefrontal cortex.

## Key Events & Developments

- 384-322 BCE: [[Aristotle and Aristotelianism|Aristotle]] defined emotions as feelings that alter judgment and involve pleasure or pain.
- medieval period: Moral thinkers such as Thomas Aquinas treated emotions as central to good human life and moral disorder.
- modern psychology: Scientific study divided emotion into bodily, behavioural, cognitive, social, and neural structures.
- late 20th century: Joseph E. LeDoux clarified rapid and cortical pathways for fear processing.
- contemporary neuroscience: Research linked emotional states to the amygdala, prefrontal cortex, limbic structures, and hemispheric differences.

## Key People

- [[Aristotle and Aristotelianism|Aristotle]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aristotle)): Ancient philosopher who emphasized emotion's power to change judgment and involve pleasure or pain.
- Thomas Aquinas ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Aquinas)): Medieval thinker associated with moral reflection on emotion and human flourishing.
- Joseph E. LeDoux ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph_E._LeDoux)): Neuroscientist who explained rapid thalamo-amygdala fear processing and cortical emotional appraisal.

## Interesting Facts

- Emotion ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emotion)) can be nearly invisible to observers or strongly shown through face, voice, posture, and action.
- Some emotions last seconds, while love or resentment may last for years and shape personality.
- People may feel an emotion before they can explain why they feel it.
- The amygdala can support rapid fear evaluation before full conscious recognition.
- Positive and negative emotions may involve different patterns of prefrontal cortex activity.
