# Mao Zedong

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> Mao Zedong was the principal Chinese Marxist revolutionary and the founding leader of the People's Republic of China. His career links the Chinese Communist Party, revolutionary warfare, state formation, Marxism-Leninism, and the Cultural Revolution's profound social and political disruption.

## Wade-Giles romanization Mao Tse-tung

![[attachments/mao_zedong_001_mw.webp|420]]

*Mao Zedong and Zhang Guotao in Yan'an in 1938*

Mao Zedong, born in 1893 in Shaoshan, Hunan, and died in 1976 in Beijing, was the principal Chinese Marxist theorist, soldier, and statesman of the communist revolution. He led the Chinese Communist Party from 1935, chaired the People's Republic of China from 1949 to 1959, and remained party chairman until death, making him the central architect of the new China across the era from the party's founding to the postrevolutionary state.

Mao grew from a peasant family background into a student shaped by Western reform ideas, the 1911 Revolution, military service, and study at Changsha's First Provincial Normal School. At Peking University, his work as a library assistant brought him under the influence of Li Dazhao and Chen Duxiu, founders of Chinese communism, just before the May Fourth Movement ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/May_Fourth_Movement)) pushed many young radicals from liberalism toward Marxism-Leninism.

In July 1919, Mao declared that the world, nation, and society belonged to his generation, expressing the militant responsibility felt by young Chinese activists. By 1921, he had committed to Marxism, organized socialist youth activity in Changsha, married Yang Kaihui, and attended the First Congress of the Chinese Communist Party.

Mao worked inside the Kuomintang alliance after 1923, served in Nationalist propaganda and peasant training roles, and became increasingly convinced that China's peasants held revolutionary potential. Chiang Kai-shek's break with workers and peasants in 1927 destroyed the urban strategy, but Mao led survivors of the Autumn Harvest uprising to the Jinggang Mountains, beginning a rural, Red Army-centered revolutionary path.

Mao's road to power moved through guerrilla warfare with Zhu De, the Jiangxi Soviet founded in 1931, defeat by Chiang's encirclement campaigns, the Long March ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Long_March)) of 1934-35, and the Zunyi Conference, where Mao gained de facto party leadership. In the Yan'an period, the anti-Japanese united front, guerrilla expansion behind Japanese lines, land policies, and Mao's writings on practice, contradiction, protracted war, and revolutionary strategy strengthened his authority.

## Jiang Qing and Mao

During the united-front years, Mao argued that China's revolution belonged to the world anti-imperialist struggle while still requiring a Chinese path to Marxism. In 1939, he divorced He Zizhen and married Jiang Qing. His political rivals, including Wang Ming and Zhang Guotao, were weakened as Mao emphasized the Sinification of Marxism, claiming superior understanding of Chinese conditions over Soviet-trained leaders.

The Rectification Campaign of 1942-43 taught Marxist-Leninist organization to new members while attacking blind imitation of Soviet directives. In 1943, Mao gained formal supremacy as chairman of the Secretariat and Political Bureau, and the campaign became a purge against insufficiently loyal elements under Kang Sheng.

## Founding the People's Republic

Mao later recalled that [[Stalin]] had urged cooperation with Chiang Kai-shek in 1945 and doubted a Chinese communist victory. The communist triumph forced accommodation: Mao proclaimed the People's Republic on October 1, 1949, traveled to Moscow in December, and secured a treaty of mutual assistance and limited aid after difficult negotiations.

Early People's Republic policies copied Soviet models because the communists had experience in guerrilla war and local mobilization but little in state administration or large-scale development. A Soviet-guided Five-Year Plan began in 1953. Mao soon broke from gradual Soviet-style industrial assumptions, arguing in 1955 that social transformation could run ahead of technical transformation through mass mobilization and rapid collectivization.

Mao's Hundred Flowers policy in 1956-57 invited criticism, but when criticism challenged party rule he turned harshly against the educated elite. The Great Leap Forward ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Leap_Forward)), launched in 1958, promoted people's communes and rapid transformation, yet lack of resources and administrative capacity produced chaos, economic disaster, and famine. At Lushan in 1959, Peng Dehuai's criticism led to his removal, and Mao increasingly treated criticism as political betrayal.

After 1960, Mao built support in the People's Liberation Army under Lin Biao, denounced privileged bureaucratic and technical elites as new bourgeois elements, and deepened the split with the Soviet Union. The famine and recovery policies of Liu Shaoqi and Deng Xiaoping relied on incentives and household production; by 1962, Mao saw this as a repudiation of the Great Leap and issued the call to never forget class struggle.

## The Cultural Revolution

The Great Proletarian Cultural Revolution ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cultural_Revolution)) was Mao's radical attempt to fight perceived bureaucratic degeneration and eliminate leaders who had opposed him. Beginning in 1966, Red Guards attacked authority under the slogan that rebellion was justified, while victims across the party were humiliated, detained, beaten, tortured, killed, or driven to suicide; Liu Shaoqi died after denial of proper medical care.

Mao still believed in state and party power, rejecting extreme anarchism when Zhang Chunqiao and Yao Wenyuan created the Shanghai Commune in 1967. Revolutionary committees combining cadres, activists, and the army replaced communes, the Ninth Congress in 1969 began party rebuilding, and Lin Biao's death reduced but did not remove army influence. A brief compromise associated with Zhou Enlai in 1971-72 later collapsed into political rhetoric and hostility to foreign influence.

## Assessment

Mao Zedong's career combines major revolutionary achievements with catastrophic later policies. He devised the rural guerrilla strategy that defeated the Nationalists, redistributed land, restored Chinese sovereignty, and gave force to the 1949 declaration that China had stood up. These achievements carried enormous weight in a country marked by social injustice and foreign humiliation.

After 1949, Mao's record became far more ambiguous. The official Chinese verdict of 1981 judged his leadership basically correct until summer 1957 but mixed or wrong afterward. His goals of fighting bureaucracy, encouraging participation, and pursuing self-reliance were often significant, and industrialization under his rule laid groundwork for later growth, but the Great Leap Forward ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Leap_Forward)) and Cultural Revolution ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cultural_Revolution)) caused famine, violence, political destruction, and lasting trauma.

## Key Events & Developments

- 1911: The revolution against the Qing exposed Mao Zedong to military and political upheaval.
- 1921: Mao attended the First Congress of the Chinese Communist Party.
- 1927: Mao moved revolutionary struggle toward rural guerrilla bases after Chiang's purge.
- 1934-35: The Long March ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Long_March)) led to Mao's de facto leadership at Zunyi.
- 1949: Mao proclaimed the People's Republic of China.
- 1958: The Great Leap Forward ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Leap_Forward)) began.
- 1966: The Cultural Revolution ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cultural_Revolution)) began.

## Interesting Facts

- Mao Zedong first entered revolutionary military life during the 1911 uprising in Hunan.
- The May Fourth Movement ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/May_Fourth_Movement)) helped move Mao's generation toward Marxism-Leninism.
- Mao's slogan about power growing from a gun matched the rural Red Army path he later developed.
- The official 1981 verdict judged Mao's merits greater than his faults, but only by a finely balanced account.
