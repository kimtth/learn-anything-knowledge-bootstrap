# African American Literature

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> African American literature is the body of writing by Americans of African descent. It has used poetry, autobiography, journalism, fiction, and criticism to examine freedom, race, equality, culture, and national identity.

## Introduction and Antebellum Period

![[attachments/african_american_literature_001.webp|420]]

*Phillis Wheatley frontispiece portrait*

African American literature is a body of literature written by Americans of African descent. From its beginning before the American Revolution, it argued with American letters about freedom, equality, race, and national identity. This tradition gained national and international attention long before the late 20th century.

The earliest writers used Christian universal brotherhood, classical literary forms, autobiography, sermons, and journalism to answer claims that black people were intellectually or spiritually inferior. Phillis Wheatley, Jupiter Hammon, and Olaudah Equiano helped establish poetry, religious writing, and the slave narrative ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slave_narrative)) as major foundations of the tradition.

## Early 19th Century and Pre-Civil War Era

In the early 19th century, the standard-bearers of African American literature spoke with heightening urgency of the need for whites to address the terrible sin of slavery. Freedom's Journal ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Freedom%27s_Journal)), founded in 1827, helped create a black newspaper culture that praised black achievement and demanded abolition.

Writers such as David Walker, Maria W. Stewart, and Jarena Lee made literature a form of public agitation. Their works linked antislavery protest with black nationalism, women's public speech, religious authority, and community building.

## The Civil War and Reconstruction

With the outbreak of the Civil War, many African Americans deployed their pens and voices to convince President Abraham Lincoln that the nation was engaged in a war to end slavery.

## Paul Laurence Dunbar and the Turn of the Century

Paul Laurence Dunbar, a 21-year-old Ohioan who published his first volume of poetry Oak and Ivy in 1893, became the most successful writer in Negro dialect poetry, both critically and financially.

## Washington versus Du Bois and the Emerging Novel

As segregation regimes took hold in the South in the 1890s, Booker T. Washington offered self-help and economic progress in Up from Slavery as a path toward dignity. W.E.B. Du Bois challenged that program in The Souls of Black Folk in 1903, insisting that voting rights, civil rights, culture, and liberty were essential. His idea of double-consciousness became one of the central concepts of African American thought.

## The Harlem Renaissance

![[attachments/african_american_literature_002.webp|420]]

*Portrait of Langston Hughes*

The Harlem Renaissance ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harlem_Renaissance)) represented the flowering in literature and art of the New Negro movement of the 1920s, epitomized in The New Negro (1925), an anthology edited by Alain Locke featuring the early work of gifted writers including poets Countee Cullen, Langston Hughes, and Claude McKay, and novelists Rudolph Fisher, Zora Neale Hurston, and Jean Toomer.

## The Great Depression and Urban Realism

Despite enormous outpouring of creativity during the 1920s, the vogue of black writing, art, and culture waned in the early 1930s as the [[Great Depression]] took hold.

## Ellison, Baldwin, and Mid-Century Transformation

In 1949 James Baldwin, a Wright protégé, published Everybody's Protest Novel, criticizing protest fiction's tendency to categorize humanity.

## The Black Arts Movement

The assassination of Malcolm X in 1965 and the espousal of Black Power by civil rights organizations helped galvanize a generation of young black writers into rethinking the purpose of African American art.

## Women Writers and New Directions

Not all African American writers in the 1960s and early '70s subscribed to the Black Arts credo. Robert Hayden, Ishmael Reed, Adrienne Kennedy, Ernest J. Gaines, and James Alan McPherson showed other routes through poetry, parody, avant-garde drama, and short fiction.

From the 1970s onward, Toni Morrison, Alice Walker, Maya Angelou, Audre Lorde, June Jordan, and others brought race, gender, class, memory, sexuality, and family life to the center of the literature. Walker won major awards for The Color Purple, while Morrison later became the first African American to receive the Nobel Prize for Literature.

## Neo-Slave Narratives and Historical Fiction

The reclamation of African American history propelled numerous black novelists into the past, producing a new literary genre called the neo-slave narrative.

## Drama and Late 20th Century Achievement

August Wilson, the most accomplished African American dramatist in the second half of the 20th century, opened his first major play Ma Rainey's Black Bottom on Broadway in 1984 with great critical and commercial success.

## Key Events & Developments

- 1773: Phillis Wheatley publishes the first African American book.
- 1827: Freedom's Journal ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Freedom%27s_Journal)) launches African American newspaper culture.
- 1903: W.E.B. Du Bois publishes The Souls of Black Folk.
- 1925: Alain Locke edits The New Negro, a landmark of the Harlem Renaissance ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harlem_Renaissance)).
- 1984: August Wilson brings Ma Rainey's Black Bottom to Broadway.

## Key People

- Phillis Wheatley ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phillis_Wheatley)): Early poet who proved black literary mastery in the revolutionary era.
- Olaudah Equiano ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Olaudah_Equiano)): Pioneer of the slave narrative ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slave_narrative)).
- Frederick Douglass ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frederick_Douglass)): Major autobiographical voice and abolitionist writer.
- Langston Hughes ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Langston_Hughes)): Major Harlem Renaissance ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harlem_Renaissance)) poet who used jazz, blues, and vernacular speech.
- Toni Morrison ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Toni_Morrison)): Novelist whose works helped define late 20th-century African American fiction.

## Interesting Facts

- The slave narrative ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slave_narrative)) became one of the most important early African American literary forms.
- Double consciousness is one of the tradition's most influential critical ideas.
- August Wilson planned a 20th-century cycle of plays, with one play treating African American life in each decade.
