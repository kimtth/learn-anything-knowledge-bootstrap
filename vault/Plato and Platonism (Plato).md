# Plato and Platonism (Plato)

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> Plato was the Greek philosopher who used dialogue, dialectic, myth, and dramatic conversation to examine knowledge, ethics, politics, soul, and reality. Building on Socratic inquiry, he argued that Forms such as Justice and Beauty make the sensible world intelligible and that human flourishing requires ordered reason and character.

## Life and Importance

![[attachments/plato_and_platonism_plato_001.webp|420]]

*Raphael portrait of Plato from The School of Athens*

Plato, born 428/427 BCE in Athens and died 348/347 BCE in Athens, was an ancient Greek philosopher, student of [[Socrates]], teacher of [[Aristotle and Aristotelianism|Aristotle]], and founder of the Academy ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Platonic_Academy)). His dialogues are among the most influential philosophical works ever written and shaped metaphysics, ethics, politics, epistemology, aesthetics, theology, cosmology, language, mathematics, and science.

Building on Socrates' exposure of ethical ignorance, Plato argued that true understanding requires engagement with Forms ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theory_of_forms)), such as Justice, Beauty, and Equality. These entities are accessible to mind rather than sense and provide the intelligibility of the sensible world. In ethics, he held that the good life requires knowledge, emotional habituation, and harmony among reason, spirit, and appetite.

Plato's importance also lies in his literary method. He wrote philosophy as dialogue, placing arguments inside conversations shaped by character, irony, myth, refutation, and dramatic setting. This form lets readers see philosophy as inquiry rather than as a finished doctrine delivered from outside the discussion.

## Family and Socratic Formation

Plato was the son of Ariston and Perictione. His aristocratic family claimed descent from Poseidon and was related to Solon; less honourably, relatives such as Critias and Charmides were associated with the Thirty Tyrants. Plato's brothers Glaucon and Adeimantus appear in the Republic ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Republic_%28Plato%29)), and his half brother Antiphon appears in the Parmenides.

As a young man, Plato joined the Socratic circle. Socrates wrote nothing, so Plato's Socratic dialogues are a key source for his method: questioning supposed experts about courage, piety, virtue, and justice until contradictions reveal their lack of knowledge. Socrates' trial and execution in 399 BCE deeply affected Plato and made him wary of public life under both oligarchy and democracy.

## Travels and Academy

After Socrates' death, Plato may have traveled in Greece, Italy, and Egypt, though details are uncertain. His later political hopes involved Dion of Syracuse and attempts to influence Syracusan rulers. In Athens, he founded the Academy ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Platonic_Academy)), a school that fostered philosophy, mathematics, and scientific inquiry and became one of the most important institutions in Western intellectual history.

The Academy ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Platonic_Academy)) mattered because it turned philosophical inquiry into an organized community, influencing later students, including Aristotle, and shaping Hellenistic methods of argument.

## Dialectic

Dialectic ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dialectic)), from the verb meaning to converse or talk through, names Plato's philosophical method. In Socratic dialogues it tests a respondent's claim by asking connected questions, revealing contradiction when the supposed expert lacks genuine knowledge. The method presupposes that knowledge cannot be isolated but must belong to a larger explanatory structure.

In later dialogues such as the Republic ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Republic_%28Plato%29)), dialectic becomes more constructive. It is no longer only a test of ignorance but a method for grasping what each thing is, ordering Forms, and reaching the highest principles, especially the Good. Plato's dialectic therefore moves from refutation toward systematic metaphysics.

## Dialogue Form

Plato wrote philosophy as dialogue, not as a simple textbook of doctrines. Speakers question, evade, contradict, persuade, joke, tell myths, and sometimes fail to reach final answers. This form matters because it makes philosophy an activity rather than a list of conclusions. The reader must watch how arguments unfold, how character affects reasoning, and how Socratic questioning exposes the difference between opinion and knowledge.

## Forms and the Good

Plato's Forms ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theory_of_forms)) are not ordinary visible things but intelligible realities that make sensible things what they are. Particular just acts, beautiful objects, and equal things are unstable and imperfect; Justice, Beauty, and Equality themselves are stable and knowable. The Form of the Good is the highest principle, illuminating truth and making knowledge possible.

## Soul, Ethics, and Politics

Plato's moral psychology divides the soul into reason, spirit, and appetite. Justice in the person occurs when reason rules with spirit's support and appetite obeys. His political theory analogously imagines a just city ordered by rulers, auxiliaries, and producers. Philosophy, education, music, gymnastics, mathematics, and dialectic train the soul toward harmony and truth.

## Dialogues and Authorship

Plato wrote in dialogue form, making philosophy dramatic, literary, and argumentative. Some works are regarded as Socratic, others as middle or late dialogues, though chronology is debated. Epigrams and letters attributed to Plato survive; the Seventh Letter is especially important for biography and political projects, though its authenticity is disputed. Some dialogues are uncertain, and many short works are widely considered spurious.

The disputed works matter because authorship affects how scholars reconstruct Plato's development, his Sicilian political ambitions, and the relation between Socratic questioning and later metaphysics.

## Key Events & Developments

- 428/427 BCE: Plato was born in Athens.
- 399 BCE: [[Socrates]] was executed, profoundly shaping Plato's philosophy.
- Academy ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Platonic_Academy)): Plato founded a school for philosophy, mathematics, and science.
- Republic ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Republic_%28Plato%29)): Plato developed major accounts of justice, soul, education, and the Good.
- 348/347 BCE: Plato died in Athens.

## Key People

- Plato ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plato)): Philosopher of Forms, dialectic, soul, and political order.
- [[Socrates]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Socrates)): Plato's teacher and central figure in many dialogues.
- [[Aristotle and Aristotelianism|Aristotle]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aristotle)): Plato's student and later philosophical critic.
- Dion of Syracuse ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dion_of_Syracuse)): Political ally in Plato's Sicilian projects.
- Glaucon ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glaucon)) and Adeimantus: Plato's brothers and interlocutors in the Republic.
- Critias ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Critias)) and Charmides ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charmides)): Relatives linked to the Thirty Tyrants.
