# Disease

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> Disease is a condition that impairs normal bodily function through infectious agents, genetic changes, environmental exposure, or disordered physiological processes. Its study links causes, diagnosis, prevention, and treatment.

## Introduction

Disease is a harmful change in the normal structural or working state of an organism. A diseased body often shows clear signs or symptoms, but the line between health and disease is not always sharp, because normal life depends on many linked systems working together. In pathology, doctors and scientists study the cause of the disease, how it develops, and what changes it makes in the body. This matters because correct treatment depends on finding the real cause, not only on noticing the visible symptoms.

Disease is tied to a loss of homeostasis, the delicate balance that keeps chemical, physical, and functional processes in order. When this balance breaks down, the result may be a simple problem in one organ or a long chain of changes across many systems. Disease can start inside the organism itself, which is called idiopathic, or it can come from outside causes such as environment or infection. The same harmful factor may affect humans, animals, and plants in different ways, because each organism has its own normal state.

## Epidemiology

Epidemiology studies the relationship between host and parasite populations. For infectious disease, the pattern of spread depends on the biology of both sides, including which hosts a parasite can live in and how those hosts behave as populations. Many pathogens cannot survive well outside the host body, so they must move quickly from one infected organism to another. In zoonosis, a disease moves from animals to humans, which shows how closely human health can be linked to animal disease.

The route of infection is often a major part of the disease pattern. Some organisms enter through bites and broken tissue, some through the nose and throat, and others through food and water. A parasite may behave differently when it can live in more than one host species, because that can change how long it survives and how it spreads. In practice, epidemiology is not only about germs, but also about movement, contact, reservoirs, and the wider ecological setting that supports transmission.

## Immunity

![[attachments/disease_002.webp|420]]

*EB1911 - Pathology - Plate III*

Humans and other vertebrates respond to parasites with two broad kinds of immune mechanisms: innate immunity and acquired immunity. Innate immunity is present from birth and includes protective substances and cells such as interferon, macrophages, granulocytes, and natural killer cells. Acquired immunity develops during life and depends on B and T lymphocytes, which recognize foreign material and respond in different but connected ways. B cells make antibodies, while T cells attack invaders directly, so the body uses both defense and memory to limit disease.

The immune response is usually protective, but it can also help cause disease. In rheumatic fever, for example, the reaction against the streptococcus organism also harms the host because the immune system cross-reacts with host tissue. Similar responses can produce allergies, where the body reacts strongly to substances such as pollen or certain drugs. Immune weakness can also make later infections more likely, and in some diseases the body may attack itself through autoantigens, which leads to autoimmune diseases such as rheumatoid arthritis and systemic lupus erythematosus. In this sense, the control of disease is not only about fighting infection, but also about keeping the immune response balanced.

Most diseases can be prevented at least to some degree, except for some idiopathic conditions such as inherited metabolic defects. When disease comes from environmental causes, prevention means removing or sharply reducing the harmful factor. This is often a problem of industrial hygiene and public action, not just personal effort, because many harmful materials are created by human activity. A prevention plan therefore depends on recognizing the risk, changing the environment, and stopping exposure before damage begins.

In infectious disease, prevention works in two main ways: stopping contact between the susceptible host and the source of infection, or making the host less susceptible through selective breeding or artificial immunity. Quarantine can work in theory, but in practice it has had limited success, especially for diseases that spread through the air. Reservoirs in wild animals are also hard to eliminate, though some sources of infection can be reduced, such as tuberculosis in humans or cattle. When disease spreads through living vectors or contaminated objects, prevention must address the route of transmission itself, not only the sick person.

## Treatment

Treatment has two main goals: to restore a normal physiological state and to remove the cause of the disease. The diseased body does not only suffer damage; it also tries to repair itself by growing new tissue, walling off harmful agents, and using defense and detoxification mechanisms. Treatment supports these natural processes rather than replacing them entirely. In therapy, the right plan depends on whether the disease is caused by a metabolic fault, an environmental factor, or an infectious agent.

Some diseases can be treated by correcting the basic fault, such as using insulin for diabetes, and future gene therapy may repair defective genes directly. Other environmental diseases have no specific cure, so the main step is to remove the person from exposure; lead poisoning is an exception because chelating agents can bind the lead and help the kidney remove it. Infectious disease treatment can be highly specific, as with antitoxin for diphtheria, or strongly supportive, as with replacing fluid and salts in cholera. More often, treatment must combine symptom relief, removal of the organism, and support for the body’s own recovery.

## Key Events & Developments

- ancient medicine: Disease is often explained by imbalance, spiritual forces, or environmental harm.
- 19th century: Germ theory transforms understanding of infection and prevention.
- modern period: Public health, vaccination, and laboratory medicine become central to disease control.

## Key People

- Hippocrates ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hippocrates)): Associated with early natural explanations of illness.

- Robert Koch ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Koch)): Important in identifying disease-causing microbes.
