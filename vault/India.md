# India

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> India is a populous, internally diverse South Asian constitutional republic framed by the Himalayas, Indo-Gangetic Plain, Deccan, long coastlines, and island territories. Its exceptional civilizational continuity includes the Indus tradition and the development of Hinduism, Buddhism, Jainism, Sikhism, and later political institutions.

## Overview

India ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/India)) occupies most of South Asia and is a constitutional republic made up of states, union territories, and the Delhi national capital territory, which includes New Delhi. With roughly one-sixth of the world's population, it is one of the largest human societies on Earth and one of the most internally diverse. Its political geography is framed by the Himalayas, the Indo-Gangetic Plain, the Deccan, long coastlines on the Arabian Sea and Bay of Bengal, and island territories in the Arabian Sea and eastern Indian Ocean.

Indian civilization has unusually long continuity. Urban life appeared in the Indus civilization in the third millennium BCE; religious and philosophical traditions associated with Hinduism, Buddhism, Jainism, and later Sikhism developed on the subcontinent; Islamic polities and cultures became major forces from the northwest; European maritime power arrived after Vasco da Gama reached Calicut in 1498; British rule transformed administration and economy; and independence in 1947 was accompanied by partition before the republic took constitutional form in 1950.

## Land Overview

![[attachments/india_002.webp|420]]

*India-0037 - Flickr - archer10 (Dennis)*

India's frontier is partly coastline and partly continental border. Pakistan lies to the northwest; Nepal, China, and Bhutan lie to the north; Myanmar lies to the east; Bangladesh is surrounded by India on three sides; and Sri Lanka lies about 40 miles, or 65 km, from the southeast across the Palk Strait and Gulf of Mannar. The mainland peninsula is flanked by the Arabian Sea to the west and the Bay of Bengal to the east, while Lakshadweep and the Andaman and Nicobar Islands extend Indian territory offshore.

Relief reflects plate tectonics on a continental scale. The Indian-Australian Plate broke away from Gondwana and moved north toward Eurasia; its collision about 50 million years ago produced the Himalayas and bent the crust south of the mountains into a subsiding trough. Eroded Himalayan sediment filled that trough, creating the Indo-Gangetic Plain. India's major structural units are therefore the Himalayas, the Indo-Gangetic Plain ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indo-Gangetic_Plain)), and the Deccan or peninsular plateau.

The Himalayas, whose name means abode of snow, form the world's loftiest mountain system and India's northern wall. The arc runs about 1,550 miles, or 2,500 km, from near Nanga Parbat to Namcha Barwa, crossing India, Nepal, Bhutan, and Tibet. Within India, the Himalayas are divided into the Outer Himalayas, the Lesser Himalayas, and the Great Himalayas. Continued subduction keeps the region tectonically active, with severe earthquakes such as the Bihar disaster of 1934 and the Bhuj earthquake of 2001.

The Outer Himalayas, or Shiwalik Range, are the southern belt, with crests usually between 3,000 and 5,000 feet. Their weak rocks, deforestation, heavy rainfall, and intense erosion feed sediment to the plains. North of them, the Lesser Himalayas rise to roughly 11,900 to 15,100 feet and are cut by deep gorges. Farther north, the Great Himalayas stand mostly above the snow line and contain many glaciers and peaks, including Kanchenjunga, Nanda Devi, Kamet, and Trisul.

The Indo-Gangetic Plain lies between the mountains and the Deccan. It is a vast alluvial foredeep, in places filled with river deposits to depths of 6,000 feet. The plain stretches from the Indus basin in the west across the Ganges basin to the Brahmaputra valley and the Ganges-Brahmaputra delta. Farmers distinguish the older, slightly elevated bhangar alluvium from the newer and more fertile khadar of the floodplains, while the Chambal badlands form a deeply gullied exception to the plain's flatness.

The Deccan is India's old and stable plateau region, descended from Gondwana and made of gneiss, granite-gneiss, schist, granite, and basaltic lava. It generally lies between 1,000 and 2,500 feet above sea level and slopes eastward. The Western Ghats form a steep western escarpment and receive heavy rainfall, while the Eastern Ghats are lower, discontinuous ranges along the Bay of Bengal side. The Nilgiri, Anaimalai, Palni, and Cardamom hills help give southern India its complex plateau relief.

The Western Ghats, or Sahyadri, are important both as mountains and as water sources. Major rivers such as the Godavari, Krishna, and Kaveri rise there or near related highlands and flow generally east across the peninsula. Anai Peak, also called Anai Mudi, reaches 8,842 feet. The Eastern Ghats include the Dandakaranya sector, Arma Konda, the Godavari gorge, and ranges such as the Nallamala, Velikonda, and Palkonda.

Drainage is dominated by rivers flowing to the Bay of Bengal. More than 70 percent of India's territory drains eastward through the Ganges-Brahmaputra system and peninsular rivers, about 20 percent drains to the Arabian Sea, less than 10 percent has interior drainage, and less than 1 percent drains toward the Andaman Sea. The Ganges is about 1,560 miles long, and its sacred headwater, the Bhagirathi, rises near the Gangotri Glacier.

Major rivers structure both geography and culture. The Yamuna, Ghaghara, Gandak, Kosi, and Son join the Ganges system. The Brahmaputra flows through Assam before entering the Bengal delta. In the northwest, the Indus tributaries shaped Punjab, the land of five waters. The Narmada and Tapi are the main west-flowing peninsular rivers, while the Luni, Mahi, and shorter Western Ghats streams also drain toward the Arabian Sea or interior basins.

Indian soils include in situ soils derived from parent rocks and transported soils laid down by water, wind, or ice. Red-to-yellow and lateritic soils are common over acidic rocks in peninsular uplands and are often low in fertility because rainfall leaches soluble minerals. Black regur soils, formed from basaltic lava, are moisture-retentive and traditionally associated with cotton. Alluvial soils dominate the Indo-Gangetic Plain and coastal deltas, with fertile khadar and older bhangar terraces.

[[Climate and Weather (Climate)|Climate]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Climate)) is the world's clearest example of a monsoon system. Over much of India, the year has a hot wet season from about mid-June to late September, a cool dry season from early October to February, and a hot dry season from March to mid-June. The southwest monsoon brings about three-fourths of annual rainfall, while the retreating or northeast monsoon especially affects the southeast. Cyclones from the Bay of Bengal and Arabian Sea can bring violent winds, heavy rain, and storm surges.

Agriculture ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agriculture)) depends heavily on monsoon timing and quantity. Good rains bring bumper crops, while poor rains or floods can cause severe crop failure, especially in unirrigated areas. Temperature ranges are smaller along coasts and larger inland and at higher latitudes. May or June is usually hottest before monsoon rains arrive, and a secondary temperature peak may occur as rains withdraw in September or October.

Vegetation ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vegetation)) follows rainfall and elevation. High-rainfall regions support tropical evergreen and mixed forests; less rainy zones support moist and dry deciduous forests, scrub, grassland, and desert vegetation. The Himalayas have elevation belts from mixed foothill forests through pine, oak, fir, deodar, and spruce to alpine shrubs and meadows. India has about 17,000 flowering plant species, but forest depletion accelerated in the late 20th century.

Animal life is rich because India spans many habitats in the Oriental, or Sino-Indian, biogeographic region. Elephants, Indian rhinoceroses, gaur, wild buffalo, antelope, deer, primates, the hoolock gibbon, the Asiatic lion of Gir, the Bengal tiger, leopards, snow leopards, wolves, bears, and many smaller mammals represent the country's fauna. More than 1,200 bird species and perhaps 2,000 subspecies occur, including peafowl, cranes, flamingos, vultures, pheasants, and bustards.

[[Conservation of Natural Resources (Conservation)|Conservation]] is a major public concern. Elephants, rhinoceroses, tigers, and many other species are endangered or vulnerable. Protected species laws, national parks, biosphere reserves, and environmental clearance procedures seek to slow damage, but the reduction of forests, savannas, and grasslands makes restoration to 19th-century abundance unlikely. The peacock is India's national bird, Keoladeo National Park is internationally important for birds, and the Rann of Kachchh supports a huge flamingo breeding colony.

## People Overview

India's population is multiethnic, multilingual, multireligious, and regionally varied. This complexity grew from long migration, settlement, and intermarriage. The Indus civilization is often thought to have been Dravidian-speaking; Indo-Aryan peoples entered the northwest and north-central regions in the second millennium BCE; and later Persians, Scythians, Arabs, Mongols, Turks, Afghans, and Europeans added political and cultural layers, though European rule had little effect on ethnic composition.

Regional affinities vary across the subcontinent. North-central and northwestern populations often show links with Indo-European peoples of Southwest and Central Asia and southern Europe. Northeastern India, parts of West Bengal, Ladakh, and high Himalayan regions show affinities with Tibetan and Burman populations. Tribal peoples in the Chota Nagpur Plateau and other upland zones have connections with groups such as the Mon and with Southeast Asian populations.

Languages belong mainly to four families: Indo-Iranian, Dravidian, Austroasiatic, and Tibeto-Burman. There are also isolates such as Nahali. Boundaries between language and dialect are often fluid, and long contact has made India a linguistic area, or sprachbund, in which languages borrow words and grammatical forms from one another. Many Indians are bilingual or multilingual, using a mother tongue, a literary language, and a wider lingua franca.

Indo-Iranian languages are spoken by nearly three-fourths of the population. Hindi, used in some form by about three-fifths of Indians, is nationally prominent and official in many northern states and Delhi. Bengali, Marathi, Gujarati, Punjabi, Assamese, Oriya, Kashmiri, Konkani, Nepali, Sindhi, Sanskrit, and Urdu also have official or literary status. Urdu is especially important among Muslims in northern and peninsular India.

Dravidian languages are spoken by about one-fourth of the population, mainly in the south. Kannada, Malayalam, Tamil, and Telugu are constitutionally recognized and have state-level importance. Dravidian-speaking tribal peoples in central India and the Brahui-speaking region of Balochistan suggest a wider ancient distribution. Other recognized languages include Bodo, Manipuri, and Santhali, while tribal languages such as Bhili, Gondi, Kurukh, Ho, and Mundari survive with varying strength.

Hindustani developed from Khari Boli around Delhi and became a Mughal-era commercial and imperial lingua franca. In the 19th century it divided into literary Hindi, using Devanagari and Sanskrit vocabulary, and Urdu, using Perso-Arabic script and much Persian and Arabic vocabulary. English, inherited from British rule, links the central government with many non-Hindi states and dominates elite higher education, commerce, science, scholarship, and much of the influential press.

Religion ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Religion)) is central to social identity. Hinduism is followed by about three-fourths of the population. Muslims form the largest minority, more than one-ninth of the total, and India's Muslim population is among the world's largest. Christians are concentrated in the northeast, Mumbai, and the far south; Sikhs mainly in Punjab; Buddhists in Maharashtra, Sikkim, Arunachal Pradesh, and Jammu and Kashmir; Jains in Maharashtra, Gujarat, and Rajasthan; Parsis in Mumbai and coastal Gujarat; and tribal animist traditions especially in the northeast.

Partition in 1947 made India more predominantly Hindu because perhaps 10 million Muslims emigrated to Pakistan and nearly as many Hindus and Sikhs entered India from Pakistan. Still, major religious minorities remain across the country. Jammu and Kashmir had a Muslim majority, Punjab a Sikh majority, Meghalaya, Mizoram, and Nagaland mainly Christian populations, Arunachal Pradesh many animists, and Lakshadweep a heavily Muslim population.

Hindu sectarian life includes Vaishnavism, Shaivism, Shaktism, reform movements, devotional communities, and many regional cults. Sunni Muslims are the majority among Muslims, with Shiite communities in Gujarat, Lucknow, Hyderabad, and other urban or trading centres. Roman Catholics form the largest Christian group, while Protestant mergers created the Church of North India and Church of South India. Buddhist communities include Tibetan-oriented groups, Theravada-linked border groups, and neo-Buddhists in Maharashtra.

Caste, usually expressed through birth groups called jati, has shaped South Asian social organization for centuries. Jati rules often govern marriage, diet, occupation, ritual rank, and interaction with other groups. More than 2,000 jati names can be identified, though identical names do not always indicate one unified community. Among Hindus, jatis are often ranked within the varna model of Brahmans, Kshatriyas, Vaishyas, Sudras, and historically excluded Panchamas or untouchable groups.

Purity and pollution have traditionally influenced caste hierarchy, especially through ideas about contact with blood, saliva, leather, dirt, hair, and other pollutants. Many non-Hindu communities also recognize caste-like birth groups despite doctrines that might reject caste in theory. Modern law and politics challenge caste discrimination, but caste continues to shape marriage, settlement, occupation, local power, and electoral mobilization.

Settlement ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Human_settlement)) is overwhelmingly tied to cultivation. More than three-fifths of the population lives on fertile Indo-Gangetic alluvium, eastern deltas, or western coastal alluvial and marine soils. In places such as the eastern Gangetic Plain and Kerala, densities can exceed 2,000 persons per square mile. Rural villages are often nucleated and divided into caste wards, with higher-ranking groups nearer the core and lower service castes or minority groups toward the edges.

Urban India includes more than 4,500 places classified as urban, although only about one-fourth of the population is urban in the source period. Mumbai, Kolkata, and Delhi are among the world's largest urban areas. Old cities often have congested walled cores, bazaar streets, religious and caste clustering, and buildings combining workshop, shop, and residence. Colonial cities added civil lines, cantonments, industrial zones, central business districts, parks, and government buildings.

Postindependence urbanization produced planned refugee towns, residential extensions, industrial estates, government colonies, slums, and informal settlements. Urban growth has been driven by bureaucracy, commercialization of agriculture, factory industry, services, and migration. The contrast between planned quarters and crowded informal neighbourhoods remains one of the central social facts of modern Indian cities.

## Economy Overview

![[attachments/india_003.webp|420]]

*Darjeeling, India, Tea plantations on hills*

India's economy is large and diversified, but its vast population makes per capita income much lower than the aggregate scale suggests. After independence, India promoted a mixed economy in which the state acted as planner, regulator, investor, manager, and producer. Five-Year Plans began in 1951; the First Plan emphasized savings and development, while the Second Plan of 1956-1961 shifted toward import-substituting industrialization and capital goods.

Economic reform accelerated in the early 1990s after the collapse of the Soviet model and a domestic crisis. Free-market reforms encouraged private enterprise, high-technology growth, service exports, and a larger middle class. Agriculture still employed about half the workforce but contributed only about one-fifth of GDP, while services became the largest component and manufacturing remained an important industrial base.

Agriculture ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agriculture)) occupies more than half the land and remains the livelihood of a large share of Indians. In fertile plains and deltas, cultivation may cover more than nine-tenths of land. Water is the decisive constraint: Indo-Gangetic agriculture benefits from Himalayan rivers and groundwater in deep alluvium, while peninsular hard-rock regions depend more heavily on seasonal rainfall and have less accessible groundwater.

Farm structure is marked by small and unequal holdings. The average farm is about 5 acres, or 2 hectares, and has tended to decline in size. Soil erosion, alkalinization from irrigation without drainage, hardpan formation, and nutrient depletion reduce productivity in many regions. Irrigation, fertilizers, improved seeds, and crop selection therefore matter as much as cultivated area.

Crops are led by cereals, which occupy more than three-fifths of cultivated land. Rice dominates high-rainfall and irrigated regions; wheat is second and especially important as a winter rabi crop in the north and northwest. Sorghum, pearl millet, maize, and finger millet grow on less fertile or drier soils. Pulses, especially chickpea, are vital protein sources, while sugarcane, oilseeds, spices, tea, coffee, tobacco, cotton, jute, coconuts, coir, and toddy palms support regional economies.

Livestock is economically and culturally important. India has one of the world's largest bovine populations, with cattle and buffalo used for draft power, milk, manure, fuel, leather, and limited meat. Cow slaughter is illegal in many states. Sheep, goats, poultry, eggs, fish, and pigs supplement the rural economy, though religious and caste customs affect meat consumption and animal use.

Forestry and fishing provide fuel, construction material, food, exports, and rural income. Teak, deodar, sal, sissoo, chir pine, bamboo, cane, gums, resins, dyes, lac, tanning agents, and medicinal plants are significant. Marine and freshwater fishing are both important; Kerala is a leading fishing state, carp dominate inland waters, and shrimp exports and aquaculture grew in importance. Mechanization, however, has often shifted benefits away from traditional fishing families.

Resources and power include iron ore, manganese, chromite, bauxite, copper, zinc, lead, gold, coal, petroleum, natural gas, and hydroelectric potential, though per capita resource availability is low. Public-sector mining was once dominant, but private investment increased. Energy development, irrigation, and industrial growth often create conflicts with environmental protection and local displacement.

Manufacturing ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manufacturing)) combines small household craft production with large modern factories. Textiles employ many workers; cotton mills spread across several regions, while jute mills cluster along the Hugli near Kolkata. Heavy industry includes steel, machinery, fertilizers, chemicals, cement, petroleum products, and transport equipment. Tata Steel at Jamshedpur began production in 1911, symbolizing early modern Indian industry.

Finance ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Finance)) is centred on the Reserve Bank of India, founded in 1935. Major commercial banks were nationalized in 1969 and 1980, and regional rural banks were created in 1975 to serve small farmers, tenants, and rural borrowers. Stock exchanges exist, but public banking, state planning, and development finance long shaped investment more than capital markets alone.

Trade and services became increasingly important after reform. Exports include engineering goods, gems and jewelry, garments, leather, chemicals, agricultural goods, software, and services. Imports include petroleum, precious metals, chemicals, and machinery. Service growth includes software and technology centres around Bengaluru and Hyderabad, call centres using English-speaking workers, public administration, tourism, transport, telecommunications, finance, and education.

Transportation and communications tie together a large subcontinent. Railways, national and state highways, ports, aviation, and public works were shaped by colonial infrastructure and postindependence state building. Telecommunications were historically state-dominated, with strong urban-rural gaps, but private cellular service, broadcasting, cybercafes, and later digital networks changed access to information and commerce.

## Government and Society Overview

India's constitution, promulgated on January 26, 1950, combines British parliamentary practice, federalism, judicial review, and a written constitutional framework. It declared India a sovereign socialist secular democratic republic and drew many administrative mechanics from the Government of India Act of 1935. The constitution began with hundreds of articles and schedules and has been frequently amended.

Executive power is formally vested in the president, but real authority normally rests with the prime minister and the Council of Ministers. The president has special powers in emergencies, including national emergency proclamations, president's rule in states, and dissolution of the Lok Sabha after loss of confidence. The prime minister is chosen from the parliamentary majority or coalition and leads the cabinet.

Parliament consists of the Lok Sabha and Rajya Sabha. The Lok Sabha is the more powerful elected lower house, with members chosen from states and union territories; the president may nominate Anglo-Indian members under the older constitutional arrangement. The Rajya Sabha represents states and union territories. Universal adult suffrage, party competition, and the Election Commission are central to the political process.

Federalism ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Federalism)) gives states their own governors, chief ministers, legislatures, and administrative systems. Districts are divided into tahsils, taluqs, subdivisions, and development blocks. Local self-government includes gram pancayats, pancayat samitis, zila parishads, municipalities, and other bodies. Local grants can support development but can also become channels for corruption or patronage.

Bureaucracy is shaped by the Indian Administrative Service and specialized services selected by competitive examination. The Union Public Service Commission and state commissions recruit administrators, police officers, engineers, and specialists. The bureaucracy expanded after independence and often provides continuity, but regulation and procedural complexity can slow administration.

Justice is anchored in an independent judiciary. The Supreme Court reviews union laws, union-state disputes, state disputes, and appeals, while state high courts review state laws, issue writs, hear appeals, and supervise lower courts. Judges are appointed formally by the president. Judicial review and writ jurisdiction make courts important guardians of constitutional rights.

[[Political Parties and Interest Groups (Political Party)|Political parties]] have ranged from the nationally dominant Indian National Congress to regional, caste-based, religious, socialist, communist, and Hindu nationalist formations. Reserved constituencies for Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes rotate to improve representation. Party symbols help illiterate voters identify candidates. Congress was the only continuous nationwide party after independence, but later decades brought coalition politics and stronger regional parties.

Foreign policy emphasized nonalignment, leadership among newly independent states, Commonwealth membership, and participation in the United Nations. India helped launch the South Asian Association for Regional Cooperation in 1985. Security institutions include state police forces, the Central Bureau of Investigation, Border Security Force, Central Reserve Police Force, Central Industrial Security Force, armed services, and a nuclear arsenal under strategic command.

Health and welfare improved after independence, with life expectancy rising sharply, but access to services remains uneven by region, class, gender, and rural-urban location. Public health, vaccination, hospitals, sanitation, water supply, food distribution, and welfare programs all became state responsibilities. Housing shortages, homelessness, slums, high urban prices, unreliable piped water, and limited sewage systems remain persistent problems.

[[Education]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Education)) is constitutionally valued, with free and compulsory education to age 14 included among directive principles. Literacy rose greatly, but disparities by gender, caste, class, and state persisted; Kerala approached universal literacy, while Bihar lagged. Education is divided into primary, middle, lower secondary, higher secondary, and university levels, with English-language private and church schools especially prized by affluent families.

Higher education expanded from elite metropolitan institutions to smaller cities and state universities. The University Grants Commission, state language policies, reserved quotas, and growing demand transformed universities. Elite institutions still produced many highly trained graduates, while weak primary and secondary preparation, politicization, and resource shortages strained the system.

## Cultural Life Overview

Indian culture is polyglot, plural, and regionally rooted. Urban middle-class lifestyles may resemble global patterns, but rural life often remains tied to local agriculture, caste, kinship, ritual, and manual labour. Even where Western influence is strong, Indian food, clothing, films, music, festivals, religious practices, and family customs remain central.

Family life is shaped by ideals of hierarchy, age, gender, marriage, and kin obligation. The joint-family ideal recognizes a senior male head and a senior woman who organizes domestic tasks among women. Sons are often valued more than daughters, which has contributed to unequal care, female mortality, allegations of female infanticide, sex-selective abortion, dowry pressures, domestic conflict, and dowry deaths.

Cuisine varies strongly by region and religion. Rice is common in the east and south, chapati in the north and northwest, and millet breads in drier western regions. Dal, vegetables, yogurt, pickles, spices, tea, and coffee structure everyday meals. Meat consumption differs by caste, region, and religion, while the cow's sacred status in Hindu life shapes dietary rules and law.

Clothing also varies regionally. Men wear dhotis, lungis, turbans, shawls, shirts, trousers, or Western-style garments depending on class, religion, and locality. Women commonly wear saris, though shalwar-kamiz is important in Punjab and cities, and skirts with blouses occur in Rajasthan and Gujarat. Sandals, regional jewelry, tribal dress, and urban Western clothing add further variety.

Architecture ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Architecture)) is one of India's greatest artistic achievements. Its long sequence includes Buddhist caves at Ajanta, rock-cut and structural temples at Ellora, the Sun Temple at Konarak, temple cities such as Bhubaneshwar, Khajuraho, and Kanchipuram, Mughal monuments such as Humayun's tomb and the Taj Mahal, and modern works such as Le Corbusier's High Court at Chandigarh and Charles Correa's Bhopal State Assembly.

Visual and craft arts include painting, embroidery, pottery, woodwork, metalwork, sculpture, lacquerware, textiles, jewelry, cave painting, court miniatures, temple sculpture, folk art, and household objects. Patronage came from courts, temples, wealthy individuals, merchants, and communities, while craft traditions continued through hereditary skill, local markets, ritual demand, and export trade.

Dance and music include Bharata natyam, odissi, manipuri, kathakali, kuchipudi, kathak, bhangra, devotional music, Hindustani classical music in the north, and Carnatic classical music in the south. Ravi Shankar and Ali Akbar Khan carried Indian classical music abroad, while Uday Shankar helped popularize Indian dance internationally. Film music became the most widely shared modern soundscape.

Literature, theatre, and film span ancient Sanskrit works, Tamil traditions, regional literatures, Persianate courtly writing, modern Indian-language novels and poetry, and English-language Indian writing. Rabindranath Tagore renewed theatre and literature, while the Mumbai-based film industry, often called Bollywood, became one of the world's largest centres of popular cinema and a dominant form of mass entertainment.

## Historical Overview

Indian history covers a region that, before 1947, included present India, Pakistan, and Bangladesh. Geography helped shape its development: seas protected much of the south, the Himalayas formed a northern barrier, and the northwest and northeast remained corridors for trade, migration, and conquest. The Indus and Ganges plains supported two major early urban phases, first in the Indus civilization and later in the first-millennium BCE Ganges states.

Paleolithic India includes very early stone artifacts near the Shiwalik Range and Rawalpindi area, with later Acheulean hand axes and sites such as Didwana in Rajasthan. Climate shifts in the Great Indian Desert affected technology and settlement. After the Paleolithic, regional communities moved through hunting, gathering, pastoralism, agriculture, copper and bronze use, and eventually wider iron use.

Indus civilization flourished from about 2600 or 2500 BCE to about 2000 BCE, with late and post-urban phases continuing to about 1700 BCE. Its sites extended across the Indus and Saraswati regions, Baluchistan, Kachchh, Saurashtra, Punjab, and even connections toward Afghanistan. Major centres included Mohenjo-daro, Harappa, Kalibangan, Lothal, Dholavira, Surkotada, and Shortughai.

Harappan cities had grid plans, citadel mounds, defensive brick walls, lower towns, standardized bricks, drains, bathrooms, and craft quarters. Mohenjo-daro had a raised citadel, the Great Bath, flood deposits, drains, and large public structures. Harappa had granary platforms, furnaces, workers' quarters, and cemeteries. Kalibangan preserves early and mature phases, while Lothal may have functioned as a port and trading centre with a dock-like basin.

Indus economy included floodplain agriculture, wheat, barley, rice at Lothal, cotton, dates, peas, sesame, cattle, buffalo, sheep, goats, pigs, dogs, cats, camels, asses, and debated horse evidence. Bullock carts, pack animals, riverboats, seagoing craft, standardized weights, seals, metallurgy, bead making, shell work, ivory, faience, pottery, and long-distance trade with the Persian Gulf and beyond show urban sophistication.

Indus art and religion left stone figures, bronze dancers and animals, terra-cotta mother figures, toys, painted pottery, and seals showing animals such as unicorns, bulls, elephants, rhinoceroses, and tigers. Motifs later associated with Indian tradition, such as ritual bathing, bull imagery, tree spirits, seated yogi-like figures, and linga-like objects, suggest continuity, though the undeciphered script leaves many questions unresolved.

Early Vedic society followed the decline of the Indus urban system and the arrival or expansion of Indo-Aryan-speaking groups in northwestern and north-central India, roughly from 2000 to 1500 BCE. Pastoral and agrarian communities developed into more settled, stratified societies. Later Vedic culture saw growing agriculture, iron use, lineage polities, ritual specialization, social ranking, and the religious speculation preserved in the Brahmanas, Aranyakas, and Upanishads.

First-millennium BCE India produced the mahajanapadas, or major states, and renewed urban life in the Ganges basin. States and regions included Gandhara, Kamboja, Kuru-Pancala, Matsya, Kashi, Koshala, Avanti, Vatsa, Cedi, Malla, Vrijji, Magadha, and Anga. Some were monarchies, while others were oligarchic republics or clan confederacies. Coins, northern black polished ware, trade routes, guilds, and fortified cities marked economic change.

Religious change in the 6th and 5th centuries BCE produced major heterodox movements. Mahavira shaped Jainism, and Gautama Buddha founded Buddhism. Ajivikas, materialists, skeptics, and other teachers challenged Brahmanical authority. Monastic orders, nuns, missionary activity, ethics, renunciation, and debate over ritual, violence, rebirth, and liberation transformed Indian intellectual life.

The Mauryan Empire arose from Magadha. Chandragupta Maurya overthrew the Nandas and made a treaty with Seleucus I Nicator in 305 BCE, gaining trans-Indus territories in exchange for elephants. Megasthenes visited the Mauryan court, and Kautilya, or Canakya, became associated with statecraft in the Artha-shastra tradition. Jain sources connect Chandragupta's later life with asceticism in southern India.

Bindusara succeeded around 297 BCE and probably extended Mauryan influence into the Deccan. Ashoka, the most famous Mauryan, came to power in the 3rd century BCE and was transformed by the Kalinga campaign of about 260 BCE. His edicts, written in Prakrit and also in Greek and Aramaic in some areas, promoted dhamma: nonviolence, respect for parents and teachers, tolerance, humane treatment of servants, and generosity.

Ashokan rule connected India with Sri Lanka and Hellenistic kingdoms and gave Buddhism imperial support without making the state narrowly sectarian. The Sarnath lion capital from an Ashokan pillar later became the emblem of modern India. After Ashoka, Mauryan power contracted rapidly. The last Maurya, Brihadratha, was assassinated in 185 BCE by Pushyamitra, founder of the Shunga dynasty.

Post-Mauryan India from about 150 BCE to 300 CE was politically fragmented but economically and culturally dynamic. Indo-Greeks, Shakas, Parthians, Kushans, Satavahanas, and many smaller kingdoms linked northwestern India to Central Asia and the Mediterranean. Menander, or Milinda, became the best-known Indo-Greek ruler, while Kanishka made the Kushan realm a major power with Buddhist and Central Asian connections.

The Satavahanas ruled much of the northern Deccan, competing with Shaka satraps and supporting trade, agriculture, and religious patronage. Guilds called shrenis regulated crafts and trade, made donations, used seals, and acted as financial bodies. Coins in gold, silver, copper, lead, and potin circulated. Texts such as the Periplus, Ptolemy's geography, and Roman accounts show the subcontinent's role in Indian Ocean commerce.

Religious and cultural synthesis deepened in the early centuries CE. Buddhist monasteries, stupas, and cave sanctuaries grew at sites such as Karli and Mathura. Gandhara art mixed Indian themes with Hellenistic and Roman styles. Mahayana Buddhism developed alongside other Buddhist traditions, Jain communities divided into Digambara and Shvetambara streams, and Brahmanical religion absorbed local cults while elevating Vishnu, Shiva, Krishna, Rama, and goddess traditions.

Gupta India, beginning with Chandra Gupta I around 320 CE, is often called classical, though its achievements built on earlier centuries. Samudra Gupta, Chandra Gupta II, Kumaragupta, and Skanda Gupta ruled a north Indian empire less centralized than the Mauryan state. Sanskrit literature, temple religion, art, mathematics, astronomy, medicine, law, and philosophy flourished, with figures such as Kalidasa, Vatsyayana, Caraka, Sushruta, and Patanjali linked to the wider intellectual tradition.

Post-Gupta politics featured regional kingdoms, land grants, temple centres, and decentralizing power. In the Deccan, the Vakatakas connected with the Guptas, and the Calukyas of Vatapi rose under rulers such as Pulakeshin II. In the south, the Ceras, Colas, Pandyas, and Pallavas shaped Tamil and Deccan politics, with Mahendravarman I and Narasimhavarman I prominent in Pallava cultural and military life.

Early medieval north India saw a tripartite struggle for Kannauj among the Gurjara-Pratiharas, Palas, and Rashtrakutas. Rulers such as Gopala, Dharmapala, Bhoja I, Devapala, Amoghavarsa, and Krishna III competed across large regions. Rajput lineages, including Pratiharas, Paramaras, Cauhans, and Caulukyas, claimed prestigious origins and built forts, temples, and courts.

The Cholas, centred at Thanjavur, created a powerful south Indian state. Rajaraja I and Rajendra expanded into Sri Lanka, the Maldives, the Malabar coast, and maritime trade routes. Chola administration relied on temples, land revenue, and local assemblies. Later Hoysalas and Pandyas became important, while village councils, merchant associations, irrigation committees, and temple endowments shaped social life.

Religious life in early medieval India included temple Brahmanism, bhakti devotion, Shakti worship, Tantric traditions, Krishna-Radha poetry, Vajrayana Buddhism, Jain patronage, and philosophical schools. Shankara taught nondualism, while Ramanuja developed qualified nondualism. Temple architecture took northern nagara, southern dravida, and mixed vasara forms at sites such as Osian, Khajuraho, Konarak, Bhubaneshwar, Puri, Aihole, Pattadakal, Halebid, Belur, and Somnathpur.

The early Muslim period began with Arab contacts and conquest in Sind in the 7th and 8th centuries but became decisive after Turkish and Afghan military expansion from the northwest. Mahmud of Ghazna raided north India more than 20 times between 1001 and 1027, establishing Punjab as an eastern province while mainly seeking plunder and prestige.

Muhammad of Ghur and his successors established the basis of the Delhi sultanate. Qutb al-Din Aybak, Iltutmish, Balban, Ala al-Din Khalji, and Muhammad ibn Tughluq built and strained the sultanate through military expansion, revenue extraction, noble management, and frontier defense. Timur's invasion in 1398 devastated Delhi and exposed the fragility of central power.

Deccan Muslim states and Hindu-led Vijayanagar grew as Delhi's southern control weakened. The Bahmani kingdom was founded in 1347 by Hasan Gangu, or Ala al-Din Bahman Shah, and fought Vijayanagar over the Tungabhadra-Krishna Doab. Mahmud Gawan centralized Bahmani administration and expanded power before his execution helped hasten decline into successor states such as Bidar, Bijapur, Ahmadnagar, Berar, and Golconda.

Vijayanagar, founded in 1336 by Harihara and Bukka, became the dominant south Indian power. It drew on older Deccan and Tamil traditions while adopting cavalry, artillery, and trade strategies shaped by Muslim neighbours and Portuguese merchants. Krishna Deva Raya represented its high point, while Rama Raya's defeat at Talikota in 1565 shattered the capital's power, though successor polities continued.

The Mughal Empire began when Babur, a Timurid and Chagatai ruler descended from Timur and Genghis Khan, defeated the Lodi forces at the First Battle of Panipat in 1526. His son Humayun lost power to Sher Shah, fled to Iran, and returned only shortly before dying in 1556. Sher Shah's interlude strengthened roads, revenue administration, and Afghan power.

Akbar, ruling from 1556 to 1605, consolidated Mughal power through conquest, alliance, administration, and religious experimentation. He defeated rivals after the Second Battle of Panipat, expanded into Malwa, Gujarat, Bengal, Kabul, Kashmir, and the Deccan, and integrated Rajputs through marriage and service. The mansabdari system, jagirs, provincial officers, revenue reform under Todar Mal, trimetallic coinage, and a broad nobility made the empire durable.

Akbar's state reduced sectarian restrictions, abolished jizyah and forced conversion of prisoners, held religious debates in the Ibadat-Khaneh, and issued the mahzar of 1579. His ideal was not modern secularism but imperial sovereignty above sectarian conflict. Mughal administration depended on revenue, military ranking, local intermediaries, and constant negotiation with regional elites.

Jahangir, ruling from 1605 to 1627, faced the rebellion of Khusraw, the execution of Guru Arjun, loss of Kandahar to the Safavids, conflict and settlement with Mewar, Khurram's rebellion, and Mahabat Khan's coup. Shah Jahan, ruling from 1628 to 1658, pursued court grandeur, Deccan settlement, campaigns, and architecture while Mughal resources remained vast.

Aurangzeb, ruling from 1658 to 1707, expanded Mughal territory but exposed imperial weaknesses. Jat, Pathan, Satnami, Sikh, Rajput, and Maratha challenges multiplied. Guru Tegh Bahadur was executed in 1675, Bijapur was annexed in 1686, Golconda in 1687, and Aurangzeb spent his last 25 years in the Deccan fighting Maratha resistance.

Mughal decline followed Aurangzeb's death. Bahadur Shah I tried conciliation but lacked durable control, while Sikhs under Banda Singh Bahadur, Jats, Rajputs, Marathas, governors, and regional chiefs asserted autonomy. Later emperors retained symbolic prestige while provinces such as Avadh, Hyderabad, Bengal, and the Deccan acted increasingly independently.

Regional states between about 1700 and 1850 were not simply signs of collapse. Marathas, Sikhs, Jats, Rajputs, Mysore, Travancore, Avadh, Hyderabad, and Bengal adapted Mughal institutions, local warrior traditions, commerce, revenue systems, and religious identity. Some regions suffered warfare and revenue pressure; others, such as Pune, Srirangapatna, Trivandrum, and Murshidabad, became dynamic centres.

The Marathas grew from western Deccan warrior-peasant society under the Bhonsle clan. Shivaji raided Surat in 1664, negotiated with the Mughals, escaped Mughal pressure, and built a regional kingdom before his death in 1680. Later peshwas turned Maratha power into a confederacy that became the strongest 18th-century challenger to Mughal and British authority.

The Sikhs developed military and political power after the Gurus and the campaigns of Banda Singh Bahadur. Kapur Singh, Jassa Singh Ahluwalia, and other leaders organized armed groups, resisted Afghan power, and gained control around Lahore by the mid-18th century. Ranjit Singh later built a centralized Sikh state in Punjab.

Mysore and the south became central to 18th-century conflict. Haidar Ali and Tipu Sultan challenged British expansion with military reform, diplomacy, and control of a productive inland state. Tipu was killed in 1799 during the Fourth Mysore War. Travancore, the Carnatic, and other southern polities also adapted to the pressure of European trade and Indian warfare.

European expansion began by sea. Vasco da Gama's arrival in 1498 opened a new phase after older Greek, Roman, Arab, and traveler contacts. The Portuguese sought spices, Christians, and control over Muslim maritime commerce. Afonso de Albuquerque took Goa in 1510, Malacca in 1511, and Hormuz in 1515; Goa remained Portuguese until 1961.

Dutch, English, and French companies competed for Asian trade. The Dutch built a Batavia-centred spice monopoly and then used Indian textiles in wider commerce. The English East India Company, chartered in 1600, gained strength after victory over the Portuguese at Swally Hole in 1612 and Sir Thomas Roe's embassy to Jahangir. The French East India Company, backed by Colbert and the crown, established Pondicherry, Chandernagore, Mahe, and Karaikal.

Company rule became territorial after Robert Clive and the grant of revenue rights in Bengal in 1765. The Company Bahadur used Mughal forms, Persian language, Indian intermediaries, and British military force. Warren Hastings became governor of Bengal in 1772, reformed revenue and administration, fought council opponents, and faced impeachment over controversies involving Chait Singh, the Begums of Avadh, and other matters.

Lord Cornwallis disciplined Company service, raised salaries, restricted corruption, reorganized courts and police, strengthened district collectors, and pushed Anglicization. Later expansion through wars with Mysore, the Marathas, and the Sikhs extended British power. Lord Dalhousie promoted railways, telegraphs, public works, grant-in-aid education, universities, annexations, and administrative modernization.

Social reform and new education emerged under colonial influence. Ram Mohun Roy attacked sati, supported women's rights, advocated monotheism, and helped found the Brahmo Samaj in 1828. Hindu College, Young Bengal, English education, missionary pressure, print, legal reform, and new public debate reshaped elite society, while many Indians feared cultural disruption.

The revolt of 1857 grew from annexations, land-revenue disruption, military grievances, fears of religious interference, missionary activity, social reform, railways, telegraphs, and cartridge controversies. Rebels seized Delhi and invoked the Mughal emperor, but British forces held key bases, drew support from Punjab and Sikh troops, and defeated centres of resistance. Rani Lakshmi Bai, Tantia Topi, Henry Havelock, Colin Campbell, and Hugh Rose became associated with the struggle.

Crown rule began with the Government of India Act of 1858, transferring authority from the Company to the British crown. Queen Victoria's proclamation promised support to princes and noninterference in religion. A secretary of state and Council of India worked in London, while viceroys and the Indian Civil Service administered the empire. The army was reorganized with a higher proportion of British troops and artillery held under tighter control.

Imperial frontiers involved Afghanistan, Burma, and northwest borderlands. The Second Afghan War began in 1878, the Treaty of Gandamak followed in 1879, and Abd al-Rahman became emir in 1880. The Durand Line was negotiated in 1893, and the North-West Frontier Province was created in 1901. Burma was absorbed after British wars, extending imperial India eastward.

Indian nationalism grew through educated elites, public associations, newspapers, and economic criticism. The Indian National Congress was founded in 1885 with figures such as A.O. Hume, Dadabhai Naoroji, and later Gopal Krishna Gokhale. Bal Gangadhar Tilak promoted swaraj, festivals, and assertive politics. Partition of Bengal under Lord Curzon provoked swadeshi, boycott, and national education campaigns.

Muslim politics took organizational form when the Muslim League met at Dacca in 1906. The Morley-Minto reforms of 1909 introduced elective principles and separate electorates, with Satyendra P. Sinha joining the viceroy's council. The capital shifted from Calcutta to Delhi, and constitutional reform mixed elite inclusion with communal separation.

[[World Wars (World War I)|World War I]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_War_I)) brought large Indian contributions in soldiers, labour, money, and materials. More than one million troops and noncombatants served overseas, with heavy casualties. Tata Steel produced large wartime output. The Congress-League Lucknow Pact of 1916, associated with Mohammad Ali Jinnah and others, showed a temporary Hindu-Muslim political compromise.

Jallianwala Bagh became a turning point. After arrests in Amritsar, Reginald Dyer fired without warning on an unarmed crowd on April 13, 1919, using 1,650 rounds; official figures gave nearly 400 killed and about 1,200 wounded. Martial law and the Hunter inquiry intensified nationalist anger and undermined faith in British justice.

Mahatma Gandhi transformed nationalism through satyagraha, noncooperation, civil disobedience, village symbolism, and mass mobilization. Noncooperation began on August 1, 1920 and was suspended after the Chauri Chaura violence of 1922. Purna Swaraj, complete independence, was proclaimed on January 26, 1930, later echoed in Republic Day. Gandhi's fast against the Communal Award in 1932 led to compromise with B.R. Ambedkar over representation for depressed classes.

[[World Wars (World War II)|World War II]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_War_II)) widened the crisis of empire. The Cripps Mission offered postwar dominion status and provincial options, but Congress rejected it. The Quit India movement of 1942 brought mass arrests and repression. The Muslim League's demand for Pakistan strengthened, British exhaustion grew, wartime economic strain deepened, and communal violence made partition increasingly likely.

Independence and partition came in 1947. British India was divided into India and Pakistan, producing massive refugee movements, communal killing, and long-term disputes. Lord Mountbatten was the first governor-general of independent India; Jawaharlal Nehru became first prime minister; Vallabhbhai Patel integrated princely states; and [[Gandhi]], trying to stop communal violence, was assassinated on January 30, 1948.

The Nehru era from 1947 to 1964 built parliamentary democracy, planning, secular nationalism, nonalignment, and scientific modernization. The republic began in 1950, with Rajendra Prasad and later Sarvepalli Radhakrishnan as presidents. Nehru led Congress through the elections of 1951-1952, 1957, and 1962. Linguistic state demands, refugee settlement, food production, industrialization, and caste reform shaped domestic politics.

Kashmir became a defining conflict when Pashtun tribesmen entered the princely state in 1947, the ruler acceded to India, and war with Pakistan followed. A cease-fire took effect on January 1, 1949, but a plebiscite was not held. Foreign policy rested on Panch Shila, Afro-Asian solidarity, and nonalignment, but China-India relations worsened after Aksai Chin road construction, asylum for the Dalai Lama, and the Sino-Indian War of 1962.

Indira Gandhi rose after Nehru's death and later Congress struggles. Poor monsoons, wartime spending, rupee devaluation, and political factionalism weakened Congress in 1967. Zakir Husain became the first Muslim president, V.V. Giri won the presidency with Indira's support, and Congress split, producing Congress (I) around her leadership.

The Bangladesh war transformed South Asia. In 1971, Pakistan's military suppressed the Awami League after its electoral victory, causing atrocities in East Pakistan and about 10 million refugees to enter India. After the monsoon, Indian forces advanced, Dhaka surrendered in December, and Bangladesh became independent. India's regional prestige rose, while Pakistan intensified its search for nuclear capability.

The Emergency of 1975-1977 followed inflation, unemployment, protests, and legal challenges. Opposition leaders were jailed, the press was controlled, Sanjay Gandhi gained influence, and coercive birth-control campaigns damaged the regime. The Janata Party won in 1977, but its government under Morarji Desai was unstable. Indira Gandhi returned to power through Congress (I).

Late 20th-century politics became more fragmented. Punjab militancy, Operation Blue Star, and Indira Gandhi's assassination in 1984 led to Rajiv Gandhi's premiership. Rajiv promoted modernization but faced corruption allegations and conflicts involving Sri Lanka and Tamil militants. He was assassinated near Madras in 1991.

Coalition and reform politics followed. V.P. Singh implemented Mandal Commission reservations in 1990, prompting upper-caste protests. L.K. Advani's Ayodhya campaign reshaped Hindu nationalist politics. Narasimha Rao became prime minister in 1991, led a minority Congress government, pursued market liberalization, and faced the destruction of the Babri Masjid in 1992, which caused severe interreligious violence.

BJP and UPA eras brought coalition government. Atal Bihari Vajpayee briefly served in 1996 and then led a BJP coalition from 1998. India conducted five nuclear tests in May 1998, Pakistan responded, and the Kargil conflict erupted in 1999. In 2004, Congress returned through the United Progressive Alliance under Manmohan Singh, with Sonia Gandhi as central party leader. The 2008 Mumbai attacks killed about 174 people and injured more than 300.

## Introduction

India ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/India)) occupies the greater part of South Asia and is a constitutional republic organized into states, union territories, and the Delhi national capital territory, where New Delhi is the capital. With roughly one-sixth of humanity, India is one of the world's largest demographic, cultural, and political arenas. Its history reaches from the urban Indus civilization of the 3rd millennium BCE through Vedic, Buddhist, Jain, Hindu, Islamic, European, British imperial, nationalist, and republican phases.

Indian civilization developed within a subcontinent partly enclosed by the Himalayas, desert margins, seas, and monsoon rhythms. Hinduism, Buddhism, Jainism, and Sikhism arose in this setting, while Islam, Christianity, Zoroastrianism, Judaism, and many tribal traditions became part of its religious landscape. Mathematics, astronomy, architecture, literature, music, dance, political thought, and trade networks gave India a long intellectual and artistic influence beyond its borders.

Modern India emerged from British rule and partition in 1947, became a sovereign republic in 1950, and built a parliamentary democracy under a federal constitution. It has faced poverty, linguistic diversity, caste inequality, religious conflict, regional movements, wars with neighbours, rapid urbanization, and economic transformation. Its scale makes almost every generalization partial, but its recurrent pattern is diversity held inside shifting forms of political and cultural unity.

## Land

*Hampi, India, Rocky landscape of Hampi, Granite rocks of Matanga Hill*

India's frontier includes a long coastline and land borders with Pakistan, Nepal, China, Bhutan, Myanmar, and Bangladesh, while Sri Lanka lies across the Palk Strait and Gulf of Mannar. The mainland projects into the Indian Ocean between the Arabian Sea and the Bay of Bengal. India also includes island territories: Lakshadweep coral atolls in the Arabian Sea and the Andaman and Nicobar Islands between the Bay of Bengal and Andaman Sea.

[[Plate Tectonics|Plate tectonics]] explains the basic relief. The Indian-Australian Plate broke from Gondwana, moved northward, and collided with the Eurasian Plate about 50 million years ago. The collision thrust crustal rocks upward to form the Himalayas and depressed the land to the south, creating the foredeep later filled by the alluvium of the Indo-Gangetic Plain. The three major structural units are the Himalayas, the Indo-Gangetic Plain ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indo-Gangetic_Plain)), and the Deccan.

The Himalayas form India's northern mountain wall and the world's loftiest mountain system. Within India they are usually divided into the Outer, Lesser, and Great Himalayas. They remain geologically young and active, so earthquakes and landslides are common. Major earthquakes since 1900, including the Bihar disaster of 1934 and the Bhuj earthquake in Gujarat in 2001, show the continuing force of the Indian plate pressing into Eurasia.

The Outer Himalayas, or Shiwalik Range, are the southern belt, with crests commonly between 3,000 and 5,000 feet. Their weak rocks, deforestation, heavy rain, and erosion send large quantities of sediment to the plains below. Between ridges lie flat cultivated valleys called duns, where dense settlement contrasts with unstable slopes.

The Lesser Himalayas rise north of the Shiwaliks to heights of about 11,900 to 15,100 feet. They contain ancient crystalline rocks and younger rocks displaced by thrust faulting. Swift rivers, some older than the mountains themselves, cut deep gorges through them as they descend from glaciers and snowfields farther north.

The Great Himalayas form the highest belt, with crests generally above 16,000 feet. Peaks such as Kanchenjunga, Nanda Devi, Kamet, and Trisul stand within or near India's Himalayan arc, while [[Mount Everest]] lies on the China-Nepal border. The Great Himalayas hold most Himalayan glaciers and include fertile longitudinal valleys such as the Vale of Kashmir.

The Indo-Gangetic Plain lies between the Himalayas and the Deccan. It is a former subsidence zone filled by river-borne alluvium to depths as great as 6,000 feet. The plain stretches from the Indus basin through the Ganges basin to the Brahmaputra valley. Older, slightly raised alluvium is called bhangar, while newer floodplain alluvium, khadar, is generally more fertile.

The Deccan is the old, stable plateau region of peninsular India, built on rocks that once belonged to Gondwana. It usually stands between 1,000 and 2,500 feet and slopes generally eastward. Its bedrock includes gneiss, granite-gneiss, schist, granite, and broad basaltic lava flows, and its eroded ranges preserve traces of much older mountain systems.

The Western Ghats, or Sahyadri, form the steep western edge of the Deccan. Their western escarpment receives heavy rainfall, while the eastern slopes are gentler. Important rivers, including the Krishna, Godavari, and Kaveri, rise in or near these mountains. Southern highlands such as the Nilgiri, Anaimalai, Palni, and Cardamom hills contain some of peninsular India's highest peaks, including Anai Mudi.

The Eastern Ghats are discontinuous low ranges roughly parallel to the Bay of Bengal. They include the Dandakaranya sector, Arma Konda, ranges cut by the Godavari, and hills such as the Nallamala, Velikonda, Palkonda, Javadi, and Shevaroy. They are less continuous and less elevated than the Western Ghats, but they shape drainage, settlement, and forest zones along eastern India.

Drainage is dominated by rivers flowing to the Bay of Bengal. More than 70 percent of India's land drains that way through the Ganges-Brahmaputra system and peninsular rivers. About 20 percent drains to the Arabian Sea, while less than 10 percent has interior drainage. The Ganges, sacred to Hindus, runs about 1,560 miles, and its headwaters rise from Himalayan glaciers such as Gangotri.

The Indus tributaries give Punjab its name, from words meaning five waters. The Sutlej, Beas, Ravi, Chenab, and Jhelum connect Himalayan runoff, canal irrigation, and the plains of northwestern India and Pakistan. Farther south, west-flowing rivers such as the Narmada and Tapi cut across peninsular India toward the Arabian Sea, while many shorter Western Ghats streams are seasonally torrential.

[[Soils]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soil)) include in situ soils formed from parent rock and transported soils deposited by water, wind, or glaciers. Red-to-yellow and lateritic soils cover large nonalluvial tracts of peninsular India and are often acidic and infertile. Black regur soils, derived from basalt, hold moisture well and are linked with cotton. Alluvial soils dominate the Indo-Gangetic Plain and deltas, where khadar is especially productive.

[[Climate and Weather (Climate)|Climate]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Climate)) is the world's clearest large-scale monsoon system. Much of India experiences hot wet weather from about mid-June to September, cool dry weather from October to February, and hot dry weather from March to mid-June. The southwest monsoon brings about three-fourths of annual rainfall, making agriculture depend on timing, quantity, and regional distribution of rain.

Monsoon variability makes both abundance and disaster possible. Good rains bring bumper crops; weak or delayed rains cause drought and crop failure. The retreating or northeast monsoon gives important rainfall to parts of southeastern India. Tropical cyclones from the Bay of Bengal and Arabian Sea can bring violent winds, heavy rain, and storm surges, especially along the coasts of Andhra Pradesh, Odisha, and West Bengal.

Temperature patterns vary with latitude, elevation, and distance from the sea. May and June are often the hottest months before the southwest monsoon, while coastal areas have smaller ranges than inland plains. Himalayan hill stations, many developed under British rule, became refuges from oppressive summer heat.

Vegetation ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vegetation)) reflects rainfall. Tropical evergreen and partly evergreen forests grow in high-rainfall regions such as upper Assam, the Western Ghats, parts of Odisha, and the Andaman and Nicobar Islands. Moist and dry deciduous forests, scrub jungle, grassland, and desert vegetation occupy progressively drier zones. Himalayan vegetation changes with elevation from foothill mixed forests to pine, oak, fir, deodar, spruce, alpine shrubs, and meadows.

Flora includes about 17,000 flowering plant species. Forests cover roughly one-fourth of the country, but forest depletion accelerated with agricultural expansion, urban growth, and industrial development. Mangroves fringe some deltaic areas, palms mark tropical landscapes, bamboo is widespread, and revered Ficus species such as the pipal and banyan carry cultural as well as ecological significance.

Fauna belongs largely to the Oriental, or Sino-Indian, biogeographic region. Mammals include elephants, one-horned rhinoceroses, gaur, wild buffalo, antelopes, deer, primates, the hoolock gibbon, lion-tailed macaque, Asiatic lion, Bengal tiger, leopard, snow leopard, wolves, jackals, foxes, bears, and mongooses. Conservation areas such as Gir, Kaziranga, Manas, Periyar, and Bandipur protect some of the best-known species.

Bird life is exceptionally rich, with more than 1,200 species and perhaps 2,000 subspecies. The peacock is India's national bird. Keoladeo National Park near Bharatpur is important for water birds, and the Rann of Kachchh supports one of the world's largest flamingo breeding colonies. Rivers, deltas, backwaters, grasslands, deserts, alpine tundra, and rainforests create many avian habitats.

Other animals include the gavial in northern rivers, nearly 400 snake species, many turtles and lizards, and about 2,000 fish species. Commercially useful insects include silkworms, bees, and the lac insect, which produces shellac and red dye. Conservation laws protect endangered species such as elephants, rhinoceroses, and tigers, but reduced forest, savanna, and grassland areas limit restoration.

## People

India's population is extremely diverse and multiethnic, shaped by migration, intermarriage, conquest, trade, and regional adaptation. The Indus civilization, often thought to have been Dravidian-speaking, flourished from about 2500 to 1700 BCE. Indo-Aryan-speaking peoples entered northwestern and north-central India roughly between 2000 and 1500 BCE, and later Persians, Scythians, Arabs, Mongols, Turks, Afghans, and Europeans added to the social mixture.

Regional affinities differ sharply. North-central and northwestern peoples often show links with Indo-European and Central or Southwest Asian populations. Northeastern groups, some peoples of West Bengal, Ladakh, and high Himalayan regions share affinities with Tibetan and Burman peoples. Tribal peoples of the Chota Nagpur Plateau and nearby regions show connections with Mon and other Southeast Asian groups.

Languages number in the hundreds, with many recognized dialects. They belong mainly to Indo-Iranian, Dravidian, Austroasiatic, and Tibeto-Burman families, with a few isolates such as Nahali. Long contact has made India a linguistic area, or sprachbund, where languages borrow sounds, vocabulary, and grammar from one another. Dialect and language boundaries are often gradual rather than sharp.

Indo-Iranian languages are spoken as mother tongues by nearly three-fourths of the population. Hindi, in one form or another, is used by a very large share of Indians and is official in many northern states and Delhi. Other major Indo-Aryan languages include Bengali, Assamese, Gujarati, Kashmiri, Konkani, Marathi, Nepali, Odia, Punjabi, Sanskrit, Sindhi, and Urdu.

Dravidian languages are spoken by about one-fourth of Indians, mainly in the south. Kannada, Malayalam, Tamil, and Telugu have official state status and long literary histories. Dravidian-speaking groups in central India and the Brahui-speaking region of Balochistan suggest a wider ancient distribution. Smaller Austroasiatic and Tibeto-Burman languages remain important among tribal and northeastern peoples.

Lingua francas include Hindustani and English. Hindustani developed from Khari Boli around Delhi and spread through Mughal commerce and later British administration. In the 19th century it diverged into Sanskritized Hindi in Devanagari script and Persian-Arabic-influenced Urdu in Perso-Arabic script, though the two remain grammatically close and mutually intelligible in common speech.

English ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/English_language)) is an associate official language and a major link between the central government and non-Hindi-speaking states. Fewer than 5 percent of Indians speak it fluently in the source account, yet India's population makes it one of the world's largest English-speaking communities. English is especially important in higher education, commerce, science, scholarship, law, administration, media, and technology.

Religion ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Religion)) is central to many identities. Hinduism is followed by about three-fourths of the population and includes many doctrines, sects, and ways of life. Muslims form the largest minority, more than one-ninth of the population, and India's Muslim population is among the world's largest. Christians, Sikhs, Buddhists, Jains, Parsis, Jews, Bahais, and tribal animists add further diversity.

Partition in 1947 made India more predominantly Hindu because perhaps 10 million Muslims moved to Pakistan and nearly as many Hindus and Sikhs moved into India. Even so, India retained a very large Muslim population. Religious majorities vary by state: Punjab is Sikh-majority, several northeastern states are mainly Christian, Jammu and Kashmir was Muslim-majority in the source account, and Lakshadweep is overwhelmingly Muslim.

Hindu traditions include Vaishnavism, Shaivism, Shaktism, reform movements, local cults, and sects organized around charismatic teachers. Sunni Islam is the majority form among Muslims, with Shiite minorities in places such as Gujarat, Lucknow, and Hyderabad. Roman Catholics form the largest Christian group, while Protestant mergers created the Church of North India and the Church of South India.

Caste is a hereditary form of social organization usually named by jati, or birth group. Jatis regulate marriage, diet, occupation, kinship, and interaction. Hindu jatis are often placed within four varnas: Brahmans, Kshatriyas, Vaishyas, and Sudras, with historically excluded Panchamas or untouchables outside the scheme. Ideas of purity and pollution shaped ranking, though caste practice varies regionally and also affects some non-Hindu communities.

Settlement ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Human_settlement)) is concentrated in cultivable regions. More than three-fifths of the population lives on the alluvial soils of the Indo-Gangetic Plain, the eastern deltas, or western coastal alluvial and marine soils. Densities in parts of Kerala and the eastern Gangetic Plain exceed 2,000 persons per square mile, especially where rainfall, irrigation, and fertile soils support intensive agriculture.

Rural villages are often nucleated and divided by caste or community wards. Dominant and higher castes may occupy central areas, while lower service castes and Muslim groups often live nearer the edges. In arid regions where farming is uncertain, herders of cattle, sheep, goats, and camels maintain reciprocal ties with cultivators.

Urban India contains thousands of urban places, although only about one-fourth of the population was urban in the source account. Mumbai, Kolkata, and Delhi are among the world's largest urban areas. Old cities often have congested cores, bazaars, combined workshop-shop-homes, and residential segregation by religion or caste. Colonial cities added civil lines, cantonments, broad roads, parks, offices, clubs, and industrial zones.

Postindependence urbanization produced planned towns for refugees, new residential colonies, industrial suburbs, and expanding informal settlements. Bureaucracy, commercialization of agriculture, factory industry, services, transport, and migration drove urban growth. Slums and unauthorized colonies grew beside planned districts, making infrastructure, housing, water, sanitation, and transport persistent problems.

## Economy

India's economy is large and diversified, but per capita income remained low in the source account because of the country's enormous population. After independence, India adopted a mixed economy in which the state acted as planner, regulator, investor, manager, and producer. Five-Year Plans began in 1951, first emphasizing savings and later import-substituting industrialization and capital goods.

Economic reform accelerated in the early 1990s after the Soviet system collapsed and India liberalized markets. A growing middle class, a well-trained workforce, and expansion in technology and services helped make India a global centre of high-technology growth. Agriculture still employed about half the workforce, but services became the largest component of GDP.

Agriculture ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agriculture)) remains crucial because roughly half of Indians derive livelihoods directly from it. More than half the country's land is cultivated, and in fertile plains or deltas cultivated land can exceed nine-tenths of the area. Monsoon dependence creates risk where irrigation is lacking. The Indo-Gangetic Plain has major advantages from Himalayan rivers and groundwater, while peninsular hard-rock regions often face more difficult water access.

Crops reflect climate and irrigation. Cereals occupy most cultivated land, with rice dominant in wet and irrigated zones and wheat especially important in northern and northwestern rabi cultivation. Millets, sorghum, maize, and pulses occupy less fertile or drier areas. Sugarcane, oilseeds, spices, tea, coffee, tobacco, cotton, jute, coconuts, and coir support regional economies and export trades.

Livestock are economically and culturally important. India has the world's largest bovine population in the source account. Cattle and buffalo provide draft power, milk, manure, fuel, leather, and limited meat. Cow slaughter is illegal in many states, and shelters care for aged cattle. Sheep, goats, poultry, pigs, fish, and aquaculture add protein, income, and regional specialization.

Forestry and fishing mix commercial and subsistence uses. Teak, deodar, sal, sissoo, chir pine, bamboo, cane, gums, resins, lac, medicinal plants, fuelwood, and charcoal are important, but deforestation and overgrazing have damaged forests. Marine and freshwater fisheries are both significant; Kerala is a leading fishing state, and shrimp exports, inland aquaculture, carp, sardines, and mackerel matter economically.

Resources and power include iron ore, ferroalloys, bauxite, copper, zinc, lead, gold, coal, petroleum, hydroelectricity, and other minerals, though per capita availability of land, water, timber, and petroleum is limited. Public ownership once dominated mining and heavy industry, but private investment became more important after liberalization.

Manufacturing ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manufacturing)) combines household crafts, small workshops, and large factories. Many workers remain in small-scale production, but most value comes from factory industry. Textiles employ many people; cotton mills are widespread, and jute mills concentrate along the Hugli near Kolkata. Steel, machinery, fertilizers, chemicals, aluminum, cement, engineering goods, processed foods, and consumer goods show the scale of industrial diversification.

Finance ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Finance)) developed under strong state influence. The Reserve Bank of India was founded in 1935, major commercial banks were nationalized in 1969 and 1980, and regional rural banks were created in 1975 to serve small farmers and weaken dependence on village moneylenders. Stock exchanges exist, but banking and public finance long played larger roles than securities markets.

Trade ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trade)) has often been modest relative to the size of the domestic economy. Exports include engineering products, gems and jewelry, garments, leather, chemicals, agricultural products, fish, and services. Imports include petroleum, precious metals, chemicals, machinery, and technology. Major partners include the United States, the European Union, China, Hong Kong, and the United Arab Emirates.

Services expanded rapidly in government, construction, finance, education, transport, telecommunications, software, business services, call centres, and tourism. Bengaluru and Hyderabad became especially important for information technology. English-speaking labour and technical education helped global service integration, while domestic tourism and foreign visitors brought further income.

Infrastructure includes one of the world's major railway systems, extensive national and state highways, ports, civil aviation, public works, and telecommunications. Roads and railways connect agricultural hinterlands, industrial regions, ports, and cities, but congestion, maintenance, and uneven access remain chronic. Telephone and Internet access expanded from a state-dominated base into private cellular and digital services.

## Government and Society

The constitution, promulgated on January 26, 1950, created a sovereign, socialist, secular, democratic republic. It drew from British parliamentary practice, American-style federalism and judicial review, and the Government of India Act of 1935. The constitution originally contained hundreds of articles and has been amended many times.

The president is head of state and usually performs ceremonial functions, but emergency powers are significant. The president can proclaim national emergency, impose president's rule in a state, and dissolve the Lok Sabha after loss of confidence. Real executive authority normally rests with the prime minister and Council of Ministers, who depend on majority or coalition support in the Lok Sabha.

Parliament has two houses. The Lok Sabha, or House of the People, is the more powerful chamber and is elected from states and union territories. The Rajya Sabha, or Council of States, represents states and union territories. Universal adult suffrage, regular elections, party symbols, reserved constituencies for Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes, and the Election Commission are central to democratic practice.

Bureaucracy is led by the Indian Administrative Service and specialized civil services selected through competitive examinations. The bureaucracy expanded after independence because the state planned economic development, regulated industry, administered welfare, and managed public works. Regulation could support national integration and development but also created delay, corruption, and administrative rigidity.

Federalism ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Federalism)) gives states governments resembling the union structure, with governors, chief ministers, and legislatures. Districts are divided into tahsils, taluqs, subdivisions, and community development blocks. Local institutions include gram panchayats, panchayat samitis, zila parishads, and municipal bodies. Grants can support development but can also become sources of patronage.

Local government links village society to state administration through a nested structure. A gram panchayat represents the village, the panchayat samiti operates at the community-development-block level, and the zila parishad connects that system to the district. Municipal councils serve urban areas separately. In principle these bodies give local communities a voice in development; in practice they also depend on officials, grants, party networks, caste alignments, and state-level bargaining, so decentralization can strengthen participation while also creating new channels for corruption or patronage.

Justice is built around an independent judiciary. The Supreme Court reviews union laws, hears union-state and interstate disputes, and decides appeals. State high courts review state laws, issue writs, supervise lower courts, and hear appeals. Judges are appointed through constitutional procedures and serve fixed retirement ages.

Foreign policy was officially nonaligned after independence. India helped shape the Nonaligned Movement, remained in the Commonwealth after becoming a republic, joined the United Nations as a charter member, and helped launch the South Asian Association for Regional Cooperation in 1985. Security concerns with Pakistan, China, Kashmir, the Indian Ocean, and nuclear weapons have repeatedly shaped diplomacy.

Security includes state police, central investigation and paramilitary forces, and one of the world's largest armed forces. The army accounts for most military personnel, while the navy, air force, and strategic forces command support wider defense. India developed nuclear capability and conducts internal security operations against insurgency, terrorism, border challenges, and communal violence.

Health and welfare improved after independence, with life expectancy rising by more than 25 years in the source account. Public health, hospitals, vaccination, family planning, water supply, and rural services expanded unevenly. Poverty, malnutrition, sanitation gaps, gender inequality, and regional disparities continued to limit gains.

Housing ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Housing)) differs sharply between cities and villages. Major cities face high land prices, homelessness, slums, overcrowding, and unreliable services. Rural houses use regional materials such as mud, thatch, brick, timber, stone, or tile, and access to piped water and sewage remains uneven. Some villages use cow-dung gas plants for fuel.

[[Education]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Education)) is a constitutional aspiration and an expanding public system. Free and compulsory education to age 14 was included among directive principles. Literacy rose after independence but remained uneven by gender and state; Kerala approached universal literacy while poorer states lagged. Schools run through primary, middle, lower secondary, and higher secondary levels, and higher education expanded into universities, technical institutes, and professional colleges.

## Cultural Life

Indian culture is both ancient and intensely regional. Urban middle-class life may resemble global city life, but rural life often retains older rhythms of family, village, manual work, seasonal agriculture, and local ritual. Food, clothing, film, music, language, religious festivals, and family customs remain distinctively Indian even amid Western and global influences.

Family custom traditionally emphasizes hierarchy by age, gender, seniority, and kinship. Joint-family ideals place authority in senior men and household management among senior women. Male preference has affected nutrition, education, inheritance, medical care, and sex ratios, while dowry practices can produce severe pressures, abuse, and violence.

Cuisine varies widely by region, religion, caste, income, and climate. Rice dominates much of the east and south, chapati and wheat breads much of the north and northwest, and millet breads some drier western regions. Dal, vegetables, yogurt, spices, pickles, tea, coffee, fish, meat, and sweets appear in different combinations. Cow reverence limits beef consumption among many Hindus.

Clothing also varies. Men may wear dhoti, lungi, turbans, shawls, shirts, trousers, or Western-style dress. Women commonly wear saris, with regional draping styles, or shalwar-kamiz, especially in Punjab and cities. Tribal dress, ornaments, caste markers, religious clothing, and urban fashion all signal identity and social change.

Architecture ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Architecture)) is one of India's great artistic achievements. Buddhist caves at Ajanta and Ellora, Hindu temples at Konarak, Bhubaneshwar, Khajuraho, and Kanchipuram, Mughal monuments such as Humayun's tomb and the Taj Mahal, and modern works such as Le Corbusier's Chandigarh and Charles Correa's Bhopal assembly building show long continuity and innovation.

Visual arts include sculpture, painting, embroidery, pottery, metalwork, woodwork, jewelry, lacquer, textiles, and folk crafts. Court patronage, temple patronage, wealthy households, religious institutions, and regional markets sustained traditions. Cave paintings, miniature paintings, bronze icons, temple sculpture, and village crafts all belong to India's artistic range.

Music and dance have classical, folk, devotional, and popular forms. Hindustani music dominates much of the north, Carnatic music the south, and performers such as Ravi Shankar and Ali Akbar Khan carried Indian classical music abroad. Bharata natyam, odissi, manipuri, kathakali, kuchipudi, kathak, bhangra, and film dance express regional and modern creativity.

Literature and performance range from Sanskrit epics and classical drama to medieval bhakti poetry, regional literatures, modern novels, theatre, and English-language writing. Rabindranath Tagore transformed modern literature and theatre. Mumbai's film industry, widely known as Bollywood, became the dominant mass entertainment form, with music and cinema linking language regions and diasporic audiences.

## Early History

Indian prehistory includes Paleolithic stone tools, Mesolithic adaptations, early pastoralism, agriculture, and regional cultural traditions. Artifacts near the Shiwalik region may be about 2 million years old, while Acheulean hand axes appear hundreds of thousands of years ago. Climate shifts in the Great Indian Desert and tool-making centres such as the Rohri Hills show long adaptation to changing environments.

The Indus civilization flourished roughly from 2600 or 2500 BCE to about 2000 BCE, with late and post-urban phases afterward. Its sites spread across the Indus basin, Saraswati-related zones, Baluchistan, Punjab, Kachchh, Saurashtra, the Makran coast, and even links toward Afghanistan. Major centres include Harappa, Mohenjo-daro, Kalibangan, Lothal, Dholavira, Surkotada, and Shortughai.

Indus cities had planned streets, citadel mounds, brick platforms, drains, baths, defensive walls, lower towns, standardized brickwork, craft quarters, and long-distance trade. Mohenjo-daro contained the Great Bath, drainage systems, and large public structures. Harappa had granary-like platforms, furnaces, barracks, and cemeteries. Lothal likely functioned as a port or trading station with bead-maker, goldsmith, and coppersmith activity.

Indus economy rested on agriculture, craft production, animal husbandry, transport, and trade. Wheat, barley, peas, sesame, cotton, dates, and perhaps rice at Lothal were cultivated. Cattle, buffalo, sheep, goats, pigs, dogs, cats, camels, asses, and perhaps horses appear in the record. Bullock carts, boats, river traffic, sea routes, standardized weights, seals, pottery, faience, shell, ivory, and metallurgy supported exchange.

Indus culture left sculptures, bronze figurines, terra-cotta toys and mother figures, painted pottery, and steatite seals showing animals such as the unicorn, bull, elephant, rhinoceros, and tiger. Motifs later associated with religion, including bulls, tree spirits, yogic postures, and linga-like forms, suggest continuities but must be interpreted cautiously. The script remains undeciphered.

Post-Harappan change did not simply erase older cultures. Urban life declined, regional cultures developed, and peninsular traditions show much indigenous development rather than a single migration-driven explanation. Early agriculture, copper-bronze technology, stone tools, craft specialization, trade, and chiefdom-like settlements laid foundations for later regional society.

## Vedic and Early State Formation

Vedic culture developed after Indo-Aryan-speaking groups entered northwestern and northern India. Early Vedic society was pastoral and clan-based, while later Vedic society became more settled, agricultural, stratified, and ritualized. Iron use, plow agriculture, forest clearance, and population growth contributed to the emergence of larger states in the Ganges basin.

Mahajanapadas, or great realms, marked early state formation in the 1st millennium BCE. Sixteen major polities included older northwestern and central regions such as Gandhara, Kamboja, Kuru-Pancala, Matsya, Kashi, and Koshala, along with eastern powers such as Magadha, Anga, Vrijji, Malla, and others. Some were monarchies, while others were oligarchic republics or clan confederacies.

Ganges urbanization brought towns, trade routes, coins, guilds, and new social categories. Punch-marked coins circulated by the 5th century BCE. Northern black polished ware, trade routes from the Ganges to Taxila and from Ujjain through the Narmada to western ports, merchant financiers, and guilds show expanding commerce.

Religious questioning produced Upanishadic speculation and heterodox movements. Jainism, shaped by Mahavira, and Buddhism, founded by Gautama Buddha, arose in the 6th and 5th centuries BCE. They challenged Brahmanical ritual, formed monastic communities, included women, attracted merchants and rulers, and sent teachings beyond their birthplaces.

## Mauryan and Post-Mauryan India

The Mauryan Empire began when Chandragupta Maurya overthrew the Nandas of Magadha. In 305 BCE he made a treaty with Seleucus I Nicator, receiving trans-Indus territories while giving war elephants. Megasthenes observed the Mauryan court, and Kautilya, or Canakya, is associated with the Artha-shastra tradition of statecraft.

Bindusara, who came to the throne about 297 BCE, probably extended Mauryan power in the Deccan. Ashoka, the most famous Mauryan ruler, came to power in the 3rd century BCE and fought the Kalinga campaign around 260 BCE. The suffering of that war helped transform his kingship toward Buddhist patronage and the moral policy called dhamma.

Ashokan dhamma urged nonviolence, tolerance, obedience to parents, respect for Brahmans and teachers, humane treatment of servants, generosity, and restraint. His edicts, written in Prakrit, Greek, and Aramaic in different regions, addressed subjects and neighbouring rulers. The lion capital from Sarnath later became a model for the emblem of independent India.

Mauryan decline followed Ashoka's death. Within about half a century the empire contracted toward the Ganges valley. The last Mauryan ruler, Brihadratha, was assassinated in 185 BCE by Pushyamitra, founder of the Shunga dynasty. Regional powers then replaced imperial unity.

Post-Mauryan India saw Indo-Greeks, Shakas, Parthians, Kushans, Satavahanas, Kalinga rulers, and many local oligarchies. Menander became the best-known Indo-Greek ruler, while Kanishka of the Kushans linked northwestern India with Central Asia and Buddhist developments. Satavahana rulers in the Deccan struggled with Shakas and controlled important inland and coastal routes.

Commerce and culture expanded through guilds, merchant financiers, ports, coins, monasteries, and long-distance trade with the Mediterranean, Persian Gulf, Central Asia, and Southeast Asia. Buddhist stupas, monasteries, Gandhara art, Mathura art, Jain sectarian developments, Brahmanical absorption of local cults, Sanskrit grammar, medical treatises by Caraka and Sushruta, and epics such as the Mahabharata and Ramayana all deepened cultural complexity.

## Gupta and Early Medieval India

The Gupta period, beginning around 320 CE, is often called classical, though many achievements had earlier roots and the empire was less centralized than the Mauryan state. Chandra Gupta I, Samudra Gupta, Chandra Gupta II, Kumaragupta, and Skanda Gupta ruled a northern and central Indian power that supported Sanskrit literature, art, mathematics, astronomy, philosophy, and Hindu religious development.

Gupta culture included poets and thinkers such as Kalidasa and Vatsyayana, the Puranic reshaping of religious memory, philosophical systems, temple worship, sculpture, and advances in science and mathematics. Political power depended on alliances, tribute, subordinate rulers, and regional negotiation rather than only direct central control.

The Deccan and south developed powerful dynasties. The Vakatakas allied with the Guptas. The Chalukyas of Vatapi, especially Pulakeshin II, challenged northern and southern rivals. The Pallavas at Kanchipuram, including Mahendravarman I and Narasimhavarman I, sponsored architecture, literature, and warfare against the Chalukyas.

Northern India after Gupta decline saw shifting powers and the tripartite struggle for Kannauj among the Gurjara-Pratiharas, Palas, and Rashtrakutas. Rulers such as Gopala, Dharmapala, Bhoja I, Devapala, Amoghavarsa, and Krishna III show the interregional scale of early medieval politics.

Rajput lineages claimed prestigious origins and ruled many western and northern states. Pratiharas, Paramaras, Chauhans, and Caulukyas competed for territory, revenue, and ritual status. In Gujarat, Kumarapala and the Jain scholar Hemacandra represent the close link between royal courts, religion, and scholarship.

The Cholas built one of the great south Indian powers from Thanjavur. Rajaraja I conquered northern Sri Lanka and campaigned toward the Maldives, Malabar, and the Deccan, while Rajendra extended Chola power and maritime reach. Chola temples, bronzes, inscriptions, administration, irrigation, and overseas trade made the dynasty a major force in South and Southeast Asian connections.

Early medieval society was decentralized but institutionally rich. Villages, sabhas, ur assemblies, merchant associations, temples, land grants, irrigation committees, and regional courts managed resources and status. Temples became landholders, employers, ritual centres, banks, cultural sponsors, and political symbols.

Religion and art flourished through temple Brahmanism, bhakti devotion, Tantric traditions, Shakti worship, Krishna-Radha poetry, Vajrayana Buddhism, Jain patronage, and philosophical systems such as Shankara's nondualism and Ramanuja's qualified nondualism. Nagara, Dravida, and Vesara temple styles appeared at sites such as Khajuraho, Konarak, Bhubaneshwar, Puri, Aihole, Pattadakal, Halebid, Belur, and Somnathpur.

## Early Muslim Period

Islamic contact began through Arab traders and early raids in Sind and along the west coast, but large-scale northern political change came with Turkish and Afghan military movements from the northwest. Mahmud of Ghazna raided north India more than 20 times between 1001 and 1027, mainly for plunder and prestige, while the Ghurids later created conditions for the Delhi sultanate.

The Delhi sultanate began with rulers such as Qutb al-Din Aybak and Iltutmish, who shaped a mamluk military-political order. Balban strengthened kingship, while the Khaljis, especially Ala al-Din Khalji, expanded authority and centralized revenue, army, market, and noble control. The sultanate brought Persianate administration, Islamic institutions, military slavery, and new forms of urban and agrarian extraction.

The Tughluqs reached a high point under Muhammad ibn Tughluq but also faced rebellion, overextension, and southern losses. Firuz Shah Tughluq later ruled a weaker but still significant state. In 1398, Timur invaded and sacked Delhi, executing captives and devastating wealth, after which 15th-century Delhi became one power among many regional states.

Southern Muslim states arose after Tughluq withdrawal. The Bahmani kingdom, founded in 1347 by Hasan Gangu, struggled with Vijayanagar over the Tungabhadra-Krishna Doab. Firuz Shah Bahmani represented cultural ambition, while the vizier Mahmud Gawan centralized administration and expanded power before his execution helped weaken the kingdom. Successor states included Bidar, Bijapur, Ahmadnagar, Berar, and Golconda.

Vijayanagar, founded in 1336 by Harihara and Bukka, became the dominant Hindu-ruled south Indian empire. Its rulers balanced provincial power, horse trade, military reform, temple patronage, Bahmani rivalry, Orissa rivalry, and Portuguese contact. Krishna Deva Raya marked a high point, while the defeat at Talikota in 1565 shattered the capital's power, though the polity survived in reduced form.

## Mughal India

The Mughal Empire began when Babur, a Timurid and Chagatai ruler descended from Timur and Genghis Khan, turned from Central Asian ambitions to India. He took Kabul and Ghazni in 1504 and defeated the Lodi sultan at the First Battle of Panipat in 1526, using artillery and mobile tactics to take Delhi and Agra.

Humayun struggled to hold Babur's conquests and lost to Sher Shah at Chausa in 1539 and Bilgram or Kannauj in 1540. After exile and Persian support, he recovered Delhi and Agra in 1555 but died in 1556. Sher Shah's Sur interlude left important administrative and road-building precedents.

Akbar, ruling from 1556 to 1605, consolidated Mughal power after victory at Panipat in 1556. He expanded into Malwa, Gujarat, Bengal, Kabul, Kashmir, Sind, and the Deccan, while forming alliances with Rajputs. His administration divided the empire into provinces with governors, revenue officers, military paymasters, judges, and local officials.

Akbar's state integrated Turanis, Iranis, Afghans, Indian Muslims, and Rajputs in a mansabdari system of ranked service and jagirs. Todar Mal shaped revenue reforms, trimetallic coinage stabilized exchange, and imperial ideology moved toward tolerance. Akbar abolished the jizyah, discouraged forced conversion, hosted religious debate, and promoted a nonsectarian vision of sovereignty.

Jahangir, ruling from 1605 to 1627, faced Prince Khusraw's rebellion, the execution of Guru Arjun, loss of Kandahar to Safavid Iran, and the rebellion of Prince Khurram. Mughal relations with Rajputs, Sikhs, Persians, and Deccan powers remained central, while court art and naturalistic painting flourished.

Shah Jahan, ruling from 1628 to 1658, is associated with court grandeur, architectural splendour, and continued imperial expansion. He fought in the Deccan, negotiated with Bijapur, contested Kandahar, and shaped monuments that expressed imperial order. His reign ended in a succession struggle won by Aurangzeb.

Aurangzeb, ruling from 1658 to 1707, expanded Mughal territory but strained imperial cohesion. He faced Jat, Pathan, Satnami, Sikh, Rajput, and Maratha resistance, executed Guru Tegh Bahadur in 1675, annexed Bijapur in 1686 and Golconda in 1687, and spent his final decades in Deccan warfare against Maratha power.

Mughal decline after Aurangzeb did not mean immediate collapse, but the centre increasingly depended on regional elites. Bahadur Shah I tried conciliation but lacked resources. Jats, Sikhs, Rajputs, Marathas, Afghans, and provincial governors gained autonomy, and later emperors lost control even as Mughal legitimacy remained valuable.

## Regional States and European Expansion

Regional states after Mughal weakening included Avadh, Hyderabad, Bengal, Maratha territories, Sikh misls, Jat Bharatpur, Rajput states, Mysore, and Travancore. Some drew on older Mughal administrative forms, while others grew from caste, clan, sectarian, or regional mobilization. The 18th century was not only decline; it was also a period of new centres, armies, fiscal systems, and commercial growth.

The Marathas grew from western Deccan warrior-peasant society under the Bhonsle family. Shivaji raided Surat in 1664, negotiated and fought with the Mughals, built forts, organized revenue, and created a mobile regional power before his death in 1680. Later peshwas and Maratha chiefs made a confederacy that dominated much of 18th-century India.

The Sikhs developed from religious community to military and territorial power. After Guru Gobind Singh and Banda Singh Bahadur, leaders such as Kapur Singh and Jassa Singh Ahluwalia organized revenue and armed groupings. Sikh resistance to Afghan incursions helped create authority in Punjab, later centralized by Ranjit Singh.

Mysore under Haidar Ali and Tipu Sultan challenged British power with military innovation, diplomacy, and state-building. Tipu was killed in 1799 during the Fourth Mysore War. Warfare after 1750 disrupted irrigation, cattle wealth, population, and agrarian stability in many regions.

European sea contact changed Indian Ocean politics after Vasco da Gama reached Calicut in 1498. The Portuguese sought spice trade, Christian alliances, and anti-Muslim maritime control. Afonso de Albuquerque took Goa in 1510, Malacca in 1511, and Hormuz in 1515, making fortified ports central to Portuguese strategy.

Dutch and English companies followed. The Dutch built a Batavia-centred Asian trade empire and used Indian textiles and silver in wider commerce. The English East India Company, chartered in 1600, gained standing after defeating the Portuguese at Swally Hole in 1612 and receiving Mughal privileges through Sir Thomas Roe's embassy to Jahangir.

The French created a state-supervised East India Company under Jean-Baptiste Colbert in 1664, with bases at Pondicherry, Chandernagore, Mahe, Karaikal, and Mauritius. French trade remained smaller than English trade, but French and British rivalry became important in the 18th-century struggle for influence in Indian regional politics.

Company rule became territorial after Robert Clive and the grant of revenue rights in Bengal in 1765. The Company used Mughal forms, Persian administration, Indian intermediaries, and a British-led army. Warren Hastings, governor of Bengal from 1772, reorganized revenue and administration but faced controversies involving the council, Chait Singh, the Begums of Avadh, and impeachment.

Cornwallis and successors disciplined Company service, raised salaries, Anglicized administration, reorganized collectors, courts, police, and revenue, and helped create the Permanent Settlement context. Later wars against Mysore, the Marathas, and the Sikhs expanded British power. Punjab was annexed in 1849, and Lord Dalhousie promoted public works, education, and annexations.

Social reform developed beside imperial rule. Hindu College, the Young Bengal movement, English education, anti-suttee efforts, debates over widow and women's rights, and reformers such as Ram Mohun Roy and the Brahmo Samaj in 1828 shaped new public arguments about religion, society, and modernity.

The revolt of 1857 grew from annexations, land revenue disruption, military grievances, religious anxiety, reform pressures, missionaries, railways, telegraphs, and anger within the Bengal army. Delhi, Kanpur, Lucknow, Jhansi, and central India became major theatres. British reinforcements, Punjab and Bengal bases, Sikh support, and military leadership eventually defeated rebels such as Tantia Topi and Rani Lakshmi Bai.

## British Imperial Rule and Nationalism

Crown rule began after the Government of India Act of 1858 transferred power from the Company to the British crown. A secretary of state and Council of India in London supervised policy, while viceroys and the Indian Civil Service ruled locally. Queen Victoria's proclamation promised support for princes and noninterference in religion.

Frontier policy focused on Afghanistan, Burma, Tibet, and the northwest. The Second Afghan War began in 1878, and the Durand Line was negotiated in 1893 and completed in 1896. The North-West Frontier Province was created in 1901. Burma was absorbed through Anglo-Burmese conflicts, extending British imperial reach eastward.

Indian nationalism took organized form with the Indian National Congress, founded in 1885. Early leaders petitioned for representation and administrative reform. Bal Gangadhar Tilak promoted swaraj and mass politics through newspapers and festivals, while Dadabhai Naoroji articulated economic critique and the demand for self-rule.

Bengal's partition under Lord Curzon sparked swadeshi, boycott, national education, and a more assertive nationalist politics. The Muslim League first met in Dacca in 1906. The Indian Councils Act of 1909, or Morley-Minto reform, introduced elective principles and separate electorates, shaping later communal politics.

[[World Wars (World War I)|World War I]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_War_I)) drew major Indian support, with more than 1 million troops and noncombatants serving overseas and heavy casualties. War contributions intensified demands for political concessions. The Congress-League Lucknow Pact of 1916 showed temporary cooperation between nationalist and Muslim leaders, including Mohammad Ali Jinnah.

Jallianwala Bagh in 1919 became a turning point. After arrests in Amritsar, Reginald Dyer ordered troops to fire without warning on an unarmed crowd in an enclosed space, firing 1,650 rounds. Official figures reported nearly 400 killed and 1,200 wounded. The massacre and martial law discredited liberal imperial claims.

Gandhi's leadership transformed nationalism into mass politics. Noncooperation began on August 1, 1920 and brought boycotts, spinning, resignations, and arrests, but Mohandas Gandhi suspended it after violence at Chauri Chaura in 1922. Civil disobedience and salt protest later carried nationalist action into villages, towns, and international attention.

Purna Swaraj, or complete independence, was proclaimed on January 26, 1930, a date later echoed by Republic Day. The Communal Award of 1932 produced conflict over representation for depressed classes, leading to Gandhi's fast and negotiations with B.R. Ambedkar over reserved seats within the Hindu fold.

[[World Wars (World War II)|World War II]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_War_II)) deepened crisis. The Cripps Mission offered postwar dominion status and provincial opt-out, which Gandhi dismissed as inadequate. The Quit India movement began in 1942; Congress leaders were arrested, tens of thousands imprisoned, and repression spread. The Muslim League grew stronger as British exhaustion and communal polarization intensified.

Partition and independence came in 1947 amid violence, migration, and the creation of India and Pakistan. The subcontinent was divided along religious and political lines, producing one of the largest population transfers in history. Independence did not resolve Kashmir, princely-state integration, refugee settlement, communal fear, or the economic dislocation of partition.

## The Republic

Nehru's era began with Jawaharlal Nehru as prime minister and Vallabhbhai Patel as the other crucial integrator of the new state. Lord Mountbatten served as first governor-general. [[Gandhi]] tried to stop communal killing and was assassinated on January 30, 1948, a shock that helped delegitimize some communal violence.

The republic began in 1950, with elections, Parliament, a president, a prime minister, a Supreme Court, and federal states. Rajendra Prasad and later Sarvepalli Radhakrishnan served as presidents, while Nehru led Congress to victories in 1951-1952, 1957, and 1962. Planning, secularism, public-sector development, and nonalignment defined much of the period.

Kashmir became a central dispute after Pashtun tribesmen invaded in 1947 and the ruler acceded to India. A cease-fire took effect on January 1, 1949, but the promised plebiscite did not occur. Relations with Pakistan remained shaped by Kashmir, refugees, water, war, and competing national identities.

Foreign policy under Nehru used nonalignment and Panch Shila principles, but border realities complicated ideals. India took Goa from Portugal by force in 1961. Tensions with China increased after disputes over Aksai Chin and asylum for the Dalai Lama in 1959, culminating in the Sino-Indian War of 1962.

Indira Gandhi rose after food shortages, inflation, war costs, and Congress factional conflict weakened the old party system. The 1967 elections reduced Congress dominance. She backed V.V. Giri for president, split Congress, and built Congress (I) around her own leadership, populist rhetoric, and centralizing style.

[[Bangladesh]] emerged after Pakistan's 1970 election crisis, refusal to transfer power to the Awami League, and the army's repression in East Pakistan in 1971. Millions of refugees entered India. After war began, Indian forces advanced and Dhaka surrendered in December 1971, creating Bangladesh and raising India's regional stature.

The Emergency of 1975-1977 followed inflation, unemployment, protest movements led by Jayaprakash Narayan and Morarji Desai, and legal challenges to Indira Gandhi's election. Opposition leaders were jailed, press freedom curtailed, and Sanjay Gandhi promoted coercive programmes. The Janata Party won in 1977, but its government proved unstable and Indira returned to power.

Punjab and Sikh tensions escalated in the early 1980s, leading to military action at the Golden Temple and Indira Gandhi's assassination in 1984 by Sikh bodyguards. Rajiv Gandhi became prime minister, associated with modernization, technology, youth, and later corruption allegations and Sri Lankan Tamil conflict. He was assassinated near Chennai in 1991.

Coalition politics expanded after Congress dominance weakened. V.P. Singh implemented Mandal Commission reservations in 1990, prompting upper-caste protests. L.K. Advani's Ayodhya campaign helped mobilize the Bharatiya Janata Party. The destruction of the Babri Masjid in 1992 caused severe interreligious rioting and changed national politics.

Narasimha Rao became prime minister in 1991 and led a Congress minority government that launched major economic liberalization. The BJP rose sharply, and the 1990s saw unstable coalitions. Atal Bihari Vajpayee briefly became prime minister in 1996, then led a BJP coalition after 1998.

Nuclear tests in 1998 declared India's nuclear capability, and Pakistan responded with its own tests. The Kargil conflict in 1999 renewed war fears. In 2004, Congress returned through the United Progressive Alliance with Manmohan Singh as prime minister. The 2008 Mumbai attacks killed about 174 people and injured more than 300, worsening relations with Pakistan.

## Key Events & Developments

- Plate collision: The Indian-Australian Plate collided with Eurasia about 50 million years ago, forming the Himalayas and Indo-Gangetic foredeep.
- Indus civilization: Mature urban culture flourished from about 2600 or 2500 BCE to about 2000 BCE.
- Vedic transformation: Indo-Aryan-speaking peoples and later Vedic society reshaped north Indian language, ritual, agriculture, and social hierarchy.
- Heterodox religions: Jainism and Buddhism arose in the 6th and 5th centuries BCE.
- Mauryan unification: Chandragupta, Bindusara, and Ashoka built the first large subcontinental empire.
- Ashoka's Kalinga war: The 260 BCE campaign helped turn imperial ideology toward dhamma and Buddhist patronage.
- Gupta age: From about 320 CE, northern India saw major developments in Sanskrit culture, art, science, and political alliance.
- Delhi sultanate: Turkish and Afghan rulers established Persianate Islamic rule in north India after the Ghurid conquests.
- Vijayanagar and Bahmani rivalry: South Indian politics centred on military, commercial, and religious competition from the 14th to 16th centuries.
- Mughal Empire: Babur's victory in 1526, Akbar's consolidation, and Aurangzeb's expansion created and strained a vast empire.
- European sea route: Vasco da Gama's arrival in 1498 opened a new era of maritime competition.
- Company rule: British territorial dominion grew after revenue rights in Bengal in 1765.
- Revolt of 1857: A broad military and civil uprising ended Company rule and led to British crown rule.
- National movement: Congress, swadeshi, Gandhi's mass campaigns, Muslim League politics, and wartime crises led toward independence.
- Partition: India and Pakistan became independent in 1947 amid migration, violence, and unresolved Kashmir conflict.
- Republic: The constitution came into force on January 26, 1950.
- Bangladesh war: India helped East Pakistan become Bangladesh in 1971.
- Emergency: Democratic freedoms were curtailed from 1975 to 1977.
- Liberalization: Economic reforms beginning in 1991 reshaped growth, markets, technology, and the middle class.
- Nuclear era: Nuclear tests in 1998 and Kargil in 1999 changed security politics.
