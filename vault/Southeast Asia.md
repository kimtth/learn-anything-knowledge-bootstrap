## Introduction

![[attachments/southeast_asia_001_mw.webp|420]]

*Location map of mainland and insular Southeast Asia*

Southeast Asia is the large region east of the Indian subcontinent and south of China. It has two main parts: mainland Southeast Asia and insular, or archipelagic, Southeast Asia. The Malay Peninsula extends from the mainland into the islands and acts as a bridge between the two worlds.

Mainland Southeast Asia includes Cambodia, Laos, Myanmar, Thailand, Vietnam, Singapore, and the mainland part of Malaysia. Cambodia, Laos, and Vietnam are often grouped as the Indochinese Peninsula. Insular Southeast Asia includes Indonesia, the Philippines, Brunei, and eastern Malaysia on Borneo.

The region stretches about 4,000 miles at its greatest extent and includes about 5,000,000 square miles of land and sea. [[Indonesia]] alone extends more than 3,000 miles from west to east. The highest mainland peak is Mount Hkakabo in northern Myanmar, at 19,295 feet.

## The Land

The land of Southeast Asia was shaped by the meeting of the Eurasian, Indian-Australian, and Pacific plates. Faulting, folding, uplift, and volcanism created mountains, islands, basins, deltas, and volcanic arcs. The mainland and island regions are structurally different.

## Mainland Southeast Asia

Mainland Southeast Asia ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mainland_Southeast_Asia)) is marked by north-south mountain ranges divided by great river valleys and deltas. These ranges spread southward like ribs of a fan from the southeastern edge of the Plateau of Tibet. The western ranges run through Myanmar and continue toward the Andaman and Nicobar Islands.

The mainland has been affected by the slow collision of the Indian subcontinent with the Eurasian Plate over roughly the past 50 million years. Even so, it is relatively stable compared with the islands, with few active volcanoes and limited seismic activity except in the northwest and north.

## Insular Southeast Asia

Insular Southeast Asia ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maritime_Southeast_Asia)) consists of island chains formed along crustal boundaries. It is much more unstable than the mainland, with frequent earthquakes and volcanic activity along the southern and eastern margins. The Greater and Lesser Sunda islands form part of a large volcanic arc.

The Sunda Shelf extends from the Gulf of Thailand to the Java Sea. During periods of lower sea level, land bridges connected islands and allowed plants, animals, and people to move across the region. The far southeastern islands, including eastern Maluku and New Guinea, lie on the Sahul Shelf and are structurally linked to Australia.

## Drainage

![[attachments/southeast_asia_002.webp|420]]

*Satellite view of Vietnam's Mekong Delta*

Mainland Southeast Asia is drained by five major rivers: the Irrawaddy, Salween, Chao Phraya, Mekong, and Red. The Irrawaddy, Salween, and Mekong rise in the Plateau of Tibet and flow through narrow valleys before reaching lowlands and deltas.

The Mekong is the longest mainland river and has the largest drainage basin. It flows through southern China, Laos, Cambodia, and Vietnam. The Tonle Sap in Cambodia, Southeast Asia's largest lake, changes greatly with the rainfall cycle and connects to the Mekong system.

The Irrawaddy drains western Myanmar and has a large, rapidly expanding delta. The Salween remains a highland river through most of its course and has a smaller delta. The Chao Phraya is Thailand's main river and supports the country's central lowlands.

## Soils

Compared with many tropical regions, Southeast Asia has a relatively high share of fertile soils, and erosion is less severe than in some other tropics. Yet many tropical soils are nutrient-poor. Heat and moisture support lush vegetation but also speed leaching and chemical weathering when forest cover is removed.

Laterite soils, reddish clays rich in iron and alumina, occur in parts of Myanmar, Thailand, Vietnam, and the Sunda Shelf islands, especially Borneo. The most fertile soils are volcanic soils, found in parts of Sumatra and much of Java, and alluvial soils in river valleys and deltas.

## Climate

All of Southeast Asia lies within the warm, humid tropics. Its climate is generally monsoonal, with seasons defined more by rainfall than by temperature. Sea-level temperatures stay fairly constant, often near 80 F, while higher elevations such as the Cameron Highlands and Baguio are cooler.

Rainfall is controlled mainly by the northeast monsoon and southwest monsoon. The northeast monsoon, roughly November to March, brings dry, cool air to much of the mainland but heavy rain to places where air crosses warm seas and rises over land, such as the east coast of peninsular Malaysia and parts of the Philippines and eastern Indonesia.

The southwest monsoon, from about May to September, brings the mainland much of its rain. Equatorial and southern island areas often lack a long dry season. Relief, sea breezes, convection, and tropical storms add local complexity. Typhoons especially affect the Philippines.

## Plant Life and Environment

![[attachments/southeast_asia_004.webp|420]]

*Karst islands in Ha Long Bay, Vietnam*

The hot, humid climate and varied habitats created extraordinary tropical vegetation. Rain forests, monsoon forests, mangroves, swamp forests, grasslands, and mountain vegetation all occur. Much natural vegetation has been modified by human settlement, shifting cultivation, plantation agriculture, logging, and urban growth.

Forest removal can quickly reduce soil fertility because the humus supply disappears and heavy rains leach nutrients. Volcanic islands and river valleys have supported intensive cultivation, while mountainous and remote areas retain more forest but are also vulnerable to logging and land conversion.

## Religions and Peoples

![[attachments/southeast_asia_003.webp|420]]

*Angkor Wat reflected in water in Cambodia*

[[Buddha and Buddhism (Buddhism)|Buddhism]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buddhism)), Islam, Christianity, Hinduism, and animism all shape Southeast Asia. Theravada Buddhism dominates much of the mainland, while Mahayana Buddhism is more common in northern Vietnam. Islam predominates in the southern Malay Peninsula, the Malay Archipelago, and the southern Philippines, and Indonesia's large population makes Islam the religion of about two-fifths of Southeast Asians.

[[Christianity]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christianity)) came with European contact. Roman Catholicism entered the islands through Spanish and Portuguese activity in the 16th century and later reached parts of Indochina through the French. It remains especially important in the Philippines and southern Vietnam. Hinduism survives in Indian communities and in Bali, where it is mixed with local forms.

Population growth varies widely. The region's natural increase is slightly above the world average, but growth is higher in countries such as the Philippines, Laos, Malaysia, Vietnam, and Brunei and lower in Singapore and Thailand. Urbanization, migration, and economic change have altered older settlement patterns.

## Agriculture

Agriculture ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agriculture)) remains the main livelihood in every Southeast Asian country except Brunei and Singapore, though agricultural employment has declined as industry and services have grown. Cambodia and Laos still have very high agricultural workforces.

The dominant crop system is wet-rice cultivation. Where water permits, farmers often plant two crops a year. Corn, cassava, pulses, and other crops grow in drier areas. Rice production depends on reliable water; Thailand and the Philippines rely heavily on rain-fed systems, while Indonesia uses irrigation more extensively.

The Green Revolution introduced high-yielding rice varieties, chemical fertilizers, pesticides, and water-control needs from the 1960s onward. It increased output but produced mixed social effects. Farmers with land, credit, and irrigation benefited most, while landlessness, rural poverty, malnutrition, and unequal access to resources remained serious problems.

## Industry

Industrialization in Southeast Asia is recent, with much growth after the early 1960s. In the market economies of ASEAN countries, industrialization became a major policy goal. Manufacturing grew especially in Singapore, Thailand, the Philippines, Indonesia, and Malaysia.

Small factories dominate in number and employment. Agricultural processing is important in most countries, while Singapore focuses on electrical, electronic, transport, and other manufacturing. Textiles and clothing are important in Thailand, Myanmar, and the Philippines, and chemical industries matter in Thailand and Indonesia.

The region has important mineral and energy resources. Tin from Thailand, Malaysia, and Indonesia has long been valuable, though deposits and prices have declined. Nickel, copper, and chromite are mined in smaller quantities. Oil and natural gas are significant in Indonesia, Malaysia, and Brunei.

## Trade

Trade is crucial because of Southeast Asia's strategic location and long commercial history. Market economies dominate regional trade. Singapore, Malaysia, and Brunei have especially high export dependence, while Cambodia, Myanmar, Vietnam, and Laos have smaller export shares.

Export structures differ. [[Indonesia]] long relied on oil but diversified toward plywood, rattan, coffee, rubber, and textiles. Malaysia used revenue from palm oil, hardwoods, tin, and petroleum to build industry. Thailand exports food and manufactured goods, while Brunei relies heavily on petroleum.

Singapore used its location and educated labour force to attract multinational corporations and expand manufacturing and services. Intraregional ASEAN trade is important but still accounts for only about one-fifth of total Southeast Asian trade. Japan, Europe, the United States, Taiwan, China, Hong Kong, and South Korea are major external partners.

## Key Events & Developments

- Past 50 million years: Collision of India with Eurasia helped deform mainland Southeast Asia.
- Early 14th century: Islam began spreading strongly through contact with Muslim traders in northern Sumatra.
- 16th century: Spanish and Portuguese contact introduced Roman Catholicism to insular Southeast Asia.
- 1960s onward: Industrialization and Green Revolution agriculture reshaped economies and rural life.
- 1980s: Indonesia, Malaysia, and Thailand made large manufacturing gains.

## Key People

- Muslim traders: Helped spread Islam through northern Sumatra and the archipelago.
- Spanish and Portuguese missionaries: Introduced Roman Catholicism to much of insular Southeast Asia.
- French missionaries: Helped spread Catholicism in parts of the Indochinese Peninsula.
- Farmers of wet rice: Formed the agricultural base of much mainland and island Southeast Asian society.
