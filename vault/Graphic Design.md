# Graphic Design

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> Graphic design arranges type, images, symbols, and color to make messages intelligible, attractive, and effective. Its history and practice connect artistic imagination with changing technologies of manuscript, print, visual communication, and digital production.

## Introduction

Graphic design ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Graphic_design)) is the art and profession of arranging visual elements such as type, images, symbols, and color so that a message is clear, attractive, and effective. that it is not only about decoration but also about communication: designers combine words and pictures into a coordinated whole, whether for a book, poster, logo, magazine, or website. It also stresses that graphic design has always depended on both artistic imagination and technological change, from manuscript illustration and printing to modern digital production.

## Historical Development

![[attachments/graphic_design_001.webp|420]]

*FridaBurssens-salon van de duitse kunstambachten poster 1957*

The history of graphic design is traced from early manuscript traditions in ancient China, Egypt, and Greece to the rise of printing in Europe in the 15th century. How Renaissance book design improved type and page layout: Aldus Manutius founded his Aldine Press in 1495, issued inexpensive pocket-sized editions of the Greek and Latin classics, and about 1500 introduced the first italic typeface, cut by Francesco Griffo, whose narrow slanting letters fit more text on a page. The Aldine Press's 1499 Hypnerotomachia Poliphili, attributed to Francesco Colonna, became the prototype of Renaissance design, conceiving facing pages as unified double-page spreads. During the 19th century, industrial printing and lithography expanded the scale and reach of visual communication, helping posters, advertisements, and packaged goods become part of everyday urban life.
Printing technology ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Printmaking)) changed both the look and the social role of design. Movable type, page formats, title pages, illustrations, and later mass reproduction made visual organization essential to reading and commerce. Lithography ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lithography)), invented about 1798 by Alois Senefelder of Bavaria, and the chromolithographs that followed in the later 19th century, let designers draw text and image as one artwork and brought colourful pictures into ordinary homes; Jules Chéret, beginning in 1867, designed more than a thousand vivid posters of la belle époque Paris. Designers had to make information legible, persuasive, and memorable for expanding publics.

## Modern Movements

Major stylistic movements shaped the field. The Art Nouveau ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Art_Nouveau)) period of the 1890s used sinuous curvilinear lines, decorative pattern, and flat colour inspired by Japanese ukiyo-e woodblock prints. Henri de Toulouse-Lautrec, though he made only about three dozen posters, reduced his 1893 image of the dancer Jane Avril to simple flat shapes that signified rather than depicted the scene, while the Czech artist Alphonse Mucha, working in Paris, took the style to its idealized peak with swirling hair and Byzantine-inspired ornament. In the early 20th century, designers increasingly pursued simple geometric forms and bold structure, as seen in the Vienna Secession (formed 1897 under Gustav Klimt), the German Plakatstil ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plakatstil)) launched by Lucian Bernhard in 1905, Peter Behrens's AEG identity work of 1907, and later the modernist movements of Cubism, Constructivism, and De Stijl.

Modern design also became closely tied to corporate identity and public communication. Logos, signs, packaging, exhibitions, magazines, and advertising required consistent visual systems. This pushed designers toward grids, simplified forms, standardized typography, and integrated relationships between image and text.

## Swiss Design and Modern Practice

After [[World Wars (World War II)|World War II]], graphic design became more systematically organized through the International Typographic Style, often called Swiss Design. This approach valued clarity, objectivity, modular grids, photography, and sans-serif typography, and it influenced corporate identity, publishing, and visual systems worldwide. A leading figure was Josef Müller-Brockmann, whose Zurich concert posters—such as a 1955 design for music by Stravinsky, Fortner, and Berg—used a single typeface in just two sizes to keep the message clear, showing how design could combine mathematical order with strong visual expression.

## Legacy

Graphic design is a collaborative and constantly changing profession, shaped by technology, commerce, politics, and art. Its history shows how visual communication has moved from handcrafted books and hand-drawn posters to highly standardized systems for branding, publishing, and digital media. The central idea is that design is not only aesthetic but also practical: it structures information, shapes public perception, and helps make communication memorable.

## Key Events & Developments

- 1495: Aldus Manutius founded the Aldine Press, issuing pocket-sized editions and soon the first italic typeface.
- 1798: Alois Senefelder of Bavaria invented lithography, the basis of later colour posters.
- 1867: Jules Chéret helped popularize large chromolithographic posters.
- 1893: Henri de Toulouse-Lautrec's Jane Avril poster pushed design toward flat, signifying imagery.
- 1905: Plakatstil ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plakatstil)) developed as a simplified poster style based on signs and shapes.
- 1922: William A. Dwiggins coined the term graphic design.
- Post-1945: Swiss Design codified the International Typographic Style.
