## Introduction

Muscle ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muscle)) is contractile animal tissue that produces motion by converting chemical energy, chiefly ATP from food metabolism, into mechanical energy and heat. Movement lets organisms seek food, bring food inward, circulate fluids, and respond to internal or external stimuli. In humans and other vertebrates, muscle is usually divided into striated or skeletal muscle, cardiac muscle, and smooth muscle.

Striated muscle ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Striated_muscle_tissue)) is usually attached to the skeleton and under voluntary control through the somatic nervous system. Cardiac muscle forms the heart, contracts rhythmically under pacemaker control, and is involuntary. Smooth muscle lines blood vessels, viscera, and skin structures, uses centrally placed nuclei in short tapering cells, and is also involuntary. Simpler organisms and motile cells use cilia, flagella, or amoeboid motion rather than large muscle systems.

## Striated Muscle Structure

Striated muscle structure is organized into fibres, myofibrils, sarcomeres, and filaments. Its transverse banding comes from the regular arrangement of thick and thin filaments, which interact through cross bridges. This ordered structure allows rapid, forceful, and precisely controlled contraction.

## Muscle Work and Heat

The myofilament arrangement explains how muscle work uses energy. When muscles shorten while producing tension, some chemical energy becomes mechanical work and some becomes heat. When muscles lengthen while resisting a load, chemical energy and absorbed mechanical energy become heat. Heat production is important in warm-blooded animals, and shivering is a muscle activity that warms the body.

## Diversity of Muscle

Muscles vary by structure, activation, force, operating length, and speed. Vertebrate voluntary muscles are striated, most involuntary muscles are smooth, and cardiac muscle is involuntary but striated. Some invertebrates have obliquely striated muscle, with staggered bands and paramyosin in thick filaments. Some muscles need nerve action potentials for every contraction, while others are spontaneously active or require only occasional impulses.

Muscles also differ in force control. Mammalian voluntary muscles often vary force by recruiting different numbers of fibres, while other muscles vary the intensity of activation in each fibre. Smooth muscles often work across wider length ranges than striated muscles, and some specialized striated muscles can shorten dramatically. Fast muscles develop force and shorten quickly; slow muscles conserve energy when maintaining tension.

## Rigid Skeletons

Rigid skeletons work through levers, joints, tendons, and antagonistic muscles. Most skeletal systems require muscle pairs with opposite effects, such as the tibialis anterior and soleus at the human ankle. Joints vary in degrees of freedom: hinges allow flexion and extension, ankles allow flexion-extension and inversion-eversion, and ball-and-socket joints such as the hip allow three degrees of movement.

Muscle architecture affects movement and force. Parallel-fibred muscles, such as many arm or hamstring muscles, shorten far and quickly but produce less force. Pennate muscles contain many short fibres angled into a tendon, so they generate more force but shorten less. Tendons and arthropod apodemes can store elastic energy, reducing running costs or powering jumps, as in locust hind legs.

## Hydrostatic Skeletons

Soft-bodied animals use a hydrostatic skeleton rather than rigid levers. Longitudinal and circular muscles act against constant-volume fluids or tissues: longitudinal contraction shortens and widens the body, while circular contraction lengthens and thins it. Left and right longitudinal muscles can also bend the body. The same principle works in wormlike bodies, mollusk muscles, and structures with fibres running in several directions.

## Cnidarians

[[Cnidarians]], including hydras, jellyfishes, and sea anemones, show simple muscle systems. Hydras have two cell layers separated by mesoglea, with musculoepithelial cells acting as both covering and muscle. Outer body cells have longitudinal fibres, and gut-lining cells have circular fibres. Sea anemones use water in the gut cavity as a hydrostatic skeleton, changing shape by coordinating longitudinal and circular fibres.

Jellyfish medusae swim by rhythmic contractions of musculoepithelial cells on the underside of the bell. Contraction reduces bell diameter and forces out water; elastic recoil of the mesoglea restores shape. These regular contractions make medusae among the simplest animals that use muscles for rhythmic movement.

## Worms

Worms include many unrelated groups with different muscle arrangements. Flatworms have circular, diagonal, longitudinal, and dorsoventral fibres that make the body lengthen, shorten, thicken, flatten, bend, or crawl in waves. Their muscular pharynx can protrude into prey and use peristalsis to suck food inward.

Roundworms, or nematodes, have obliquely striated longitudinal muscle but no circular muscle. Their muscle cells are unusual because narrow projections extend to nerve cells rather than nerves traveling to the muscle. A thick outer cuticle and internal pressure shape their movement.

## Mollusks

[[Mollusks]] use muscular feet, shell muscles, and mantle muscles. Gastropods crawl by waves of contraction in a flat foot, while bivalves use a bulbous or tongue-like foot to burrow. Gastropod shell muscles withdraw the body into the shell, and bivalve adductor muscles close the shell against the hinge ligament.

Bivalve adductor muscles often have fast and slow regions. Fast translucent muscle closes the shell quickly, while slow opaque muscle holds it shut with little energy use during low tide or danger. Squids and other cephalopods swim by jet propulsion: circular mantle muscles squeeze water out, radial muscles and elastic recoil reopen the mantle cavity, and collagen prevents lengthening.

## Arthropods

[[Arthropods]] have jointed appendages and a cuticular skeleton that works as a lever system. Adult arthropod muscles are striated. Direct insect wing muscles pull on wing bases, while more advanced insects use indirect flight muscles that distort the body wall. Longitudinal muscles bow the tergum upward, and dorsoventral muscles pull it downward, causing wing movement through the jointed skeleton.

## Echinoderms

[[Echinoderms]], including sea stars and sea urchins, have internal calcium carbonate skeletons, muscles, and a water-vascular system. Tube feet contain suckers, connective tissue, and longitudinal muscles. Water forced from ampullae extends tube feet, while muscles attach, shorten, bend, move, anchor, or manipulate prey.

## Vertebrate Muscle Systems

Vertebrate musculature is divided into smooth, cardiac, and striated muscle. Smooth and cardiac muscle are involuntary and associated with viscera, while striated skeletal muscle is mainly voluntary and associated with bones or cartilage. Somatic musculature includes axial muscles of trunk, tail, eyeballs, and hypobranchial groups, plus appendicular muscles of limbs.

Development begins with germ layers. The mesoderm forms most body tissues, including somites that develop into vertebrae, somatic muscles, and skin. Tetrapod limbs share primitive features: five digits, one proximal bone, two distal bones, wrist or ankle, elbow or knee, and shoulder or hip. Limb muscles resist body weight, press feet to the ground, create thrust, and swing limbs forward.

## Tetrapod and Mammalian Musculature

Tetrapod limb musculature can be traced to lobe-finned fishes, especially forms like lungfishes that use fins as supports. In salamanders, axial trunk muscles remain important for propulsion, while limbs are weak. In reptiles and mammals, limb muscles became increasingly specialized for support, thrust, adduction, rotation, flexion, and extension.

Mammalian hip and thigh muscles preserve many functional equivalents of reptilian muscles but differ because the tail is reduced and the limbs are positioned differently. Reptiles have a strong caudofemoralis from the tail; mammals retain only small remnants. Mammalian quadriceps, gluteal muscles, adductors, hamstrings, and ankle and digit flexors reflect evolutionary modification of older tetrapod patterns.

## Primitive Contractile Systems

Cilia and flagella are primitive motile systems. Paramecia swim with coordinated cilia; vertebrate respiratory passages use ciliated cells to move mucus; sperm and many algae or protozoans move with flagella. Both contain a regular nine-pair outer tubule arrangement around a central pair, and dynein uses ATP to drive movement. Some bacterial flagella instead use hydrogen ion gradients across membranes.

Amoeboid motion occurs when cytoplasm extends as pseudopodia and the rest of the cell follows. Amoebas and many multicellular nonmuscle cells use this form of movement. These cells contain actin and myosin related to muscle proteins, though structurally different in some details.

## Sarcoplasmic Reticulum and Myofilaments

*Diagram of sarcomere structure showing actin and myosin filament arrangement*

Striated muscle fibres contain myofibrils surrounded by transverse tubules and sarcoplasmic reticulum. The sarcoplasmic reticulum stores and releases calcium ions, with terminal cisternae near transverse tubules forming triads in many vertebrates. Calcium release triggers contraction, and ATP-powered calcium pumps return calcium to storage sites, allowing relaxation.

A sarcomere, the region between two Z disks, is the functional unit of contraction. Thick filaments create the dark A band; thin filaments form lighter regions; overlap zones contain cross bridges from thick to thin filaments. The H zone, pseudo-H zone, and M band reflect different filament arrangements within the sarcomere.

## Myosin

Myosin is the main thick-filament protein, with structural and enzymatic roles. Each thick filament contains about 250 myosin molecules. Myosin tails form the filament core, while globular heads project outward as cross bridges, bind actin, and hydrolyze ATP. Cross-bridge arrangement around the filament creates the repeating pattern needed for force generation.

Thin filaments contain actin, tropomyosin, and troponin. When calcium binds troponin, the troponin-tropomyosin inhibition of actin-myosin interaction is removed. Myosin heads can then interact with actin to generate force and movement, powered by ATP breakdown.

## The Neuromuscular Junction

The neuromuscular junction transmits a nerve signal to a muscle fibre chemically. A motor nerve loses its myelin near the muscle, branches, and forms an end plate separated from the muscle membrane by a narrow synapse. There is no electrical continuity; instead, vesicles release acetylcholine into the synaptic space.

Acetylcholine is made in nerve terminals from choline and acetyl-CoA and stored in vesicles. When the nerve impulse arrives, acetylcholine crosses the synapse and activates the folded postsynaptic membrane, initiating the muscle action potential that leads to calcium release and contraction.

## Mechanical Properties

Striated muscles produce only pulling force, never pushing force. If the load is small, the muscle shortens in isotonic contraction; if load equals maximal force, length remains constant in isometric contraction; if load is larger, the active muscle lengthens. In coordinated movement, different muscles may shorten, hold length, or lengthen while contracting.

Mechanical response depends on temperature, muscle type, stimulation, calcium movement, and ATP. The latent period between electrical signal and tension reflects the time needed for excitation to reach the contractile apparatus. Muscle work can be summarized as ATP plus water and contractile elements producing ADP, phosphate, work, and heat.

## Energy Stores

Muscle ATP ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adenosine_triphosphate)) must be continuously replenished. Resting vertebrate muscle stores enough ATP for less than one second of maximal work. Phosphagens rapidly rebuild ATP from ADP for a few seconds during intense contraction. Glycogen breakdown supplies glucose-derived fuel, and calcium also activates glycogen breakdown during contraction. Recovery rebuilds glycogen from blood glucose.

## Cardiac Muscle

Cardiac muscle forms the myocardium, the pumping tissue of the heart. Its key properties are contractility and rhythmicity. Cardiac output depends on the force developed by myocytes and how often they are activated. Heart muscle cells are branched, connected end to end by intercalated disks, and arranged in layers around chambers so contraction ejects blood into pulmonary and systemic vessels.

Cardiac cells contain myosin and actin filaments, transverse tubules, sarcoplasmic reticulum, mitochondria, plasma membrane, and structures for excitation and recovery. Unlike skeletal muscle, cardiac muscle contracts rhythmically and involuntarily to meet changing metabolic needs of tissues such as muscle, kidney, brain, skin, liver, heart, and gastrointestinal tract.

## Interesting Facts

- Shivering is muscle activity used mainly to generate heat.
- Pennate muscles create more force than parallel muscles of equal mass but shorten less.
- Tube feet in echinoderms use water pressure and muscle to attach and move.
- Human muscle ATP stores can sustain maximal work for less than one second without replenishment.
