# Logic, Philosophy of

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> Philosophy of logic examines the nature of valid reasoning, logical truth, inference, formal structure, and logic's relations to mathematics, language, psychology, law, and computing. It asks whether logic describes laws of thought, meanings, arguments, or a distinctive kind of abstract necessity.

## Introduction

![[attachments/logic_philosophy_002.png|420]]

*Truth table and logic gate examples*

The philosophy of logic ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philosophy_of_logic)) studies the nature and kinds of logic, its problems, and its relation to mathematics and other disciplines. The word logic comes from Greek logos, whose meanings include reason, discourse, rule, account, rational principle, and definition.

Logic has been described as laws of thought, rules of right reasoning, principles of valid argument, study of logical constants, and truths based only on meanings. These descriptions overlap, because correct thinking can be seen as the inward counterpart of valid argumentation.

Valid argumentation does not depend on particular facts. If an inference from p to q is valid, it holds because of form or meaning, not because of what someone happens to believe about p and q.

## Logical Truth

[[Aristotle and Aristotelianism|Aristotle]]'s example, “If sight is perception, the objects of sight are objects of perception,” shows logical truth. Its truth depends on terms such as if-then, is, are, and object of, not on factual opinions about sight or perception.

A broad view treats all truths depending only on meanings as logical, which allows fields such as epistemic, doxastic, deontic, scientific, and inductive logic. A narrower view limits logic to truths depending on logical constants, such as not, and, or, if-then, and quantifiers.

Quantifiers such as “for some x” and “for every x” use bound variables ranging over a universe of discourse. This gives modern logic its power, but it also raises questions about what counts as an individual, what exists, and how formal systems connect with ordinary language.

## Individuation

![[attachments/logic_philosophy_of_001.webp|420]]

*Set of possible truths*

Possible-world semantics raised the problem of cross-identification: how an individual in one possible world is identified as the same or different from an individual in another. This issue is central to individuation because understanding an individual involves locating it across possible situations.

W.V. Quine's criticisms of modal logic and analyticity deepen the problem. Ordinary discourse and science may use several methods of cross-identification, some descriptive and some tied to personal perspective, while philosophers still dispute the role of essential properties and spatio-temporal frameworks.

Existence also remains difficult despite the existential quantifier. Ontology asks what kinds of entities are admitted by a theory, and logic alone does not settle whether possible objects, abstract objects, properties, or merely actual individuals should be counted.

## Mathematics

[[The History of Mathematics (Mathematics)|Mathematics]] can usually be formulated in axiomatic set theory, so pure mathematics may fall under logic in a broad sense. Yet this claim is limited unless the meanings of set, membership, and related notions tell which set-theoretic axioms should be accepted.

Gottlob Frege, Bertrand Russell, and their successors tried to define basic mathematical concepts, such as number, in logical terms. Even so, the relation between mathematics and logic remains ambiguous because strong set-theoretical assumptions are not obviously forced by logic alone.

Normal mathematical argument uses logical tools, especially first-order logic, with second-order reasoning and set theory. Historically, mathematics supplied many early nontrivial examples of logical reasoning beyond traditional syllogistic.

## Computers

Recursive function theory studies idealized automata and sits on the border between logic and computer science. Turing machines assume potentially infinite tape, so the move to finite automata is important but does not separate computer science from logic.

Propositional logic models simple switching circuits, and automata theory connects with formal languages. Mechanical theorem proving is especially important because it belongs both to logic and to artificial intelligence, where computers must reproduce heuristic forms of thinking.

## Linguistics

The revival of semantics in theoretical linguistics during the late 1960s renewed interest in relations between logic and linguistic theory. Some grammatical problems proved closely related to logical concepts.

George Lakoff claimed a near-identity between linguistics and natural logic. Other developments included Jerrold J. Katz's attempts to characterize analyticity linguistically, Richard Montague's universal grammar based on intensional logic, and proposals identifying deep structure with logical form.

Less controversially, recursive function theory and related logical tools became useful in formal grammars and formal models of language users. Logic therefore served linguistics both philosophically and technically.

## Psychology

The laws of thought studied in logic ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logic)) are not empirical psychological generalizations, but they can frame psychological theories. The major example is Jean Piaget's mid-20th-century attempt to describe children's developmental stages by reference to logical structures they can master.

Psychology also uses logic inside mathematical models, automata-based models, and information-theoretic models. Direct large-scale use is limited, partly because earlier problems about logic and information make psychological application difficult.

## Law

Legal argument uses many kinds of reasoning, some persuasive rather than strictly logical. Studies of legal logic, already called Lawiers Logike in 1588, have found examples from many departments of logic.

Wesley Newcomb Hohfeld's theory of fundamental legal conceptions is the strongest connection between law and modern logic. His ideas are closely related to deontic logic, especially notions such as right, duty, privilege, and permission.

## Education

Logic education was once central to general schooling but almost disappeared from liberal education in the first half of the 20th century. It returned partly through the new mathematics curriculum of the 1960s, which included propositional logic and set theory.

Logic also fits computer-based teaching because formal rules can be represented, tested, and practiced through machines. This made logic useful both as a subject and as a tool for educational experiments.

## Core Equations

Philosophical logic uses symbols, sets, functions, and logical relations to clarify reasoning:

$$f:X\to Y$$

This notation represents a function that maps each input from one set to an output in another set.

$$A\subseteq B \quad \Rightarrow \quad (x\in A \Rightarrow x\in B)$$

This states the meaning of subset: every member of A is also a member of B.

## Key Events & Developments

- 1588: Legal reasoning was discussed under the name Lawiers Logike.
- 1884: Gottlob Frege proposed logical definitions of mathematical concepts.
- 1903: Bertrand Russell continued logicist work on mathematics.
- mid-20th century: Jean Piaget used logical structures to describe children's cognitive development.
- late 1960s: The revival of linguistic semantics renewed links between logic and linguistics.
- 1960s: Logic returned to education through new mathematics curricula.

## Interesting Facts

- Logos ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logos)) has many meanings, which helps explain why logic is hard to define narrowly.
- Logical truth ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logical_truth)) can be recognized without knowing the facts of a subject matter.
- Possible worlds create the problem of identifying the same individual across different situations.
- Turing machines link logic to computer science through idealized computation.
- Deontic logic connects formal logic with legal notions of duty and permission.
