# Moss Animals - Phylum Bryozoa

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> Bryozoans, or moss animals, are aquatic colonial invertebrates built from connected feeding zooids with lophophores. The account distinguishes freshwater and marine groups, explains budding, reproduction, colony growth, ecology, and classification, and notes their extensive fossil record.

## Introduction

Moss animals ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bryozoa)), or bryozoans, are aquatic colonial invertebrates in the phylum Bryozoa, also called Polyzoa or Ectoprocta. About 5,000 living species and 15,000 fossil species are known. They feed with a lophophore and form colonies of connected zooids, usually less than one millimetre long.

Bryozoans were once mistaken for [[Plants|plants]], which explains the name moss animals. They are divided into freshwater Phylactolaemata, marine Stenolaemata, and mostly marine Gymnolaemata. Cheilostomes are the most successful living group. Colonies grow on vegetation, stones, shells, ship hulls, pipes, seaweeds, boulders, and seabeds.

## Budding

Bryozoan colonies grow by asexual budding from a primary zooid called the ancestrula or from a statoblast. The ancestrula forms when a larva metamorphoses, then new zooids bud from it to create the colony's shape. Phylactolaemates may form branched or compact colonies.

In stenolaemates, repeated wall divisions build the colony and septa form internal walls. In gymnolaemates, transverse septa cut off buds from parent zooids; in cheilostomes, rows of similar zooids radiate from the primary zooid and divide as the colony grows.

## Reproduction

Many gymnolaemate and phylactolaemate zooids are hermaphroditic, though some species have separate male and female zooids. In living stenolaemates, most zooids have testes, while a few female zooids enlarge into brood chambers called gonozooids. Polyembryony can produce many larvae in one chamber.

Phylactolaemates develop eggs in an internal embryo sac and produce statoblasts, hard dormant buds that survive freezing and drying. Gymnolaemate oocytes are fertilized and transferred to brood chambers, often special ooecia in cheilostomes.

## Ecology

*Membranipora membranacea bryozoan colony growing as a sheet of tiny zooids*

Freshwater bryozoans live mainly on leaves, stems, and roots in shallow, clear, quiet, or slow water. Marine bryozoans are most abundant below tidemarks and on continental shelves, though they also occur at great depths and on kelp, stones, shells, pilings, overhangs, and sea floors.

Bryozoans feed on minute planktonic particles captured by ciliated tentacles. They do not need light, so shaded habitats help them avoid algae. Membranipora grows worldwide on kelp and can cover large areas with a million or more zooids. Sea slugs and sea spiders are important predators.

## Zooids

A zooid has an external skeleton or wall, a lophophore, a retractable tentacle sheath, a deep-looped digestive canal, and an orifice through which feeding structures emerge. Hydrostatic pressure and muscles evert the lophophore, while retractors pull it inward. Cheilostomes often have a hinged operculum.

Zooid walls range from thin cuticle to thick calcified skeleton. Primitive forms are cylindrical, but many living bryozoans are flattened. Stenolaemates differ because their calcareous tubes cannot bend to evert tentacles; muscles instead force body fluid between parts of the zooid.

## Colonies

Bryozoan colonies are integrated organisms rather than simple piles of zooids. Interzooidal pores and tissues, especially the funiculus in gymnolaemates, distribute nutrients to nonfeeding regions. A small ganglion controls zooidal organs, and some colonies coordinate withdrawal responses across nearby zooids.

Colonies are still only partly individualistic. They may lack a fixed shape, grow in many directions, survive partial destruction, and live indefinitely if budding continues. Fossil bryozoans are abundant from the Lower Ordovician onward, and cheilostomes become abundant from the Upper Jurassic.

## Classification

Bryozoan classification ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taxonomy)) uses colony type and zooid morphology, but zooidal characters are usually more reliable. Wall structure, embryo chambers, frontal-wall structure, orifice shape, ooecia, polymorphism, statoblasts, gizzards, tentacle number, and embryo colour all help distinguish groups. The phylum lacks blood vascular and excretory organs.

## Class Phylactolaemata

Phylactolaemates are exclusively freshwater bryozoans with cylindrical zooids, crescentic lophophores, an epistome, noncalcareous muscular walls, continuous coelom, monomorphic zooids, and statoblasts. They are cosmopolitan, apparently primitive, and include about 12 genera and 50 species.

## Class Stenolaemata

Stenolaemates are mostly fossil bryozoans except for some cyclostomes. Their cylindrical zooids have calcified nonmuscular walls, septa, limited polymorphism, and marine habits. They range from the Ordovician to today.

## Order Cyclostomata

Cyclostomes have circular zooid orifices, circular lophophores, no epistome, open interzooidal pores, and sexual reproduction commonly involving polyembryony in reproductive zooids. They occur in all seas from the Ordovician to the present and include about 250 genera.

## Order Cystoporata

Cystoporates have long tubular zooid skeletons connected by pores, with diaphragms and supporting cystopores between zooid skeletons. They are Paleozoic fossils ranging from the Ordovician to the Permian, with about 80 genera.

## Order Trepostomata

Trepostomes generally formed massive colonies of long tubular zooid skeletons with lamellate calcification. They lacked interzooidal pores, often had polygonal orifices and diaphragms, and had walls that thickened distally. They ranged from the Ordovician to the Permian and include about 100 genera.

## Order Cryptostomata

Cryptostomes formed colonies with foliaceous or reticulate fronds or branching stems. Their zooid skeletons were tubular but shorter than trepostomes, lacked pores, had diaphragms, and showed extensive distal calcification. They ranged from the Ordovician to the Triassic and include about 130 genera.

## Class Gymnolaemata

Gymnolaemates have cylindrical or squat zooids with circular lophophores, no epistome, sometimes calcified nonmuscular walls, tissue-plugged pores, polymorphism, and budding behind growing points. They are mainly marine, include about 3,000 species, and are known from the Jurassic to the present.

## Order Ctenostomata

Ctenostomes have cylindrical to flat zooids with noncalcified walls and a terminal or near-terminal orifice often closed by a pleated collar. They lack ooecia and avicularia, range from the Jurassic to the present, and include about 20 families and 250 species.

## Order Cheilostomata

Cheilostomes usually have flat box-shaped zooids with calcified walls, hinged opercula, specialized zooids, and embryos often developed in ooecia. They range from the Upper Jurassic to the present and include about 70 families and 2,750 species. Proposed relationships with entoprocts remain disputed.

## Key Events & Developments

- Mid-18th century: Bryozoans were still commonly regarded as plants like corals.
- 1837: Freshwater and marine bryozoans were separated into major classes.
- 1852: Cheilostomata, Ctenostomata, and Cyclostomata were named.
- 1964: Cystoporata was introduced as a separate order.
