## Introduction

Ecuador is a northwestern South American country named for the Equator. It includes Pacific coast, Andean highlands, Amazonian lowlands, and the Galapagos Islands, making it one of the world’s most environmentally diverse countries. Research there by Charles-Marie de La Condamine, Alexander von Humboldt, and Charles Darwin helped shape modern geography, ecology, and evolutionary biology.

The country is known for Panama hats ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Panama_hat)), cacao, bananas, flowers, petroleum, shrimp, and tourism. Its history includes Inca conquest, Spanish rule, independence, rivalry between Quito and Guayaquil, military governments, oil booms, inequality, and repeated constitutional change.

## Relief

Ecuador has three main mainland regions: the Costa, or coastal lowlands; the Sierra, or Andean highlands; and the Oriente, or Amazonian east. The Galapagos are volcanic islands about 600 miles west of the mainland.

The Sierra contains high volcanic peaks, including Cayambe, Antisana, Cotopaxi, Chimborazo, Altar, and Sangay. Cotopaxi is one of the world’s highest active volcanoes, and Chimborazo is Ecuador’s highest mountain.

## Drainage

Ecuador’s [[Rivers|rivers]] begin mostly in the mountains and flow either west to the Pacific or east toward the Amazon. The Guayas River is the main watercourse of the Costa and is navigable for much of its length.

The Oriente has the greatest water volume. Rivers such as the Napo, Pastaza, Morona, and Santiago drain toward the Amazon system through Peru.

## Soils

Ecuador’s [[Soils|soils]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soil)) are highly varied. Volcanic activity in the Andes created fertile andosols and mollisols, though erosion can expose a hardpan called cangahua.

Indigenous soil management included manure, irrigation canals, raised fields, and fertilizing muck from drainage ditches. Coastal floodplains are fertile but clayey, while Amazonian soils include alluvial soils, histosols, and oxisols.

## Climate

Because Ecuador lies on the Equator, most areas outside the Sierra are humid tropical. The Oriente is wet and hot year-round, while the Costa has a wet season and a drier season.

El Nino can bring warm coastal waters and destructive rains. The Sierra has rainfall peaks near the equinoxes and dry periods from June to September and around December-January.

## Plant and animal life

The Oriente and parts of the Costa contain tropical rainforest, while the Andes support cloud forests, high grasslands, and cultivated basins. Vegetation changes sharply with altitude and rainfall.

The Galapagos Islands are famous for distinctive species that helped Charles Darwin develop evolutionary ideas. Ecuador’s biodiversity is rich but pressured by farming, roads, settlement, oil production, and forest clearing.

## People

The population of Ecuador is diverse, and identity is often regional and self-defined. Many people identify as mestizo, but mestizo culture differs between highlands, coast, cities, and rural areas.

### Ethnic groups

Major ethnic groups include Indigenous peoples, mestizos, Afro-Ecuadorans, and descendants of immigrants from several world regions. Many highland Indigenous people speak Quichua, while Amazonian and coastal groups include Shuar, Achuar, Huaorani, Awa, Chachi, and Tsachila communities.

Afro-Ecuadorans live mainly in Esmeraldas and the Chota River valley. Identity can be complex: some Spanish speakers identify as Indigenous, while some people of Indigenous descent identify as mestizo or cholo.

### Languages

Spanish is the official language of government and business, with regional differences between Sierra and Costa speech. Quichua, Shuar, and other ancestral languages are also spoken.

Bilingual education has become important as Indigenous communities defend language and culture. Many Indigenous men are bilingual, and increasing numbers of women are bilingual too.

### Religion

[[Roman Catholicism]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catholic_Church)) is dominant and influences education, social services, festivals, and pilgrimages. Protestantism, especially Evangelical and Pentecostal forms, has grown among disadvantaged groups and some Indigenous communities.

### Settlement patterns

Prehistoric settlement ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Human_settlement)) spread through coastal valleys, highland basins, and Amazon riversides. European diseases in the 16th century devastated Indigenous coastal populations, and later colonial settlement concentrated in the Sierra.

By the late 20th century, population shifted toward lowlands and cities, especially the Costa. Roads and oil fields brought highland migrants into the northern Oriente.

### Demographic trends

Ecuador grew rapidly as death rates fell while birth rates stayed high. Birth rates declined after the late 1990s, but migration and urbanization continued to reshape the population.

## Economy

Ecuador has major agricultural, marine, forest, mineral, and petroleum resources. Its dependence on primary exports has produced boom-and-bust cycles and persistent inequality.

### Agriculture, forestry, and fishing

Agriculture ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agriculture)) has traditionally employed many Ecuadorans. Subsistence crops include corn, potatoes, beans, and cassava, while export crops include bananas, cacao, coffee, flowers, asparagus, snow peas, and strawberries.

Livestock includes cattle, sheep, chickens, pigs, goats, and guinea pigs. Fishing, shrimp production, forestry, and tropical forest products also contribute to the economy.

### Resources and power

Ecuador’s resources include fertile soils, petroleum, gold, forests, and marine products. Oil from the northeast became the major mineral export and a key source of tax revenue.

Gold mining has long existed, including dangerous work in remote districts such as Nambija. Ecuador was once in OPEC but withdrew in 1992.

### Manufacturing

Manufacturing ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manufacturing)) is relatively limited and often processes primary products. Industries produce cement, sugar, chocolate, beer, pasta, bread, meat products, fruit products, instant coffee, pharmaceuticals, tires, and assembled automobiles.

Craft industries include Panama hats, woolen textiles, sweaters, woodwork, straw goods, ceramics, leather, and tagua nut products.

### Finance

The financial system includes the Central Bank of Ecuador, the National Bank of Promotion, private banks, and some foreign branches. Ecuador adopted the U.S. dollar as national currency in 2000.

### Trade

Ecuador’s exports include crude oil and derivatives, shrimp, bananas, coffee, cut flowers, cocoa, and Panama hats. Major destinations include the United States, Colombia, Peru, Chile, and Italy.

Imports include machinery, industrial materials, vehicles, consumer goods, food, and chemical products, mainly from the United States and nearby Latin American countries.

### Services

The service sector ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tertiary_sector)) produces about half of GDP. Tourism is important in the Galapagos, Quito, Guayaquil, Mitad del Mundo, Banos, Puyo, and Amazon excursion areas.

### Labour and taxation

About three-fifths of the labour force works in services, especially trade, restaurants, and hotels. Many working women remain concentrated in domestic service, agriculture, family businesses, and informal labour.

### Transportation and telecommunications

For much of its history, Ecuador relied on trails, animals, canoes, and rivers. Railways were difficult to build and maintain because of mountains and weather.

Modern [[Transportation|transportation]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transport)) uses roads, ports, airports, and rivers. Telecommunications expanded through cellular phones, cable television, high-speed Internet, and Internet cafes.

## Government and society

Ecuador is a constitutional republic shaped by repeated attempts to reduce political instability. Constitutional conventions became common after the crisis years of the late 1990s and early 2000s.

### Constitutional framework

The president is both chief of state and head of government. The constitution approved in 2008, the country’s 20th since independence in 1830, created a unicameral National Assembly and allowed two consecutive presidential terms.

### Local government

The president appoints provincial governors. Provinces are divided into cantones and parroquias, while elected mayors have become important in infrastructure and environmental controls.

### Justice

Ecuador’s judicial system includes provincial courts, higher courts, and a Supreme Court. Reform efforts have tried to reduce inconsistent rulings and outside influence.

### Political process

Voting is required for literate Ecuadorans ages 18 to 64. Women gained suffrage in 1929, open-list voting began in 1998, and later laws required increasing numbers of women candidates.

### Security

Ecuador’s security forces include an army, navy, and air force. The navy includes naval infantry, naval aviation, and the coast guard, while national and metropolitan police maintain internal order.

### Health and welfare

The National Social Security Institute covers public and private employees and provides medical insurance, clinics, loans, pensions, and benefits. Public health funding is limited, and diseases such as malaria and tuberculosis have persisted.

### Housing

Housing ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Housing)) varies by region. Sierra houses have shifted toward tile, metal roofs, brick, and cement block, while coastal rural houses often stand on stilts with bamboo walls and thatch roofs.

### Education

Public [[Education|education]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Education)) expanded to promote literacy. Primary school is free and compulsory for six years from age six. Higher education includes the Pontifical Catholic University, the Polytechnic School in Quito, the Military Polytechnic School, and San Francisco University.

## Cultural life

Ecuador has diverse cultural life shaped by region, ethnicity, class, religion, food, music, dress, crafts, literature, sport, and media.

### Cultural milieu

Folk-cultural regions include highland Indigenous and mestizo communities, coastal Afro-Ecuadoran traditions, Amazonian Indigenous societies, and urban cultures.

### Daily life and social customs

Most Ecuadorans value family strongly, including godparent relationships. Important life events include baptism, quinceanera celebrations, marriage, and funerals.

### The arts

Ecuador has rich folk art. Quito was a colonial centre of wood carving and painting, and artisans continue works connected with the Quito school. Literature includes writers and poets from several regions.

### Cultural institutions

The Central Bank of Ecuador supported major museums, research, and publishing. The House of Ecuadoran Culture, founded in the early 1940s, sponsors research, publications, and events.

### Sports and recreation

Football, or soccer, is Ecuador’s national sport. Basketball, volleyball, beach trips, family outings, beauty contests, cockfights, bullfights in some highland areas, and pelota de mano are also popular.

### Media and publishing

Many Ecuadorans read newspapers and periodicals. El Comercio in Quito is known for serious political, economic, environmental, and cultural coverage.

Ecuador’s history includes early ceramics, pre-Inca chiefdoms, Inca conquest, Spanish colonization, independence, regional rivalry, liberal-conservative conflict, military governments, oil booms, Indigenous politics, and constitutional change.

### Pre-Spanish era

The area now called Ecuador produced pottery from about 3000 to 2500 BCE, among the earliest ceramics in the New World. By the 1400s, chiefdoms used raised-field agriculture and trade networks.

Topa Inca Yupanqui and Huayna Capac conquered the region for the Inca, but rule was brief. Atahuallpa defeated Huascar near Riobamba in 1532, just as Francisco Pizarro arrived.

### The colonial period

During the colonial period, present-day Ecuador was governed through the audiencia of Quito. In the Sierra, Spanish estates used Indigenous labour, while Quito, Ambato, and Cuenca became administrative and religious centres.

The Costa developed differently after disease reduced Indigenous populations. Guayaquil grew through shipbuilding and cacao exports, while missionaries in the Oriente spread Christianity and Quichua among resistant Indigenous peoples.

### Independence and Gran Colombia

Quito claimed an early patriotic uprising against Spain in 1809. After wider liberation struggles, the region joined Gran Colombia and then separated as Ecuador in 1830.

### Early national history, 1830c. 1925

Early republican Ecuador was unstable because regional leaders, generals, merchants, clergy, landowners, conservatives, and liberals competed for power.

#### Liberal-Conservative hostilities

The main regional rivalry opposed conservative, clerical Quito and liberal, commercial Guayaquil. Quito’s power rested on highland estates, while Guayaquil’s merchants favoured trade and free markets.

#### Rivalry between Flores and Rocafuerte (183045)

From 1830 to 1845, Juan Jose Flores and Vicente Rocafuerte struggled for power. Flores drew support from Quito, while Rocafuerte drew support from Guayaquil.

#### Breakdown of national government (184560)

Between 1845 and 1860, Ecuador suffered weak leaders, regional conflicts, and near-collapse of national authority.

#### The regime of Garcia Moreno (186075)

Gabriel Garcia Moreno ruled from 1860 to 1875 and built an authoritarian conservative state closely tied to Roman Catholicism.

#### Shift to liberalism (187597)

After Garcia Moreno died, liberals and conservatives again fought for power. The coast gained influence as Ecuador became more tied to world markets.

#### Problems of the early 20th century

After Eloy Alfaro, liberals stayed in office, but Guayaquil merchants and bankers often held real power.

### Modern history

The period from 1925 to 1948 was especially turbulent, with economic hardship, weak parties, military intervention, and social conflict.

#### Economic development and loss of territory in the 1940s

Ecuador was still affected by the Great Depression when World War II began. The 1940s brought economic change but also territorial loss after conflict with Peru.

### Domination of Velasco Ibarra after World War II

Jose Maria Velasco Ibarra dominated postwar politics. He was elected president five times but completed only one term, combining mass popularity with policy reversals, suspended liberties, and military interventions.

### Ecuador from the late 20th century

After Velasco Ibarra’s fall in 1972, military officers ruled for about seven years. Civilian rule returned on July 16, 1979, but oil wealth, debt, inequality, Indigenous activism, and presidential crises continued.

### Correa and the 2008 constitution

Rafael Correa expanded state control over oil, raised social spending, reduced ties with U.S. development organizations, and allied with Hugo Chavez. The 2008 constitution reflected many of his leftist ideals and strengthened the presidency.
