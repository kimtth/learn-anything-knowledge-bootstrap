# Elizabeth I of England

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> Elizabeth I ruled England from 1558 to 1603, securing the Tudor state amid religious division and foreign threats. Her political judgment and cultivated royal image helped make the Elizabethan Age a period of national power and cultural achievement.

## Introduction

Elizabeth I, known by the bynames The Virgin Queen ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elizabeth_I)) and Good Queen Bess ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elizabeth_I)), was born on Sept. 7, 1533, at Greenwich near London and died on March 24, 1603, at Richmond in Surrey. She ruled England from 1558 to 1603, a period so distinctive that it is called the Elizabethan Age ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elizabethan_era)), when England asserted itself as a major European power in politics, commerce, and the arts.

Although her kingdom was small and threatened by deep internal divisions, Elizabeth's mix of shrewdness, courage, and majestic self-display inspired strong loyalty and helped unite the nation against foreign enemies. The adoration she received was not spontaneous but the result of a carefully crafted campaign in which she fashioned herself as the glittering symbol of the nation's destiny. Yet she was no mere figurehead: while she lacked the absolute power dreamed of by Renaissance rulers, she firmly kept the right to make the central decisions of state and church.

Elizabeth's early years were not promising. She was the daughter of Henry VIII and his second wife, Anne Boleyn. Because Henry desperately wanted a male heir, the birth of a second daughter weakened her mother's position. Before Elizabeth was three, her father had Anne Boleyn beheaded for adultery and treason, and an act of Parliament declared the marriage invalid, making Elizabeth illegitimate. She was raised in a separate household at Hatfield, and observers noted her precocious seriousness, saying that at six she had as much gravity as a person of 40.

When Jane Seymour bore a son, Edward, in 1537, Elizabeth slipped further into obscurity but was not neglected; Henry treated all his children with affection and declared her third in line to the throne. From her tenth year she gained the loving attention of her stepmother Catherine Parr. Under tutors such as the Cambridge humanist Roger Ascham, she received the rigorous education usually reserved for male heirs, mastering classical languages, history, rhetoric, and moral philosophy, and becoming fluent in French and Italian. She also absorbed the doctrines of English Protestantism, though observers thought she was more fascinated by languages than by religious dogma.

## Position under Edward VI and Mary

With Henry's death in 1547 and the accession of her frail brother Edward VI, Elizabeth's life grew dangerous. Her guardian Catherine Parr married Thomas Seymour, who schemed against his brother, the protector. In 1549 Seymour was arrested for treason and accused of plotting to marry Elizabeth to rule the kingdom. Under humiliating questioning, the young princess was extraordinarily poised, and when told that Seymour had been beheaded she showed no emotion.

The danger increased after Edward's death in 1553 and the accession of her Catholic half sister Mary, who aimed to return England to Rome and married Philip II of Spain. Although Elizabeth outwardly conformed to Catholic worship, she became the obvious focus of Protestant plots. After Sir Thomas Wyatt's rebellion in 1554, she was sent to the Tower of London and narrowly escaped her mother's fate, then was held at Woodstock. These years were a sustained lesson in survival through self-discipline and the careful management of appearances, creating a lasting gap between her dazzling surface and a concealed interior.

## Accession

![[attachments/elizabeth_i_of_england_002.webp|420]]

*Elizabeth I in coronation robes*

When Mary died on Nov. 17, 1558, Elizabeth came to the throne amid bells, bonfires, and public jubilation. Her entry into London and her coronation procession were masterpieces of political courtship, and every gesture was read for signs of the new regime. When a girl in a pageant gave her an English Bible, banned under Mary, Elizabeth kissed it and held it to her breast, assuring spectators that England had returned cautiously but decisively to the Reformation.

She at once began to form her government, reducing the Privy Council to purge Catholic members and make it more efficient, and assembling trusted advisers. Chief among them was William Cecil (later Lord Burghley), whom she appointed principal secretary of state on the morning of her accession and who served her with great skill for 40 years, later as lord treasurer.

## The woman ruler in a patriarchal world

In Mary's last year the Scottish Calvinist preacher John Knox attacked female rule in The First Blast of the Trumpet Against the Monstruous Regiment of Women. With Elizabeth's accession his trumpet was quickly muted, but a widespread belief remained that women were unfit to govern. Men were seen as rational, women as ruled by impulse; gentlemen were trained in eloquence and war, gentlewomen urged to keep silent.

Apologists countered with exceptions such as the biblical Deborah, and crown lawyers developed the mystical theory of the king's two bodies. According to this idea, when Elizabeth ascended the throne her mortal "body natural" was wedded to an immortal "body politic" that was timeless and perfect. Skilfully, she turned the language of rights into a discourse of mutual love, telling her subjects that she valued their love above any jewel, and so transformed her gender from a liability into an advantage in dealing with Parliament, suitors, and courtiers.

## Religious questions and the fate of Mary, Queen of Scots

Elizabeth restored England to Protestantism. The Act of Supremacy of 1559 revived Henry VIII's anti-papal laws and named the queen supreme governor of the church, while the Act of Uniformity established a slightly revised prayer book as the official order of worship. Her government moved cautiously but steadily to carry these reforms into local parishes, requiring oaths of supremacy and fining absence from Sunday service.

Many nobles, gentry, and common people stayed loyal to the old faith, but Protestants held the key positions and used patronage, pressure, propaganda, and threats to secure outward conformity. To militant Protestants returning from [[Geneva]] and other centres of reform, however, the settlement seemed only half-finished, leaving Elizabeth caught between Catholic resistance and Protestant impatience.

## The queen's image

Elizabeth moved among palaces such as Whitehall, Greenwich, Richmond, and Hampton Court, and on her royal progresses she wooed her people and enjoyed lavish entertainments. Artists such as the poet Edmund Spenser and the painter Nicholas Hilliard celebrated her in mythological guises as Diana, Astraea, and Gloriana, and she took to herself some of the veneration once given to the Virgin Mary.

[[Francis bacon|Francis Bacon]] later wrote cynically that she relied on the glitter of her jewels to distract people from her fading attractions, reflecting the darker tone of her last decade. Bad harvests, inflation, and unemployment caused hardship, while hated monopolies granted to favourites bred resentment. A crisis came with her last great favourite, Robert Devereux, Earl of Essex, who failed in Ireland, defied her, attempted a rebellion, and was executed in 1601. Elizabeth continued to make brilliant speeches and exercise authority, but she had become, in Sir Walter Raleigh's phrase, "a lady surprised by time."

## Key Events & Developments

- 1533: Elizabeth is born at Greenwich, the daughter of Henry VIII and Anne Boleyn, at a time when the Tudor dynasty wanted a male heir.
- 1554: After Wyatt's rebellion, she is imprisoned in the Tower under Mary and narrowly survives.
- 1558: She becomes queen amid public rejoicing and at once appoints William Cecil.
- 1559: The Act of Supremacy and Act of Uniformity restore Protestantism.
- 1601: The Earl of Essex is executed after a failed rising, marking the troubled close of the reign.

## Key People

## Interesting Facts

- Elizabeth was legally declared illegitimate after her mother's execution, yet became one of England's most celebrated monarchs.
- She received the rigorous classical education usually reserved for male heirs and was fluent in French and Italian, which later aided her [[Diplomacy|diplomacy]].
- Contemporaries noted a constant gap between her dazzling public surface and a carefully hidden inner self, so her true religious convictions remain a mystery.
