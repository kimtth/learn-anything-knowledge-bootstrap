# Caricature, Cartoon, and Comic Strip (Comic Strip)

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> The comic strip is sequence-based visual storytelling in which adjacent panels create narrative, timing, and character development. It evolved from European picture stories into a newspaper-based mass medium, then broadened through international comics, underground work, graphic novels, and critical study into a major cultural art form.

## Introduction

The comic strip ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Comic_strip)) is a sequence of adjacent drawn images, usually arranged horizontally, that tells a narrative or a chronological sequence. Comic strips can use words, balloons, or no words at all, and the form belongs to mass media in newspapers, magazines, books, and later graphic novels.

If words dominate the images, the work becomes closer to illustration; in a comic strip, the sequence of pictures carries the storytelling force.
The comic strip is also distinguished from related forms such as caricature, editorial cartoons, comic books, and the longer graphic novel. The medium developed unevenly across countries, but everywhere it became a major form of popular visual storytelling.

## Definitions and Early Forms

A comic book is a bound collection of strips or short stories, while the term comic strip became standard in the United States around 1900. Earlier forms were not always funny, and many early strips were serious, satirical, didactic, or narrative rather than comic in the modern sense.

Different languages name the form differently, such as bande dessinee ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bande_dessin%C3%A9e)) in French, fumetto in Italian, and picture story terms in German.

European predecessors such as sequential art ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sequential_art)), picture stories, and satirical image sequences established the form before the modern newspaper strip. The vocabulary for the form differs by language, but the underlying idea is the same: pictures arranged in sequence to produce meaning, story, and rhythm.

Sequence is what gives the form its special power. A single caricature can mock a face or event, but a strip can show timing, surprise, repetition, pursuit, transformation, and character development. Panels divide time into visible units, letting readers infer movement and speech between drawings.

Europe is the main cradle of the modern strip. Swiss artist Rodolphe Topffer was a foundational figure, and later creators such as Wilhelm Busch made the form more fully professional and widely popular. Cheap image broadsheets, satirical magazines, and children's picture stories all contributed to the evolution of the medium.

In Britain, characters such as Ally Sloper became important early recurring comic figures, while continental European artists developed adventure, satire, and social comedy. These works helped establish both the continuing character and the serialized visual gag or story.

## The United States

![[attachments/caricature_cartoon_and_comic_strip_comic_strip_003.webp|420]]

*The Yellow Kid, an early American newspaper comic-strip character*

The modern newspaper strip developed in the newspaper wars of the late 19th century. The Yellow Kid in the New York World and later in Hearst's papers helped establish the regular colour comic supplement and the use of word balloons as a standard feature.

The newspaper wars also encouraged full-colour supplements, recurring characters, syndication, and formula-based longevity.

Strips such as the Katzenjammer Kids gave the medium its fully developed newspaper form, with recurring characters, tight panel structure, and wide newspaper syndication. From there, the comic strip became a mass-market form that could move from gag to continuity and from children to adults.

## Europe, Latin America, and Asia

British strips developed specialized children's papers and later influential figures such as Rupert, Dandy, and The Beano. In the postwar era, British comics ranged from patriotic adventure to crude adult satire, while Europe also developed sophisticated graphic novels and politically engaged strips.

Latin America produced major national comics traditions, especially in Mexico, Argentina, and Chile, where comic books, satirical strips, and political criticism became important. In Asia, manga and related forms grew into major cultural industries, with Japan becoming the most influential center of long-form graphic storytelling.

## The Second Half of the 20th Century and Beyond

After 1965 the medium underwent a renewed expansion into parodic, satirical, erotic, surreal, and experimental comics. Mad, underground comix, and later graphic novels widened the medium's audience and subject matter, and artists such as R. Crumb, Garry Trudeau, and others helped define a more adult and socially self-aware comics culture.
Women and minority creators rose, feminist comics grew, and comics were eventually institutionalized in museums, universities, publishers, and scholarly criticism. Comics have become a legitimate cultural field with its own history, theory, and canon.

## Conclusion

The comic strip is a flexible form that moved from satire and children's entertainment into a broad art of narrative, politics, identity, and cultural critique. Comics are not a minor form but a major modern art of sequence and image.

- 1952: Mad magazine launches a new satirical mode.
- 1965: Underground comix begin in San Francisco.
- Late 20th century: Graphic novels and comics scholarship expand.
