# Newton

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> Isaac Newton transformed natural philosophy by uniting mathematical method, mechanics, astronomy, and universal gravitation in the Principia. His work on light and color, later published in Opticks, alongside his Cambridge career and scientific controversies, made him foundational to modern physical science.

## Death and Legacy

![[attachments/newton_001.webp|420]]

*Portrait of Sir Isaac Newton*

Sir Isaac Newton, who died in London on March 20 [March 31], 1727, was the culminating figure of the Scientific Revolution ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scientific_Revolution)) of the 17th century. In optics, he showed that white light is composed of colours and laid the foundation for modern physical optics. In mechanics, his three laws of motion and law of universal gravitation became basic principles of modern physics. In mathematics, he independently discovered infinitesimal calculus.

Newton's Philosophiae Naturalis Principia Mathematica ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philosophi%C3%A6_Naturalis_Principia_Mathematica)), published in 1687, was one of the most important works in modern science. It joined mathematical method, mechanics, astronomy, and universal gravitation into a system that transformed natural philosophy. His later Opticks ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Opticks)) gave final published form to decades of work on light, colour, and speculative natural philosophy.

## Formative Influences

Newton was born in Woolsthorpe after his father, also Isaac Newton, had died. His mother Hannah Ayscough remarried Barnabas Smith, leaving Newton with his grandmother for years. This separation has been linked to his insecurity, rage in controversy, and intense defensiveness. Though initially expected to manage family property, he was unsuited to farming and returned to school at Grantham before entering Trinity College, Cambridge, in 1661.

## Scientific Revolution at Cambridge

At Cambridge, the official curriculum remained Aristotelian, but Newton discovered [[Descartes and Cartesianism (Descartes)|Descartes]], mechanical philosophy, and the new science of Copernicus, Kepler, and Galileo. He filled notebooks with Quaestiones Quaedam Philosophicae, declaring truth his closest friend above Plato and Aristotle. He absorbed the new mathematics, discovered the binomial theorem, and developed calculus, which he called fluxions.

## Early Mathematical Work

By 1669, Newton had written De Analysi, which circulated in manuscript and made his name known among a limited group. He later revised his methods in On the Methods of Series and Fluxions. Though little published, he had already become one of Europe's leading mathematicians through private work on infinite series, slopes of curves, areas under curves, and infinitesimal analysis.

## Plague Years

When plague closed Cambridge in 1665, Newton returned to Woolsthorpe and made foundational advances. He developed calculus, wrote the essay Of Colours, analyzed circular motion, and derived the inverse-square relation between planetary force and distance from the Sun. These discoveries remained mostly unknown at the time but later became central to optics and gravitation.

## Career and Optics

Newton became a Trinity fellow in 1667 and Lucasian professor of mathematics in 1669, succeeding Isaac Barrow. His first lectures treated optics, developing earlier work into what later became Book One of Opticks ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Opticks)). He accepted a mechanical view of light but chose a corpuscular theory, treating light as material particles in motion.

Newton's decisive optical discovery was that white light is not simple but heterogeneous, composed of rays with immutable properties corresponding to colours. Prism experiments in 1665-1666 showed that colour comes from analyzing white light into components, not from modifying pure white light. This overturned older Aristotelian and Cartesian theories of colour.

## Controversy

Newton's optical paper provoked criticism from Robert Hooke, a leader of the Royal Society, and Newton reacted with extraordinary anger. The experience led him toward withdrawal from public debate. In 1675, he submitted another paper on colours in thin films, discovering the concentric periodic rings later called Newton's rings. He also sent a speculative Hypothesis of Light, which renewed conflict with Hooke.

## Forces, Ether, and Natural Philosophy

Newton's thinking moved from mechanical ether explanations toward attractions and repulsions between particles. By about 1679, he began applying such forces to chemical affinity, heat generation, surface tension, capillary action, cohesion, and related phenomena. He later partially revived ether, but his central move was to make forces mathematically treatable, bridging mechanical imagery and the Pythagorean belief in mathematical reality.

## The Principia

Hooke's letter of 1679 suggested planetary motion as continuous diversion from straight-line motion by central attraction. Newton disliked correction by Hooke but accepted the dynamic insight. Edmond Halley visited Newton in 1684, learned that Newton had solved the problem of orbital dynamics, and persuaded him to send a demonstration. The small tract De Motu expanded into the Principia within two and a half years.

## Universal Gravitation

The law of universal gravitation states that every particle of matter attracts every other particle with force proportional to the product of their masses and inversely proportional to the square of the distance between their centres. Newton connected circular motion, Kepler's third law, Jupiter's satellites, the Moon's orbit, falling bodies, tides, and comet orbits into one quantitative law. Hooke charged plagiarism, but Newton had the essential derivations independently and earlier.

## London, the Mint, and Personal Crisis

After the Principia, Newton entered wider public life. He resisted James II's attempt to Catholicize Cambridge, represented the university in the revolutionary settlement, met John Locke, and sought a London position. In 1696, he became warden of the mint and later moved permanently to London. Around 1693, the collapse of his intense relationship with Nicolas Fatio de Duillier coincided with a nervous breakdown and accusatory letters to Locke and Samuel Pepys.

## Leader of English Science

In 1703, Newton became president of the Royal Society, and in 1705 Queen Anne knighted him, the first such honour for a scientist. He ruled English science forcefully, sometimes tyrannically, as shown in his conflict with Astronomer Royal John Flamsteed over star data and publication. Newton used institutional power to press Flamsteed and later removed acknowledgments of his help from editions of the Principia.

## Conflict with Leibniz and Final Works

Newton's dispute with Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz over calculus priority dominated his last 25 years. He fought the charge of dishonesty intensely and repeatedly. He issued new editions of Opticks ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Opticks)) in Latin and English, expanded its speculative Queries, and oversaw the second and third editions of the Principia in 1713 and 1726. He also worked on chronology, prophecy, arithmetic, and religious writings.

## Final Years and Posthumous Works

In his final years, Newton presided over the Royal Society, supervised the mint, and lived with his niece Catherine Barton Conduitt and her husband. Posthumous works included The Chronology of Ancient Kingdoms Amended and Observations Upon the Prophecies of Daniel and the Apocalypse of St. John. His scientific authority and personal controversies both shaped his legacy.

## Key Events & Developments

- 1642: Newton was born at Woolsthorpe.
- 1661: Newton entered Trinity College, Cambridge.
- 1665-1666: Plague years brought major advances in calculus, optics, and gravitation.
- 1687: Principia was published.
- 1696: Newton became warden of the mint.
- 1703: Newton became president of the Royal Society.
- 1705: Newton was knighted.
- 1727: Newton died in London.

## Key People

- Isaac Newton ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isaac_Newton)): Physicist, mathematician, natural philosopher, and author of the Principia.
- Galileo Galilei ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Galileo_Galilei)): Earlier scientist whose mathematical science of motion Newton fulfilled.
- Rene Descartes ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ren%C3%A9_Descartes)): Mechanical philosopher whose ideas influenced Newton's early studies.
- Robert Hooke ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Hooke)): Critic and correspondent whose disputes with Newton shaped optics and gravitation controversies.
- Edmond Halley ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edmond_Halley)): Astronomer who prompted Newton to produce the Principia.
- Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gottfried_Wilhelm_Leibniz)): Rival in the calculus priority dispute.
- John Flamsteed ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Flamsteed)): Astronomer Royal whose data became the focus of conflict with Newton.

## Interesting Facts

- Newton's rings are coloured rings produced by thin-film interference in air between glass surfaces.
- Fluxions was Newton's private term for his calculus.
- Gravitas, Latin for heaviness or weight, supplied Newton's name for universal gravitation.
- Newton was knighted before science itself commonly received such public honour.
