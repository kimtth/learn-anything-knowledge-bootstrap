# Extraction And Processing Industries (Mining)

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> Mining extracts commercially useful minerals and ores from Earth, including the seafloor. It links geological deposits and mineral processing to industrial demand, technical methods, economic viability, and environmental consequences.

## Introduction

Mining ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mining)) is the process of extracting useful minerals from the Earth, including the sea floor. The basic terms in the field are straightforward: a [[Minerals and Rocks (Mineral)|mineral]] is a naturally occurring inorganic substance with a definite chemical composition, while an ore is a mineral or mineral mixture that can be mined at a profit. It also explains the difference between a mineral deposit and an ore deposit, because many mineral occurrences are not commercially valuable.

This distinction matters because mining decisions depend on profit. The quantity of a mineral in a deposit is not the same as the quantity that can be mined economically. As market prices rise or costs fall, more material may become ore; if prices fall or costs rise, what was once ore may become unprofitable and cease to be mined.

The economically recoverable portion is the ore reserve, while the larger total amount in the ground is the mineral inventory.

## History

![[attachments/extraction_and_processing_industries_mining_001.webp|420]]

*Tirey L. Ford and Mining Industry representatives*

Mining has a long history and is an ancient activity. Flint was one of the first useful minerals, and early people used it for tools and weapons. In the Neolithic Period ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neolithic)), shafts were dug in chalk deposits in France and Britain to reach flint pebbles. Other early minerals included ochre, copper, gold, silver, and iron. Early Egyptian mining included copper in the Sinai, and ancient stone quarrying for the pyramids of Egypt required large blocks of limestone and granite to be cut, transported, and raised.

The history section emphasizes that mining developed alongside early civilization. It was not only a source of raw material, but also a technical challenge that required knowledge of geology, transport, and labor organization.

## Prospecting and Exploration

![[attachments/extraction_and_processing_industries_mining_002.webp|420]]

*Timber Extraction*

Mining begins with prospecting, the search for a mineral deposit, and continues with [[Exploration|exploration]], the more detailed testing of a promising site. Prospecting once relied mainly on direct observation of rocks, soils, and outcrops, but modern methods use geology, geophysics, and geochemistry to detect anomalies, or unusual patterns, that may point to valuable deposits.

These modern techniques include aerial and satellite imagery, gravity and magnetic surveys, electrical and seismic methods, and the chemical testing of soils, water, rocks, vegetation, and gases. The goal is to narrow the search area and identify the most promising prospects before expensive mining begins.

## Delineation

Once a prospect is identified, exploration drilling is used to determine the size, shape, depth, and quality of the mineral body. Core drilling produces cylindrical samples of rock, which geologists examine to identify rock types, structures, and the mineral content of the deposit. These samples are used to build maps and sections, allowing geologists to estimate the ore body at depth.

Many deposits are tabular, so geologists describe them by dip, strike, hanging wall, and footwall before deciding how they can be mined.
Key economic terms include grade, cutoff grade, and break-even grade. Grade refers to the concentration of valuable mineral in the ore. The cutoff grade is the level below which mining or processing is not profitable. The break-even grade is the point where extraction costs equal revenues. These concepts show that mining is not only a geological activity, but also a financial one.

Reserve estimates therefore change as knowledge and conditions change. Additional drilling can enlarge or reduce a deposit, metallurgy can improve recovery, and roads, power, water, labour, regulation, and commodity prices can alter feasibility. A mine plan is consequently a technical, economic, and environmental judgment rather than a simple measurement of rock in the ground.

## Surface Mining

More than two-thirds of world mineral production comes from surface mining. Common methods include open-pit mining, strip mining, and quarrying. Surface mining is often chosen when deposits are near the surface or when the mineral body is large enough to justify removing large amounts of overburden. The choice between open-pit mining and underground mining depends partly on depth, cost, and the grade of the ore.

At increasing depth, an open pit may become too costly, forcing planners to compare surface stripping with underground development and higher required ore grades.

## Quarrying

Quarrying is described as the extraction of stone for building and monument use. Good dimension stone must have uniform texture and color, be free of flaws, and resist weathering. The process involves removing vegetation, stripping soil, and cutting benches or slices from the rock face. Large blocks are separated and then cut into usable sizes for construction and ornament.

## Underground Mining

Underground mining is more complicated than surface mining because the ore body must be accessed through shafts, drifts, ramps, or adits. The mining system must also handle rock pressure, ventilation, and the transport of ore and waste. Underground openings are limited by the strength of the rock and the stresses created by the overlying material.

Underground operations use several methods, including room-and-pillar mining, longwall mining, blasthole stoping, cut-and-fill mining, sublevel caving, and panel/block caving. These methods are chosen according to the shape and depth of the ore body, the strength of the rock, and the economic value of the mineral.

## Placer Mining
Placer mining recovers valuable minerals from loose deposits such as river or beach sediments. The main methods are panning, sluicing, and dredging. Panning is the simplest hand method, while sluicing and dredging use water flow and machinery to separate heavy minerals from lighter material. Placer mining is especially important for gold, tin, diamonds, and similar minerals.

## Marine Mining

Finally, marine mining deserves mention. Although the sea contains large mineral resources, exploitation is limited because the technology is difficult and the economic need is not yet pressing. Seawater is a major source of salts and magnesium, while beaches, shelves, and the ocean floor may hold further mineral wealth in the future.

## Key Events & Developments

- Mining began in prehistoric times with flint, ochre, copper, and gold.
- Modern mining developed through prospecting, geological surveys, drilling, and ore-body modeling.
- Surface, underground, placer, and marine techniques expanded the range of mineral extraction.
