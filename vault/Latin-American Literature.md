# Latin-American Literature

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> Latin-American literature includes the Spanish-language literatures of the Americas and the oral traditions that preceded conquest. Colonial chronicles, Indigenous memory, Baroque forms, independence-era writing, modernismo, and later regional experimentation created a distinctive and diverse literary field.

## Introduction

![[attachments/latin_american_literature_001.webp|420]]

*Portrait of Gabriel Garcia Marquez*

Latin-American literature ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_American_literature)) means the national literatures of the Spanish-speaking countries of the Western Hemisphere. It also reaches back to the oral expression of the American Indian civilizations conquered by Spain, whose prayers, hymns, myths, and theatre survived through memory, native record keepers, friars, priests, and chroniclers.

Before 1492, these cultures did not use alphabetic writing, but their oral narratives later entered colonial and modern literary traditions. Spanish ballads sung by [[Columbus]]'s sailors, Renaissance verse forms, and early chronicles joined native materials to create a mixed literary world after conquest.

Colonial writing began with conquest accounts, legal petitions, histories, religious works, and poetry. Writers such as Bartolome de Las Casas, Bernal Diaz del Castillo, Felipe Guaman Poma de Ayala, and Inca Garcilaso de la Vega shaped early debates over conquest, native suffering, Spanish power, and Creole memory.

By the late colonial period, Renaissance ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Renaissance)) forms in Mexico and Peru had moved toward Baroque expression. The result was the Barroco de Indias, a distinctive colonial style and one of the first strongly regional artistic forms in Spanish America.

## The Barroco de Indias

The Barroco de Indias grew from admiration for Luis de Gongora y Argote, whose difficult syntax, mythological allusions, daring metaphors, and dense poetic language inspired colonial poets through the 17th century and into the 18th century.

Baroque poetry also encouraged satire. Mateo Rosas de Oquendo attacked colonial Peru in a poem of 1598, while Juan del Valle y Caviedes became the best-known satirist of the period through poems that mocked bodily weakness and colonial society.

The period reached a high point with Sor Juana Ines de la Cruz, whose poetry, plays, and prose combined learning, wit, religious seriousness, and intellectual independence. Her answer to Sor Filotea argued that women should be allowed intellectual interests, making her a major colonial writer with ideas that look forward to later periods.

## The Eighteenth Century

The 18th century opened under Bourbon rule after the War of the Spanish Succession. The early Bourbon kings sought order, defense reform, new taxes, and a literary language that made political change seem like a return to Castilian tradition.

## The Bourbon Reforms

Philip V, Luis I, and Ferdinand VI began reforms that culminated under Charles III. His Caroline reforms altered Spanish America, while the expulsion of the Jesuits in 1767 changed intellectual life across the viceroyalties.

The Viceroyalty of New Granada became important for science and commerce after being founded in 1717, suppressed in 1723, and reestablished in 1739. European scientists measured and classified plants, stones, and animals, while merchants sought wealth and marriage into Creole ruling families.

## Historiographies

[[The Study of History (Historiography)|Historiography]] became a major Enlightenment form in Spanish America. Jose Gumilla wrote a modern account of the Orinoco region, while Juan Jose de Eguiara y Eguren gathered New Spain's writings in the incomplete Bibliotheca mexicana of 1755.

Other Creole and colonial writers connected scholarship to regional pride. Mariano Beristain de Souza, Jose Martin Felix de Arrate y Acosta, and Jose Eusebio de Llano Zapata described libraries, Havana, South American nature, economics, and science with both humanist learning and modern intellectual interests.

Some historical writing moved toward independence. Fray Servando Teresa de Mier and Juan Pablo Viscardo y Guzman attacked Spanish rule, presented colonial government as tyranny, and gave later independence writers a forceful language of accusation.

## Plays

Eighteenth-century theatre often celebrated royal power rather than revolution. Occasional dramas marked arrivals, birthdays, and military victories, and they reinforced hierarchies of estate, caste, and gender for both literate and illiterate audiences.

Playwrights reworked Calderon and Lope de Vega while also producing dramas about conquest and kingship. Fernando de Orbea used Spanish and Quechua in a conquest drama, Pedro de Peralta Barnuevo adapted French Neoclassical forms, and Francisco del Castillo moved between Baroque and French Neoclassical theatre.

## Poetry

Spiritual and lyrical poetry continued in the 18th century. Mother Francisca Josefa de la Concepcion de Castillo y Guevara wrote mystical prose and verse, while Juan Bautista Aguirre joined spiritual, lyrical, and satirical modes during the long shift from late Baroque to Neoclassicism.

Epic and descriptive poetry also appeared. Pedro de Peralta Barnuevo's Lima fundada mixed conquest themes with literary footnotes, while Francisco Ruiz de Leon's Hernandia made Cortes appear more like a courtier than a conqueror and showed the Rococo influence of opera.

## Late Colonial Writing

Rafael Landivar's Latin poem Rusticatio mexicana of 1782 praised the animals, plants, minerals, agriculture, textiles, and mining of New Spain. Satirical poetry remained strong, especially in attacks on Lima's social fabric, false nobility, smuggling, fashion, and prostitution.

Late colonial fiction introduced the Latin American novel. Francisco Javier Eugenio de Santa Cruz y Espejo ridiculed scholastic education and promoted reform, while Jose Joaquin Fernandez de Lizardi's El Periquillo Sarniento of 1816 helped establish the novel as a vehicle for rational criticism of society.

## The Nineteenth Century

The 19th century followed political independence with a search for cultural independence. Writers adapted Neoclassical forms to new national purposes or rejected them as reminders of colonial authority.

## Romanticism

Romanticism ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Romanticism)) grew beside the independence movements. Jose Maria de Heredia joined Neoclassical form with emotional intensity, nature, exile, and Cuban political protest, while Andres Bello and Jose Joaquin de Olmedo praised agriculture and independence heroes.

In Argentina, Esteban Echeverria brought Romantic ideas from Paris and tied literature to democracy. Domingo Faustino Sarmiento's Facundo explored civilization and barbarism, while Cuban antislavery fiction by Gertrudis Gomez de Avellaneda, Anselmo Suarez y Romero, and Cirilo Villaverde exposed slavery and racial hierarchy.

## The Romantic Novel

Jorge Isaacs's Maria and Jose Marmol's Amalia belonged to the major Romantic narratives named for women. Cirilo Villaverde's Cecilia Valdes joined Romantic feeling to Realist detail and denounced slavery in Cuba under Spanish rule.

The cuadro de costumbres described rural people and poor urban groups whose customs differed from modern elites. Ricardo Palma created a Peruvian version in his tradiciones, published across the period from 1872 to 1910.

English selections from Ricardo Palma's Tradiciones peruanas appeared in 1945, showing the later importance of those brief historical narratives for Latin American literary history.

## Modernismo

Modernismo ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modernismo)) was the first major New World literary movement after the Baroque. Its leader, Ruben Dario, made Azul in 1888 a turning point by rejecting Romantic rhetoric and seeking musical, beautiful, modern, and often exotic poetic language.

Dario experimented with metre, rhythm, rhyme, prose music, and asymmetrical stanza forms. His Prosas profanas of 1896 was daring in subject and form, while Cantos de vida y esperanza of 1905 became more mature, reflective, and political.

Jose Marti matched poetic innovation with journalism and political writing. His Versos sencillos of 1891 became widely known, and his essays argued for Latin American cultural and political independence.

## The Vanguardia

Jose Marti's Nuestra America of 1891 served as a manifesto for cultural and political independence. The next major movement, the vanguardia, reacted when Modernismo's innovations began to feel routine.

The vanguardia drew especially on European Surrealism but developed distinctive Latin American forms. It sought new themes, confrontational styles, and unusual forms, with poetry changing most quickly because it reached a smaller and more experimental audience.

## Vanguard Poets

Vanguard poets included Pablo Neruda, Octavio Paz, Nicolas Guillen, Jose Lezama Lima, Luis Pales Matos, Jorge Luis Borges, Oliverio Girondo, and Ernesto Cardenal. Gabriela Mistral, Neruda, and Paz later won Nobel Prizes.

Neruda's Residencia en la Tierra of 1925-35 used free association, dark imagery, and a restless poetic self. After the Spanish Civil War of 1936-39, his voice became more collective, leading to Canto general in 1950, a vast poem of Latin American history.

The period also produced the Mexican Revolution novel and the novela de la tierra. Mariano Azuela's Los de abajo of 1915 became the best-known Mexican Revolution novel, while regionalist fiction made nature, industry, and national identity central themes.

## The Novela de la Tierra

Ricardo Guiraldes's Don Segundo Sombra, Romulo Gallegos's Dona Barbara, and Jose Eustasio Rivera's La voragine presented rural struggle against nature. Cattle ranching in the llano and pampa, and rubber prospecting in the Colombian jungle, became national and almost mythic conflicts.

At the same time, Jorge Luis Borges offered a bookish alternative to direct regional realism. He treated gaucho stories as repetitions of Greek and biblical patterns, rejected the long novel, and mastered stories built from invented references and apocryphal sources.

Indigenous and African traditions also entered modern narrative. Miguel Angel Asturias's Leyendas de Guatemala of 1930 retold Maya stories, and Lydia Cabrera's Cuentos negros de Cuba of 1940 drew on Afro-Cuban materials.

## Toward Magic Realism

Jorge Icaza's Huasipungo gave regionalism a sharper political edge by showing Indians as victims of oppression and economic exploitation. Regionalism and the avant-garde then joined in early magic realism.

Miguel Angel Asturias's El senor presidente of 1946 used Surrealist methods to represent dictatorship and fear. Alejo Carpentier's El reino de este mundo of 1949 treated Haiti from 1750 to 1820 and presented the fantastic as real within African-derived belief.

Juan Rulfo became another key bridge to the later novel through El llano en llamas and Pedro Paramo. His fiction used violence, lust, incest, stream of consciousness, flashbacks, and multiple narrators to create a harsh, compact fictional world.

## The Boom

In the second half of the 20th century, Pablo Neruda, Octavio Paz, Miguel Angel Asturias, and Gabriel Garcia Marquez received Nobel Prizes. Jorge Luis Borges, Alejo Carpentier, Juan Rulfo, and Joao Guimaraes Rosa gained broad international recognition.

The boom centered especially on Garcia Marquez, Julio Cortazar, Carlos Fuentes, and Mario Vargas Llosa, with writers such as Juan Carlos Onetti, Jose Donoso, Jose Lezama Lima, and Guillermo Cabrera Infante also important.

Boom novelists adapted modern techniques from Marcel Proust, James Joyce, Franz Kafka, John Dos Passos, William Faulkner, and Ernest Hemingway. Stream of consciousness, unreliable narrators, fragmented plots, cinematic influence, humour, and sexual frankness entered Latin American themes.

Major boom works included Garcia Marquez's Cien anos de soledad of 1967, Cortazar's Rayuela of 1963, Fuentes's La muerte de Artemio Cruz of 1962, and Vargas Llosa's La ciudad y los perros of 1963.

Other writers widened the field. Manuel Puig used popular culture and film forms, Severo Sarduy wrote playful and difficult novels linked to Parisian theory, and Miguel Barnet helped create testimonial narrative through a former slave's first-person life story.

## Testimonial Narrative

Miguel Barnet's Biografia de un cimarron of 1966 began testimonial narrative, in which a writer interviews a marginal person and presents the result in first person. The centenarian former slave and Maroon Esteban Montejo made the work especially influential.

In the 1980s and 1990s, boom writers continued to publish important books. Fuentes wrote La campana, Vargas Llosa wrote La fiesta del chivo, and Garcia Marquez returned both to fiction and journalism.

Women novelists gained new visibility near the end of the 20th century. Isabel Allende, Luisa Valenzuela, and other women writers became part of the post-boom scene, while essayists continued to examine culture, politics, identity, and literary history.

## The Essay Tradition

Mariano Picon Salas's De la conquista a la independencia of 1944 joined a major essay tradition that also included Jose Carlos Mariategui, Pedro Henriquez Urena, and Octavio Paz. Paz's El laberinto de la soledad of 1950 strongly influenced Mexican writing.

At the turn of the 21st century, literature seemed to move from modernism toward postmodernism. The newer writing kept self-conscious technique but moved away from heavy claims about identity, nation, and humanity toward play, chance, shifting sexuality, and less stable plots.

Severo Sarduy was presented as a herald of this postmodern change, though no later writer of equal stature fixed the tendency. Antonio Benitez Rojo's La isla que se repite of 1989 offered the most significant statement on postmodernism in the essay tradition.

## Key Events & Developments

- 1492: [[Columbus]]'s arrival began the colonial encounter that joined Spanish forms with native oral traditions.
- The 17th century: gongorismo shaped colonial poetry and helped define the Barroco de Indias.
- 1767: Charles III expelled the Jesuits, changing Spanish American intellectual life.
- 1816: Jose Joaquin Fernandez de Lizardi's El Periquillo Sarniento helped establish the Latin American novel.
- 1888: Ruben Dario's Azul made Modernismo a decisive literary movement.
- 1960s: The boom brought international attention to writers such as Garcia Marquez, Cortazar, Fuentes, and Vargas Llosa.

## Interesting Facts

- Testimonial narrative grew from interviews with marginal people and presented their lives in first person.
- Postmodern fiction in this account moves away from national identity themes toward play, chance, and unstable social forms.
