# Economic Systems

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> Economic systems organize the production, distribution, and consumption of goods and services. Markets, property, institutions, and public policy create different arrangements of incentives and control.

## Introduction

Economic systems ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economic_system)) are the ways human societies arrange material provisioning. Although human cultures have been very diverse, history has produced only a few basic ways to organize economic life. These are systems based on tradition, systems organized by command, and systems coordinated by the market. Each system must solve the same basic problem. It must bring many separate human activities into a working social whole so that the society can continue to live and carry out its aims.

## Distribution by Tradition

![[attachments/economic_systems_001.webp|420]]

*Fundamentals of Business - Fig. 2.2 - Economic Systems*

Marshall Thomas described the sharing of meat among Bushmen as a system that could look unequal at first but became equal in the end. A hunter might receive a large share because he had made the kill, and relatives or in-laws might receive pieces through social ties. Yet the hunter was expected to pass meat to others, and the final result was that no person ate more than another. The example shows how distribution by tradition can work through kinship, duty, and shared expectations rather than through prices or official orders.

## The Power of Command

![[attachments/economic_systems_002.webp|420]]

*Common Economic Systems Typology (v1)*

Herodotus described how the pharaoh Khufu ordered Egyptians to work for him, with some drawing stones from Arabian mountain quarries to the Nile and others receiving the stones after river transport. He wrote that groups of 100,000 men worked for three months at a time and that ten years were spent building the road used to move the stones. The example shows the central power of command systems: they can gather labour and resources on a scale that tradition alone cannot achieve.

## Equity

Equity ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equity_%28finance%29)) is the third major criticism of capitalist growth. It concerns how fairly capitalism distributes both its growing wealth and its recurring hardships. The specific criticism focuses on income differences. At the turn of the 21st century in the United States, the lowest fifth of households received only 3.6 percent of total income, while the top fifth received 49 percent. This gap reflects the concentration of assets among higher-income groups and corporate reward patterns in which chief executive officers could earn 50 to 100 times more than ordinary office or factory workers.

## Assessment

Economic systems ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economic_system)) may be losing some of the sharp differences that once separated them. Instead of three clearly divided types, modern societies may form a continuum where market and planning exist in different proportions. A country may still call itself capitalist or socialist, but its actual solutions to production, distribution, instability, equity, and public welfare may resemble those of countries with different labels.

## Key Events & Developments

- 3rd millennium BC: Centralized states emerged in [[Egypt]], [[China]], and [[India]], using command power to mobilize labour for temples, irrigation, cities, and fortifications.
- Around 1400 BC: Trade between Levantine kingdoms and the pharaohs of [[Egypt]], recorded in the tablets of Tell el-Amarna, showed that markets existed long before full market societies.
- In the 10th and 11th centuries: A rising mercantile estate in Europe began bargaining with lords and monarchs for recognition and protection.
- In the 16th and 17th centuries: Aristocratic groups became more commercial, and wealthy merchant families became socially important in a more money-oriented order.
- In 1776: [[Adam Smith]]'s The Wealth of Nations described commercial capitalism, competition, capital accumulation, and a limited role for government.
- From the late 18th century into the 19th century: The Industrial Revolution ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Industrial_Revolution)) transformed capitalism through mechanization, factories, steam power, iron, and later steel.
- Between 1788 and 1839: British pig iron output rose from 68,000 tons to 1,347,000 tons, showing the growth of industrial productive power.
- In 1867: Karl Marx published the first volume of Das Kapital, giving a major criticism of capitalist instability and concentration.
- In the 1930s: The Great Depression shook capitalism, and John Maynard Keynes argued that capitalism could remain stuck with high unemployment.
- In 1917: The Russian Revolution made socialist planning a practical problem rather than only a moral or theoretical idea.
- By the end of 1920: The Soviet economy neared collapse, leading Vladimir Lenin to introduce the New Economic Policy.
- In the 1930s: Joseph Stalin eliminated the NEP and built a centralized planning system around ministries, planning agencies, and Gosplan.
- In 1985: Mikhail Gorbachev launched perestroika, reducing central planning and allowing more enterprise freedom.
- In 1989-90: Eastern European countries began moving away from Soviet-style control as the Soviet Union lost power over the region.
- In 1991: The Soviet Union broke into independent republics after central planning eroded and the Communist Party lost power.

## Key People

- [[Adam Smith]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adam_Smith)): Explained commercial capitalism, competition, capital accumulation, and the limited government functions of defense, justice, and public works.
- Karl Marx ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karl_Marx)): Criticized capitalism for instability, exploitation, alienation, concentration of capital, and the social costs of factory production.
- Friedrich Engels ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Friedrich_Engels)): With Karl Marx, described capitalism's immense productive power and disruptive force in The Communist Manifesto.
- Marshall Thomas ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marshall_Thomas_Wilton_Eady)): Described the Bushmen meat-sharing system to show how traditional distribution could look unequal but end in broad sharing.
- Marshall Sahlins ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marshall_Sahlins)): Argued that some preliterate peoples were not trapped in scarcity and could be understood as part of the first affluent society.
- Herodotus ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Herodotus)): Gave the account of Khufu's use of Egyptian labour, illustrating the power of command systems.
- Khufu ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Khufu)): The pharaoh cited as an example of a ruler able to mobilize enormous labour for pyramid-related works.
- [[Aristotle and Aristotelianism|Aristotle]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aristotle)): Expressed the ancient belief that a free person should not live for the benefit of another, showing why nonslave labour was not treated as a normal market commodity.
- Thomas Mun ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Mun)): Defended mercantilist trade by arguing that treasure sent abroad could serve national interest if exports exceeded imports.
- Thomas Newcomen ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Newcomen)): Invented steam-driven engines used in coal mines, an early sign of the mechanization that later transformed capitalism.
- William Blake ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Blake)): Condemned factories as dark Satanic mills, capturing the cultural reaction to industrial labour.
- Charles Dickens ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_Dickens)): In Hard Times, depicted factory inhumanity and criticized the doctrines associated with industrial capitalism.
- Paul Bairoch ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_Bairoch)): Estimated long-run changes in per capita GNP, showing the widening gap between industrialized and less-developed countries.
- John Maynard Keynes ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Maynard_Keynes)): Argued that capitalism could remain in high-unemployment equilibrium and needed public action to prevent stagnation.
- [[Franklin D. Roosevelt]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Franklin_D._Roosevelt)): Expanded U.S. government welfare and recovery roles during the Great Depression.
- Gerrard Winstanley ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gerrard_Winstanley)): Represented an early egalitarian socialist moral idea through the Diggers movement in the English Civil Wars.
- Vladimir Lenin ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vladimir_Lenin)): Led the first attempt to organize socialism after the Russian Revolution and later introduced the NEP when collapse threatened.
- Joseph Stalin ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph_Stalin)): Abandoned the NEP and imposed collectivization and centralized planning in the Soviet Union.
- Leon Trotsky ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leon_Trotsky)): Took part in the post-Lenin struggle over Soviet direction and the unresolved debate about planning and incentives.
- Nikolay Bukharin ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nikolay_Bukharin)): Took part in the same post-Lenin struggle and debate over the Soviet economy.
- Mikhail Gorbachev ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mikhail_Gorbachev)): Began perestroika in 1985 to restructure Soviet planning and introduce more market-like enterprise freedom.

## Interesting Facts

- Economic systems ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economic_system)) are fewer in basic form than the many cultures of the world might suggest: they reduce mainly to tradition, command, and market.
- Tradition-based societies can distribute goods through social claims so effectively that a hunter's large share may still end with equal eating.
- Marshall Sahlins's idea of the first affluent society challenges the assumption that preliterate peoples always lived in constant want.
- [[Markets]] existed in ancient societies, but they usually handled luxuries or surplus goods rather than the main provisioning of society.
- [[Aristotle and Aristotelianism|Aristotle]]'s view of freedom helps explain why free labour was not commonly for sale in ancient societies.
- Capitalism ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Capitalism)) required not just markets but a new social order where individuals were free and expected to seek material improvement.
- [[Adam Smith]] did not use the word capitalism in The Wealth of Nations, yet the society he described is recognizable as capitalist.
- Industrial capitalism changed the size of workplaces from Smith's 10-person pin factory to railway workforces of tens of thousands by the end of the 19th century.
- 1788 to 1839 saw a 20-fold rise in British pig iron output, helping create the tools, rails, pipes, and machines of industrial growth.
- During the 1930s Great Depression in the United States, business investment fell by 94 percent and unemployment rose to nearly 25 percent.
- State capitalism expanded because governments became expected to protect public economic well-being, not only provide defense and law.
- By the beginning of the 21st century, foreign-exchange transactions were estimated to be at least 20 times larger than foreign movements of goods and services.
- Soviet planning worked better for crash goals such as industrialization, postwar repair, and space technology than for ordinary civilian provisioning.
- Perestroika weakened Soviet central planning without quickly creating a full market system, helping produce economic paralysis by 1990.
- Mixed economies are the practical norm because tradition, command, and market almost always appear together in different proportions.
