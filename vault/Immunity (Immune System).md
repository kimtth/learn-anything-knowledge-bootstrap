# Immunity (Immune System)

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> Immunity is the coordinated defense by which vertebrates resist pathogens and remove abnormal cells through rapid broad innate responses and antigen-specific acquired responses with memory. Barriers, inflammation, complement, B cells, antibodies, T cells, and other mechanisms work together to protect the body.

## Introduction

Immunity is the set of defense responses by which humans and other advanced vertebrates resist pathogens and remove abnormal cells. It has two cooperating arms: innate immunity ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Innate_immune_system)), which acts broadly and quickly against many invaders, and specific acquired immunity, which targets particular antigens and often leaves memory for later encounters.

Innate defenses include barriers, antimicrobial chemicals, complement proteins, interferons, scavenger cells, natural killer cells, inflammation, and fever. Acquired defenses depend on lymphocytes: B cells make antibodies for humoral immunity, while T cells regulate responses and destroy infected or abnormal body cells. The two systems constantly reinforce one another.

## Innate Barriers

Skin is a mechanical and chemical barrier. Its tough outer layer is renewed from below and contains keratin, while skin glands secrete fatty acids such as oleic acid and enzymes such as lysozyme that can damage bacteria. Severe burns show how vital intact skin is, because normally harmless microbes can then invade.

Mucous membranes protect the respiratory, gastrointestinal, and genitourinary tracts. Respiratory mucus traps particles, and cilia sweep mucus toward the throat and nose. Gut mucus blocks microbial attachment, while hydrochloric acid in the stomach kills many organisms. Protective antibodies in mucus show that innate barriers and acquired immunity cooperate at body surfaces.

Chemical barriers include substances that limit microbial growth. Transferrin binds iron and leaves less free iron for bacteria. Normal intestinal bacteria secrete proteins that inhibit invading bacterial species. These ordinary ecological and chemical conditions help prevent infection before specialized immune cells are recruited.

## Complement and Interferons

Complement is a protein system that helps destroy microorganisms and links innate and acquired immunity. Some microbes directly activate complement, while antibody-coated targets activate the classical pathway. Complement can lyse pathogens, coat them for phagocytes, amplify its own response, and promote inflammation.

Interferons ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interferon)), discovered as a category in 1957, inhibit viral replication. Alpha interferon is produced by white blood cells other than lymphocytes, beta interferon by fibroblasts, and gamma interferon by lymphocytes. They interfere with viral nucleic-acid transcription, regulate surface molecules, and stimulate natural killer cells.

## Cellular Innate Defenses

Scavenger cells are mainly leukocytes that destroy infectious agents. Elie Metchnikoff recognized their importance in 1884 and named microphages and macrophages. They engulf invaders by phagocytosis, digest them with enzymes and toxic molecules, and help prepare later immune responses.

Granulocytes include neutrophils, eosinophils, and basophils. Neutrophils make up about 60 to 70 percent of white blood cells and chiefly attack bacteria. Eosinophils damage larger parasites, while basophils release heparin, histamine, and other substances important in allergic reactions. Mast cells resemble basophils and also contribute to immune responses.

Macrophages mature from monocytes, settle in tissues such as spleen, lymph nodes, and liver, and filter blood or lymph for microbes and foreign particles. They move more slowly than granulocytes and arrive later at invasion sites, but they live longer and are especially important because they present antigens and stimulate specific immunity.

Natural killer cells, first recognized in 1975, kill virus-infected or cancerous body cells without requiring antigen-specific recognition. They contain cytotoxic granules that enter target cells and cause leakage or death. Gamma interferon increases their activity, making them part of the bridge between early defense and later immune control.

## Fever and Inflammation

Acute-phase responses begin when macrophages release interleukins such as IL-1 and IL-6. These signals act on the hypothalamus to raise body temperature and on the liver to produce acute-phase proteins. Fever may help because many bacteria grow best below normal body temperature, while acute-phase proteins bind microbes and activate complement.

Inflammation brings pain, redness, swelling, heat, and altered blood flow. Macrophage chemicals make capillaries leakier, encourage clotting, and attract leukocytes. Neutrophils and eosinophils can arrive within about an hour; macrophages follow after a few hours. Persistent infection then draws in antibodies and T cells.

## Acquired Immunity

Specific acquired immunity recognizes particular antigens and can produce memory. Thucydides observed during the plague in [[Athens]] in the 5th century BC that recovered people did not catch the disease again. Smallpox, chicken pox, measles, and mumps often produce lasting protection, while colds recur because many different viruses can cause similar symptoms.

Lymphocytes are the central cells of acquired immunity. Adult humans have roughly 2 trillion lymphocytes, but only about 1 percent circulate in the blood at a given time. Most are in bone marrow, spleen, thymus, lymph nodes, tonsils, and intestinal lymphoid tissue. T cells mature in the thymus; B cells mature from bone marrow precursors.

Antigens are foreign materials that bind lymphocyte receptors. Antigens that provoke immune responses are immunogens. Haptens bind receptors but become immunogenic only when joined to larger molecules. Distinct surface patterns called epitopes, or antigenic determinants, allow lymphocytes to recognize complex antigenic mosaics and explain cross-reactions between antigens sharing an epitope.

Receptor diversity allows the immune system to recognize a huge range of molecules without having separate inherited genes for every receptor. Diversity arises through random gene-segment rearrangement, junctional diversification, and somatic mutation. Each mature lymphocyte carries one main receptor type, so antigen recognition selects particular clones.

## B Cells and Antibodies

*Diagram of an antibody molecule with heavy chains, light chains, and antigen-binding regions*

B cells recognize free or extracellular antigens and can become plasma cells that secrete antibodies. Antibodies are immunoglobulins shaped like flexible Y molecules, with two heavy chains and two light chains. Variable regions form antigen-binding sites, while constant regions determine biological effects such as complement activation or binding to immune cells.

IgG is the most common immunoglobulin in blood and tissue fluids. It has four subclasses and can cross the placenta, giving some fetal protection. IgM is the first class made by maturing B cells and is secreted as a pentamer with 10 antigen-binding sites, making it effective at clumping bacteria.

IgA is the main antibody in tears, saliva, respiratory and intestinal secretions, and colostrum. More IgA is synthesized daily than any other antibody, though it is less stable than IgG. IgD is found mainly on early B cells, and IgE binds mast cells and basophils, triggering allergy-related histamine release and helping defend against parasites.

Class switching lets some B cells move from IgM production to IgG, IgA, or IgE, influenced by cytokines and antigen location. Plasma cells can release several thousand immunoglobulin molecules per minute for several days, while memory B cells persist for months or years and respond rapidly after reexposure.

## T Cells and Clonal Selection

T-cell receptors, identified in 1983, are membrane-bound and recognize antigen fragments displayed by body cells. Most are alpha-beta receptors, while fewer are gamma-delta receptors. A typical T cell may carry about 20,000 receptor molecules, but it does not bind free-floating antigen the way a B cell does.

MHC proteins, called HLA proteins in humans, display antigen fragments. Class I MHC appears on almost all nucleated body cells and is recognized by cytotoxic T cells using CD8. Class II MHC appears mainly on B cells, macrophages, and related cells and is recognized by helper T cells using CD4. CD3 transmits activation signals inside the T cell.

Clonal selection ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clonal_selection)) activates lymphocytes whose receptors match an antigen, producing effector cells for the present response and memory cells for future encounters. Many self-reactive T cells are eliminated in the thymus before release, though self-reactivity is less completely removed in B cells. Memory produces faster and stronger secondary responses.

Helper T cells require antigen presentation plus a second signal, such as cytokine stimulation or B7-CD28 costimulation. Without this second signal, they may become anergic. TH1 cells mainly support macrophages and cytotoxic T cells; TH2 cells mainly help B cells make antibodies through cytokines and CD40 ligand-CD40 interaction.

## Antibody-Mediated Mechanisms

Antibodies protect by neutralizing toxins, blocking microbial attachment, immobilizing flagellated organisms, activating complement, and marking pathogens for phagocytosis. Toxins from diphtheria and tetanus can be neutralized when antibodies bind them before they attach to body cells.

Complement cascades involve more than 30 proteins. Classical activation begins with antigen-antibody complexes, especially IgM or IgG, while the alternative pathway can start on pathogen or tumour surfaces. Both converge at C3, producing C3a and C3b. C3b supports membrane attack, amplification, alternative activation, and opsonization.

Opsonization coats microbes so macrophages and neutrophils bind and ingest them more readily. The membrane attack complex, made from C5 through C9, punches holes in pathogen membranes. Complement fragments also attract leukocytes and cause mast cells and basophils to release histamine, strengthening inflammation.

Mucosal and parasite defense uses IgA and IgE in different ways. IgA blocks microbial entry and helps sweep organisms from secretions. IgE on mast cells and basophils increases vascular permeability, fluid secretion, and smooth-muscle contraction, and it works with eosinophils against large parasites such as schistosome flatworms.

## Maternal Antibodies and Rh Disease

Maternal antibody transfer gives newborn mammals temporary passive protection before their own acquired immunity develops. In humans, maternal IgG crosses the placenta during the second two-thirds of pregnancy. In horses and cattle, placental transfer does not occur, so colostrum rich in IgA, IgM, and IgG is essential during the first days of life.

Passive maternal immunity is temporary. Antibodies are diluted as the young animal grows and are broken down metabolically, so susceptibility can increase as maternal protection fades. A newborn is protected only against pathogens for which the mother already has antibodies.

Rh hemolytic disease, or erythroblastosis fetalis, occurs when maternal antibodies destroy fetal red blood cells. The severe form requires an Rh-positive fetus, an Rh-negative mother, and prior activation of the mother's immune system against Rh antigen. Anti-Rh antibody after birth of an Rh-positive child can prevent later sensitization.

## Cell-Mediated Immunity and Cancer

Cell-mediated immunity protects against pathogens that hide inside body cells, including viruses and bacteria that cause tuberculosis and leprosy. Helper T-cell gamma interferon strengthens macrophage killing and stimulates natural killer cells. Cytotoxic T cells kill infected cells by releasing perforin-containing granules or by inducing apoptosis through Fas.

Cancer immunity reflects the immune system's ability to recognize abnormal cells. Paul Ehrlich proposed in the early 20th century that immune mechanisms eliminate abnormal cells during development, and immune surveillance became influential in the 1950s and 1960s. Tumour-specific and tumour-associated antigens can provoke responses, but tumours may evade immunity by losing antigens, reducing class I MHC, secreting suppressive chemicals, shedding blocking antigens, or growing too fast.

## Immunization

Passive immunization introduces preformed antibodies or lymphocytes for immediate but temporary protection. It can help against snakebite, botulism, diphtheria, tetanus, gas gangrene, rabies exposure, hepatitis B risk, B-cell deficiencies, and Rh sensitization. Human IgG falls by about half every three weeks and usually protects for only two or three months.

Active immunization uses vaccines to stimulate the body to make its own lymphocytes, antibodies, and memory cells. Toxoids are formaldehyde-inactivated toxins used against tetanus and diphtheria. Killed vaccines include typhoid, pertussis, plague, and influenza. Attenuated live vaccines include yellow fever, oral polio, measles, rubella, and tuberculosis, though they can endanger immune-deficient people.

Vaccine design depends on presenting protective antigens safely and persistently enough. Mucosal vaccines may be powerful because they stimulate IgA. Recombinant DNA methods can make harmless organisms express protective antigens. When humans are the only reservoir and at least 95 percent of the at-risk population is protected, eradication is possible, as smallpox shows.

## Evolution and Genetic Origins

Immune evolution begins with broad self-nonself recognition. Invertebrates such as sponges, annelids, starfish, and insects can reject foreign tissue or engulf invaders, but lymphocytes and immunoglobulins appear only in vertebrates. Jawless fishes have weak responses, while sharks, rays, and bony fishes have thymus, spleen, and IgM.

Terrestrial vertebrates possess more complete immune systems, including thymus, spleen, bone marrow, lymph nodes, IgM, and IgG. IgA appears only in birds and mammals, and IgE only in mammals. Complement is highly conserved and may have evolved earlier than the antibody system.

Immunoglobulin superfamily proteins share structural domains involved in recognition. Lymphocyte receptors, MHC proteins, CD4, CD8, CD28, Fc receptors, and some nonimmune proteins all contain immunoglobulin-like domains. Gene duplication, crossing over, and mutation likely diversified an ancestral cell-recognition gene; IgM and IgG may have diverged from an IgM-like ancestor about 200 million years ago.

## Core Equations

Health and behavioural sciences often quantify risk, dose, change over time, and population variation:

$$C(t)=C_0e^{-kt}$$

This models concentration falling exponentially over time, often used for clearance, decay, or elimination processes.

$$z=\frac{x-\mu}{\sigma}$$

A z-score tells how many standard deviations a value lies above or below the mean.

$$\text{risk}=\frac{\text{cases}}{\text{population}}$$

This ratio turns the preceding idea into a measurable comparison, with the numerator as the quantity of interest and the denominator as the scale used for comparison.

## Key Events & Developments

- Early observation: Thucydides noted disease-specific protection during the 5th century BC plague in Athens.
- Phagocytosis: Elie Metchnikoff identified scavenger-cell importance in 1884.
- Interferons ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interferon)): Antiviral interferon activity was discovered in 1957.
- Natural killer cells: NK cells were recognized in 1975 as a distinct killing population.
- T-cell receptors: Membrane-bound T-cell antigen receptors were identified in 1983.
- Clonal selection ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clonal_selection)): Matching lymphocytes proliferate into effector and memory cells, explaining lasting immunity.
- Vaccination: Passive immunization gives temporary antibodies, while active immunization creates immune memory.
