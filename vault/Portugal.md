# Portugal

> [!abstract] Reference navigation

Its Roman, Moorish, and independent past, overseas expansion, relationship with Spain, and modern economy and culture connect national identity to the sea.

## Introduction

Portugal ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Portugal)), officially the Portuguese Republic, lies on the Atlantic coast of the Iberian Peninsula in southwestern Europe. It was once continental Europe's greatest power and shares geographic and cultural features with both northern Europe and the Mediterranean world. Its north is rocky, mountainous, and often sparsely settled, while the southern Algarve is warmer and more fertile.

The country's identity is closely tied to the sea. After early settlement by the Celtic Lusitani, Roman rule, Germanic peoples, and Moorish control, Portugal became an independent kingdom and then turned outward across the Atlantic and Indian oceans. The era of exploration gave Portugal a global empire but also left long-term tensions between overseas wealth and domestic underdevelopment.

Portugal's name comes from Portus Cale ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Portus_Cale)), a settlement near the Douro River and the present city of Porto. Lisbon, the capital, is linked in legend with Odysseus and Calypso, though historically the land was already occupied by the Lusitani before Roman influence. The country later fought and competed with Spain while building a separate political and cultural identity.

## Land

![[attachments/portugal_001.webp|420]]

*Topographic map of Portugal*

Portugal ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Portugal)) occupies about one-sixth of the Iberian Peninsula. Spain borders it to the north and east, while the Atlantic Ocean lies to the west and south. The Azores and Madeira Islands are also part of metropolitan Portugal, giving the country an Atlantic island dimension as well as a mainland one.

The mainland has great physical variety. Low coasts, plains, plateaus, limestone hills, and mountain ranges all appear within a small territory. The Estrela Mountains, between the Tagus and Mondego rivers, contain Portugal's highest mainland point, rising to nearly 6,500 feet or about 2,000 metres.

Portugal's main rivers, including the Douro, Tagus, and Guadiana, rise in Spain's Meseta and flow toward the Atlantic. These rivers shape settlement, agriculture, transport, and regional identity. The Tagus divides the country in broad terms: north of it the land is higher and wetter, while south of it plains, plateaus, and Mediterranean influences become stronger.

## Relief and Regions

The north is mountainous and geologically old. In the Minho, mountains form an amphitheatre facing the Atlantic, with granite, schist, quartzite, hot springs, deep valleys, and fertile pockets. The northern interior, including Tras-os-Montes, Beira Alta, and Beira Baixa, contains high plateaus, ancient rocks, deep canyons, cold uplands, and warmer sheltered valleys.

The south is lower and more Mediterranean. Beira Litoral and Estremadura descend toward sandy and marshy coasts. The Alentejo has plateaus, poor soils, quartz and marble ridges, and undulating relief. The Algarve is sheltered by hills and scarps and includes limestone, sandstone, and the Monchique Mountains, which rise at Mount Foia.

The Madeira Islands are volcanic and lie about 600 miles southwest of the mainland. Madeira itself is a mountainous island of basalt, ash, gorges, canyons, and high plateaus. Porto Santo is lower and flatter. The Azores include nine volcanic islands spread across the mid-Atlantic, with crater lakes, plateaus, massifs, valleys, and rugged coasts.

## Drainage, Soils, Climate, Vegetation, and Wildlife

Portugal's drainage is dominated by rivers flowing from Spain through defiles. Their usefulness for navigation and transport is limited, though dams and locks have made the Douro navigable near Porto to the Spanish frontier. The Mondego is the longest river wholly within Portugal, and the Minho and Guadiana form parts of the Spanish border.

The country has more than 500 miles of coastline, mostly facing west. Natural harbours are few except at major river mouths. Lisbon's harbour on the Tagus and Setubal's harbour on the Sado are especially important. Many other harbours depend on headlands or artificial protection.

Most [[Soils|soils]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soil)) are arid, acidic, and sandy, with rocky soils in the north and fertile alluvium along major rivers. Intensive farming is difficult without irrigation in many central and southern areas. Climate combines Atlantic, continental, and Mediterranean influences. The northwest is mild and rainy, the interior has harsher winters and summer drought, and the south has longer drought and warmer winters.

Portugal's vegetation ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vegetation)) mixes Atlantic and Mediterranean species. Human activity has left only about one-fourth of the land under woodland. Northern landscapes contain deciduous trees, pine, cork oak, chestnut, heaths, and maquis, while southern landscapes include cork oak, olive, carob, figs, and Mediterranean scrub. The fauna mixes European and North African forms, including deer, wild pig, wolf, lynx, fox, rabbit, rich birdlife, Atlantic fish, and distinctive island species.

## People

The Portuguese people reflect long settlement and many influences. Neolithic and Bronze Age remains, northern castros, Phoenician, Greek, Celtic, Roman, Suebi, Visigothic, Moorish, and Jewish elements all shaped the territory. More than nine-tenths of the population is ethnic Portuguese, with smaller communities from Brazil, China, and former African and Asian colonies.

Portuguese is the first language of nearly the entire population and is a strong national bond. Roman Catholicism remains the majority religion, though regular attendance declined in cities and towns, especially in the south. Protestants, Jews, and Evangelical groups form small minorities.

Settlement patterns show strong regional contrasts. Many towns date to Roman, medieval, or Reconquista origins, and fortification shaped much of settlement history. Rural forms vary from dense holdings in the Minho to nucleated villages in Tras-os-Montes and estate settlements in the south. Population is denser in the north and coastal urban areas, while some interior rural areas have suffered decline.

## Economy

![[attachments/portugal_003.webp|420]]

*Vineyards in the Douro Valley of Portugal*

Portugal was extremely wealthy when its overseas empire was at its height, but imperial wealth did not build a strong domestic industrial base. By the 19th and 20th centuries, Portugal had become one of western Europe's poorer countries. After the 1974 revolution, the economy disconnected from its African colonies and turned more fully toward Europe.

Joining the European Economic Community in 1986 encouraged strong growth, structural funds, private capital, and direct investment. Services came to dominate output, manufacturing remained significant, and agriculture became a small share of the economy. Early 21st-century growth improved living standards, but the euro-zone crisis after 2009 hit Portugal hard, leading to a major EU and IMF bailout in 2011.

Agriculture ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agriculture)) suffers from low investment, limited mechanization, little fertilizer use, and fragmented land tenure. Main crops include cereals, potatoes, grapes, olives, tomatoes, and rice. Forestry produces cork and pulp, while fishing remains important along the Atlantic coast. Tourism, especially in Lisbon, the Algarve, and the Douro valley, became a major service industry.

Manufacturing ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manufacturing)) is concentrated around Lisbon and Setubal in the south and Porto, Braga, and Aveiro in the north. Lisbon and Setubal support oil refining, chemicals, cement, automobiles, electronics, cork, pulp, fish, and beverages. Northern industry includes textiles, footwear, furniture, wine, processed foods, wood products, clothing, cutlery, and electronics.

Portugal's financial system changed sharply after the revolution. Banks and insurance were nationalized in the mid-1970s, then liberalized and privatized from the 1980s. Portugal adopted the euro in 1999, and it replaced the escudo as sole currency in 2002. Trade is heavily oriented toward the EU, especially Spain, Germany, France, Italy, and the United Kingdom.

## Government and Society

Portugal ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Portugal)) has been a republic since the overthrow of King Manuel II and the Braganca dynasty in 1910. The First Republic lasted from 1910 to 1926 but was unstable. A bloodless military coup in 1926 led to authoritarian rule, and in 1932 Antonio de Oliveira Salazar established the New State dictatorship.

The New State lasted until the Revolution of the Carnations on April 25, 1974. Its institutions looked democratic but were controlled by Salazar's supporters, and political freedoms were suppressed. After 1974, Portugal became a democratic republic with a constitution adopted in 1976 and later revised in 1982 and 1989.

Portugal's system is semipresidential. The president is directly elected for a five-year term and may serve only two consecutive terms. The president guarantees national independence and unity, commands the armed forces, appoints and dismisses the prime minister, can dissolve parliament, and sets election dates after consultation.

The judiciary is formally independent. The Supreme Tribunal of Justice is the highest judicial organ, and the Constitutional Tribunal rules on constitutionality. The Council of the Revolution, created after 1974, was abolished by the 1982 constitutional revision and replaced by the Council of State and the Constitutional Tribunal.

The political process includes universal voting from age 18, direct presidential elections, and proportional representation for the Assembly of the Republic. Two main parties dominate: the centre-left Socialist Party and the centre-right Social Democratic Party. Portugal also has autonomous island regions in the Azores and Madeira.

## Security, Welfare, Housing, and Education

The Portuguese military is commanded by the president. Conscription was abolished in the early 21st century, and the armed forces include army, air force, and navy. Portugal was a founding member of NATO in 1949. Police forces include the Public Security Police, Republican National Guard, Fiscal Guard, and judicial police.

Portugal's drug policy became internationally notable after 2001, when virtually all drugs were decriminalized. Addiction rates declined, and rehabilitation programs replaced incarceration in many cases, making Portugal an important test case in public-health-oriented law enforcement.

The welfare system insures workers against sickness, disability, and old age and provides pensions and family allowances. Housing has long been difficult, especially in cities, though Article 65 of the constitution declares a right to adequate housing. The government addressed poor housing conditions in the 1990s after years of shortages and shantytowns near Lisbon and Porto.

[[Education]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Education)) is free for ages 3 to 6 and compulsory from ages 6 to 18. Since the late 1980s, education became a high funding priority after studies showed high adult illiteracy. By the early 21st century, literacy exceeded 90 percent and nearly every child was enrolled, though school failure and child labour remained concerns.

## Cultural Life

![[attachments/portugal_002.webp|420]]

*Jeronimos Monastery in Belem, Lisbon*

Portuguese culture preserves traces of prehistoric, Roman, Moorish, and later European influences. Archaeological remains include cave paintings at Escoural, the Roman town of Conimbriga, the Roman temple in Evora, and Moorish architecture in southern towns. Explorers such as Ferdinand Magellan and Vasco da Gama also shaped cultural memory.

Portuguese [[The Art of Literature (Literature)|literature]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Literature)) grew from a 12th-century lyrical language. Camoes gave epic expression to national identity in Os Lusiadas in 1572. Later writers included Eca de Queiros, Aquilino Ribeiro, Ferreira de Castro, Vitorino Nemesio, Fernando Namora, Miguel Torga, Vergilio Ferreira, Antonio Lobo Antunes, and Jose Saramago, who won the Nobel Prize for Literature in 1998.

Portugal's architecture ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Architecture)) includes medieval castles, Roman ruins, Romanesque and Gothic cathedrals, and the Manueline style. Important works include Jeronimos Monastery, Lisbon Cathedral, the Convent of Christ in Tomar, Santa Maria da Vitoria in Batalha, Braga's cathedral, and Lisbon's Pombaline Baixa rebuilt after the 1755 earthquake.

Visual and decorative arts include medieval tomb sculpture, Baroque wood sculpture, painting, and especially azulejos, the glazed tiles that cover many churches, palaces, and houses. Music ranges from plainsong and Renaissance polyphony to orchestral music, fado, folk traditions, and popular performers. Amalia Rodrigues became the most famous fadista, and her death in 1999 brought three days of national mourning.

## History

Portugal's earliest known remains include Neanderthal-type bones from Furninhas. Mesolithic culture appeared in the lower Tagus valley around 5500 BCE, followed by Neolithic agriculture, pottery, metalwork, and megalithic monuments. Celtic peoples entered in the first millennium BCE, and the Lusitani resisted Roman conquest under Viriathus until his assassination about 140 BCE.

[[Rome]] organized Lusitania, while Christianity arrived by the 3rd and 4th centuries. Germanic peoples and later Muslim rulers controlled parts of the territory before Christian reconquest and kingdom formation. Portugal then developed as an independent kingdom in rivalry with Spain and turned toward maritime expansion.

Constitutional crisis followed the Napoleonic era. Brazil was raised to kingdom status in 1815, John VI returned to Portugal in 1821, and Pedro declared Brazilian independence on September 7, 1822. The loss of Brazil transformed the empire, leaving Portugal with mainly African and smaller Asian possessions.

The overseas empire after 1822 focused on Angola, Mozambique, Portuguese Guinea, Goa, East Timor, and Macau. The Rose-Coloured Map claimed a corridor across Africa from Angola to Mozambique, but Britain rejected it with the ultimatum of January 11, 1890. This crisis damaged the monarchy and strengthened republicanism.

The First Republic began in 1910 after the monarchy fell. It disestablished the Roman Catholic Church, expelled religious orders, and attempted reforms in education, health, liberties, and colonial development. Yet instability, factionalism, labour unrest, public violence, and military intervention weakened the regime until the 1926 coup.

The Revolution of the Carnations in 1974 ended dictatorship. Army officers in the Armed Forces Movement, influenced by colonial war discontent and Antonio de Spinola's call for a political solution, overthrew Marcello Caetano. Decolonization followed rapidly, and by 1976 Portugal had moved through radical and moderate phases toward pluralist democracy.

Postrevolution European integration gave Portugal a new strategic direction after empire. Entry into the European Economic Community in 1986 tied democratization to infrastructure funding, trade, migration, agricultural adjustment, and modernization of public institutions. The shift from Atlantic empire to European membership did not erase ties to Brazil, Africa, or the wider Portuguese-speaking world, but it changed the main framework for economic policy and national development.
