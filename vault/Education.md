## Introduction

Education ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Education)) is presented as the deliberate transmission of knowledge, skills, values, habits, and social meanings from one generation to another. Education not is one fixed institution but as a changing human process. In early societies it was inseparable from family life, religion, work, custom, and initiation. In later civilizations it became connected to writing, bureaucracy, class rank, priesthoods, citizenship, economic production, and the state.

Education history ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_education)) runs across many regions and periods. It shows how schooling moved from informal imitation and ritual toward specialized schools, universities, teacher training, state systems, compulsory attendance, mass literacy campaigns, vocational training, and higher education. A central theme is that education always reflects the society around it: its economy, religion, political power, social hierarchy, language, and idea of the good person.

## The Old World civilizations of Egypt, Mesopotamia, and North China

*1887 Bettannier Der Schwarze Fleck anagoria*

The earliest complex civilizations used education to support writing, administration, religion, law, and technical skills. In [[Egypt]], Mesopotamia, and North China, the spread of organized government created a need for scribes, priests, officials, and trained elites. Schooling was therefore linked to record keeping, sacred knowledge, and service to rulers rather than to equal access for all children.

These ancient systems also show a repeated pattern: literacy first served small governing groups. Writing made it possible to preserve laws, rituals, accounts, and political orders, but the ability to read and write usually remained restricted. Education therefore strengthened culture and administration while also reinforcing social divisions.

## Egypt

In ancient [[Egypt]], education was closely tied to the state, temple life, and the work of scribes. The scribe held a valued position because written records were needed for taxation, administration, religious texts, building projects, and communication across the kingdom. Training emphasized copying, memorization, discipline, and mastery of written forms.

Egyptian schooling ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/School)) also had a moral and social purpose. Students were taught obedience, respect for authority, proper conduct, and loyalty to the established order. Education prepared a limited group of boys for service in offices, temples, and royal administration, while most people learned their work through family and apprenticeship.

## Mesopotamia

In Mesopotamia, education developed around cuneiform writing and the needs of temples, palaces, trade, and law. Scribal schools trained pupils to handle complex signs, lists, accounts, contracts, and literary or religious texts. The curriculum reflected the practical importance of accounting and administration in cities such as those of Sumer and Babylon.

The Mesopotamian scribal school was demanding and hierarchical. It preserved technical knowledge, legal formulas, myths, and scholarly traditions, but it mainly served elites. As in Egypt, formal education was a path into official and priestly roles, while craft skills and daily knowledge were learned outside schools.

## North China

In North China, early education became connected with ritual, family authority, ancestor reverence, and service to rulers. Chinese education is one of the most durable traditions because it linked learning with moral order and political administration. Education was not only practical training but a way of forming the person according to inherited values.

Chinese classical learning later became especially important because officials were expected to know texts, rites, and moral principles. The roots of that system lay in early civilization, where education helped preserve continuity between family, state, and cosmic order.

## The Maya

Among the Maya, education was shaped by a complex civilization with writing, calendrical knowledge, religion, mathematics, and social hierarchy. Elite training preserved sacred and astronomical knowledge, while ordinary children learned practical duties through family and community life.

Maya education reflected the society's religious and political structure. Knowledge of time, ritual, and record keeping belonged especially to trained specialists. This example shows that the Americas also developed organized intellectual traditions before European conquest.

## The Aztecs

The Aztecs created a more formal educational structure than many ancient societies. Boys and girls were trained for social duty, religion, warfare, domestic work, and public service. Education was connected to discipline and moral formation, and it prepared people to accept their place in a strongly organized society.

Aztec schooling ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/School)) included institutions for different social groups and purposes. Elite boys could receive training for priestly, military, or administrative roles, while commoner children were prepared for practical and communal responsibilities. Education therefore supported both social order and imperial power.

## The Incas

In the Inca world, education served the needs of a large empire built on administration, labour organization, religion, and social discipline. Elite boys were instructed in history, religion, language, and duties of leadership, while most people learned through work, family, and community practice.

The Inca example shows that education need not depend on alphabetic literacy alone. Social memory, oral instruction, ritual, and administrative techniques could also transmit knowledge. Education helped hold together a broad imperial system with many local communities.

## Ancient India

In ancient India, education was strongly connected to religion, sacred texts, caste, and the teacher-pupil relationship. Learning often took place in close personal association with a teacher, and the highest forms of education centered on the Vedas, ritual knowledge, grammar, philosophy, and moral discipline.

The Indian gurukula pattern gave education a spiritual and ethical meaning. Students were expected to practice obedience, self-control, memorization, and service. Access to advanced learning was shaped by social rank, so education preserved both sacred knowledge and inherited hierarchy.

## Classical India

In classical India, education expanded through religious and philosophical traditions, including Brahmanical, Buddhist, and other schools. Centres of learning preserved grammar, logic, medicine, astronomy, law, literature, and metaphysics. Indian education is intellectually rich but not organized as a universal public system.

Classical Indian higher learning depended on teachers, monasteries, scholarly communities, and long textual traditions. Education aimed to form memory, reasoning, moral conduct, and spiritual insight. It also helped connect India with wider Asian intellectual and religious networks.

## Ancient China

In ancient China, education was tied to the formation of government, ethics, family duty, and official service. The Chinese tradition made learning a way to cultivate moral character and to prepare for public responsibility. This gave education a lasting prestige unmatched in many other societies.
Chinese education developed around both aristocratic training and later classical study. The ideal educated person was not only skilled but morally disciplined. This pattern shaped later civil service examinations and the authority of Confucian learning.

## Xi (Western) Zhou (1046771 BCE)

During the Western Zhou period, education reflected a feudal and ritual order. Training was intended for aristocratic youth and combined military practice, rites, music, writing, mathematics, and moral behaviour. Learning supported the duties of ruling families and their service to the larger political order.

The Zhou educational ideal joined practical abilities with ritual correctness. Education helped form nobles who could govern, fight, perform ceremonies, and uphold inherited relationships. It was therefore both cultural and political.

## Dong (Eastern) Zhou (770256 BCE)

The Eastern Zhou period brought political division, intellectual debate, and the rise of major schools of thought. Education became less narrowly aristocratic as teachers and thinkers offered competing visions of order. [[Confucius and Confucianism (Confucianism)|Confucius]] became central because he treated education as moral cultivation and public responsibility.

This period deepened the idea that learning could improve both the individual and society. Confucian, Daoist, Legalist, and other approaches differed sharply, but all showed that education had become a major instrument for thinking about government, ethics, and human conduct.

## Ancient Hebrews

Ancient Hebrew education was grounded in religion, law, family, memory, and covenant. Parents and religious leaders transmitted the commandments, stories, rituals, and moral obligations of the community. Education was therefore a duty of faith and identity, not simply a path to office.

The Hebrew model made religious instruction central to daily life. Children learned through family practice, worship, oral teaching, and later textual study. This tradition is one in which education preserved a people through belief, law, and historical memory.

## Origins

The origins of Greek education lay in heroic, aristocratic, military, and civic ideals. Early Greek teaching valued physical excellence, courage, music, poetry, speech, and the formation of character. Over time, the city-state became the main setting in which educational ideals took shape.

Greek paideia developed as a broad idea of culture and personal formation. It was not only instruction in skills but the shaping of a citizen's mind, body, taste, and public conduct. Different Greek cities interpreted this ideal in sharply different ways.

## Sparta

In Sparta, education was controlled by the state and designed for military discipline, endurance, obedience, and loyalty. Boys were trained from childhood to serve the city as soldiers. Girls also received physical training because the society valued strength and service to the collective order.

Spartan education minimized individual freedom and intellectual variety. It formed citizens for one central purpose: the survival and power of the warrior community. Athens, by contrast, made education more varied and cultural.

## Athens

In [[Athens]], education developed around civic participation, rhetoric, music, gymnastics, literature, and philosophy. Athenian boys were prepared for public life, household responsibility, and cultural refinement. The society valued speech, judgment, and participation in the polis.

Athenian education also became a seedbed for philosophical reflection. Thinkers such as [[Socrates]], [[Plato and Platonism (Plato)|Plato]], and [[Aristotle and Aristotelianism|Aristotle]] asked what education should do, whether it should serve the state, train reason, cultivate virtue, or guide the soul toward truth. Their questions shaped later Western educational thought.

## The Hellenistic Age

In the Hellenistic Age, Greek education spread across a wider world after the conquests of [[Alexander the Great]]. Schools, libraries, rhetoric, grammar, philosophy, and scientific studies became part of a broader cosmopolitan culture. Education increasingly served urban elites across many regions.

Hellenistic learning emphasized literary study, rhetoric, and philosophical schools. It helped standardize a pattern of liberal education that later influenced Roman, Byzantine, Islamic, and European traditions. The educated person was expected to master language, texts, and reasoned argument.

## Ancient Romans

Roman education began in the household, where children learned duty, discipline, religion, family tradition, and civic loyalty. As Rome came under Greek influence, formal schooling expanded. Boys studied reading, writing, grammar, rhetoric, and law to prepare for public life.

The Romans adapted Greek learning to practical and political ends. Rhetoric became especially important because public speaking was central to law, administration, and political advancement. Education served the state, the family, and the ambitions of the elite.

## Ancient Persia

In ancient Persia, education emphasized moral discipline, truthfulness, physical training, military readiness, and service to the ruler. It aimed to form loyal members of an imperial society. Elite education prepared young people for leadership, administration, and war.

Persian education also shows 's repeated link between empire and training. Large political systems needed disciplined officials and soldiers, and education helped produce people who could maintain order across diverse lands.

## The Byzantine Empire

In the Byzantine Empire, education preserved Greek language, classical texts, Christian doctrine, law, and administrative skills. Schools helped sustain a literate bureaucracy and a religious culture that connected ancient learning with Eastern Christianity.

Byzantine schooling ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/School)) valued grammar, rhetoric, philosophy, and theology. Although access remained limited, education played a major role in maintaining cultural continuity after the western Roman world changed. It also influenced Slavic and Orthodox Christian lands.

## The Islamic Era

During the Islamic Era, education grew around the Quran, Arabic language, law, theology, philosophy, science, medicine, and translation. Mosques, madrasahs, libraries, and scholarly circles transmitted knowledge across a wide region. Learning connected religious duty with intellectual inquiry.

Islamic education preserved and transformed knowledge from Greek, Persian, Indian, and other sources. Scholars studied works associated with [[Aristotle and Aristotelianism|Aristotle]], medicine, mathematics, astronomy, and logic, while also developing Islamic jurisprudence and theology. Education therefore joined sacred and worldly knowledge.

The Islamic world also carried learning into [[Spain]], North Africa, the Middle East, and parts of Asia. Cities such as Cordoba, Toledo, and Granada became important centres for scholarship and cultural exchange. This later influenced medieval European learning through translation and contact.

## Medieval and Early Modern India

In medieval and early modern [[India]], education included Hindu, Buddhist, Jain, and Islamic traditions. Muslim rule brought madrasah education, Persianate culture, and instruction in law, theology, literature, and administration. Older Sanskritic and local traditions continued alongside these institutions.

Indian education therefore remained plural. It served religious learning, courtly service, scholarship, and practical community life. Indian education changed through contact among traditions rather than through one uniform system.

## The Tang dynasty (618907 CE)

During the Tang dynasty, Chinese education and the examination system gained renewed importance. State schools, classical learning, and examinations helped recruit officials and strengthen the connection between education and government service. The dynasty also interacted with Buddhist, Central Asian, and wider East Asian cultures.

Tang education reinforced the idea that moral and literary learning could qualify men for office. Although aristocratic privilege remained powerful, examinations gave scholarly achievement a recognized political role.

## The Song (9601279)

During the Song dynasty, education expanded further through schools, academies, printing, and Neo-Confucian scholarship. The examination system became more important and helped create a scholar-official class. Learning was tied closely to moral self-cultivation and administration.

Song Neo-Confucian education emphasized texts, principle, moral discipline, and public responsibility. It influenced later Chinese schooling and also affected Korea, Japan, and Vietnam. This is a major moment in the development of East Asian educational culture.

## Early Japan

Early [[Japan]] absorbed much from Chinese and Buddhist education, including writing, state schools, court learning, and religious study. Over time, Japanese education developed its own patterns through aristocratic culture, warrior training, temple schools, and later institutions for commoners.

Japanese education before modern reforms was diverse. It included Buddhist temple instruction, Confucian learning, samurai moral and military education, and practical schools for merchants and townspeople. This prepared the ground for rapid modernization in the 19th century.

## European Renaissance and Reformation

The European Renaissance revived interest in classical languages, literature, rhetoric, history, and moral philosophy. Humanist educators argued that study of ancient texts could form eloquent, virtuous, and active people. Education became linked to civic life, court culture, and the ideal of the cultivated person.

The Reformation transformed education by making religious instruction, literacy, and vernacular teaching central to Protestant communities. Reformers wanted believers to read scripture and understand doctrine. This increased pressure for schools, catechisms, teacher preparation, and state or church supervision.

## The English Reformation

In England, the Reformation reshaped schools through religious change, dissolution of older institutions, and new grammar-school foundations. Education remained unequal, but Protestantism strengthened the value of reading, scripture, and moral discipline.

English grammar schools continued to emphasize Latin, classical texts, and preparation for university or clerical and administrative careers. English education is shaped by religion, social class, local initiative, and later state involvement.

## The French Reformation

In [[France]], religious conflict and humanist thought affected education in different ways. Catholic, Protestant, and humanist forces competed over the control and purpose of schools. Education became part of a larger struggle over belief, authority, and cultural formation.

French educational thought also included literary and philosophical critiques of rigid schooling. Writers questioned empty verbalism and argued for broader experience, judgment, and moral formation. These debates helped prepare later educational reform traditions.

## The Calvinist Reformation

The Calvinist Reformation placed strong emphasis on literacy, discipline, doctrine, and organized schooling. In Calvinist communities, education helped form believers who could read scripture, participate in church life, and live according to a strict moral order.

In places influenced by John Calvin, schools were often connected to civic and religious governance. This made education a tool for both faith and social discipline. The Calvinist pattern later affected education in Scotland, the Netherlands, parts of France, and North America.

## The Roman Catholic Counter-Reformation

The Counter-Reformation strengthened Roman Catholic education through renewed religious orders, seminaries, and schools. The Jesuits became especially influential by developing a disciplined curriculum based on classical studies, rhetoric, philosophy, theology, and moral training.

Catholic schooling ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/School)) aimed to defend doctrine, train clergy, educate elites, and form loyal believers. It also spread through missions in the Americas, Asia, and Africa. Education became a major instrument in the global competition among religious traditions.

## The Sensationists

The Sensationists treated knowledge as rooted in sensory experience. Their ideas challenged older assumptions about innate knowledge and encouraged educators to attend to observation, experience, and the formation of ideas through contact with the world.

This movement influenced educational method by making teachers think more carefully about how children learn. Instead of only transmitting verbal formulas, education could begin from sense experience, objects, and gradual development of understanding.

## The Rousseauists

The Rousseauists followed ideas associated with [[Jean-Jacques Rousseau]], especially the belief that education should respect the child's natural development. Rousseau criticized artificial, bookish, and socially corrupting education and proposed a slower, experience-based formation.

Rousseau's child-centred education influenced later reformers by shifting attention from the demands of curriculum to the nature of the learner. This as a turning point in modern educational thought, even when later educators rejected or modified Rousseau's proposals.

## Revolutionary France

In revolutionary [[France]], education became a matter of national citizenship, political change, and public responsibility. Reformers sought systems that would replace church control, spread literacy, and form citizens loyal to the nation and to republican ideals.

French educational reform moved between liberal, secular, centralized, and sometimes social visions. The problem was not only what children should learn but who should control schools: church, family, local community, or state.

## England in the Age of Reform

In England, modern education developed more slowly and less centrally than in France or Germany. Voluntary societies, churches, charity schools, grammar schools, and private institutions played large roles. The state was reluctant to intervene deeply until social and industrial change made education harder to ignore.

English school reform ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Education_reform)) was shaped by class, religion, and industrialization. Philanthropic and religious groups expanded elementary schooling, but access and quality remained uneven. This pattern helped explain the later tension between voluntary tradition and national responsibility.

## Spanish and Portuguese America

In Spanish and Portuguese America, colonial education was shaped by church institutions, missions, universities, and the needs of colonial administration. Education served conversion, elite formation, law, theology, and social hierarchy. Access was limited and strongly affected by race, class, and local conditions.

Colonial Latin American education mixed European forms with local realities. Universities and colleges trained clergy and officials, while mission schools taught religion and basic skills. Education became one of the arenas in which colonial power and cultural transformation met.

## French Quebec

In French Quebec, education was strongly influenced by Roman Catholic institutions, French language, and colonial society. Schools helped preserve religion and culture in a setting marked by political change and later British rule.

Quebec education shows 's wider theme of minority cultural survival. Schooling could protect language and faith, but it could also become a source of conflict when political authority, church control, and social modernization pulled in different directions.

## New England

In New England, Protestant belief encouraged literacy so that people could read scripture and participate in a disciplined religious community. Town schools, laws requiring education, and the founding of colleges reflected the region's strong connection between religion, civic order, and schooling.

New England schooling ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/School)) influenced later American ideas about public education. The common school ideal grew from the belief that literacy and moral instruction were necessary for community life, religious duty, and later democratic citizenship.

## Germany

In [[Germany]], modern education developed through state action, compulsory schooling, teacher training, and philosophical theories of learning. German states, especially Prussia, connected education to discipline, administration, morality, and national strength.

German educational thought was also important. Thinkers such as Johann Friedrich Herbart, Friedrich Froebel, and others shaped ideas about instruction, child development, kindergarten, moral formation, and the organization of lessons. Their influence spread beyond Germany.

## Nineteenth-Century France

In 19th-century France, education reflected the struggle between church influence, state centralization, republican citizenship, and social change. Governments worked to build a national system, but reforms often depended on political shifts.

French public education became increasingly secular and centralized. The state sought to control curricula, train teachers, and create schools that could form citizens. This pattern later culminated in the Third Republic's free, compulsory, and secular elementary system.

## Nineteenth-Century England

In 19th-century England, elementary education expanded through voluntary and religious agencies before stronger state intervention. Industrialization, urban poverty, political reform, and concern about social order pushed the government toward greater responsibility.

English education remained marked by class division. Elite public schools and grammar schools served upper groups, while elementary schools served the masses. The slow emergence of state responsibility prepared the way for the major reforms of the 20th century.

## Russia

In 19th-century Russia, education developed under autocratic rule and remained limited for much of the population. The state, church, and local bodies all played roles, but literacy and elementary schooling lagged far behind western Europe.

Russian school reform ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Education_reform)) was repeatedly affected by political fear. Liberalizing impulses were often followed by restrictions, because rulers worried that education could spread dissent. This tension between modernization and control carried into the revolutionary period.

## The Nineteenth-Century United States

In the [[United States of America (United States)|United States]], education expanded through common schools, state systems, local control, normal schools, high schools, and universities. Reformers argued that free public education was necessary for democracy, social mobility, moral order, and economic development.

American common school reform was associated with figures such as Horace Mann, who promoted tax-supported schools, trained teachers, graded classrooms, and nonsectarian moral instruction. U.S. education was decentralized but driven by strong ideals of equal opportunity.

## Nineteenth-Century Canada

In [[Canada]], education developed through provincial systems shaped by religion, language, settlement patterns, and local authority. English and French traditions, Protestant and Roman Catholic interests, and regional differences prevented a single national pattern.

Canadian schooling ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/School)) therefore grew through plural arrangements. Public systems expanded, but minority rights and denominational schooling remained important. This pluralism became a lasting feature of Canadian education.

## Colonial Australia

In [[Australia]], colonial education moved from denominational and voluntary schooling toward state systems. Governments created public schools, teacher training, and administrative structures while still leaving room for Roman Catholic and other private schools.

Australian education had to solve the problem of distance. Rural and isolated communities required special arrangements, later including correspondence education and consolidated schools. Education became part of national development in a vast territory.

## Nineteenth-Century New Zealand

In [[New Zealand]], modern education moved toward secular public primary schooling and centralized administration. Religious and regional issues were less obstructive than in some other British Commonwealth countries, but equal opportunity remained difficult.

New Zealand school reform ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Education_reform)) gradually removed barriers to progression through the system. Primary, secondary, technical, rural, Maori, and later private Roman Catholic schooling all became part of a coordinated national pattern.

## British India

In 19th-century India, British rule introduced Western-style schooling, English-language education, universities, and administrative training. These institutions created new opportunities but also served colonial purposes. Indian critics objected that Western learning dominated at the expense of Indian languages and culture.

Indian educational nationalism grew alongside political nationalism. Reformers and organizations demanded compulsory primary education, national schools, mother-tongue instruction, vocational training, and education suited to Indian needs. This issue became central before independence.

## Meiji Japan

In [[Japan]], the Meiji period transformed education after the opening of the country to the West. The government borrowed from Western systems, especially European models, but kept a moral core tied to loyalty, emperor-centred nationalism, and social discipline.

Japanese modern education expanded rapidly. Elementary schooling became compulsory, teacher training developed, universities and higher schools were founded, and vocational and social education grew. Education supported modernization, industrialization, and nationalism.

## The United Kingdom

In the [[United Kingdom]], the modern school system grew from a mixture of state action, voluntary agencies, church influence, local government, elite schools, and later national reform. The Elementary Education Act of 1870 accepted state responsibility for elementary schools but did not immediately replace voluntary agencies or solve secondary education.

The Balfour Act of 1902 created local education authorities and enabled them to provide secondary and technical education. The Fisher Act of 1918 aimed at a public system available to all who could profit from it and raised the school-leaving age. These reforms moved Britain toward a national system while preserving local and voluntary traditions.

The Education Act of 1944 recast education in England and Wales. It replaced the Board of Education with a minister, required plans for primary, secondary, and further education, preserved religious compromise, and made secondary education free for all in publicly maintained schools. It also organized education into primary, secondary, and further stages.

The tripartite system after 1944 divided secondary education into grammar, secondary modern, and technical schools, with selection at age 11. In practice, technical schools remained few, grammar places were limited, and most children went to secondary modern schools that often did not lead to qualifications. The eleven-plus examination became the weak point of this system.

The comprehensive movement grew because selection at 11 seemed unfair and unreliable. Labour governments promoted comprehensive schools, while Conservative governments left more decisions to local authorities. By the late 20th century most state-maintained secondary schools were comprehensive, but local variation and independent private schools continued.

British primary education included infant and junior stages, with uneven preschool provision but much innovation in early childhood learning. Play, flexible curriculum, interrelated studies, and teacher guidance became important in infant schools. Administration balanced local education authorities, school professional responsibility, governing boards, national policy, and inspection.

Further and higher education expanded greatly. Civic universities challenged Oxford and Cambridge, technical colleges grew, polytechnics offered degree-level technological and general courses, and the Open University began in 1971 with flexible access for adults and part-time students. Later reforms emphasized economic relevance, vocational education, standardized assessment, and microelectronics.

## Imperial Germany

In Imperial Germany after 1871, education served state purposes, class order, discipline, morality, and economic efficiency. The Volksschule was universal, free, and compulsory for the mass of the population, but it led directly to work and remained separate from elite secondary schooling.

German secondary education ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Secondary_education)) was divided into the classical Gymnasium, the Realgymnasium, and the Oberrealschule. The Gymnasium emphasized Latin and Greek and retained prestige even as modern languages and sciences gained support. Girls' secondary education expanded, but it largely served upper social groups.

Vocational continuation schools developed for the working class, especially under the influence of Georg Kerschensteiner. These schools added part-time education to apprenticeship training and later became important Berufsschulen. They helped extend schooling for youth up to age 18.

## Weimar Republic

The Weimar Republic after 1919 brought creative debate and reforms. A free and compulsory four-year Grundschule was created for all children and became the foundation for later social liberalization in education. Elementary schooling included basic subjects, religion, drawing, singing, physical training, and manual work.

Weimar education preserved religious traditions but made participation in religious instruction voluntary for pupils and teachers. Secondary schools kept older divisions, while new institutions such as the Aufbauschule and Deutsche Oberschule broadened alternatives. Reform widened access without fully eliminating inherited social divisions.

## Nazi Germany

After Adolf Hitler came to power in 1933, the Nazi regime tried to reconstruct education as an instrument of totalitarian control. Schools were infused with National Socialist ideology, and the curriculum was reshaped to serve racial, military, and nationalist aims.

Nazi schooling ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/School)) inserted race science into biology, geopolitics into geography, compulsory physical training, youth labour service, and intense patriotic indoctrination. Many basic academic subjects remained, but they were placed within a political effort to control the minds and bodies of the young.

## West Germany after World War II

After [[World Wars (World War II)|World War II]], the occupying powers in Germany removed Nazi influence and introduced reforms shaped by their own educational values. In West Germany, the Basic Law of 1949 gave educational authority to the Land governments, leaving the system decentralized and politically diverse.

West German education remained divided by debates over confessional schooling and the tripartite secondary structure. Although comprehensive schools appeared, they often included separate paths. Reform instead produced more openness through orientation stages, transfer possibilities, curriculum changes, and broader opportunities within the existing structure.

Education was compulsory from ages 6 to 18. Pupils generally attended the Grundschule, then moved into Hauptschule, Realschule, or Gymnasium, with vocational education and apprenticeship forming a strong parallel path. The Abitur remained the university entrance qualification, and reforms in 1973 diversified the upper Gymnasium through course choices and cumulative points.

## The Third Republic

Under the French Third Republic, education became a national, free, compulsory, and secular enterprise. In 1882 primary education became compulsory for children aged 6 to 13; in 1886 clergy were forbidden to teach in public schools; and in 1904 teaching congregations were suppressed.

French education remained marked by two parallel systems: public elementary and higher primary schools, and selective intellectual secondary lycees with preparatory schools. Lycees emphasized classical studies, though reforms in 1902 promoted modern languages and science. Administration stayed highly centralized under the minister, academies, rectors, and inspectors.

## France after World War II

After 1946, French education entered national planning and reform. Goals included administrative efficiency, technological education, better articulation among system parts, school-community links, and correction of inequalities affecting minorities, immigrants, social regions, and handicapped pupils.

A 1947 commission proposed compulsory education from age 6 to 18 and three stages: basic mastery from 6 to 11, guidance from 11 to 15, and diversified specialized education from 15 to 18. The later system developed a five-year elementary school, a four-year college unique, and upper secondary routes through general-technological or vocational lycees.

French upper secondary education led to the baccalaureat through literary-philosophical, social-science, mathematical, biological, technological, and vocational options. Technical baccalaureats, technician diplomas, vocational diplomas, certificates, apprenticeships, universities, institutes, and grandes ecoles created many routes but also strong status differences.

French teacher status remained stratified by competitive examinations and grades. Reforms tried to create more common education, options, technological studies, school-work links, flexible time and content, extracurricular activities, and decentralization. Change was gradual because traditions and institutional resistance remained strong.

## Italy

In [[Italy]], the Casati Law of 1859 organized education on a centralized French model. A national reform in 1923 reinforced state supremacy and introduced state examinations for public and private pupils. Eight years of compulsory schooling were declared in 1948 but were not realized until 1962.

Italian education included a five-year elementary school, an undifferentiated middle school from ages 11 to 14, and a highly diversified upper secondary level with classical and scientific licei, vocational programs, industrial technical institutes, teacher institutes, and art schools. Universities were the main postsecondary route, leading to the laurea and professional state examinations.

Italian advances in lower-level unification and upper-level vocational-technical options, but also fragmentation, weak articulation, low achievement, regional inequality, and conservative religious and political resistance to change.

## The Netherlands

In the [[Netherlands]], the modern school law of 1801 recognized parish rights to maintain schools. The long conflict between denominational and nondenominational schooling ended in 1920, when denominational schools became fully equal with state schools and eligible for public funds.

Dutch education was therefore strongly decentralized and plural. About two-thirds of school-age children attended private schools, including Protestant, Roman Catholic, or secular schools, which had to provide curricula equivalent to public schools. Secondary education included preuniversity, general, vocational, and miscellaneous part-time forms, with examinations shaping selection.

## Switzerland

The Swiss constitution of 1874 required each canton or half canton to organize and maintain free and compulsory elementary schools. The federal government did not control education below the university level except through financial aid.

Swiss education consisted of many cantonal systems. Diversity remained despite efforts by the Swiss Conference of Cantonal Directors of Education. Schooling was usually compulsory for eight or nine years, and lower and upper secondary education became highly differentiated, with vocational courses and apprenticeships playing a major role.

## Sweden

After [[World Wars (World War II)|World War II]], [[Sweden]] created one of Europe's most notable comprehensive reforms. In 1950 the National Board of Education introduced a nine-year compulsory comprehensive school, the grundskola, replacing other compulsory forms by 1972–73.

Swedish reform unified elementary and lower secondary education and integrated upper secondary education in the gymnasieskola for ages 16 to 19. It combined general and vocational lines, recognized theory and practice in all programs, and used long experimentation and implementation periods. Equality went beyond access to compensatory measures, including sex quotas in underrepresented occupations.

Sweden also promoted recurrent education, the idea that school and work could alternate across life. Local councils helped coordinate school and work, making the relationship between education and labour a practical community matter rather than only a national policy.

## The Twentieth-Century United States

At the start of the 20th century, U.S. education was free, compulsory, universal in principle, state-controlled, locally administered, and articulated from kindergarten to university. Public schooling coexisted with religious and private schools because the state could require education but not compel public-school attendance.

The dominant American education debate shifted from religion to equality. Equal opportunity came to include wealth, region, disability, race, sex, and ethnic origin. From the 1950s, passive access gave way to affirmative action, multicultural courses, bilingual instruction, English as a second language, textbook revision, women's studies, and expanded access for women and minorities.

Education for children with disabilities expanded through federal and state action. The Education for All Handicapped Children Act of 1975, later the Individuals with Disabilities Act, promoted facilities, teacher preparation, employment training, mainstreaming, and the least-restrictive environment.

American school organization shifted from the eight-four elementary-high school pattern toward six-three-three, middle schools, and expanded preschool and kindergarten provision. High school enrollment grew enormously in the first half of the century, and as more students graduated, pressure increased for postsecondary access.

American progressive education drew from the Dalton, Winnetka, and Gary plans and from Francis W. Parker and John Dewey. It emphasized flexible methods, cooperation, child experience, community, and school as a learning society. Later curriculum expanded to consumer education, multicultural education, environmental education, sex and family-life education, substance-abuse education, and vocational-technical preparation.

The fall in Scholastic Aptitude Test scores between 1963 and 1982 brought public concern. A Nation at Risk in 1983 emphasized performance, curriculum, standards, testing, credentials, teacher preparation, and the link between schooling and career. Computers then became both a subject and a teaching tool, and the Internet in the 1990s greatly increased access to educational resources.

Federal involvement remained indirect but influential. Land grants, the Morrill Act, vocational legislation, the GI Bill, Head Start under the Economic Opportunity Act of 1964, and compensatory education for disadvantaged children all expanded federal influence. Early-childhood programs added medical, dental, social, nutritional, psychological, and parent-participation elements.

Higher education included progressive experiments at Antioch and Bennington, conservative great-books ideals associated with Robert M. Hutchins, a major general education report, expansion from about 1955 to 1975, debates over quality, and the rise of junior and community colleges. Professional organizations such as the NEA and AFT influenced teaching conditions, standards, accreditation, and teacher preparation.

## Twentieth-Century Canada

In [[Canada]], education in the 20th century remained provincial, plural, and regionally uneven. Minority religious and linguistic rights, settlement patterns, local responsibility, and economic differences among provinces prevented a single unified system.

Canadian secondary education ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Secondary_education)) changed from an uneven postelementary structure toward junior and senior secondary stages, composite high schools, regional schools, and comprehensive programs. Quebec moved from private classical colleges and special terminal schools toward public high schools after 1956 and a ministry of education in 1964.

From the 1960s, Canadian reforms expanded access for women, native peoples, immigrants, and low-income groups. Early reforms stressed openness, comprehensive schooling, open classrooms, curriculum choice, and continuity; later reforms returned to basics, standards, values, administrative control, and assessment.

Canadian multicultural and bilingual education had a strong impact. French-language instruction expanded in English-speaking areas, English schooling was restricted in Quebec, Christian exercises were challenged in courts, other languages gained attention, and curricula were revised to reduce exclusively Western bias.

Higher education expanded through universities and provincially funded colleges. The federal government had no general constitutional authority in education, but influenced vocational education, aboriginal education, research, statistics, armed-forces education, and mass communication through other responsibilities.

## Twentieth-Century Australia

In [[Australia]], education remained a state responsibility influenced by British models and strong central authority within each state. Primary schools, continuation programs, state secondary schools, private elite schools, and Roman Catholic schools shaped the system.

Australian rural education required special measures because of distance and isolation. Correspondence education began in Victoria in 1914, and provisional schools, itinerant teachers, central schools, one-teacher bush schools, and later consolidated schools served remote communities. Australia became known for expertise in one-room school methods.

After [[World Wars (World War II)|World War II]], the Commonwealth government used financial power to influence tertiary education. The Murray, Martin, and Karmel reports and the 1988 higher education policy moved toward a unified national tertiary system, rationalization, efficiency, economic relevance, fees, privatization signs, and direct negotiation with institutions.

Australian schooling ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/School)) generally included six years of primary school and five or six years of secondary education, often in comprehensive schools. The TAFE sector strongly influenced upper secondary and tertiary training, serving hundreds of thousands of students and linking technical, further, and adult education. Later reforms emphasized equality, diversity, devolution, national unity, school-work transition, Australian content, Aboriginal cultures, and disadvantaged groups.

## Twentieth-Century New Zealand

In [[New Zealand]], secular primary schooling and central administration were settled in the late 19th century. The main 20th-century issue became equal opportunity and the removal of barriers to progress through the system.

The Education Act of 1914 consolidated earlier changes. Reforms in the 1930s and after World War II broadened access: the school-leaving certificate was broadened, secondary entrance barriers were abolished, the leaving age rose to 15, a common early secondary core was established, and universities accepted accredited school courses without further examination.

New Zealand secondary education ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Secondary_education)) developed through district high schools, academic one-sex secondary schools, and technical schools, which gradually moved toward a more common pattern through inspection, curriculum, examinations, and postprimary expansion. Roman Catholic private schools were subsidized after 1970 and mostly integrated into the state system after 1975.

Rural and Maori education received increasing attention. Consolidated schools, transportation, correspondence schools, rural education programs, and policies balancing remediation with Maori cultural rights showed how equity and school-work relations became central issues.

## Before 1917

In [[Russia]] before 1917, education lagged behind many European countries. The 1897 census showed only 24 percent literacy above age nine, rising to about 40 percent by 1914. Only about half of children aged 8 to 12 attended school.

Russian education was divided among zemstvo local agencies, the Orthodox church, the state, and the Ministry of Education. Secondary and higher institutions served lower classes only slightly. Reform efforts before and during 1917 aimed at modern secondary schools, vocational and technical education, university autonomy, native-language instruction for non-Russian peoples, and a common school based on local control.

## Russian Revolution

After the 1917 October Revolution, the Bolsheviks proclaimed a radical transformation of education guided by Karl Marx, progressive education, and Russian revolutionary thought. Leaders such as Nadezhda Krupskaya and Anatoly Lunacharsky promoted free compulsory general and polytechnical education to age 17, preschool education, access to higher education for workers, vocational training, and mass adult education joined to communist propaganda.

Soviet revolutionary education abolished religious instruction, introduced atheistic indoctrination, coeducation, student self-government, abolition of marks and examinations, and productive labour. Rabfaks were created in 1919 to prepare a new proletarian intelligentsia. During the New Economic Policy, practical problems of elementary schooling, juvenile delinquency, and adult illiteracy became priorities.

Under Joseph Stalin, education stabilized around compulsory schooling, academic subjects, marks and examinations, teacher authority, discipline, patriotism, and centralized party-state control. Progressive ideas were rejected, manual work disappeared from school curricula, higher and specialized education expanded, and evening and correspondence study paralleled full-time routes.

The Khrushchev reforms after 1953 tried to strengthen ties between school and life. The eight-year general and polytechnical labour school became basic, secondary education was linked to production training, and evening and correspondence education expanded. But polytechnization largely failed, and reforms between 1964 and 1966 restored a 10-year general education program while keeping limited vocational training.

## From Brezhnev to Gorbachev

Under Leonid Brezhnev, the main goal was universal 10-year education. By 1977, authorities claimed that about 97 percent of graduates from the basic eight-year school continued to secondary education. Secondary vocational-technical schools offered both academic and vocational programs, while preschool and higher education expanded.

Soviet education gained quantity but faced concerns about quality, achievement, behaviour, alienation, and mismatch between student preferences and manpower needs. The 1984 reforms raised teacher salaries, lowered school-entry age to six, extended general secondary schooling to 11 years, strengthened vocational training, and introduced computer literacy despite limited equipment.

With Mikhail Gorbachev and perestroika from 1986, some reforms were reversed. Basic education became nine years, with later secondary academic and vocational tracks. Reformers promoted cooperation, creativity, flexible curricula, participation, self-government, university autonomy, and attention to native languages. The breakup of the Soviet Union in 1991–92 left the future of former all-Soviet institutions uncertain.

## Twentieth-Century Japan

At the beginning of the 20th century, [[Japan]] combined rapid modernization with nationalism. The education system drew especially from Germany and western Europe but kept the moral ideology of the Imperial Rescript on Education of 1890. Compulsory elementary schooling was four years in 1900 and six years in 1907.

Japanese education expanded quickly. Elementary enrollment rose from 49 percent in 1890 to 98 percent in 1910. Secondary schools were separated by sex and type: middle schools for boys, girls' high schools, and vocational schools. Moral education centred on Confucian and Shinto values, supported by national textbooks.

Higher education began with the University of Tokyo in 1877, later renamed the Imperial University. It emphasized sciences and technology useful to the state, borrowed much from German universities, and inspired later imperial universities. Private colleges gained clearer legal status through the College Order of 1903.

After World War I, Taisho democracy, social change, and economic growth encouraged reform, progressive education, preschool expansion, and secondary growth. The University Order and Higher School Order of 1918 approved private universities and colleges, leading to institutions such as Keio and Waseda. Entrance examinations became increasingly severe as demand exceeded places.

During the militaristic period after the Manchurian Incident of 1931, education became intensely nationalistic. National schools, shortened secondary education, military-industrial labour mobilization, and student deployment to battlefields disrupted education during World War II.

## Education after World War II

![[attachments/education_003.webp|420]]

*Anatomy study*

After Japan surrendered on Aug. 14, 1945, Allied occupation authorities sought to abolish militaristic education and ultranationalism. The United States Education Mission to Japan shaped a new democratic system, and laws in 1947 and 1948 created the postwar framework.

Japanese postwar reform replaced the prewar system with a single-track six-three-three-four structure: elementary, lower secondary, upper secondary, and university. Compulsory attendance became nine years, coeducation was introduced, special education was provided, state textbooks were abolished, elective boards of education were created, and moral shushin was replaced by social studies.

After the peace treaty of 1952, Japan retained the basic reforms but recentralized administration, curriculum, textbooks, and teacher performance. Moral education returned, progressive child-interest curriculum gave way in 1961–63 to discipline-centred curriculum, and high school and college enrollments surged during economic growth.

By 1975, high-school enrollment had reached 91.9 percent and university or college enrollment 37.8 percent of eligible students. Yet private institutions absorbed most higher-education growth, upper secondary schooling stayed too uniform, and reform councils in 1971, 1984, and 1987 called for more individuality, diversified upper secondary education, moral education, local curricular responsibility, teacher training, and higher-education diversity.

## South Asia

In South Asia before independence, Indian nationalists criticized British Western learning and demanded more attention to Indian languages and culture. Baron Curzon surveyed education after arriving as viceroy in 1898 and found that four out of five villages had no school, three boys out of four grew up without education, and only one girl out of forty attended any school.

Curzon's reforms strengthened inspection, private-school control, universities, standards, state aid, and higher education. Indian critics saw these measures as bureaucratic and politically suspect, especially during nationalist agitation. The swadeshi movement led to national schools, a national college, technical education, and projects by Rabindranath Tagore and others.

The Sadler Commission of 1917 recommended secondary and intermediate boards, two-year intermediate colleges, three-year degree courses, teaching universities, postgraduate work, science, tutorials, and research. After the 1921 constitution, education became a transferred subject largely under provincial Indian ministers, and the Government of India Act of 1935 gave complete provincial autonomy over education.

Mahatma Gandhi promoted noncooperation and later basic education, which sought to overcome bookish instruction by correlating school subjects with productive craft. Provincial governments expanded primary schooling, adult education, vocational education, girls' education, and education for underprivileged groups, while secondary schools still mainly prepared students for college.

After independence and partition in 1947, [[India]] improved science, technology, higher education, and research but still faced high illiteracy. Five-year plans sought universal elementary education, eradication of illiteracy, vocational and skill training, modernization, technical education, morality, environmental education, school-work links, and quality schools in each district.

Indian educational administration ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Academic_administration)) divided responsibility between states and the central government. Important bodies included the All-India Council of Technical Education, the University Grants Commission, and the National Council of Educational Research and Training. From the 1950s to the 1980s, educational institutions tripled, but understaffing, weak facilities, politicization, and uneven state conditions slowed progress.

In [[Pakistan]], five-year plans emphasized manpower training, technical trade schools, vocational institutes, equipment, books, advanced engineering, literacy, and Islamic cultural ideology. The National Education Policy of 1979 proposed a three-tier structure and free primary education, but primary enrollment remained only slightly above half and was concentrated in cities.

In [[Bangladesh]], independence in 1971 brought a constitutional duty to provide education. Primary schools were largely nationalized in 1973 and 1974, but many children never attended school and many who did left too early to become literate. Reforms linked education to nationalism, democracy, socialism, and secularism.

In [[Sri Lanka]], education was free from kindergarten through university in state and state-aided institutions. The system included primary, junior secondary, and senior secondary stages, with senior students streamed into science, commerce, or liberal arts. The University Act of 1978 and the National Institute of Education in 1987 strengthened higher education, curriculum development, textbooks, teacher education, and examinations.

## Africa

Before European colonialism, [[Africa]] had many indigenous educational systems designed to prepare children for home, village, tribe, religion, work, and social responsibility. Education occurred through custom, example, initiation rites, legends, practical training, and communal obligations. Boys and girls learned their social roles, though girls' initiation was often shorter.

Islamic education spread in parts of eastern and western Africa from the 9th to 11th centuries. Quranic schools taught Arabic script and memorization of the Quran, and selected boys could continue to higher Islamic learning. Still, Islam reached only part of sub-Saharan Africa.

Western-style schooling followed European colonial rule. Mission schools came first, then official schools, and colonial education generally aimed to train clerks, interpreters, teachers, nurses, medical assistants, and workers for colonial systems rather than to educate whole populations. After independence, African states abolished formal racial segregation but often kept inherited school structures at first.

In Ethiopia, education was church-controlled for about 1,500 years, with Quranic schools in Islamic areas. Menilek II began a government school system in 1908, and Haile Selassie expanded education after World War II. After the 1974 revolution, socialist policy emphasized education for production, scientific consciousness, and social consciousness, with Soviet-influenced polytechnical education.

## Liberia

Liberia, founded by freed slaves from the United States, developed an educational system modeled on the American one. Public and mission schools served settler children in the 19th century, but little money extended schooling to indigenous people in the interior.

Liberian education became centralized in 1912, expanded after World War II, and grew under William V.S. Tubman in the 1950s. The system included preprimary education, six years of elementary school, junior and senior high school, and postsecondary institutions such as the University of Liberia, Cuttington University College, and Tubman College of Technology. Expansion still reached only a small fraction of the population.

## South Africa

In South Africa, education was deeply shaped by Protestant home Bible reading, language, race, and colonial politics. Separate English and Afrikaans schools were created for white Europeans after the Union of South Africa in 1910, while mission schools served many native Africans with a curriculum often designed to encourage service and subordination.

The apartheid education system rested on legal and institutional separation of white, black, Coloured, and Indian populations. The Bantu Education Act of 1953, later policy acts, and the Constitution Act of 1983 reinforced separate systems. White education received far greater resources, while black schools had high dropout rates, overcrowding, poor materials, lower teacher qualifications, and limited secondary access.

Resistance to apartheid education intensified after 1976, when student movements, strikes, boycotts, violence, and township unrest made schools a centre of political conflict. Universities also became centres of agitation as barriers to nonwhite admissions weakened and protests against quotas grew.

In the early postapartheid period, a single Ministry of Education was created in 1993, discriminatory teacher qualifications were standardized, and curriculum reforms removed racial stereotypes and emphasized African perspectives and oral histories. Yet rural and black schools still lacked facilities, supplies, and trained teachers.

South African schooling was organized into junior primary, senior primary, junior secondary, and senior secondary cycles. Compulsory schooling ran from age seven to 16 for all races. General high schools were mainly academic but offered streams, while specialized high schools, technikons, technical colleges, industrial centres, adult centres, and universities provided further routes. Language remained political, especially after Afrikaans as compulsory instruction helped trigger the Soweto riots.

## The Middle East

Modern education entered the Middle East in the early 19th century through rulers in Egypt, the Ottoman Empire, and Iran, and through missionary and foreign schools in the Levant. These institutions served only a small minority, while most people continued in traditional Islamic schools.

After [[World Wars (World War I)|World War I]] and the fall of the Ottoman Empire, new states needed education for skilled manpower and national loyalty. French and British colonial powers limited expansion, favoured private and missionary schools for elites, copied their own curricula, and trained only a limited number of bureaucrats. Public systems were centralized but often weak, with poor teacher supply, irrelevant curricula, and high dropout rates.

Independent Middle Eastern states nationalized private schools, expanded access, and tried to equalize opportunity. Mustafa Kemal Ataturk in Turkey closed religious schools, promoted coeducation, developed new curricula, emphasized vocational and technical education, launched adult education, created Village Institutes, and reorganized Istanbul University in 1933. Reza Shah Pahlavi in Iran centralized schooling, founded the University of Tehran in 1934, sent students abroad, promoted women's education, and challenged Islamic schools.

After World War II, leaders such as Gamal Abdel Nasser, Habib Bourguiba, and Iraq's revolutionary government made education part of administrative and social reform. Tunisia pursued universal primary education, vocational training, higher expansion, Quranic-school incorporation, women's education, and Arabicization. Egypt unified religious and secular systems, transformed al-Azhar University, made public education free, and expanded technical and vocational schooling.

In the second half of the 20th century, most Arab states adopted a six-three-three structure and centralized ministries. Enrollments and literacy rose, but population growth, rural-urban inequality, low female participation at higher levels, crowded classrooms, weak textbooks, teacher shortages, rigid examinations, and poor coordination with labour markets limited reform.

Middle Eastern higher education expanded rapidly because degrees were seen as passports to elite status. Governments used examinations, external students, and open universities to manage demand, but graduates often had skills not needed by the economy. Political control, weak academic freedom, civil war, coups, and authoritarian regimes affected universities.

The Islamic revival challenged Westernized knowledge and called for education within an Islamic framework. Saudi Arabia and Iran emphasized Islamic teachings in schools and colleges, and many other states increased religious education. Migration and brain drain also reshaped education, as skilled teachers and professionals moved to the West or oil-rich states, leaving shortages at home and encouraging students to study for work abroad.

## Latin America

Latin America is treated as a broad but internally diverse region shaped by Indigenous, African, Spanish, Portuguese, French, Dutch, and Anglo-Saxon patterns. The main generalizations apply to Spanish and Portuguese America, where independence brought republican ideals but also economic ruin, political instability, and deep inequality.

New Latin American governments considered education essential for citizenship and modernization. They faced conflict over content: liberals blamed tyranny and the Roman Catholic Church for backwardness and sought secular state education, while conservatives defended Catholicism as part of national identity. Financial weakness led governments to adopt the Lancaster method of mutual instruction from 1818 onward because it expanded schooling cheaply.

Independence leaders such as Jose de San Martin, Simon Bolivar, Bernardino Rivadavia, and Benito Juarez supported schools, libraries, teacher training, universities, and education as a defense against chaos and tyranny. By the 1870s, liberals had generally made education compulsory, free, secular, and state-directed, though lack of teachers and colleges remained severe.

Normal schools and foreign influences shaped teacher training. Domingo Faustino Sarmiento founded a Normal School for Teachers in Chile in 1842, and other schools followed in Lima and Santiago. Foreign teachers and students abroad introduced ideas from Friedrich Froebel, Johann Friedrich Herbart, German and Swiss pedagogy, and positivism, especially in Argentina, Brazil, Chile, and Mexico.

## Administration

Latin American educational administration ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Academic_administration)) moved toward central ministries because local and state authorities lacked money and trained personnel. Venezuela created a public instruction directory, Mexico a general directory, and Chile, Peru, Guatemala, Venezuela, and Brazil established ministries or similar bodies. Mexico's national ministry was reestablished in 1922 after earlier instability.

Central authorities planned, built, and administered schools; authorized curricula and textbooks; and supervised private education. In federal countries, state systems existed but national governments often had to carry much of the burden for rural elementary education because of inequality between city and countryside.

Primary education ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Primary_education)) expanded from reading, writing, catechism, arithmetic, and geometry to national language, history, geography, natural science, hygiene, civics, drawing, physical education, crafts, and needlework. Schooling became a tool against illiteracy and for citizenship. Indigenous-language needs, rural one-teacher schools, dropout problems, and high population growth made the task difficult.

Secondary education developed from colonial institutions into national colleges, liceos, preparatory schools, and later technical and vocational programs. It usually prepared students for universities and resisted practical reform until industrialization strengthened polytechnical, commercial, agricultural, and industrial education.

Higher education expanded after independence through universities, institutes of arts and sciences, professional schools, law faculties, technical institutes, and later research. The University of Mexico was suppressed in 1865 and reopened in 1910; Brazil founded its first university in 1912; and Mexico created the National Polytechnical Institute in 1936. Universities also became politically active, especially after the University of Cordoba reform movement in 1918.

By 1960, many Latin Americans saw dependency on U.S. and European powers as a root of underdevelopment and looked to education for reform. Developmentalist modernization sought efficiency, while Paulo Freire argued for mental liberation through self-consciousness. Dictatorships, authoritarian pedagogy, and the economic crisis of the 1980s obstructed educational renovation.

## Southeast Asia

In [[Southeast Asia]], education was shaped by indigenous culture, colonialism, and post-World War II independence. Before 1500 CE, education mainly transmitted cultural values through family and community life, supplemented by religious instruction in animism, Hinduism, Buddhism, Daoism, Confucianism, or Islam.

Western colonial schooling ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/School)) introduced secular curricula, grades, examinations, calendars, and diplomas. It appealed to many people because it offered access to colonial government and commercial jobs. After independence, governments sought universal primary schooling, expanded postprimary education, and shifted secondary and tertiary education toward science and technology.

Most systems used primary, secondary, and higher levels, with nursery schools and kindergartens growing privately. Primary schooling usually lasted six years, secondary education often had two three-year levels, and nonformal adult literacy and vocational programs expanded after the 1950s. Common problems included dropout, repetition, teacher shortages, buildings, rural access, cultural diversity, and curriculum fit.

## Myanmar

In Myanmar, traditional education consisted mainly of Buddhist monastic schools. The British occupation in 1886 introduced a Western system that recognized women's right to formal education and increased women's role as teachers. Rangoon and Judson colleges were incorporated into the University of Rangoon in 1920.

After independence in 1948, political instability ended with a socialist coup in 1962. Education became more centralized, science and civic education expanded, practical arts were added, and enrollments rose at primary, secondary, and higher levels.

Malaysia and Singapore shared British colonial roots but diverged after Singapore left Malaysia in 1965. British rule had created primary schools in Malay, Chinese, English, and Tamil streams, with English offering better access to higher education and employment. Malaysia adopted Malay as the medium beyond primary school, while Singapore promoted English plus one local language. Both achieved strong growth in primary, secondary, and higher education.

## Indonesia

In [[Indonesia]], early education included Hindu and Buddhist aristocratic learning, informal family education for common people, Islamic Quran schools from about 1300, and limited Western schooling introduced by Portuguese, Spanish, and Dutch influences. Dutch schooling mainly served Europeans and Eurasians, though the colonial government formally committed to native education in 1848.

After independence in 1949, Indonesia sought universal elementary schooling and expansion of secondary and higher education. Enrollments grew rapidly after 1950 despite high population growth. Most schools became Western and secular, while Islamic schools were required to include secular studies.

## Philippines

Before Spanish conquest, the [[Philippines]] had writing and many adults could read and write. Education taught reverence for Bathala, obedience, family loyalty, truth, and righteousness. Spanish colonial education developed slowly, but Santo Tomas College, founded in 1611 and later made a university, became an intellectual centre.

Under American rule, William McKinley's policy of universal primary education in 1900 led to rapid growth. The University of the Philippines was founded in 1908, and many private higher-education institutions developed. After World War II, national development plans expanded education, enrollments rose dramatically, and by the late 20th century nearly all primary pupils attended public schools while higher education included more than 1,000 institutions.

## Thailand

Traditional Thailand education rested on Theravada Buddhism, veneration of the king, loyalty to family, moral excellence, generosity, and moderation. Modern education began in 1887, when King Chulalongkorn created a department of education with foreign advisers.

Thai modern schooling expanded through temple schools, a medical school in 1888, a law school in 1897, a royal pages' school in 1902, and Civil Service College in 1910. After the 1932 revolution, education expanded further, and from 1962 five-year development plans gave schools a major role in manpower preparation.

## Cambodia

In Cambodia, education for centuries centred on Theravada Buddhist pagoda schools. French rule began in 1863, and Cambodia became part of French Indochina in 1887 before gaining full independence in 1954.

Cambodian education was devastated by civil war in the 1970s and the Khmer Rouge period. After Vietnamese forces overthrew the Khmer Rouge in 1979, schools reopened. By the mid-1980s, nearly two million students were enrolled in a four-year primary, three-year junior-secondary, and three-year senior-secondary structure, though higher education and teacher training were still being rebuilt.

## Laos

In Laos, the pagoda school was the main traditional educational unit. French protectorate status began in 1893, and inclusion in French Indochina followed in 1904. French became the medium when the French Education Service was created.

After a socialist government was established in 1975, schooling ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/School)) became a high priority. Within a decade, more than three quarters of children aged 7 to 11 were in five-year primary schools, about half of children aged 12 to 14 were in junior secondary schools, and about one quarter of 15- to 17-year-olds were in senior secondary schools.

## Vietnam

In Vietnam, long Chinese domination produced strong Confucian and Daoist influences, though education also centred on Buddhism. French rule began with the occupation of Saigon in 1859 and produced an education pattern like that of other parts of Indochina.

Vietnamese education was repeatedly disrupted by fighting from World War II onward and by the division of the country after 1954. After the war ended in 1975, the communist government reeducated the South and urgently expanded technical and vocational education. By the next decade there were eight million elementary pupils, four million secondary pupils, and more than 115,000 higher-education students.

## Global trends in education

The 20th century saw a dramatic expansion of public education. Schools increased, more children attended, and curricula broadened from language and mathematics to science, arts, technology, civics, and vocational subjects.

One explanation is social cohesion, associated with Emile Durkheim. Public schooling helped modern states transmit common history, moral discipline, civic identity, and occupational skills. Newly independent countries in Africa and Asia used schools to create national identity across tribal, ethnic, linguistic, religious, and geographic divisions.

Another explanation is social conflict, associated with Karl Marx and Max Weber. From this view, schooling can reproduce class divisions, distribute credentials as cultural capital, and help dominant groups control access to status, work, and power. Education can therefore both open mobility and preserve inequality.

John Dewey represents education as personal growth. His laboratory school work and his books argued that education must be tied to experience, student interest, critical thought, and the total development of the child. European reformers such as Ovide Decroly and Maria Montessori developed related experiential and tactile methods.

Institutional views of civil society explain schooling as part of broader changes after the Enlightenment: centralized states, citizenship rights, human progress, individual potential, and modern economic growth. Schools became worldwide carriers of modern forms, including classrooms, curricula, enrollment goals, teachers, and standardized expectations.

Curriculum and assessment became central instruments of modern schooling. Curriculum decides what counts as useful knowledge, moral formation, national culture, vocational preparation, or personal development. Examinations and standardized tests then convert that curriculum into selection, credentials, and access to later schooling or employment. This gives schools a double role: they transmit shared knowledge and skills, but they also sort students through marks, certificates, and competitive pathways. Teacher training stands between these goals, because reforms succeed only when teachers can translate official programs into daily classroom practice.

Human-capital theory, popularized by Theodore Schultz in 1960, treated education as an investment that increases individual productivity and national competitiveness. This helped governments justify educational spending as economic development rather than mere consumption.

UNESCO data show enormous enrollment growth in the second half of the 20th century. Primary enrollments more than tripled, secondary enrollments grew more than ninefold, and tertiary enrollments grew more than 12-fold. Credentialing and the queuing effect helped sustain demand as people saw degrees and certificates as necessary for work and status.

Primary education ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Primary_education)) became nearly universal in many developed regions and in Latin America, East Asia, and South Asia, but sub-Saharan Africa and some Arab countries lagged. Secondary education also expanded strongly, especially after 1999 in sub-Saharan Africa, South and West Asia, and Arab countries, yet family income and national resources still limited access.

Tertiary education became a gateway to modern sectors, prestige, and political or professional status. By the early 21st century, fewer than one-fifth of people aged 18 to 24 worldwide were in tertiary education, with much lower rates in the least-developed countries and much higher rates in North America, western Europe, and Oceania.

Early childhood care and preschool education gained importance because they prepared children for school success and national socialization. France and Italy showed different routes to strong preschool traditions, while China showed how political revolution, the Great Leap Forward, the Cultural Revolution, and later pragmatic modernization reshaped education and human capital.

Literacy improved but remained uneven. Between 1950 and 2000, the global illiteracy rate fell from about 44 percent to 20 percent among people aged 15 and older, yet the absolute number of illiterate adults rose because population grew rapidly in less-developed countries.

International agreements made education rights part of global policy. The Universal Declaration of Human Rights, the Declaration of the Rights of the Child, and the International Covenant on Economic, Social and Cultural Rights supported education that respects personality, talents, abilities, and culture. UNESCO conferences at Jomtien in 1990 and Dakar in 2000 promoted universal primary education, later tied to the United Nations Millennium Development Goals.

Access remained a major problem. More than 120 million primary-school-age children were outside formal education by the end of the 20th century, and many enrolled children did not complete required schooling. Inappropriate curricula, foreign languages of instruction, repetition, dropout, and selective examinations filtered students before postprimary levels.

Equality of educational opportunity became a central theme. Analysts asked whether students start equally prepared, run the same course, and have a fair chance of finishing. Socioeconomic status, family background, region, language, ethnicity, gender, and school quality all shaped outcomes, often more strongly as societies modernized.

Gender equality improved but remained incomplete. At the turn of the 21st century, girls made up about 60 percent of children excluded from school, and women made up nearly two-thirds of illiterate adults. Yet women also became majorities among secondary graduates and university entrants in many countries, though higher education did not always bring equal income or occupational status.

The role of the state remained disputed. Some policies reduced poverty and supported equality, while others decentralized, privatized, charged fees, or applied market logic. Critics argued that decentralization could widen regional gaps and that standardized tests often reflected family and community resources rather than school merit alone.

Successful reforms often combined education with health, nutrition, infrastructure, materials, teacher development, flexible calendars, home-language materials, and support for girls. Parent participation, convenient school communication, adult education, health programs, child care, and social services also helped schools serve disadvantaged communities.

Alternative forms of education grew through the Internet, distance learning, homeschooling, exported degree programs, nonformal education, popular education, NGOs, religious institutions, and mass media. These systems could complement, compete with, or conflict with state schooling, and they raised new questions about culture, language, socialization, local knowledge, and learner control.

## Key Events & Developments

- 1870: The Elementary Education Act in England accepted state responsibility for elementary schools and marked a major step toward national public education.
- 1882: The French Third Republic made primary education compulsory for children aged 6 to 13, helping create a free, secular, national elementary system.
- 1890: Japan's Imperial Rescript on Education fixed the moral-national ideology that shaped Japanese schooling before World War II.
- 1905: [[China]] abolished the civil service examination system, clearing the way for a modern school system.
- 1917: The Russian Revolution transformed education into a tool of communist reconstruction, mass literacy, polytechnical training, and ideological formation.
- 1944: The British Education Act recast education into primary, secondary, and further stages and made publicly maintained secondary education free.
- 1945: Japan's surrender led to Allied reforms that removed militaristic schooling and created a democratic six-three-three-four structure.
- 1947: Independence and partition reshaped South Asia, leading India, Pakistan, and later Bangladesh to pursue separate national education policies.
- 1950s: Many countries expanded secondary, higher, technical, adult, and compensatory education in response to industrial growth, decolonization, and social equality demands.
- 1975: The U.S. Education for All Handicapped Children Act greatly expanded legal support for students with disabilities.
- 1976: The Soweto unrest in South Africa showed how language policy and apartheid schooling could become a centre of political resistance.
- 1980s: Many Western and non-Western systems shifted toward standards, assessment, vocational relevance, computer literacy, administrative efficiency, and school-work links.
- 1990: The World Conference on Education for All at Jomtien made universal primary education a global goal.
- 2000: The World Education Forum at Dakar renewed the universal education target and connected it to global development goals.

## Key People

- [[Confucius and Confucianism (Confucianism)|Confucius]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Confucius)): Treated education as moral cultivation and public responsibility, shaping the long Chinese and East Asian classical tradition.
- [[Plato and Platonism (Plato)|Plato]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plato)): Connected education with philosophy, the formation of the soul, and the ordering of the ideal state.
- [[Aristotle and Aristotelianism|Aristotle]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aristotle)): Linked education to politics, virtue, habit, and the responsibilities of citizens.
- [[Jean-Jacques Rousseau]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jean-Jacques_Rousseau)): Shifted modern educational thought toward natural development and the needs of the child.
- Johann Heinrich Pestalozzi ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Johann_Heinrich_Pestalozzi)): Influenced child-centred and humane education through attention to development, activity, and the whole child.
- Johann Friedrich Herbart ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Johann_Friedrich_Herbart)): Helped make pedagogy more systematic and connected education with psychology, moral formation, and structured lessons.
- Friedrich Froebel ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Friedrich_Froebel)): Developed kindergarten principles and influenced early childhood education internationally.
- Horace Mann ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Horace_Mann)): Promoted the American common school, teacher training, public funding, and education for democratic society.
- John Dewey ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Dewey)): Made experience, democracy, inquiry, and the school as a miniature society central to progressive education.
- Maria Montessori ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maria_Montessori)): Created a child-centred method based on prepared environments, sensory materials, independence, and self-education.
- Ovide Decroly ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ovide_Decroly)): Developed interest-centred and workshop-style methods that influenced early childhood and progressive education.
- Robert M. Hutchins ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_M._Hutchins)): Defended liberal education, great books, and intellectual cultivation against vocationalism and specialization.
- Nadezhda Krupskaya ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nadezhda_Krupskaya)): Helped shape early Soviet education around literacy, polytechnical education, and revolutionary social goals.
- Anatoly Lunacharsky ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anatoly_Lunacharsky)): Led early Soviet cultural and educational policy after the Russian Revolution.
- Joseph Stalin ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph_Stalin)): Centralized and stabilized Soviet education around discipline, academic subjects, examinations, patriotism, and party-state control.
- Nikita Khrushchev ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nikita_Khrushchev)): Promoted reforms linking school to life, productive work, vocational preparation, and polytechnical education.
- Mikhail Gorbachev ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mikhail_Gorbachev)): Opened space for educational democratization, cooperation, institutional autonomy, and native-language concerns during perestroika.
- [[Mao Zedong]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mao_Zedong)): Made Chinese education part of revolutionary transformation, ideological struggle, labour, and reeducation, especially during the Cultural Revolution.
- Deng Xiaoping ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deng_Xiaoping)): Reoriented Chinese education after Mao toward modernization, examinations, academic standards, and technical training.
- Mustafa Kemal Ataturk ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mustafa_Kemal_Ataturk)): Used education to modernize Turkey through secularization, coeducation, adult education, university reform, and rural teacher training.
- Paulo Freire ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paulo_Freire)): Influenced Latin American reform by presenting education as a process of self-consciousness and mental liberation.

## Interesting Facts

- Ancient education often served small elites first because writing, accounting, ritual, and administration were needed by temples, palaces, and states before mass schooling existed.
- Aztec schooling was unusually organized for a preconquest society and trained children for social duty, religion, warfare, domestic work, and public service.
- Chinese civil service learning made education a path to official status for centuries, even though family and class still shaped access.
- Islamic education transmitted religious law and theology but also preserved and developed philosophy, medicine, mathematics, astronomy, and translated Greek learning.
- Kindergarten and early childhood education grew from reform movements that treated play, activity, movement, music, and discovery as serious parts of learning.
- Sweden's reform unified compulsory schooling early and treated integration and differentiation as complementary, not opposite, principles.
- American high school enrollment grew so much in the first half of the 20th century that college access became the next major pressure point.
- Head Start was not only a school-readiness program; it included medical, dental, social, nutritional, and psychological care.
- Japan's postwar reform replaced militaristic schooling with a democratic single-track structure, but later reforms recentralized curriculum and restored moral education.
- South African language policy was so politically charged that compulsory Afrikaans instruction helped trigger the Soweto riots in 1976.
- Latin American universities often became political actors, especially after student representation movements such as the University of Cordoba reform in 1918.
- Global enrollment growth in the second half of the 20th century was enormous: primary enrollment more than tripled, secondary enrollment rose more than ninefold, and tertiary enrollment rose more than twelvefold.
- Literacy progress was complicated by population growth: the world illiteracy rate fell sharply from 1950 to 2000, but the absolute number of illiterate adults still increased.
- Alternative education includes distance learning, homeschooling, nonformal programs, NGOs, religious instruction, workplace certificates, and mass media as parallel systems beside state schools.
