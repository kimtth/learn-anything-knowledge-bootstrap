# Public Opinion

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> Public opinion is the collective and contested pattern of views through which communities judge public issues, culture, and institutions. The source traces its rise as a political force and examines formation, measurement, media, elite influence, and democratic legitimacy.

## Introduction

![[attachments/public_opinion_001.webp|420]]

*Gallup poll chart*

Public opinion ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Public_opinion)) is the aggregate of individual views, attitudes, and beliefs about a topic, expressed by a significant part of a community. Some scholars see it as a synthesis of the views of all or part of society, while others see it as a collection of different or even opposing views. It may concern politics, culture, fashion, art, consumer behaviour, marketing, religion, or social activism.

Charles Horton Cooley emphasized in 1918 that public opinion is a process of interaction and mutual influence rather than simple agreement. V.O. Key defined it in 1961 as private opinions that governments find it wise to heed. By the 1990s, statistical and demographic analysis supported a view of public opinion as the collective view of a defined population, such as a demographic or ethnic group.

Definitions vary because scholars approach public opinion from different angles. Political scientists and historians often stress its role in government and elections. Sociologists may emphasize communication, group interaction, and social influence. Pollsters focus on how opinions can be measured in defined populations.

Walter Lippmann, writing in 1922, noted that democracies often treat public opinion as mysterious, though skilled organizers of opinion understand enough about it to create electoral majorities. This captures a lasting tension: public opinion can be treated as democratic expression, as a social process, or as something shaped by leaders, media, and institutions.

## Antiquity

Phenomena like public opinion existed long before the term appeared in the 18th century. Ancient Babylonian and Assyrian accounts include references to popular attitudes, including stories of rulers listening secretly to what people said about their governance. Ancient Israelite prophets sometimes defended government policy to the people and sometimes urged the people to oppose rulers.

In classical [[Athens]], persuasion of the people was central to politics. Wealth, reputation, and public respect could be gained or lost by speech before the populace. [[Plato and Platonism (Plato)|Plato]] distrusted public opinion and preferred rule by philosopher-kings, while [[Aristotle and Aristotelianism|Aristotle]] recognized that a king who lost popular support could no longer rule effectively, though the public he had in mind was only a limited citizen body.

During the Middle Ages, most rural Europeans lived within fixed social stations, but opinion still mattered among religious, intellectual, and political elites. Disputes between popes and the Holy Roman Empire, dynastic conflicts, and religious controversies all involved efforts to persuade people who counted politically. In 1191, William Longchamp, bishop of Ely, was attacked for hiring troubadours to influence public feeling.

## Revolutionary Public Opinion

By the late 18th century, public opinion had become a visible political force. Revolutionary opinion helped transform thirteen North American British colonies into the [[United States of America (United States)|United States of America]]. In [[France]], public opinion moved both middle classes and urban masses and became part of the force behind the French Revolution.

The French Revolution frightened many observers because public opinion seemed able to sweep aside monarchy, one of the strongest institutions of the age. Figures such as Jacques Necker became linked to the politics of reputation, finance, reform, and public confidence. Political authority increasingly depended not only on law or dynasty but also on how people judged rulers and policies.

Some 19th-century writers treated public opinion as the view of the informed and morally responsible upper classes. William A. Mackinnon distinguished public opinion from popular clamour, which he associated with passion, lack of reflection, and uneducated excitement. This distinction shows an older elite fear that mass feeling might be less legitimate than reasoned opinion.

G.W.F. Hegel argued that public opinion contains both truth and falsehood and that a great person must distinguish between them. James Bryce believed government based on consent gave stability and strength, but he doubted that public opinion could decide policy details. For him, the public set broad limits and aims while officials handled the details.

## Modern Theories of Public Opinion

Since the early 20th century, many theories of public opinion have appeared without one becoming dominant. Sherry Devereux Ferguson grouped them into three broad types. One type sees opinion as rising from the lower levels of society to the top, supporting two-way communication between representatives and represented.

The populist view accepts that opinion shifts as people interact with each other and respond to media. A second, elitist or social-constructionist view stresses manipulation and the many perspectives that form around issues. A third, critical or radical-functionalist view argues that ordinary people and minorities have little influence because opinion is largely controlled by those in power.

The critical view has been challenged by the visible plurality of voices in democracies, especially through the Internet and newer media. Public discourse can be influenced by elites, but it is rarely a single controlled stream. Competing groups, media channels, activists, officials, and citizens all help shape what becomes publicly visible.

## Formation and Change of Public Opinion

![[attachments/public_opinion_002.webp|420]]

*Newspaper public opinion article*

Once a collective view forms, public opinion can become self-sustaining. Alexis de Tocqueville observed that in democratic societies an opinion that takes root among the majority may persist with little effort because few people attack it. This makes public opinion both powerful and resistant to change.

Opinion can change through major events, persuasive campaigns, social movements, media attention, and gradual cultural shifts. Environmental opinion, for example, was influenced by Rachel Carson's Silent Spring in 1962, the Chernobyl nuclear accident in 1986, Margaret Thatcher's 1988 Royal Society address on environmental issues, the Exxon Valdez oil spill in 1989, and An Inconvenient Truth in 2006.

Mass media attention is often necessary for a body of public opinion to form and endure. Yet some changes remain hard to explain. In the second half of the 20th century, attitudes toward religion, family, sex, international relations, social welfare, and the economy shifted widely in many countries, without always being traceable to one event or campaign.

## Public Opinion and Government

Democratic politics encourages citizens to form opinions on candidates, constitutional amendments, taxes, laws, and public programs. Almost any matter before executives or legislatures can become a public issue if enough people want to make it one. Newspapers, interest groups, officials, agencies, and activists can stimulate or reinforce those attitudes.

Jeremy Bentham saw a major problem of legislation as reconciling public opinion with good law. Tocqueville feared that mass opinion might become a tyranny of the majority. Even so, few democratic politicians openly argue that government should ignore public opinion, because legitimacy depends partly on public consent.

Public opinion does not usually decide the details of policy, but it sets limits within which policymakers operate. Officials often try to satisfy broad demands or at least take them into account, while avoiding decisions believed to be widely unpopular. The role of opinion varies by issue and by democracy, so no single rule applies everywhere.

Efforts to respond to public opinion can be criticized as pandering. Lawrence R. Jacobs and Robert Y. Shapiro argued, however, that politicians often use polling less to follow the public than to craft messages and manipulate responsiveness. This shifts the issue from whether politicians listen to whether they use opinion data to govern democratically or strategically.

## Election Polls and Their Limits

Election polls have often forecast results successfully when used near voting day, but important failures show the uncertainty involved. In the [[United States of America (United States)|United States]] in 1948, nearly all polls predicted a Republican victory, yet Democrats won narrowly. In Great Britain in 1970, most major polls wrongly predicted a Labour victory, and in 1992 polls wrongly predicted a hung parliament.

Forecasting elections is difficult because of late opinion shifts and unexpected turnout. Still, polling has had a strong record over time in many countries. Public attention focuses heavily on election polls, but they make up only a small share of survey research.

Most opinion research concerns non-electoral topics. Public agencies, foundations, universities, and businesses study health needs, education, racial prejudice, drug addiction, public satisfaction, industry performance, and social issues. In countries where polls are published, electoral polling may account for no more than about 2 percent of survey researchers' work.

## Methodology

Opinion polling follows several main steps: defining the universe, choosing a sample, writing a questionnaire, interviewing the sample, tabulating results, and analyzing, interpreting, and reporting findings. Each step can affect the result, so polling is not simply asking questions and counting answers.

The universe is the body of people being studied. It may be all adults, college students, elderly people, teenagers, institutional investors, editors, politicians, or any other definable group. The chosen universe must match the issue; a survey of college students, for example, must decide whether to include part-time and nondegree students or only full-time students.

After defining the universe, researchers choose a sample. Probability sampling, especially random sampling, is the most reliable method because every member of the universe has an equal chance of selection. In principle, this can be done by numbering people and using random numbers, though in practice complete lists are often difficult to obtain.

Questionnaire design matters because wording, order, and response choices can shape answers. A question may push respondents toward one answer, hide uncertainty, or confuse different issues. Good surveys try to make questions clear, neutral, and appropriate to the population being studied.

## Interviewing

Interviewing can introduce error if interviewers ask questions badly, influence respondents, alienate them, or fail to record free answers accurately. Interviewers may also fail to locate selected respondents, which can weaken the sample. Large polling organizations train interviewers or use experienced interviewing services to reduce these problems.

## Tabulation

Tabulation is usually done by computer, and many questionnaires are precoded so answers can be quickly converted into numbers. Free-answer questions must be grouped into categories and coded, but the choice of categories can change how results appear. A response about narcotics addiction, for example, might be coded as health, crime, drug abuse, or alcoholism.

Survey reports may give only marginals, or top-line proportions for each answer, such as support for candidates and undecided voters. More detailed reports use cross tabulations to show how answers vary by group. A candidate may appear stronger among suburban voters, but income analysis may reveal that wealth, not residence, is the more important factor.

Poll interpretation also requires attention to turnout, certainty of voting, and subgroup patterns. Results can be technically correct but misleading if important social divisions are hidden. Presentation is therefore part of analysis, not a neutral afterthought.

## Key Events & Developments

- 1918: Charles Horton Cooley described public opinion as interaction and mutual influence.
- 1922: Walter Lippmann analyzed how democracies and organizers of opinion treat public opinion.
- 1961: V.O. Key defined public opinion as private opinions that governments find prudent to heed.
- 1948: [[United States of America (United States)|United States]] election polls famously failed to predict the Democratic victory.
- 1962 to 2006: Environmental opinion was shaped by major publications, accidents, speeches, spills, and climate documentaries.

## Interesting Facts

- Public opinion ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Public_opinion)) can be a majority view, a clash of opposing views, or the measured view of a defined population.
- Election polling receives great public attention but is only a small part of survey research.
- Cross tabulation can change the meaning of a poll by showing that hidden factors such as income explain apparent geographic differences.
- Mass media can help create and sustain opinion, but opinion can also persist after it becomes socially rooted.
