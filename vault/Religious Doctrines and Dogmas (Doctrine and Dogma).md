## Introduction

Doctrine ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doctrine)) and dogma are official ways of explaining religious teaching. They shape traditions, institutions, practices, and conflicts while interacting with history, science, and philosophy. Doctrine is broader; dogma is more official and essential.

In theology, doctrine means the conceptual expression of a religious community's basic insights. It turns experience, symbol, and intuition into teachings for instruction, discipline, propaganda, controversy, and defense of belief.

Dogma ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dogma)) refers to first principles or official doctrinal statutes accepted as essential. In Christianity, the New Testament uses didaskalia for basic teaching and dogma for official decree, though early writers often used dogma more broadly.

In Eastern Christianity, St. John of Damascus popularized orthodoxy, meaning correct views. In Western Christianity, St. Thomas Aquinas used articles of faith for doctrines solemnly defined by the church.

At the Council of Trent in 1545-63, doctrine and dogma were still nearly synonymous. Modern historians stressed their difference. J.K.L. Gieseler defined dogma as doctrinal statute rather than private opinion.

Adolf von Harnack explained Christian dogma as Greek metaphysics mixed with Christian thought. Karl Rahner, by contrast, saw dogma as a living form of the deposit of faith, unchanged yet continually expressed.

## Functions of Doctrines and Dogmas

The functions of doctrines and dogmas differ among religions. In more mystical traditions, doctrines may guide insight, such as nirvana in Hinduism, Jainism, and Buddhism. In more personalistic traditions, doctrines aid reflection, as with God's unity in Judaism, Christianity, and Islam.

In all higher religions, doctrine teaches the faithful, interprets scripture, explains duties, and marks boundaries between diversity and error. St. Anselm of Canterbury called theology faith seeking rational self-understanding.

Doctrine also has a polemical function, defending faith against misinterpretation inside and outside a tradition. It often marks the line between legitimate development and error.

Shared terms can produce different doctrinal systems. Hinduism, Jainism, and Buddhism all use samsara and karma, but explain them differently and draw different consequences.

## Development

Every religion has a history of doctrine that does more than repeat an original deposit. Doctrine preserves tradition but can also generate new insights and change teaching.

All forms of [[Buddha and Buddhism (Buddhism)|Buddhism]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buddhism)) appeal to the Three Jewels: the Buddha, dharma, and sangha. Yet Theravada and Mahayana differ deeply. Judaism shows similar unity and diversity through shared devotion to Torah and disagreement over observance.

Christianity shares loyalty to the Bible ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bible)) and dogmas such as the Trinity and the God-Manhood of Jesus, but its systems divided historically. Later Roman Catholic dogmas, including transubstantiation, the immaculate conception, Mary's bodily assumption, and papal infallibility, lacked full Eastern Orthodox or Protestant agreement.

## The Trinity and Organic Development

The dogma of the Trinity makes explicit the conviction that one God is three Persons: Father, Son, and Holy Spirit. Christological dogma similarly developed from testimony that Jesus is both divine and human.

Some theories describe doctrine as logical unfolding, where truth remains unchanged while implications become clearer. Others stress organic development shaped by science, philosophy, crises, and history.

John Henry Newman argued in 1845 that truths given once for all required time for full explanation. Protestants more often emphasized return to scripture and apostolic tradition when they saw church history as deviation.

## Faith, Reason, and Religious Traditions

In many Eastern traditions, reason may lead from ritual obligation to rational insight and mystical illumination. In Western traditions, reason often makes mysteries of faith more intelligible.

In [[Judaism]], Torah knowledge develops through the Bible and Talmud, guided by Halakha and Haggada. In Islam, approaches range from Hanbalite orthodoxy to Mutazilite rationalism, Asharite kalam, and al-Farabi, while remaining anchored in God's unity and [[Muhammad and the Religion of Islam (Muhammad)|Muhammad]]'s prophetic office.

In Christianity, faith and reason range from Tertullian's fideism to Thomas Aquinas's synthesis. Philipp Melanchthon warned that correct doctrine is dead unless joined to trust in divine reconciliation.

## Changing Conceptions

Doctrinal conflict has often been polemical. Disputes sometimes caused divisions, and world religions often treated doctrines and dogmas as mutually exclusive.

The 20th century brought change through ecumenism and comparative religion. The Second Vatican Council in 1962-65 showed a broader commitment to mutual religious understanding.

## Key Events & Developments

- 1545-63: The Council of Trent still used doctrine and dogma closely.
- 1845: John Henry Newman published a major theory of doctrinal development.
- 1962-65: The Second Vatican Council advanced ecumenical and interreligious openness.
