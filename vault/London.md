# London

## Introduction

London is the capital of the [[United Kingdom]], Britain's largest metropolis, and a major economic, transportation, and cultural centre. Its history spans nearly two millennia, and its position on the River Thames, about 50 miles from the North Sea estuary, shaped its growth.

Greater London was formed in 1965 and covers a compact built-up area enclosed by the Green Belt ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Green_belt)) and the M25 motorway. The metropolis crosses old county identities, with much of south London historically in Surrey and much of north London historically in Middlesex.

London's inner structure is unusually complex. It is a polycentric city, with many centres and no simple hierarchy. The City, Westminster, Southwark, the West End, and many later suburbs grew from separate nuclei, creating a city with many mayors, dioceses, cathedrals, opera houses, and social worlds.

The historical division between the City of London, Southwark, and Westminster shaped later London. The City centred on trade and banking, Southwark on inns, hospitals, fairs, and Elizabethan theatres, and Westminster on abbey, palace, Parliament, courts, parks, and fashionable districts.

By 1800, London exceeded one million people, and by 1900 it reached about 6.5 million. Unlike many continental capitals, its expansion was not held by military walls or strong state planning, so speculative building followed transport improvements and middle-class housing demand.

World War II bombing and evacuation ended the long era of outward suburban growth. After the war, policy treated London as too large and strategically vulnerable, imposed the Green Belt ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Green_belt)), diverted growth beyond it, and redrew boundaries around almost the whole physical metropolis.

Visitors often see a much smaller central London defined by attractions such as Buckingham Palace, the British Museum, the National Gallery, Westminster Abbey, the Tower of London, South Kensington museums, Tate galleries, and Madame Tussaud's. Residents, however, often understand the city as a collection of local villages and neighbourhoods.

## Landscape

London stands on the London Basin, a chalk-based geological structure filled with clays, sands, gravels, and centuries of made ground. London Clay supports many tunnels and deep foundations, while gravel, brick earth, and human deposits shape the surface.

The Thames valley gave London a broad, low site between northern and southern ridges. The river, gravel terraces, tributaries, commons, forests, and parklands influenced settlement, transport, industry, and recreation.

Open spaces such as Ruislip Common, Bushey Heath, Enfield Chase, Epping Forest, Hainault Forest, and South Weald preserved pieces of older landscape. These green areas balance the dense built fabric and connect London to its surrounding countryside.

## Climate

London's climate is mild, temperate, and typical of southeast England. Weather records go back to the 17th century, showing cycles of cold periods and a warming trend after 1919, especially through milder autumns.

Average daytime temperature is about 11 C, with January around 5.5 C and July around 18 C. Rainfall is modest and evenly distributed, snow is variable and usually short-lived, and the built-up centre forms a heat island warmer and drier than surrounding countryside.

## Environment

London was long associated with smog, a mixture of smoke and fog caused by coal fires. The East End suffered especially because factories, domestic chimneys, low land, and dense housing trapped smoke and sulfur dioxide.

The Clean Air Acts of 1956 and 1968, together with housing clearance and manufacturing decline, reduced coal smoke. Traffic fumes, nitrogen dioxide, ozone, carbon monoxide, and other pollutants remained problems, though London's changeable weather limited Los Angeles-style photochemical smog.

Water quality also improved after the 1960s. Sewage and industrial effluent had blackened rivers, but stricter standards and factory closures brought fish and other life back to the tidal Thames.

## The Thames

The Thames Barrier and related defenses respond to the serious risk of flooding. Thames waters have risen over time, and a major flood would threaten low-lying land, infrastructure, buildings, and more than a million people.

Flood risk comes from sea-level movement, regional tilting after the melting of Ice Age sheets, water extraction from the chalk aquifer, dredging, and embanking of estuary marshes. Earlier defenses included raised river walls after the Thames Flood Act of 1879 and further measures after floods in 1928 and 1953.

## Western Open Spaces

Western open landscapes such as Wimbledon Common, Richmond Park, the Royal Botanic Gardens at Kew, Hampton Court Park, and Bushey Park softened noise from Heathrow's flight path. Proximity to Heathrow also strengthened western London's favoured position.

## Internal Geography

London's internal geography includes north-south, east-west, and concentric patterns. The east-west divide once reflected docks, industry, and working-class districts in the east, and government, fashion, parks, and wealth in the west.

The decline of port and manufacturing work weakened the old east-west divide. From 1981 to 1998, the London Docklands Development Corporation regenerated derelict eastern docklands, including Wapping, Limehouse, the Isle of Dogs, and the Royal Docklands.

Concentric growth placed the City at the centre, Inner London around it as a late-18th-century to pre-World War I belt of terraces, Outer London as a mostly 20th-century suburban zone of semidetached houses, and the Metropolitan Green Belt as the outer ring.

## People

London's population grew from 5.6 million in 1891 to a wartime peak, then fell by about two million by the mid-1980s. Out-migration, automobiles, shorter working hours, suburban life, firm relocation, lower densities, and gentrification all contributed.

The decline slowed in the 1980s, and population grew again in the 1990s. Greater London is the United Kingdom's most urbanized and populous area, concentrating about one-seventh of the national population.

Immigration transformed London into a deeply multiethnic city. Communities included people from South Asia, the Caribbean, Africa, the Middle East, East Asia, Latin America, and many refugee or migrant groups such as Vietnamese, Kurds, Somalis, Eritreans, Iraqis, Iranians, Brazilians, and Colombians.

By the 21st century, nearly one-third of residents came from overseas. Poorer immigrant groups often settled in inner eastern boroughs, while international business and wealthy foreign property owners concentrated in northwest, southwest, Mayfair, Park Lane, Belgravia, Queensway, and Edgware Road areas.

London's residential geography has no rigid ghettos. Groups often follow a sequence of arrival, consolidation, outward movement, and upward mobility, as seen in Jewish movement from Whitechapel toward Ilford and Cypriot movement along Green Lanes toward Tottenham and Haringay.

## A Layered Metropolis

Michael John Hebbert is associated with the account of London's physical and human geography. The city appears as a layered metropolitan system of local identities, historic districts, transport corridors, immigrant networks, and changing economic functions.

## Economy

London's economy grew from three linked elements: trade ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trade)), administration, and leisure. The City handled shipping, commerce, and crafts; Westminster handled court and government; and Southwark and the West End supported entertainment, consumption, and later tourism.

Modern London is a global trade and financial centre alongside places such as New York, Tokyo, and Hong Kong. It also dominates British administration because the United Kingdom remains highly centralized, with legislative and fiscal power concentrated at Westminster.

Tourism and leisure became major economic forces. Aristocratic consumption left traces in West End shops, clubs, tailors, art dealers, and vintners, while international visitors filled hotels, theatres, museums, and shopping districts.

Elizabethan London still had separate centres divided by fields and the river: the City, Southwark, and Westminster. Later growth merged these centres physically but preserved their functional differences.

## Shipping

Shipping was central to London's economy for centuries. Until World War II, it remained the world's largest and busiest port, with the Port of London Authority, founded in 1909, overseeing enclosed docks, quays, wharves, shipyards, and heavy industry along the Thames.

Shipping left London rapidly between 1968 and 1981. Containerization, larger bulk cargoes, labour problems, and competition from newer private ports moved activity downstream to Tilbury and reduced London's share of national port traffic.

## Manufacturing

London was once a major manufacturing ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manufacturing)) centre, producing luxury goods, clothing, furniture, watches, instruments, food, drink, pharmaceuticals, printing, publishing, and electrical goods. Skilled trades clustered near the City, while polluting or regulated work often moved outside the walls.

In the 20th century, electrically powered mass-consumption industries drew companies such as Heinz, Hoover, Ford, and Firestone to arterial roads. Postwar Green Belt policy, regional incentives, zoning, and broader manufacturing shifts later drove deindustrialization.

## Finance

Finance ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Finance)) offset manufacturing decline. By 1990, one in six London workers was in financial or business services, and the City claimed the world's largest concentration of financial employment.

London's financial role grew from sterling trade finance in the 19th century, imperial banking in the early 20th century, exchange-control relaxation in 1958, Eurocurrency and Eurobond markets, and deregulation in the 1980s. It became a centre for international banking, foreign exchange, bond trading, reinsurance, swaps, and securities.

## The Thames (transport)

The Thames was London's oldest highway. Until Westminster Bridge opened in 1750, London Bridge was the only crossing, and watermen carried many passengers by ferry and boat.

After railways developed, the river lost most passenger traffic, although later schemes tried to revive it through hydrofoils, catamarans, and hovercraft. Modern London depends on many bridges and tunnels across the river.

## Roads

London lacks a grand road layout. Plans by Patrick Abercrombie in 1944 and the Greater London Development Plan of 1969 tried to impose high-speed roads, but many schemes failed, leaving fragments rather than a coherent network.

This failure helped preserve public transport use. Each morning about a million people entered central London, and more than three-quarters arrived by rail rather than by car.

## Railroads

London's rail infrastructure was built largely between 1836 and 1876, as competing railway companies created separate termini around the City and West End. The Metropolitan Railway, opened in 1884, linked terminals and began the Underground system.

The London Underground in the north and surface railways in the south created one of the world's densest urban rail networks. Later additions included the Heathrow extension, Jubilee Line, Victoria Line, and Docklands Light Railway.

## Taxicabs

London's black taxicabs come from licensing traditions dating to 1639. Motor cabs appeared in 1904, and a 1906 rule required tight turning ability, helping create the distinctive manoeuvrable design.

Drivers must pass “the knowledge,” a detailed test of streets and destinations within six miles of Charing Cross. Trainee drivers studying this geography are a familiar London sight.

## The City Corporation

The City Corporation and Lord Mayor form one of the world's oldest local governments. The Square Mile retains its own police, services, property, capital resources, and defended autonomy.

In 1855, the Metropolitan Board of Works began metropolitan-scale water and sewerage administration. In 1889, the London County Council replaced it as an elected authority, while the City preserved autonomy and boroughs formed a second tier.

## Greater London

In 1965, the Greater London Council replaced the LCC and extended boundaries to the built-up area inside the Green Belt. It worked with 33 boroughs, but conflict with central government and boroughs led Margaret Thatcher's government to abolish it in 1986.

A 1998 referendum restored citywide administration. The Greater London Authority ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greater_London_Authority)), established in 2000, created a directly elected mayor and assembly with responsibilities including transport, policing, development, and fire services.

## Postal Districts

London's postal districts form a familiar but irregular geography. Central codes such as EC, WC, E1, SE1, SW1, W1, and NW1 ring the centre, while outer postal numbering follows sorting-office order rather than neat spatial logic.

## Police

The Metropolitan Police ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metropolitan_Police)) was founded by Robert Peel in 1829 and remained accountable to the home secretary rather than local councillors. The City Corporation preserved its own police from 1839, so two forces coexist.

## Hospitals

London's hospital history began with medieval charity. St. Bartholomew's was founded in 1123, and St. Thomas's about 1213; later major hospitals date mainly from the 18th and 19th centuries.

In the early 21st century, National Health Service foundation trusts administered mergers after direct government control was removed. The hospital system remained one part of London's complicated public service structure.

## Education

Schooling is handled by the 33 boroughs, while private schools include Westminster, St. Paul's, Harrow, and Dulwich. Higher education developed late because London lacked unified civic leadership and was overshadowed by Oxford and Cambridge.

The University of London, founded as an examining body in 1836, became a teaching institution only in 1900. Its federation includes colleges such as Imperial, University College, King's, and the London School of Economics, with facilities spread across the metropolis.

## Museums

The British Museum began in 1753 from the Sloane, Cotton, and Harleian collections, funded by lottery and later expanded through purchase, gifts, war, and empire. Robert Smirke's rebuilt Bloomsbury building and the Reading Room became major landmarks.

The British Library separated in 1972 and moved to St. Pancras in 1998. Natural history collections moved to South Kensington, where the Natural History Museum formed part of the science and art precinct linked to Prince Albert and the Great Exhibition of 1851.

## Theatres

London's [[The Art of the Theatre (Theatre)|theatre]] ranges from the reconstructed Globe, completed in 1997, to the Royal National Theatre, West End commercial theatres, local theatres, fringe venues, pub rooms, amateur stages, and festivals.

The replica Globe Theatre, promoted by Sam Wanamaker, revived open-air Shakespearean performance near its historic context. It stands beside a much broader theatrical ecology.

Local centres such as Stratford, Ilford, Greenwich, Croydon, Battersea, Richmond, Kilburn, and Hammersmith support theatres for local audiences, adding to the nightly range of drama.

## Music

London's fragmented administration helped produce a competitive classical music scene. Major orchestras include the London Symphony, Royal Philharmonic, Philharmonia, London Philharmonic, and BBC Symphony, joined by many chamber and specialist ensembles.

The capital also has important opera orchestras and a large share of Britain's working musicians. London has also long been a major centre for recording and performing popular music, especially rock.

## Tributes

London marks historical memory through street names, statues, busts, funerary monuments, inscriptions, and blue plaques. Since 1867, plaques have identified homes of figures such as Geoffrey Chaucer, [[Mozart]], Florence Nightingale, and James Joyce.

## Football

Football is London's leading participant and spectator sport. Amateur games fill parks, and professional clubs such as Arsenal, Chelsea, Tottenham Hotspur, and West Ham United inspire strong local loyalties rather than one citywide identity.

## Cricket

Cricket is played at Lord's and the Oval, with nearly 1,000 clubs across London. The metropolis has no single county team of its own but is partly represented by historic counties such as Kent, Surrey, Essex, Hertfordshire, and Middlesex.

Other major [[Sports|sports]] include Wimbledon tennis, the Oxford-Cambridge Boat Race from Putney to Mortlake, and horse-racing trips outside London to Epsom, Ascot, and Goodwood.

## History

![[attachments/london_004.webp|420]]

*Tower of London viewed from the River Thames*

Londinium began effectively under the Romans after AD 43. The first definite mention comes from Tacitus in AD 60, when Boudicca's Iceni sacked a trading settlement already spreading across the Walbrook valley.

The Romans rebuilt London with a basilica, fort, amphitheatre, quays, riverside wall, and landward wall by about AD 200. Later decline left a smaller settlement, but medieval walls reused and extended Roman defenses.

## Medieval London

After the Norman Conquest of 1066, William I granted London a charter and built the White Tower. The Tower of London later became fortress, royal residence, mint, menagerie, record store, observatory, arsenal, prison, execution site, and home of the Crown Jewels.

Edward the Confessor made Westminster a royal and religious centre, while the City revived trade. By 1085, London had 10,000 to 15,000 inhabitants and was the largest city north of the Alps.

## Tudor London

Tudor London grew through trade monopolies, companies, industry, and population increase. The Muscovy Company, Levant Company, and East India Company supported overseas commerce, while population rose from more than 100,000 in 1550 to about 200,000 in 1600.

The Reformation opened former religious lands for housing but also ended many charities, so the City organized poor relief and supported royal hospitals. Growth continued despite attempts to control subdivision, crowding, and industry outside the walls.

## Stuart and Georgian London

Stuart and later London linked monarchy, science, architecture, and finance through sites such as Greenwich Hospital, the Royal Observatory, and Hampton Court. The Bank of England, founded in 1694, made the City's money market central to the state.

By 1700, London had overtaken Paris in population. Westminster Bridge in 1750 and later bridges opened southern expansion, while docks, government, fashionable society, and the press deepened London's east-west specialization.

Eighteenth-century London was a cultural centre of writers, artists, craftsmen, and foreign musicians, including Oliver Goldsmith, Samuel Johnson, Joshua Reynolds, Handel, Haydn, and [[Mozart]].

## Nineteenth-Century London

Nineteenth-century growth was immense. London's population exceeded 2,685,000 in 1851, when the Great Exhibition in the Crystal Palace celebrated Britain's commercial and imperial power, and reached 6,586,000 fifty years later.

Reform between 1820 and 1914 addressed policing, lighting, transport, water, sanitation, local government, bridges, railways, and public health. John Snow's Broad Street pump work in 1854 demonstrated waterborne cholera transmission.

The Metropolitan Board of Works, London County Council, Metropolitan Water Board, gas lighting, omnibuses, rail termini, underground railways, trams, and electric transport changed the city. Trafalgar Square, bridges, viaducts, and rail lines marked this era of technical and civic transformation.

## Reconstruction after World War II

World War II bombing killed about 30,000 Londoners, injured 50,000, and damaged docks, housing, commerce, industry, and the City. The Greater London Plan of 1944 guided reconstruction, relocation, Green Belt policy, and new towns.

Postwar London saw slum clearance, new housing, improved services, and planning powers, but also the Great Smog of 1952, which contributed to about 4,000 deaths. The Festival of Britain in 1951 symbolized recovery and produced the Royal Festival Hall.

## London Through the Ages

Hugh D. Clout is associated with the historical account and the “London through the ages” materials. These time capsules describe earlier Britannica views of London, including South Bank relations, old districts, the Blitz, V-2 attacks, and postwar recovery.
