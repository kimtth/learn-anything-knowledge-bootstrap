# Dress and Adornment (Dress)

## Introduction

Dress means clothing, accessories, hair styling, jewelry, and cosmetics for the human body. Its forms vary greatly because dress is shaped by sex, age, class, culture, place, and historical time. Fashionable dress develops through the styles preferred by leading groups in society, because those groups often set the standards that others copy. Dress also has a nature and purpose beyond covering the body, including protection, display, identity, and social meaning. In this way, dress appears not only as useful covering but also as a powerful sign of status, taste, and change.

The main historical line does not focus on special uniforms or local folk costume. Instead, it traces major traditions of dress across history, mainly in the Middle East and the West, while also considering parts of Asia and the Americas. Changes in clothing are tied to religion, trade, technology, conquest, and wealth. Fashion is therefore not random; it often reflects who has influence, what materials are available, and what a society values at a given moment. Dress becomes a record of human culture as well as a form of decoration, and that record can be read through fabric, shape, ornament, and custom.

## Ancient Egypt

Knowledge of ancient Egyptian dress comes mostly from wall paintings, sculptures, and ceramics, because few actual garments survive. This visual evidence must be used carefully, since Egyptian art was often stylized and did not always match current fashion exactly. Linen was the most important textile, while wool was less common. Since dyeing methods were limited, many garments were white, and color often came from jewelry instead of cloth. Deep collars made of beaded or embroidered materials were a striking feature, and in some images they were almost the only visible garment. Clothing in Egypt therefore combined simplicity with bright ornament.

Over a very long period, Egyptian dress changed slowly and remained largely draped rather than closely sewn. Early clothing included loincloths, capes, robes, and simple garments of animal skin. In the Old Kingdom, men often wore a short skirt, while women wore a sheathlike gown held by shoulder straps. In the Middle and New Kingdoms, skirts became fuller, capes and pendants became richer, and important people wore robes with more elaborate draping. Wigs, false hair, and decorative head coverings were also common, partly for beauty and partly for protection from the sun. In this climate, dress worked as both practical covering and social display.

## Continuity in Egyptian Dress

This early Egyptian phase belongs to the long development of costume. At that time, dress for both men and women remained simple and depended on wrapped or tied pieces of material rather than complex tailoring. Animal skins, linen, belts, and decorative collars all played a role. The key point is that Egyptian dress developed gradually and with great continuity, so changes in form were usually slow and carefully adjusted rather than sudden. That long continuity is itself an important feature of ancient fashion.

## Tutankhamen

The Tutankhamen material belongs to the later Egyptian world of the New Kingdom, when dress had become more ornate and layered. Important men and women wore robes or gowns made from larger pieces of fabric, pinned and belted so that folds gathered around the waist. Decorative collars, aprons, capes, and pendants remained important, and sacred emblems could appear on clothing worn by priests and royalty. Royal and elite styles associated with Tutankhamen emphasize richness, careful draping, and a taste for strong color and symbolic decoration. In this phase, dress was clearly linked to authority and ceremonial rank.

## Mesopotamia

Ancient Mesopotamia included the cultures of Sumer, Akkad, Assyria, and Babylonia, all of which developed in the land between the Tigris and Euphrates. Early Sumerian dress used sheepskin skirts with the wool combed into tufts, later replaced by woven wool that kept the same tufted appearance. This style, known from art and sculpture, was worn by both men and women and often reached from the waist to the knees or ankles. Jewelry was also highly developed, and wigs and head ornaments were common. Mesopotamian clothing therefore combined heavy texture, decorative surface, and strong visual distinction.

Later Mesopotamian dress used large draped fabrics rather than only tufted skirts. Men and women wore broad cloth wrapped around the body over a skirt, and this style became more common after about 2370 BCE. The result was a dress tradition that, like Egypt’s, relied more on wrapping than on fitted sewing. Mesopotamian elite dress could be rich in gold, stones, and ornament, while still remaining practical for a hot climate. Clothing here was a marker of power, craft, and civilization.

## The Aegean: Minoan and Mycenaean dress

The Aegean tradition begins with Crete, where Minoan culture developed into a sophisticated maritime civilization with wide contacts across the Mediterranean and the Middle East. Dress there was colorful, elegant, and lively. Men wore few garments, often a belted loincloth or short decorated skirt, while women wore long flounced skirts and short open jackets that left the breasts bare in some periods. Later women also wore bodices that shaped the torso. Aegean dress was unusually free, decorative, and expressive, especially in the way it gave women a prominent public presence and made fashion a sign of social vitality.

Mycenaean and later Aegean dress kept this taste for complexity but moved through changing forms and influences. The region was a bridge between East and West, so clothing drew from many cultural contacts while still developing a style of its own. The result was a fashion language that mixed ornament, fitted shaping, bright color, and useful draping. The Aegean world helped establish later European dress traditions while also showing that fashion could be both practical and highly imaginative.

## Ancient Greece

Ancient Greek dress first developed in a world of many related cultures, and it changed across the Archaic, Classical, and Hellenistic periods. Early Greek clothing was simple and based on wool cloth pinned and wrapped around the body. The influence of colder inland regions brought cloaks, leg coverings, and caps. In the Classical period, Greek dress became a refined draped style with little sewing. Men and women wore oblong pieces of fabric arranged in different ways, and the result was natural, unforced, and highly elegant. Greek clothing became one of the clearest examples of dress as an art of draping.

Greek clothing later became more varied and luxurious in the Hellenistic age, when trade brought in cotton and silk. The chief garments were the chiton, the chlamys, and the himation, each of which could be worn in several ways. Greek footwear included sandals, boots, and special caps for weather or travel. Hair, perfume, jewelry, and makeup were also important, especially for women. Greek dress expressed balance, simplicity, and personal refinement, while still allowing room for individual style and public display.

## Etruria

Etruscan dress developed in central Italy and shows strong connections with Greece, Egypt, Syria, and Crete. The Etruscans had bright colors, rich decoration, and abundant jewelry, especially in gold. They wore both draped garments and fitted, sewn ones, which means their clothing combined eastern and western habits. Greek items such as the chlamys, himation, and chiton appear beside garments with Middle Eastern-style turned-up toes on the shoes. Etruria became a cultural meeting place where dress blended influences instead of following one fixed model, and this blend gave clothing a rich hybrid character.

Some Etruscan garments also anticipated later Roman and Byzantine forms. The tebenna, a semicircular mantle, became an early version of the Roman toga, while a decorative Egyptian-derived collar pointed forward to later Byzantine ornament. These examples show how styles can travel across time and into new civilizations. Etruscan dress was therefore not just local costume; it was a source of forms that would remain important in the wider history of European fashion.

## Ancient Rome

Roman dress developed over more than a thousand years and changed as the empire expanded. Early Rome borrowed heavily from Greek and Etruscan culture, but as Roman power spread across many lands, clothing became richer, more varied, and more luxurious. Trade made silk, cotton, jewelry, and fine dyes more available to the wealthy. Clothing also reflected class divisions, with special colors and styles reserved for citizens and important people. Roman dress thus served as a visible system of rank, and it showed how empire could transform style into a sign of public authority.

The basic Roman masculine garment was the tunica, while the toga remained the most famous outer garment. Women wore the stola and palla, and later they used multiple layers of finer and more decorated cloth. Underwear, sandals, shoes, boots, wigs, hair switches, makeup, and beauty patches were also part of Roman dress. Roman fashion therefore combined practicality with display, and in its later years it became increasingly ornate. Rome became a culture in which clothing expressed both civic identity and luxury.

## Ancient nonclassical Europe

Before the influence of classical civilization, much of Europe relied on animal skins, hides, and fur. During the Stone Age, people held garments to the body with belts and pins, and later they used awls and stitching to make a closer fit. Archaeological finds show early knowledge of skin dressing, weaving, and sewing. In cold climates, these materials were essential, and clothing began as a response to climate before it became a matter of fashion. Even in this early world, however, people were already shaping material into recognizable forms of dress.

As Europe moved toward later historical periods, garments became more tailored and more varied, but the basic logic of protection remained important. Fashion rested on older practical habits. Furs, hides, and sewn skins were not elegant in the later sense, but they established the first durable relationship between the body, the environment, and the making of clothing. That relationship is one of the foundations of dress history.

## Native Americans

Native American dress differed widely by region, climate, and available materials. North of the Rio Grande, cloth was often limited, so many peoples relied on tanned skins, plant-fiber capes, and woven hats. Garments were frequently made from whole hides softened with animal oils and dyed in strong colors. Since metalworking was limited, people used bone or stone awls and sinew or fiber cordage to sew skins together. Decoration was often highly skilled, especially in porcupine-quill embroidery and fringed edges. Clothing here was closely tied to the natural world and to local craft traditions, and it often carried strong visual meaning.

Actual garment types also differed widely. Depending on climate, men might wear breechclouts, tunics, leggings, or cloaks, while women wore skirts or long dresses with leggings. Hair styling was also important and often dramatic. Native American clothing was not simple or undeveloped; rather, it was highly adapted to place, social role, and ornament. Dress in this context was practical, beautiful, and culturally expressive at the same time, making it a clear example of regional ingenuity.

## The Eskimo

Eskimo clothing, including Inuit, Yupik/Yupiit, and Aleut dress, was designed for Arctic cold. It was made from animal skins that were carefully sewn and tailored so that wind could not penetrate. Fur was often worn on the inside, and seal skin was especially useful for boots and waterproof garments. People used bone or ivory needles and animal sinew thread. These techniques show a practical and highly specialized use of local resources, with clothing serving first as survival equipment and then as a refined tradition of craft.

Men and women wore similar basic forms: a hooded tunic or coat, trousers, and boots. Some garment names, such as parka and anorak, later became part of modern dress vocabulary. Comparisons with Aztec, Maya, and Inca dress show how different peoples adapted clothing to climate and environment in distinct ways. Arctic dress was a direct answer to weather, movement, and the need for warmth, with beauty appearing through function and precision.

## The Aztec, the Maya, and the Inca

The Aztec, Maya, and Inca represent major dress traditions in Central and South America. The Aztec wore cotton loincloths for men and tunics or skirts for women, while rank was marked by ornamental cloaks and abundant jewelry, especially gold. Feathers were a major decorative element, and the quetzal plume was especially valued. The Maya also used few garments, but they developed skilled weaving and feather decoration. In both cultures, cloth, color, and feathers helped define social position and ceremonial presence, making dress a clear sign of rank.

The Inca, based in the Andes, wore brightly colored garments and used llama wool and animal fur. Some cloaks were made entirely of feathers woven into cotton. Their clothing was simple in shape but rich in surface effect: loincloths, tunics, dresses, ponchos, sandals, and caps or turbans were common. These civilizations used available materials with great skill and turned clothing into a visible language of empire, ceremony, and ornament.

## The Middle East from the 6th century

From the 6th century onward, dress in the Middle East remained remarkably stable because it suited the climate and because traditional clothing had strong cultural support. Loose garments protected the body from heat, dust, and sun, and many styles covered most of the body. Common garments included a wide mantle, the burnous, and especially the full trousers known by forms such as chalvar, chalwar, or salvar. These trousers were practical for work and movement, and they became part of the dress of both men and women in several areas. The style therefore joined comfort, modesty, and daily usefulness.

Women’s clothing also included veiling and enveloping outer garments, while men often wore the kaffiyeh, sometimes with modern business dress. Middle Eastern dress has been shaped by climate, religion, and custom over a long period. Older garments could remain meaningful even as they entered new settings. In this tradition, dress is both conservative and adaptable, with continuity itself becoming a form of identity.

## The Byzantine Empire

The Byzantine Empire inherited Roman dress but transformed it through its position between East and West. Constantinople became a major center of trade, wealth, and artistic influence, and this prosperity showed clearly in clothing. Early Byzantine dress still followed the draped Roman tradition, but Persian and Anatolian influences introduced closer-fitting garments, richer ornament, and more elaborate jewelry. The empire also benefited from silk production after sericulture was established in Constantinople. Byzantine dress was splendid, courtly, and highly international, with fashion acting as a sign of imperial magnificence.

Two periods of wealth were especially important, above all the time of Justinian I. During those years, luxury fabrics from Asia, Syria, and Egypt were widely used by the ruling classes, and dress became famous for splendor. The Byzantine court therefore stood at the meeting point of older Roman forms and eastern richness. Byzantine clothing helped carry forward the dress traditions of the Mediterranean while also showing how empire could create a new and highly decorative style of rule.

## Medieval Europe

After the fall of the western Roman Empire, European dress changed slowly and stayed fairly standardized for centuries. Men and women wore similar basic layers: underwear, tunics, and hose. Clothing was sewn but still loose and relatively simple. Fabrics were generally linen and wool, and decoration was usually limited to embroidered bands at the edges. This period was one of continuity, where clothing was practical, modest, and shaped by a society that changed less quickly than later fashion worlds. The result was a long phase of relatively steady everyday dress.

Later medieval dress became more fitted and more fashionable. Men wore shorter tunics, close-fitting hose, and eventually codpieces, while women wore lower necklines, fitted bodices, fuller skirts, and sideless surcoats. New decorative practices, including parti-coloring and fur trimming, increased the visual variety of dress. Head coverings such as the wimple and veil remained important for women. Medieval Europe was the stage on which tailored clothing and social fashion began to develop more clearly as distinct habits.

## Europe, 1500–1800

Between 1500 and 1800, European dress changed in step with expansion, trade, the Renaissance, and the growth of class distinctions. Wealthy centers led fashion, first in Italy, then in Germany and Flanders, and later in Spain and France. Early 16th-century styles used slashed garments, bright fabrics, and decorative sleeves, while later fashions became more structured and more heavily shaped by court taste. Clothing increasingly reflected the rise of powerful states and the desire of elites to display refinement, making fashion a tool of social competition.

In the 18th century, French influence became dominant. Men’s dress gradually became darker and less decorative, while women’s fashion became the main arena of display. The period also saw the rise of dressmaking, tailoring, millinery, and wig making as organized trades. Toward the end of the 18th century, more comfortable Neoclassical styles appeared, influenced by ideas of antiquity and by changing attitudes toward class and display. This long period formed a crucial bridge between court costume and modern fashion, where style change accelerated and spread more widely.

## Colonial America

Colonial America inherited dress habits from Europe but adapted them to local climate and social conditions. Spanish settlers in the south often kept their own styles, while settlers in colder regions needed heavier furs and stronger materials. Many colonists tried to preserve class differences through sumptuary laws, and dress became a clear sign of Puritan or Cavalier identity. At the same time, practical concerns shaped everyday clothing, since people had to make much of what they wore from local flax, wool, and skins. Dress in colonial America therefore mixed old world status codes with new world necessity.

Working clothing was especially different from European fashion because it had to be durable and warm. Men wore deerskin and buckskin garments, and women wore plain, practical garments over homespun petticoats and aprons. Wealthier people still tried to maintain elegant dress, but ordinary people often kept older styles in use longer than European elites did. Colonial American dress expressed ideology, class, and survival all at once, with plainness often standing for moral seriousness.

## The Ottoman Empire

Ottoman dress emerged from the meeting of Turkish and Byzantine traditions. The Turks brought caftans and trousers, while the Byzantines contributed silks, gold cloth, and embroidery. Over time the Ottoman court became famous for its rich clothing, and by the 16th century rulers tried to limit luxury through sumptuary laws. In the empire, dress moved between prestige and regulation, with fabric and ornament carrying political meaning. Clothing here reflected both imperial ambition and the control of elite display.

For the general population, Ottoman dress changed slowly. Men wore shirts, baggy trousers, jackets, and caftans, while women wore long chemises, drawers, trousers, and veils or cloaks when outside. The tarboosh or fez became a strong symbol of Turkish identity. Even after European styles began to enter the empire, traditional clothing remained important in cities and in the countryside. The Ottoman case shows how dress can preserve continuity while still absorbing outside influence and modern reform, especially when state policy is involved.

## The early 19th century

The early 19th century brought a sharper division between men’s and women’s dress. Men’s clothing became more standardized, with the tailcoat, waistcoat, and close trousers becoming the normal formal style. Etiquette now regulated what should be worn for different occasions, times of day, and social groups. This was the period when English styles gained wide authority, and fashion for men became more sober and stable. Masculine dress moved toward restraint and regularity, while still preserving clear signals of respectability.

Women’s clothing changed in a different direction. Neoclassical gowns with high waists and pale colors were followed by Romantic styles with fuller skirts, tighter waists, and larger sleeves. The period also saw more attention to children’s clothes, including the skeleton suit for boys. The early 19th century was a transitional moment when old aristocratic habits remained visible but modern fashion systems were beginning to form. It is a period where clothing starts to separate more clearly into categories of age, class, and gendered appearance.

## Post-World War II

After World War II, dress became far less controlled by formal etiquette. People increasingly chose clothing according to personal taste, and advertising made fashion accessible to a much wider public. Synthetic fibers changed production, because they allowed easier care, cheaper manufacturing, and faster turnover. Nylon stockings, stretch fabrics, and other innovations made clothing more convenient and more widely available. This period made dress more democratic in access, even as fashion remained a major business of mass consumption.

Men’s clothing became more casual, while women’s fashion continued to shift between femininity and practicality. The New Look restored a full skirt and narrow waist, but later decades moved toward youth styles linked to music and subcultures such as punk, hip-hop, grunge, and goth. Teenagers became important fashion leaders because they had money, leisure, and a desire to reject older norms. In this period, dress is shown as a fast-moving expression of identity, rebellion, and popular culture.

## The history of Eastern dress

Eastern and southern Asian dress forms a broad regional story. Western-style clothing is now common in many cities, especially for work, but traditional dress remains important for weddings and ceremonies. Japan, China, India, Korea, and neighboring regions each developed their own forms while also influencing one another. Chinese dress had a particularly wide impact, and that influence can be seen in Korea and Japan over many centuries. Eastern dress traditions are therefore both local and interconnected, showing how regional identities can coexist with shared models.

## China

Chinese dress has one of the longest and most developed textile traditions in world history. Sericulture and silk production became highly advanced very early, and by the Han dynasty the main garment forms were already established. Men and women wore wide-sleeved robes, tunics, jackets, and trousers, with padded or fur-lined clothing used for cold weather. In China, weaving, dyeing, and silk use reached a very high level, and dress became closely tied to state order and craft excellence.

Imperial and official clothing used special colors, emblems, and garment forms to show rank. The Ming dynasty used bird and animal squares for officials and imperial symbols for rulers, while later the Qing replaced older styles with new ones. After 1949, communist rules changed clothing again, promoting simpler and more uniform dress. After Mao’s death, fashion became more varied as the economy loosened. China is a long-running example of dress controlled by empire, state, and social change, yet always shaped by the power of silk culture.

## Japan

Japanese dress developed from early two-piece garments seen in clay figures, with jackets and trousers for men and jackets with skirts for women. During the Nara period, court dress borrowed strongly from China, especially the long pao-style robe. Some imperial and court garments still preserve those Chinese roots today, though they have become distinctly Japanese in arrangement and symbolism. Japan adopted foreign forms but transformed them into a stable court tradition of its own, with ceremonial dress expressing continuity and rank.

The most important Japanese court costumes are the sokutai for the emperor and the juni-hitoe for the empress. These outfits use layered robes, long trains, special headdresses, and emblematic colors and birds. Traditional clothing remained important even after western clothes became common in daily life. Japanese dress therefore represents a blend of borrowed forms, local refinement, and strong ceremonial meaning. It is one of the clearest examples of dress functioning as ritual identity.

## Korea

Korean dress developed from early jackets and trousers into the more familiar chogori and skirt combination. Chinese influence became important during the Three Kingdoms period, especially among the nobility and in court wear. Court costumes modeled after Tang China introduced wider sleeves, longer garments, and new overcoats. Later, Mongol rule changed the clothing of the court again, and Chinese influence returned in the shape of women’s pleated skirts and shorter jackets. Korean dress became a long conversation between local habit and outside influence.

By the 15th century, women were shortening the chogori until it reached the modern form just covering the breast. The noblewoman’s full-length skirt signaled status, while lower classes kept earlier practical clothing longer. White clothing also became a long-standing Korean preference. Korean dress shows how clothing can change slowly while preserving its basic structure. In Korea, clothing served as a visible marker of social standing, court culture, and historical memory.

## South Asia

South Asian dress covers many peoples and traditions. Ancient Indian sculpture shows men and women wearing cloth wrapped around the hips to form the dhoti and sari. Upper bodies were often bare in early forms, and turbans, scarves, and jewelry were common. After Muslim conquest in the 12th century, new garments such as the jamah, wide trousers, and long coats became important in the north and center of the subcontinent. South Asian dress can vary with religion, region, and class, yet still retain strong traditional forms of continuity.

Women’s dress also changed under Muslim influence, with longer garments and more coverage, while many older forms continued in modified shape. Some styles spread beyond India into other parts of Southeast Asia. The large number of castes and the diversity of ornament and clothing mean that no single description can cover the whole region. Still, the basic point is clear: South Asian dress is shaped by long custom, climate, religion, and status, all of which give clothing a deep cultural function.

## The nature and purposes of dress

Dress serves many purposes. It protects the body from weather, but it can also express religious meaning, social identity, occupation, age, sex, and attractiveness. Some garments are beautiful even when they are not useful, and some can even harm the wearer. Fashion exists because people change clothes for reasons beyond simple wear and tear. In this sense, dress is never only material; it is also a form of communication and self-presentation.
Fashion often develops where social classes can imitate one another. In more stable societies, styles change less quickly, but in modern economies dress shifts rapidly as elites move first and others follow. Technology, communication, and manufacturing speed up this process. Dress is therefore a social system tied to power, class movement, and the desire to be noticed. Clothing is not only what people wear; it is a way of making meaning through visible choice.

## Rebellion

Rebellion in dress has long been one of the most striking themes in costume history. People rebel by shocking others, rejecting tradition, or resisting styles that they think have become stale. One recurring form of rebellion is women wearing male clothing, especially in war or public action. Historical examples include rulers and noblewomen who adopted armor or other masculine attire. Dress in these cases becomes a challenge to accepted roles and a claim to new kinds of freedom.
Rebellion can also bring punishment, as in the case of Joan of Arc, whose male clothing was used against her in court. Later centuries saw rebellious dress in art, in youth culture, and in styles meant to reject established norms. What rebels often seek is not only attention but also a break with authority and convention. Clothing becomes a means of protest, identity, and social transformation.

## Exotica

The adoption of foreign dress elements has been another constant theme in the history of clothing. Silk from China was one of the earliest and most important imports into the West, and caftans spread from Central Asia through the Middle East into Europe. The Japanese kimono also entered western dress as a garment for private leisure. These borrowed forms often changed meaning when they crossed borders, becoming signs of novelty, comfort, or luxury. Exotica is a key force in fashion, because what is foreign can become deeply attractive and prestigious.

Foreign styles can move in many directions. Ottoman, Russian, English, French, and Italian dress all absorbed outside influences at different times. Exotic clothing was sometimes worn for pleasure, sometimes for display, and sometimes as a way of seeming modern or unusual. Fashion often advances by borrowing, not just by inventing. In this way, exotica reveals the global life of dress forms.

## Key Events & Developments

- historical societies: Dress develops as a marker of rank, gender, ritual, and group identity.
- 18th and 19th centuries: Fashion systems and urban trade make dress change faster and more public.
- modern period: Mass production and global fashion turn dress into both a personal and commercial language.
