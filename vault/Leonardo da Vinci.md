# Leonardo da Vinci

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> Leonardo da Vinci was an Italian Renaissance painter, draftsman, sculptor, architect, engineer, and investigator whose works and notebooks joined art with observation and mechanical inquiry. The Last Supper and Mona Lisa exemplify his influence on painting, while his studies anticipated later science and engineering.

## Introduction

Leonardo da Vinci, born near Vinci in 1452 and dead at Cloux in 1519, was an Italian painter, draftsman, sculptor, architect, and engineer. His genius came to stand for the Renaissance humanist ideal because he joined art, observation, mechanical invention, and scientific inquiry.

His Last Supper ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Last_Supper_%28Leonardo%29)) and Mona Lisa ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mona_Lisa)) became among the most influential paintings of the Renaissance. His notebooks show a mind centuries ahead in scientific curiosity and mechanics, guided by the idea of saper vedere, or knowing how to see.

## Life and Early Career

![[attachments/leonardo_da_vinci_002.webp|420]]

*Lady with an Ermine - Leonardo da Vinci - Google Art Project*

The self-portrait in chalk, dated about 1512 and kept in the Palazzo Reale at Turin, belongs to the image of Leonardo as a master of observation. His belief that sight was the highest sense shaped his work as painter, draftsman, scientist, and teacher.

Leonardo was born to unmarried parents, Ser Piero, a Florentine notary and landlord, and Caterina, a peasant woman. Around age 15 he entered the workshop of Andrea del Verrocchio, where he learned painting, sculpture, and technical-mechanical arts.

Accepted into the Florence painters' guild in 1472, Leonardo worked in Florence until 1481. His early drawings already included pumps, weapons, and mechanical devices, showing that technical inquiry stood beside art from the start.

In 1482, Leonardo moved to [[Milan]] to serve Ludovico Sforza. For 17 years he worked as painter, sculptor, festival designer, architectural adviser, fortification consultant, military engineer, hydraulic engineer, and mechanical adviser.

During the first Milanese period he painted The Virgin of the Rocks, worked on The Last Supper, decorated the Sala delle Asse, and spent 12 years on a huge equestrian monument to Francesco Sforza. War diverted the bronze to cannons, and the clay model was ruined.

## Workshop and Later Years

Leonardo's Milan workshop included pupils and associates such as Giovanni Antonio Boltraffio, Ambrogio de Predis, Bernardino de' Conti, Francesco Napoletano, Andrea Solari, Marco d'Oggiono, and Salai. Their roles remain difficult to define because some works connected with Leonardo involved collaboration.

After the French entered Milan in 1499, Leonardo left with Luca Pacioli, visited Mantua and Venice, then returned to Florence. He advised Venice on flooding a threatened region, studied mathematics, and later served Cesare Borgia as military architect and engineer, producing city plans and topographical maps.

Back in Florence in 1503, Leonardo studied plans to divert the Arno River behind Pisa and imagined a canal linking Florence to the sea. He also began the huge unfinished Battle of Anghiari and painted the Mona Lisa ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mona_Lisa)) during the same Florentine years.

His second Milanese period began after Charles d'Amboise requested him in 1506. Leonardo advised on architecture, canals, and palace projects, gathered pupils including Francesco Melzi, and began another unrealized equestrian monument for Gian Giacomo Trivulzio.

From 1513, Leonardo lived in Rome under Giuliano de' Medici, but received no major commission despite the activity of Bramante, Raphael, and [[Michelangelo]]. In 1516, he accepted Francis I's invitation to France, where he was honored as first painter, architect, and engineer to the king.

At Cloux, Leonardo planned the palace and garden of Romorantin, arranged scientific papers, worked on painting and anatomy treatises, and created Deluge drawings that imagined nature's destructive forces. He died in 1519, and Melzi inherited his artistic and scientific estate.

## The Last Supper

![[attachments/leonardo_da_vinci_003.webp|420]]

*Da Vinci Vitruve Luc Viatour*

The Last Supper, painted in 1495-98 for Santa Maria delle Grazie in Milan, presents the tense moment when Jesus says one apostle will betray him. Leonardo contrasts the agitation of the 12 disciples with Christ's calm isolation and Judas' hidden guilt.

The painting became a model of dramatic expression, composition, gesture, and narrative clarity, admired by artists such as Rubens, [[Rembrandt]], and [[Goethe]]. Its fame spread through reproductions, especially Raffaello Morghen's print of 1800.

Technically, the work suffered because Leonardo rejected true fresco and used tempera on a wall base of his own making. Deterioration began early, bad restorations worsened damage, and a major restoration from 1980 to 1999 revealed brilliance but little original paint.

Leonardo's notebooks began forming a vast program between 1490 and 1495. They covered painting, architecture, mechanics, anatomy, geology, hydrology, botany, air, and the visible cosmos, all rooted in experience and observation rather than book speculation.

The notebooks survive in thousands of illustrated pages, including codices, the Codex Atlanticus, Windsor materials, and the Arundel Manuscript. Leonardo's mirror writing was not secret code but a natural left-handed script, and his drawings often carried the main teaching while words explained the image.

During the Florentine years, the Mona Lisa ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mona_Lisa)), Virgin and Child with St. Anne, and Battle of Anghiari strengthened his fame. The Mona Lisa joined sitter and landscape through sfumato and harmony, while Battle of Anghiari used anatomy, movement, and mechanical ideas to make battle timeless.

After 1507, Leonardo painted little. His late works and drawings, including Leda, St. John the Baptist, Pointing Lady, and Visions of the End of the World, explored sinuous figures, ambiguous smiles, light from darkness, and cosmic forces of destruction and order.

## Sculpture

![[attachments/leonardo_da_vinci_001.webp|420]]

*Da Vinci Studies of Embryos Luc Viatour*

[[The Art of Sculpture (Sculpture)|Sculpture]] interested Leonardo from youth, but no secure youthful sculptural works survive. His two great projects, the Sforza equestrian monument and the Trivulzio tomb monument, were never completed, leaving drawings as the main evidence of his sculptural imagination.

The Madrid Codices, found in 1965 in the National Library of Madrid, revealed his technical knowledge of bronze casting and the near-utopian ambition of casting a huge horse in one piece. His horse studies influenced 16th-century equestrian sculpture even as projects failed.

A small bronze horseman in [[Budapest]] is close to Leonardo's style or immediate circle, and Giovanni Francesco Rustici showed his influence in bronze sculpture for the Florence Baptistery. Leonardo's sculptural thinking also fed back into drawing and painting through motion and volume.

## Architecture

Leonardo described himself to Ludovico Sforza as an architect, military engineer, and hydraulic engineer, but his architectural role was mostly advisory. He considered work on the Milan cathedral cupola, sketched palaces and villas, and designed the large unbuilt Romorantin palace-garden project.

His architectural studies ranged across urban planning, sacred and secular buildings, domes, steps, portals, windows, construction theory, and form. Through contact with Bramante, Francesco di Giorgio, Giuliano da Sangallo, Giovanni Antonio Amadeo, and Luca Fancelli, he registered the formation of High Renaissance architecture.

## Science

Leonardo's science of painting argued that painting belonged among the liberal arts because it communicated knowledge through the eye. In his paragone comparisons, he claimed painting surpassed other arts by imitating visible nature and requiring mastery of optics, mathematics, perspective, light, and shadow.

His anatomical work began from artistic need but became independent research by the 1490s. He dissected about 30 corpses, studied skeleton, muscles, organs, brain, heart, and lungs, and created drawings that used transparent layers, sections, dotted hidden parts, and hatching to explain the body.

The Vitruvian Man applied geometry to human proportion, placing the body within square and circle. Leonardo saw the human body as a microcosm, comparing skeleton to rocks, breath to ocean movement, and the body's elements to the earth's composition.

His [[Mechanics - Energy, Forces, and Their Effects|mechanics]] studied force, motion, friction, resistance, gears, screws, jacks, transmission, fortifications, canals, water, plants, geology, air, and flight. Designs such as a moving fortress, differential transmission, flying machine, and helical airscrew showed inventive brilliance but often lacked adequate power sources.

Leonardo's art and science formed one visual empiricism. He believed the artist could approach nature's secrets through observation and graphic representation, creating a modern form of cognition based on experience, even when his encyclopedic ambitions remained unfinished.

## Surviving Manuscripts

The Biblioteca Ambrosiana in Milan preserves important Leonardo material, including the Codex Atlanticus and drawings such as the nude study mentioned with the anatomical discussion. These collections show how his manuscripts combined text, diagrams, and visual demonstration.
