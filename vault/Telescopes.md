## Introduction

![[attachments/telescopes_001.webp|420]]

*Galileo telescope*

Telescopes ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Telescope)) are devices used to form magnified images of distant objects. They are the most important investigative tools in astronomy because they collect and analyze radiation from celestial bodies, including objects at the farthest reaches of the universe.

[[Galileo]] transformed astronomy in the early 17th century by applying the telescope to celestial study. Since then, optical telescopes have grown more powerful, and instruments have been developed to detect radiation across the electromagnetic spectrum.

Auxiliary tools such as cameras, spectrographs, photomultiplier tubes, charge-coupled devices, computers, rockets, and spacecraft greatly expanded observational power and helped transform knowledge of the solar system, the Milky Way, and the wider universe.

## Refracting Telescopes

Refractors use lenses to bend, or refract, light. They are often used to observe the Moon, planets such as Jupiter and Mars, and binary stars. The main light-gathering lens is the objective, and the viewing lens is the eyepiece.

The diameter of the objective is the aperture. Small spotting telescopes may have apertures of a few centimetres, while the largest refractors reach about one metre.

Refractors can suffer from chromatic aberration ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chromatic_aberration)), in which different colours do not focus at the same point. Adding lens components and matching glasses with different expansion properties can reduce distortion caused by colour and temperature changes.

Magnification is calculated by dividing the focal length of the objective by the focal length of the eyepiece. High magnification helps with the Moon and planets but gives little advantage for stars, which remain point sources because of their distance.

High magnification also requires a stable mounting. Even slight movement can blur images, so telescope mounts are as important as optical power.

## Reflecting Telescopes

![[attachments/telescopes_002.png|420]]

*Newton reflecting telescope diagram*

Reflectors use a primary mirror, usually concave spherical or parabolic, to reflect light to a focus. The mirror's front surface is coated with a thin metal film such as aluminum, while the back provides shape and support.

Reflectors avoid chromatic aberration ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chromatic_aberration)) because reflected light does not disperse by wavelength. They also have shorter tubes than refractors of the same diameter, reducing tube and dome costs.

In a Newtonian reflector, a small flat secondary mirror redirects light to the side of the tube. This solves the problem of observing at the prime focus, where the observer would otherwise block incoming light.

The Cassegrain design, invented by Laurent Cassegrain, uses a small convex secondary mirror to send light back through a hole in the primary mirror to a focus behind it. This allows a short tube relative to mirror diameter.

The Gregorian design, invented by James Gregory, uses a concave secondary mirror beyond the prime focus to send light back through the primary. A Gregorian version was used for the Solar Maximum Mission space observatory launched in 1980.

Large reflectors may have cages at the prime focus so observers can sit within the telescope tube. The 5-metre Palomar reflector used this arrangement. The Gran Telescopio Canarias uses an altitude-azimuth mount rather than a traditional equatorial mount.

## Special Optical Telescopes

Solar telescopes may be refractors or reflectors, but specialized solar instruments are built for studying sunspots, prominences, the corona, and individual wavelengths. Tower solar telescopes use long-focus objectives to give a large image scale.

A coelostat is an equatorially mounted plane mirror that directs sunlight into a tower telescope objective. Examples of tower solar telescopes exist at Mount Wilson Observatory and McMath-Hulbert Observatory.

The coronagraph, built by Bernard Lyot in 1930, photographs the Sun's corona outside total eclipses. It works best at high altitude, where scattered sunlight is reduced. Coronagraphs are also used on satellites studying the Sun.

Space telescopes observe above Earth's atmosphere, avoiding atmospheric distortion and reaching radiation blocked from the ground. Orbiting astronomical observatories, rockets, and spacecraft expanded astronomy beyond visible light.

Prismatic astrolabes are small telescopic instruments used to determine precise positions of stars and planets, and sometimes the observer's latitude and longitude. They use a prism and mercury reflection to compare direct and reflected images.

## Historical Development

[[Galileo]] developed astronomical telescopes in 1609. His largest instrument was only about 120 cm long with a 5 cm objective, but he used it to observe lunar mountains, Venus's phases, and the four largest moons of Jupiter.

Isaac Newton developed the reflecting telescope in 1668. John Gregory had conceived a reflector design in 1663, and Laurent Cassegrain introduced another reflector design in 1672.

In the 18th century, William Herschel made a major advance with large reflectors. His 12-metre focal-length telescope with a 120-cm mirror helped lay observational foundations for understanding extragalactic nebulae, or galaxies beyond the Milky Way.

In the 19th century, William Parsons, 3rd earl of Rosse, built a 185-cm reflector in Ireland with a focal length of about 16 metres. It remained the world's largest telescope for 75 years and was used to study nebulae and star clusters.

William Lassell built important reflectors, including a large instrument on Malta. Reflector development continued as mirror materials, mountings, domes, and optical surfaces improved.

## Auxiliary Instruments

Cameras ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Camera)) changed astronomy by creating permanent records and by integrating light over long exposures, revealing objects too faint for visual observation. John Draper photographed the Moon in 1840, Fizeau and Foucault photographed the Sun in 1845, and Harvard astronomers photographed stars by 1850.

Photographic plates used silver compounds that changed chemically when struck by light. Since the 1980s, charge-coupled devices, or CCDs, have largely replaced photographic plates for astronomical imaging.

Spectrographs split light into spectra, allowing astronomers to study chemical composition, temperature, motion, and physical conditions. Joseph von Fraunhofer discovered solar spectrum lines in 1814, laying the basis for spectroscopy.

Bright spectral lines often show glowing gas emitting at characteristic wavelengths; dark lines often show cooler gas absorbing those wavelengths. The Doppler effect, first noted in 1842 by Christian Johann Doppler, shifts lines toward blue for approaching objects and red for receding objects.

Photomultiplier tubes, CCDs, and charge injection devices electronically detect faint light. These detectors greatly improved sensitivity, precision, and data handling compared with visual observation alone.

## Computers, Rockets, and Spacecraft

![[attachments/telescopes_003.webp|420]]

*Hubble Space Telescope image of interacting galaxies*

[[Computers]] revolutionized telescopes by automating observation, pointing, timing, data collection, and analysis. Astronomers can identify a target while sensors, clocks, and computer controls handle much of the observing sequence.

Computer processing allows data analysis that once might have taken a lifetime to be done in hours or minutes. Optical disc storage and later digital systems let astronomers store and retrieve large bodies of observations.

Rockets and spacecraft opened access to [[Radiation|radiation]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radiation)) blocked by Earth's atmosphere. Sounding rockets in the late 1940s reached the upper atmosphere, and multistage rockets launched during the International Geophysical Year beginning in 1957 carried artificial satellites and instruments.

Space-based telescopes made it possible to study ultraviolet, X-ray, gamma-ray, and other radiation sources that ground-based optical telescopes cannot fully access.

## Core Equations

Physical models often begin by relating force, energy, waves, and conserved quantities:

$$\vec{F}=m\vec{a}$$

This is Newton's second law: force is the product of mass and acceleration, so a larger mass or a faster change in motion requires a larger net force.

$$E=h\nu$$

This relation says that photon energy is proportional to frequency; higher-frequency radiation carries more energy per photon.

$$E=mc^2$$

This expresses mass-energy equivalence: mass is a form of energy, with the speed of light squared as the conversion factor.

- Coronagraphs let astronomers study the solar corona without waiting for an eclipse.
