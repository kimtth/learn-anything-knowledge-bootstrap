## Introduction

![[attachments/sponges_003.webp|420]]

*Honeycomb sponge specimen*

Sponges ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sponge)) are primitive multicellular aquatic animals of the phylum Porifera. About 5,000 species are described, and they live mostly in the sea, attached to surfaces from the intertidal zone to depths of 8,500 metres or more. Only one family, Spongillidae, lives in fresh water; about 98 percent of sponge species are marine.

Adult sponges lack a definite [[Nerves and Nervous Systems (Nervous System)|nervous system]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nervous_system)), muscles, organs, and conspicuous movement. Early naturalists treated them as plants because of their branching forms and stillness. Their animal nature was confirmed in the 18th century after observations of water currents and changes in body openings.

Sponges are distinct from other animals in structure, function, and development. Some zoologists placed them in Parazoa, an isolated animal subkingdom, but molecular evidence suggests that sponges and more complex animals came from a common ancestor. The phylum is divided mainly into Calcarea, Hexactinellida, and Demospongiae.

## Importance

Certain Demospongiae ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Demosponge)), including Spongia officinalis and related species, have been used as natural household sponges since antiquity. Greeks and Romans used them for applying paint, mopping, and even as soldiers' drinking-vessel substitutes. Burned sponge was once credited with medical value.

Natural sponges are now mostly used in arts, crafts, pottery, jewelry making, painting, decorating, and surgical medicine, while synthetic sponges have replaced them for ordinary household use. A commercial sponge is the cleaned spongin skeleton, left after living cells are removed.

Sponges ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sponge)) also matter scientifically. Their cells do not form tissues or organs like those of other animals, and their ability to regenerate is exceptional. They contain unusual biochemical compounds, including antibiotics, sterols, pigments, and halogen-containing spongins.

## Regeneration

Regeneration in sponges is extraordinary. A sponge can restore damaged parts, and in some cases a complete adult can re-form from fragments or even dissociated cells. Cells can be separated mechanically, such as by squeezing through fine cloth, or chemically by changing seawater chemistry.

Dissociated cells settle, migrate, and form active aggregates. Archaeocytes play an important role, while pinacocytes form a covering in the diamorph stage. Choanocyte chambers and canals then reappear, producing a young functional sponge.

This reconstitution process is not the same as embryonic development. Existing cell types sort and rearrange rather than simply differentiating from primitive cells. Regeneration is important for studying cell recognition, adhesion, movement, sorting, and the problem of defining sponge individuality.

During unfavourable conditions, sponges may reduce to small fragments of archaeocytes covered by pinacocytes. When conditions improve, complete sponges can form again. Because cells migrate and no brain coordinates the body, zoologists define a sponge individual practically as a mass surrounded by a common cellular covering.

## Ecology

Most Porifera are sensitive to ecological factors and are difficult to raise in laboratories. Few species tolerate long exposure to air or wide variation in light, temperature, and salinity. Light, water movement, substrate, competition, and symbiosis all influence their habitats.

## Habitats

Many littoral sponges live in caves, shaded walls, crevices, or other protected places. Some tropical species live in shallow bright water. In these zones, symbiotic algae may protect sponges by depositing pigments in surface cells, and sponge colour may depend on the number of algal symbionts.

Some clionid sponges bore into shells, corals, limestone, and other calcareous materials. They remove small chips of calcium carbonate, probably through chemical and mechanical action. Their boring weakens limestone breakwaters, coral reefs, and oyster shells.

Most sponges grow attached to hard or rocky surfaces, but some anchor to firm objects on sand, mud, or debris. Unattached forms are rare. Different species compete for surfaces, and several species living together may modify the local environment in ways that help a rich sponge population survive.

## Skeleton

The sponge skeleton is crucial for classification. It may be mineral, made of calcium carbonate or silica, or organic, made of spongin and other materials. Mineral skeletons are built from spicules, which may be scattered or united into fibres.

Calcareous spicules are characteristic of Calcarea and are made mainly of calcium carbonate. They may have one, three, or four rays. Siliceous spicules occur in Demospongiae and Hexactinellida and are made mostly of silicic acid with water, trace compounds, and an organic axial fibre.

Hexactinellida, or glass sponges, often have six-rayed spicules connected into a delicate network. After soft tissues disappear, the skeleton can have a glassy texture, as in the Venus basket, Euplectella. Some deep-sea forms attach to mud with large spicule stalks.

Demospongiae ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Demosponge)) may have siliceous spicules, spongin fibres, both, or little skeleton. Commercial bath sponges are valued for their soft elastic spongin frameworks. Some demosponges have interlocking desmas forming stony skeletons.

## Functional Features

![[attachments/sponges_002.webp|420]]

*Longitudinal section of a Grantia sponge*

Sponges are mainly filter feeders. They draw water through pores and canals, capture bacteria, microorganisms, and organic particles with choanocytes, and may absorb dissolved organic substances. Cladorhizid sponges are unusual carnivores that capture prey with many body hairs.

Food captured by choanocytes is transferred by specialized amoebocytes called thesocytes through the sponge body. Digestion is intracellular, occurring inside cells rather than in a gut. A sponge can process several litres of water per day despite lacking a nervous coordinating centre.

Water flow is driven by choanocyte flagella. Water enters through pores, moves through incurrent canals and choanocyte chambers, and exits through oscula. Flow is slow at the pores and faster at the oscula, so expelled water does not contaminate incoming water. Irritating substances can cause pores to close.

Sponges have no respiratory organs. Oxygen exchange occurs directly between cells and surrounding water. Waste products, including ammonia and other nitrogen compounds, leave through oscula and body surfaces. Amoebocytes can disintegrate in the mesohyl and release granules into canals.

## Coordination and Biochemistry

Sponges react slowly to stimuli, confirming the lack of a true [[Nerves and Nervous Systems (Nervous System)|nervous system]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nervous_system)). Handling, air exposure, low oxygen, harmful chemicals, or extreme temperatures can cause contraction or closure of oscula. Coordination is probably primitive and chemical, involving myocytes and pinacocyte contraction.

Sponges produce bioactive compounds, including antibiotic substances such as ectyonin. These may help select bacteria and microorganisms used as food. Sponges also contain unusual sterols, carotenoids, melanin, chlorophyll, phycoerythrin from algae or diet, and significant silicon, calcium, and metals.

Spongins are iodine- or bromine-containing scleroproteins similar to keratin in skin, claws, hair, and feathers. Different spongins vary in composition and structure. These biochemical traits make sponges important in comparative biology and natural-products research.

## Evolution and Paleontology

Sponges evolved in a way unlike most other animals. They probably arose from flagellated protozoans, though the exact group is uncertain. Choanocytes resemble choanoflagellate protozoans, but sponge development is more complex than that of choanoflagellate colonies.

The primitive structure of Porifera suggests affinities with colonial protozoans, but sponges are true animals with embryological stages and specialized cell types. Their fossil record and living diversity show a long independent history rather than a direct line to later complex animals.

## Classification

Taxonomists classify sponges mainly by skeleton architecture, spicule types, spicule shapes, and dimensions. Other characters include body shape, colour, consistency, surface texture, oscula distribution, cytology, embryology, ecology, distribution, and biochemical traits.

## Class Calcarea

Calcarea have skeletons of calcium carbonate spicules. They are usually small, live mainly in shallow marine waters, and may be vase-shaped, tubular, or irregular colonies. They include Calcinea and Calcaronea, with differences in larval type, choanocyte flagella, spicule structure, and water-current systems.

## Class Hexactinellida

Hexactinellida, or glass sponges, have six-rayed siliceous spicules and no spongin. They are exclusively marine and usually live in deeper waters from about 25 to 8,500 metres. They may attach firmly to hard surfaces or anchor in soft sediments. Euplectella is a famous example.

## Class Demospongiae

Demospongiae ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Demosponge)) are the largest and most widely distributed sponge class, containing about 4,200 species. Their skeletons may have one- or four-rayed siliceous spicules, spongin fibres, both, or neither. They range from thin encrustations to huge cake-shaped bodies and include freshwater Spongillidae.

Some demosponges form stony lithistid skeletons through interlocking desmas. Cladorhizid carnivorous sponges lack the normal water-current system. Demospongiae include most commercial sponges and most known sponge diversity.

## Key Events & Developments

- 1755: The animal nature of sponges was first described.
- 1765: Water-current observations helped confirm that sponges are animals.
- Ancient times: Natural sponges were used in Greece and Rome for paint, cleaning, and other practical uses.
- Modern biology: Sponge regeneration became important for studying cell recognition, sorting, and adhesion.
- Modern classification: Embryology, cytology, skeletal structure, ecology, and molecular data refined sponge taxonomy.

## Key People

- Early naturalists: First debated whether sponges were plants or animals.
- E. Topsent: Developed an influential sponge classification later modified by C. Levi.
- C. Levi: Modified sponge classification using embryological evidence.
- Michele Sara: Associated with the scientific treatment of Porifera.

## Interesting Facts

- Sponges ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sponge)) have no true organs but can pump large volumes of water.
- Spongillidae are freshwater sponges, while almost all other sponges are marine.
- Venus basket glass sponges leave delicate siliceous skeletons after death.
- Regeneration can produce a functional sponge from fragments or dissociated cells.
- Clionid sponges bore into shells, coral, and limestone.
