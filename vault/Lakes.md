# Lakes

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> Lakes are inland bodies of standing or slowly moving water whose basins, sediments, chemistry, currents, waves, and biological communities are studied in limnology. Their geologic impermanence and links to rivers, wetlands, climate, and human use make them important components of hydrologic systems.

## Introduction

A lake is a relatively large body of slowly moving or standing water occupying an inland basin. Exact boundaries between lakes, ponds, swamps, marshes, rivers, and streams are not fixed, but rivers move faster, marshes and swamps contain more grasses, trees, or shrubs, and ponds are smaller than lakes.

Geologic lakes are temporary features because basins fill, drain, erode, or change. Lake study includes basins, sediments, chemistry, currents, waves, tides, hydrologic balance, and relations to the hydrologic cycle and inland water ecosystems.

## Occurrence

![[attachments/lakes_001.webp|420]]

*Gentau Pic du Midi Ossau*

Freshwater lakes hold only about 0.009 percent of all free water and less than 0.4 percent of continental fresh water, while saline lakes and inland seas add about 0.0075 percent. Even so, freshwater lakes contain more than 98 percent of the important surface waters directly available for human use.

Lake water is concentrated in relatively few large bodies. Lake Baikal, Lake Tanganyika, Lake Superior, and the Great Lakes hold enormous volumes. The Caspian Sea, sometimes treated as an inland sea rather than a lake, is the largest inland water body by area. North America, Africa, and Asia contain about 70 percent of the world's lake water.

Small lakes are countless and globally important. Canadian and northern lake districts show dense networks of small basins. Lakes also influence local climate because they store heat, cool air in summer, warm air in winter, add moisture, and create downwind snowbelts near large lakes.

## Lake Basins

Limnology is the study of lakes. Lake basins are often classified by the processes that form them: tectonism, volcanism, landslides, glaciation, solution, river action, wind, coastal building, organic accumulation, animal activity, meteorite impact, and human activity.

Tectonic basins form through crustal movement, uplift, subsidence, and faulting. Large ancient inland seas produced remnants such as the Caspian and Aral seas. Fault-block and graben basins produced major lakes such as Tahoe, Baikal, Tanganyika, and many East African Rift lakes.

Volcanic basins include crater lakes, maars, calderas, lava-flow basins, and volcanic dams. Crater Lake in Oregon is a deep caldera lake with a high rim. Lake Toba in Sumatra formed from volcanic collapse combined with tectonic structure and later volcanic activity.

Landslide dams can block drainage and produce lakes, though they may be temporary if water erodes the dam. Lake Sarez in the Pamirs is an example of a stable rockslide-dammed lake.

Glacial basins are the most abundant, especially in the Northern Hemisphere. Ice sheets and glaciers scoured rock, deepened valleys, left moraines, dammed water, created cirques, formed kettle lakes, and shaped long fjord and piedmont lakes. The Finger Lakes and the North American Great Lakes both reflect glacial action.

Fluvial and solution basins form through river processes, oxbows, levees, waterfalls, delta sediment, tidal sediment, and groundwater solution. Karst lakes develop where limestone dissolves, while gypsum and halite can also form solution basins.

Other lake basins arise from coastal barriers, organic plant dams, coral structures, wind erosion, windblown sand dams, beaver dams, mining, quarrying, artificial dams, and meteorite craters. Human-made lakes can rival large natural lakes in scale.

## Basin Form, Sediments, And Sedimentation

Lake morphology includes surface area, depth, volume, bottom shape, and bottom materials. Bathymetry is measured by sounding, now often with acoustic devices and sometimes with lasers from aircraft. Bottom topography affects navigation, currents, fish spawning, and ecology.

Lake sediments include clastic material, organic debris, chemical precipitates, or mixtures. Sediment cores preserve lake and climate history through ripple marks, pollen, fossils, chemicals, volcanic ash, industrial metals, and traces of human activity. This reconstruction is called paleolimnology.

Clastic sediments enter through rivers, shoreline erosion, waves, currents, and wind. Coarse materials settle near inflows and shores; finer silts, clays, and muds settle offshore. Colours and textures reveal organic matter, sulfides, calcium carbonate, chemistry, biology, and source areas.

Organic sediments come from plants and animals. Forna, afja, gyttja, peat, and sedges can accumulate rapidly in quiet lakes. Pollen analysis helps reconstruct older climates. Varved deposits record annual sediment cycles, especially in formerly glaciated regions.

Chemical precipitates include calcium, sodium, and magnesium carbonates, dolomite, gypsum, halite, and sulfate salts. Photosynthesis can raise pH and cause calcium carbonate precipitation. Saline lakes may contain huge gypsum or sodium-salt deposits, while alkaline lakes such as Mono Lake can still support specialized life.

Lake aging occurs as sediment accumulation makes basins shallower and allows attached plants to spread, sometimes accelerating extinction of the lake. Restoration efforts may include dredging sediments or removing nutrient-rich bottom waters.

## Lake Waters

Lake chemistry varies with basin rocks, erosion products, rainfall, human effluents, and atmospheric fallout. Salinity depends on dissolved ions such as sodium, potassium, magnesium, calcium, carbonate, silicate, sulfate, chloride, and halides. Evaporation concentrates salts, so inland seas and closed basins can become highly saline.

Nutrients and pollutants include phosphates, nitrates, silicates, heavy metals, mercury, lead, DDT, and other hydrocarbons. Sediment studies show that human disposal strongly affects these constituents. Atmospheric pollution also changes rainfall chemistry and adds nitrogen and other materials to lakes.

Oxygen is central to water quality. Photosynthesis releases oxygen, decay consumes it, and air-water exchange replenishes it. Cold-season mixing restores oxygen in many lakes, but ice cover, stratification, tropical conditions, or strong salinity gradients can leave deep waters anoxic. Biological and chemical oxygen demand measure the potential for oxygen depletion.

Carbon dioxide dissolves easily, forms carbonic acid, affects pH, and connects with carbonate and bicarbonate chemistry. In most lakes, the carbon dioxide system buffers pH between about 6 and 8. Volcanic lakes may be extremely acidic, while some natural lakes are highly alkaline.

Sulfates, nitrates, and phosphates shape productivity and pollution. Hydrogen sulfide forms under anaerobic conditions and gives some deoxygenated waters their unpleasant odour. Phosphate and nitrate are often limiting nutrients, so reducing them in municipal or industrial effluents is central to controlling eutrophication.

## Thermal Properties And Mixing

Water's density is unusual because pure water is densest near 4 C and ice is less dense than liquid water. This allows ice to float and permits liquid water to remain below ice unless lakes are shallow enough to freeze to the bottom.

Vertical mixing depends on temperature and density. Dimictic lakes, common in temperate regions, overturn twice each year. Warm monomictic lakes overturn once without cooling below 4 C, while cold monomictic lakes never warm above 4 C. Holomictic lakes mix completely at least once; meromictic lakes remain partly unmixed because salinity gradients block turnover.

Thermal stratification divides many lakes into an upper epilimnion, a thermocline, and a deeper hypolimnion. Wind mixes heat downward, the thermocline strengthens during warming seasons, and cooling plus convection eventually destroys it. Industrial waste heat can disturb this system and harm fish or stimulate unwanted algae and plankton.

Heat budgets include solar radiation, long-wave radiation, sensible heat exchange, evaporation, inflows, outflows, groundwater, bottom heat, wind-energy dissipation, and ice processes. Large lakes also require attention to heat moved by currents within the lake.

## Currents

Lake currents are driven by inflow and outflow, wind stress, pressure gradients, and density differences from temperature, sediment, or dissolved substances. Movements are usually turbulent and may be horizontal, vertical, or both.

Wind stress is often the most important external force. In large deep lakes, wind-driven currents interact with the Coriolis force; in smaller lakes, friction and hydraulic gradients may dominate. Coastal winds can cause upwelling when surface water is driven offshore and replaced by cooler deep water.

Internal waves form in stratified lakes where the epilimnion and hypolimnion move against each other. Langmuir circulation creates parallel surface streaks, sinking at convergence zones and upwelling between them, and can be an important organized mechanism for mixing heat downward.

Surface waves begin as capillary waves and grow into gravity waves as wind transfers energy. Wave height depends on wind speed, duration, and fetch, especially in large lakes. Waves reflect from walls, refract in shallow water, break into whitecaps, and produce surf at shorelines.

## Seiches

A seiche is a standing oscillation of lake water after wind stress or atmospheric pressure tilts the lake surface and then stops. Water flows back to restore level, overshoots, and oscillates until bottom friction and turbulence damp the motion.

Seiches may have one node or several harmonic nodes. Long narrow lakes exposed to winds along their length are especially likely to develop longitudinal seiches, while transverse seiches can occur across narrower dimensions. Lake Geneva was an early studied example, and Lake Erie has a long uninodal period.

## Key Events & Developments

- Pleistocene: Glaciation shaped many modern lake basins, including the Great Lakes.
- Miocene: Tectonic movement helped isolate large inland basins in Eurasia.
- 1967: Lake Hornborgasjon became a major Swedish restoration concern.
- 20th century: Modern sounding and sediment cores expanded lake history and bathymetry studies.

## Interesting Facts

- Lake Baikal and Lake Tanganyika are the two deepest lakes and occupy fault-basin systems.
- Freshwater lakes hold a tiny share of Earth's water but most usable surface fresh water.
- Lake-effect snow comes from heat and moisture exchanges between lakes and air.
- Meromictic lakes can keep deep waters permanently unmixed and oxygen-poor.
