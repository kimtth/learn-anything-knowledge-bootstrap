# Chordates

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> Chordates are animals united at some stage of development by a notochord, dorsal nerve cord, pharyngeal structures, and post-anal tail. The phylum includes fishes, amphibians, reptiles, birds, and mammals.

## Introduction

Chordates are members of the phylum Chordata, which includes the vertebrates, the most highly evolved animals, together with two other subphyla, the tunicates and cephalochordates; some classifications also add the hemichordates. At some stage of life a chordate shows a stiff dorsal rod, the notochord, along with a hollow dorsal nerve cord, gill slits opening from the pharynx, a tail extending behind the anus, and an endostyle or its derivative, though some of these features appear only in the embryo. The group is enormously diverse, ranging from tiny marine tunicates and lancelets to the largest vertebrates on Earth, yet all share a common developmental blueprint.

## General features

![[attachments/chordates_001.webp|420]]

*Anatomy diagram of a lancelet*

Tunicates are small marine animals, usually 1 to 5 cm long, that filter water through their gill slits and often form colonies up to 18 metres long; cephalochordates are 1- to 3-cm fishlike animals that swim by undulating the body and live buried in sand, while vertebrates range from tiny fish to whales, the largest animals ever to exist. Across the phylum the same systems recur in different forms: locomotion by tail, body undulation, or fins and limbs that became wings in birds; excretion by kidneys, with vertebrates keeping a low internal salt content that may point to a freshwater origin; and respiration by gills that also fed early forms, some gill supports later evolving into jaws while lungs became the main organs on land. The circulatory system drives blood through a ventral aorta to the gills and back through a dorsal aorta, with true capillaries only in vertebrates, and hormones come from glands such as the thyroid, a modified endostyle that secretes iodine-containing thyroxine.

## Evolution and paleontology

Many scientists hold that chordates originated more than 590 million years ago, well before the fossil record, since the earliest forms were soft-bodied and rarely fossilized. Doubtful fossils of all three subphyla appear in Cambrian rocks more than 505 million years old, and an extensive vertebrate record begins about 400 million years ago. Embryology places Chordata among the deuterostomes, alongside the hemichordates, echinoderms, and chaetognaths, and the hemichordates are probably the chordates' closest relatives.

## Classification

![[attachments/chordates_002.webp|420]]

*Branchiostoma lanceolatum lancelet specimen*

Chordata divides into three subphyla. The Tunicata include the sessile Ascidiacea (sea squirts), the free-swimming Appendicularia, and the colonial Thaliacea; the Cephalochordata are the lancelets; and the Vertebrata are the well-headed, often limbed line of fish, amphibians, reptiles, birds, and mammals. Modern systematics groups organisms by genealogy, so that a valid clade contains an ancestor and all its descendants, while a grade—such as the class Reptilia, since birds are really modified dinosaurs—does not, and such grades are increasingly questioned.

## Conclusion

Chordates form a structurally unified but evolutionarily broad phylum whose members differ greatly in complexity while sharing the same basic body plan.

## Key Events & Developments

- More than 590 million years ago: chordate origins likely precede the fossil record.

## Interesting Facts

- The notochord is the phylum’s namesake feature.
- Vertebrates are only one branch of the chordates.
- Tunicate larvae resemble the chordate body plan more clearly than adults do.
- The phylum includes the largest animals ever to exist, such as whales.
