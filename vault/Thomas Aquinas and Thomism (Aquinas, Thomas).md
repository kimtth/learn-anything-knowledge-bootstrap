## Introduction

Thomas Aquinas, also called Aquinas or Doctor Angelicus, was an Italian Dominican theologian and the foremost medieval Scholastic. He lived from 1224/25 to 1274 and was canonized in 1323.

Thomas systematized Latin theology through the Summa theologiae ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Summa_Theologica)) and Summa contra gentiles. He also wrote biblical commentaries, disputed questions, Aristotle commentaries, philosophical treatises, and eucharistic hymns.

His doctrinal system and later developments by followers are known as [[Thomas Aquinas and Thomism (Thomism)|Thomism]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomism)). The Roman Catholic Church has long recognized him as one of its greatest Western philosophers and theologians.

## Early Life

Thomas was born near Aquino in the Kingdom of Sicily to a family tied to the conflicts between emperor and pope. As a child he was placed at Monte Cassino, probably with family hopes that he might become abbot.

After imperial conflict forced him home, Thomas studied at the University of Naples ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Naples_Federico_II)). There he encountered Greek and Arabic philosophical and scientific works and chose to join the Dominicans, a new mendicant order.

His family opposed this choice and held him captive for about a year, but Thomas remained firm. In 1245 he went to Paris and studied at the Dominican convent of Saint-Jacques under Albertus Magnus.

## Paris and Aristotle

At Paris, Thomas met the central intellectual crisis of his age: the arrival of Aristotelian philosophy through Arabic and Latin scholarship. Many church authorities feared naturalism, rationalism, and threats to faith.

Thomas did not reject Aristotle. He studied him carefully and sought to integrate reason, nature, and Christian revelation. His work was not merely academic; it was part of a wider religious and cultural renewal.

As a Dominican, Thomas combined scholarship with evangelical mission. He was shaped by the mendicant world of Franciscan poverty, Dominican study, university debate, and the church's need to address new knowledge.

## Faith, Reason, and Theology

Thomas taught that [[Theology|theology]] is a science in the Aristotelian sense: it reasons from principles accepted by faith because they are revealed by God. Philosophy, by contrast, relies on natural reason alone.

He rejected the idea of two contradictory truths, one of faith and one of reason. Against radical Averroism, he argued that true reason and true revelation cannot finally conflict.

For Thomas, reason operates within faith but follows its own laws. The mystery of God can be expressed in human language and explored through ordered argument, though it remains grounded in revelation.

## Nature, Grace, and Providence

Thomas insisted on the real autonomy of nature. Created things have their own laws, forms, and actions. Recognizing this order does not weaken faith; it honours the wisdom of the Creator.

He avoided reducing nature to miracle or shadow. The natural world has its own integrity and can lead the mind toward God through rational inquiry.

His doctrine of Providence taught that God governs creation by enabling creatures to act according to their own natures. Human freedom is not destroyed by dependence on God; it is grounded in that relation.

Thomas also emphasized the unity of soul and body. Using Aristotle, he taught that the soul is the form of the body. Human beings stand at the horizon of the material and spiritual worlds.

## Later Life and Controversy

Thomas taught at Paris, served the papal Curia, worked in Italy, returned to Paris during the Averroist controversy, and later founded a Dominican house of studies at Naples.

In 1274, while travelling to the second Council of Lyons, he became ill and died at Fossanova. In 1277, some theses associated with him were condemned in Paris, showing how controversial his realism about nature and reason remained.

Later centuries praised him. He was named doctor of the church in 1567 and became a major symbol of orthodoxy during the modernist crisis of the 19th century.

## Key Works

- Summa theologiae ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Summa_Theologica)): Thomas's incomplete masterwork and systematic introduction to theology.
- Summa contra gentiles: a theological and philosophical defense of Christian truth.
- Commentaries on Aristotle: works on physics, soul, ethics, metaphysics, heavens, and logic.
- De ente et essentia: an early philosophical treatise on being and essence.
- Pange lingua and other hymns: liturgical poetry for Eucharistic devotion.

## Key People

- Thomas Aquinas ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Aquinas)): Dominican theologian who united Aristotelian philosophy with Christian doctrine.
- Albertus Magnus ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Albertus_Magnus)): Thomas's teacher and a major scholar of Aristotle.
- Averroes ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Averroes)) and Siger of Brabant ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siger_of_Brabant)): figures connected to the Parisian controversy over Aristotle and reason.
- Francis of Assisi ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Francis_of_Assisi)) and Dominic ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dominic)): founders whose movements shaped Thomas's mendicant religious world.

## Interesting Facts
