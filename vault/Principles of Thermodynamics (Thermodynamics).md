# Principles of Thermodynamics (Thermodynamics)

> [!abstract] Reference navigation

## Introduction

Thermodynamics ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thermodynamics)) is the science of heat, work, temperature, and energy. It studies how energy moves from place to place and changes form. Its key idea is that heat is a form of energy equivalent to a definite amount of mechanical work.

This equivalence became clear around 1798, when Count Rumford, or Sir Benjamin Thompson, observed that boring cannon barrels could generate apparently limitless heat and that the heat produced was proportional to mechanical work done. This observation became one foundation of thermodynamics.

Another pioneer was Sadi Carnot, who in 1824 introduced the heat-engine cycle and reversibility while studying the limits of steam-engine work. Later, Rudolf Clausius developed these ideas into formal statements of the first and second laws of thermodynamics.

## The Most Important Laws of Thermodynamics

The zeroth law ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zeroth_law_of_thermodynamics)) states that if two systems are each in thermal equilibrium with a third system, they are in thermal equilibrium with each other. This makes thermometers meaningful and allows a temperature scale to be defined.

The first law ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_law_of_thermodynamics)) is conservation of energy. The change in a system's internal energy equals heat added to the system minus work done by the system on its surroundings. It provides strict energy accounting for thermodynamic processes.

The second law ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_law_of_thermodynamics)) states that heat does not flow spontaneously from cold to hot and that heat at one temperature cannot be converted completely into work. Entropy in a closed system increases toward a maximum, giving physical meaning to the arrow of time.

The third law ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Third_law_of_thermodynamics)) states that the entropy of a perfect crystal in its most stable form tends toward zero as temperature approaches absolute zero. It allows an absolute entropy scale and links entropy with randomness or disorder.

## Temperature

Temperature ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Temperature)) is fundamental but must be defined objectively. A steel rod may feel colder than a wooden rod at the same room temperature because steel conducts heat away from skin faster. Temperature therefore cannot be based on sensation alone.

When two objects are brought into thermal contact, heat flows until they reach equilibrium. At that point they are at the same temperature. A thermometer works because some physical property, such as volume or electrical resistance, changes reliably with temperature.

Temperature scales use fixed points. In the Celsius scale, water freezes at 0 C and boils at 100 C at one standard atmosphere. In the Fahrenheit scale, the same points are 32 F and 212 F. Absolute scales include Kelvin and Rankine, with K = C + 273.15 and R = F + 459.67.

## Work, Energy, and the First Law

Energy ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Energy)) has a precise physical meaning, though it is difficult to define simply. In thermodynamics, internal energy U is a state function, meaning its change depends only on the initial and final equilibrium states of a system.

The first law is written U = Q - W, where Q is heat added and W is work done by the system. Q and W are not state functions because they depend on the process path. The same change in internal energy can occur through different combinations of heat and work.

This is why a system does not contain a fixed amount of heat or work. It has internal energy; heat and work describe transfers during a process. In mathematical language, dU is an exact differential, while dQ and dW are path-dependent.

## Heat Engines and the Second Law

*Temperature-entropy diagram of the Carnot cycle*

A heat engine ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heat_engine)) absorbs heat from a high-temperature reservoir, converts part of it into work, and exhausts the rest to a lower-temperature reservoir. Steam engines and electric power generators are examples of systems that convert heat into work.

The second law has Kelvin and Clausius forms. [[Kelvin]]'s form says no cyclic process can have as its only result the complete conversion of heat from a single-temperature source into work. Clausius's form says no cyclic process can have as its only result the transfer of heat from a colder body to a hotter one.

These two second-law statements are equivalent. If complete heat-to-work conversion were possible, that work could move heat from cold to hot, violating Clausius. If cold-to-hot heat transfer occurred by itself, the heat at the higher temperature could run an engine, violating Kelvin.

A heat reservoir is an ideal body that can supply or absorb heat while staying at a fixed temperature. This idealization allows the efficiency of heat engines to be analyzed cleanly.

## Entropy and Efficiency Limits

Entropy was introduced by Clausius in 1850 as a mathematical way to test the second law. If heat Q enters a reservoir at temperature T, its entropy increases by S = Q/T. Spontaneous heat flow from hot to cold gives a positive total entropy change.

A heat engine ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heat_engine)) that absorbs Q1 from a hot reservoir and exhausts Q2 to a cold reservoir does work W = Q1 - Q2. To maximize work, Q2 should be as small as possible, but it cannot be zero because that would decrease total entropy and violate the second law.

The limiting case occurs when total entropy change is zero, which corresponds to a reversible engine. This gives the maximum possible efficiency for any heat engine operating between two temperatures. Real engines are less efficient because irreversible processes increase entropy.

## Helmholtz Free Energy

For a system held at constant temperature, the maximum work obtainable from an isothermal process leads to Helmholtz free energy ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Helmholtz_free_energy)), defined as F = U - TS. Here U, T, and S are state functions, so F is also a state function.

The change in Helmholtz free energy ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Helmholtz_free_energy)) is the negative of the maximum work that can be extracted from a system at constant temperature, aside from restrictions in the chosen setup. Actual work may be less than this ideal maximum, and equality requires reversibility.

When Helmholtz free energy ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Helmholtz_free_energy)) reaches a minimum, the system is at equilibrium and no further useful work can be extracted. Thus, maximum entropy for isolated systems becomes minimum Helmholtz free energy for systems held at constant temperature and volume.

## Gibbs Free Energy

Most real processes occur at constant pressure rather than constant volume. Gibbs free energy ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gibbs_free_energy)) handles this by including pressure-volume work: G = U + PV - TS. At constant temperature and pressure, G gives the maximum useful work other than atmospheric expansion work.

The direction of spontaneous change at constant temperature and pressure is toward lower Gibbs free energy ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gibbs_free_energy)). This allows entropy of the system itself to decrease if the decrease is offset by energy and pressure-volume terms. Steam condensing to water is an example: water has lower entropy than steam, but condensation occurs if enough heat is removed.

A charged automobile battery has high Gibbs free energy, and a discharged battery has low Gibbs free energy. The difference is the maximum electrical work obtainable at constant temperature and pressure, with heat exchange and atmospheric work included in the balance.

## Chemical Reactions, Enthalpy, and Heat of Reaction

For a chemical reaction, G is the free energy of products minus that of reactants. Once free energies are known, possible battery reactions and other transformations can be judged quickly. The word reaction can include chemical, phase, nuclear, or particle transformations.

Enthalpy, H = U + PV, is useful for reactions at constant pressure. If a reaction proceeds freely without useful work, the heat absorbed equals the enthalpy change. This heat of reaction is easy to measure by allowing substances to react in a beaker while maintaining temperature.

The relation G = H - TS shows the second-law limit on useful work. H is the heat of reaction when no useful work is done, while TS corresponds to the heat term in the reversible maximum-work case. The difference is the maximum useful work available.

At the boiling point of water, liquid water and steam are in equilibrium. The latent heat of vaporization at 373 K is balanced by the entropy term, so G = 0 and no useful work can be extracted from a reversible water-steam transformation other than atmospheric work.

## Thermodynamic Properties and Relations

Thermodynamics uses tables of thermodynamic properties such as entropy, enthalpy, free energy, heat capacity, and equations of state. These allow scientists and engineers to calculate changes under different conditions of temperature and pressure.

Because a thermodynamic state depends on variables such as temperature, pressure, and volume, partial differential equations are essential. One must decide which variables are independent and which are held constant during a process.

Heat capacity connects heat absorbed with temperature change. The molar specific heat is the heat required to raise one mole of a substance by 1 K. For water, 1 calorie raises 1 gram by 1 K, giving a molar heat capacity of about 75 joules per K.

For gases, heat capacity depends on path, especially whether volume or pressure is held constant. This reflects again that heat is not a state function, while internal energy, enthalpy, entropy, and free energies are.

## Informal Summary of the Laws

The laws of thermodynamics are often summarized informally. The first law says one cannot get something for nothing because matter and energy are conserved. The second says one cannot break even because entropy always increases. The third says one cannot get out of the game because absolute zero cannot be reached.

These informal statements are not substitutes for the mathematical laws, but they express their practical force. Energy is conserved, useful work is limited by entropy, and perfect zero-temperature order is unattainable in real processes.

## Core Equations

The first law tracks heat, work, and internal energy:

$$\Delta U=Q-W$$

The first law of thermodynamics tracks internal energy: heat added to a system raises it, while work done by the system lowers it.

Carnot efficiency gives the reversible upper limit between two reservoirs:

$$\eta_{\text{Carnot}}=1-\frac{T_c}{T_h}$$

Carnot efficiency gives the ideal upper limit for a heat engine operating between hot and cold reservoirs.

Gibbs free energy links enthalpy and entropy:

$$G=H-TS$$

Gibbs free energy combines enthalpy, temperature, and entropy to indicate whether a process is thermodynamically favourable.

## Interesting Facts

- Heat is not contained in a system; it is energy transferred during a process.
- Entropy gives the arrow of time for closed systems.
- Gibbs free energy ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gibbs_free_energy)) predicts whether reactions proceed spontaneously at constant temperature and pressure.
- Absolute zero is approached but not reached.
- A fully charged battery is a store of Gibbs free energy.
