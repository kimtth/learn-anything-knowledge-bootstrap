# The Study and Classification of Religions (Religion, Study of)

> [!abstract] Reference navigation

## Introduction

![[attachments/religion_study_001.webp|420]]

*Illustration associated with Edward Burnett Tylor*

The study of religion ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Religious_studies)) is the attempt to understand religious beliefs, practices, institutions, experiences, symbols, myths, rituals, and communities through disciplined inquiry. It uses history, philology, philosophy, psychology, sociology, anthropology, archaeology, art history, economics, and comparative study.

The field became a formal discipline in the 19th century, when scholars began applying modern historical and scientific methods to religion. Yet no single method became dominant, partly because religion touches so many dimensions of human life and partly because questions about religion's nature often overlap with questions about truth.

This overlap made the field controversial: explanations of religion's origin or function were often mistaken for judgments about whether religious claims are true.

## Defining Religion

Defining religion has been difficult. Some definitions emphasize gods, sacred power, the numinous, moral order, ritual, symbols, ultimate concern, salvation, or the division between sacred and profane.

Each definition faces problems. Some traditions give little importance to gods, some emphasize practice more than belief, and some are woven into communal life rather than separated into a formal institution. A better approach often describes typical dimensions of religion rather than one universal essence.

These dimensions include [[Religious Doctrines and Dogmas (Doctrine and Dogma)|doctrine]], myth, ritual, ethics, social organization, inner experience, sacred objects, specialists, art, music, architecture, law, and daily custom. Different religions combine these elements in different proportions.

Classification is therefore useful but risky. Terms such as monotheism, polytheism, mysticism, animism, salvation religion, tribal religion, or world religion can organize comparison, yet they can also impose outside categories on living traditions. The study of religion has to balance general patterns with historical particularity.

## Early Reflections on Religion

Ancient Greek thinkers already asked questions about the plurality of cults, the morality of myths, and the nature of divine reality. Philosophers such as Xenophanes criticized traditional gods, while others interpreted myths allegorically or identified foreign gods with Greek gods.

Some ancient writers offered skeptical or rational explanations. Protagoras questioned certainty about the gods; Prodicus explained deities as useful natural powers; Critias viewed religion as a human invention to support order and morality.

Later European thought continued to debate religion through philosophy ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philosophy)), theology, travel reports, missionary writings, and comparisons with ancient paganism. Early modern and Enlightenment writers tried to explain religion through reason, nature, morality, fear, ignorance, or social need.

Writers such as Charles de Brosses and the Abbe Bergier compared Greek polytheism, West African fetishism, and spirit belief, anticipating later theories of animism and comparative religion.

## Romanticism, History, and Comparison

The rise of Oriental studies, ethnology, and anthropology widened European knowledge of Asian, African, ancient, and indigenous traditions. Scholars could no longer treat non-Christian religions only as errors or as simple pagan parallels.

Herder gave myth a positive value as symbolic expression, while Schelling and Romantic thinkers saw religion as a deep part of culture. [[Hegel and Hegelianism (Hegel)|Hegel]] interpreted religion historically and symbolically as a stage in the development of spirit.

The 19th century also produced major advances in philology. Sanskrit, Iranian, Semitic, Germanic, Celtic, Scandinavian, biblical, patristic, and classical texts were edited and compared. Textual study helped scholars reconstruct older traditions and trace religious change.

## Basic Aims and Methods

The study of religion works both historically and cross-sectionally. A religion can be examined across time, from origin to later development, or through its structures at a particular moment.

History asks how traditions form, borrow, reform, split, institutionalize, and respond to change. It studies founders, scriptures, rituals, sects, councils, migrations, missions, conflicts, reform movements, and political settings.

Historical study also uses philology, inscriptions, edited texts, and manuscript comparison to reconstruct earlier stages of traditions and the settings in which they changed.

Psychology studies religious experience, conversion, mysticism, fear, guilt, dependence, ecstasy, trance, healing, symbols, and belief formation. It may ask how religion shapes the inner life without deciding whether religious claims are true.

Sociology and social anthropology study religious groups, institutions, authority, class, gender, economy, ritual roles, communal identity, and social function. They ask how religion organizes life and how society shapes religion.

Literary study examines sacred texts, myths, hymns, prayers, epics, commentaries, and oral forms. It asks how language, genre, metaphor, narrative, and interpretation carry religious meaning.

## Archaeology and Material Culture

![[attachments/religion_study_003.webp|420]]

*Display of religious artifacts*

Archaeology transformed knowledge of ancient religions. Discoveries at Troy, Crete, Egypt, Elam, Anatolia, Ugarit, Masada, the Indus Valley, and prehistoric sites widened evidence beyond written sources.

Finds such as cave paintings and burials encouraged theories about prehistoric ritual, but their meaning remains partly conjectural because the evidence is fragmentary.

Material remains reveal temples, tombs, inscriptions, images, offerings, altars, ritual objects, burial customs, and settlement patterns. Yet interpretation can remain uncertain, especially in prehistoric religion where evidence is fragmentary.

The study of visual arts and iconography helps interpret sacred images, architecture, symbols, pilgrimage sites, and ritual spaces. Music, though central to many religions, has often received less scholarly attention than texts and images.

## Anthropological Theories

Anthropologists often studied tribal and small-scale societies to think about religion's origins and functions. Earlier scholars proposed evolutionary stages such as atheism, fetishism, nature worship, animism, polytheism, and monotheism.

Later fieldwork complicated these schemes because participant observation showed that ritual, kinship, economy, myth, and social authority work together in local contexts.

E.B. Tylor's theory of animism explained religion as belief in souls and spirits, arising from dreams, death, trance, and human attempts to understand life. J.G. Frazer compared magic, religion, and science in an evolutionary pattern.

Later anthropologists criticized simple evolutionism. They emphasized fieldwork, participant observation, social context, ritual function, symbolism, myth, kinship, and the danger of forcing diverse religions into one universal sequence.

## Psychological and Psychoanalytic Study

![[attachments/religion_study_002.webp|420]]

*Portrait of Mircea Eliade*

Psychological approaches often examine [[Religious Experience|religious experience]]. William James described conversion, saintliness, mysticism, and individual experience. Rudolf Otto explored the numinous as a feeling of awe, fear, mystery, and fascination before the holy.

Some psychologists offered naturalistic explanations. J.H. Leuba compared mystical states with physiological and drug-induced experiences. Other scholars argued that psychology can describe religious states without proving or disproving transcendent reality.

Psychoanalysis gave religion another explanation. Sigmund Freud connected religion with desire, guilt, father figures, taboo, illusion, and social control. Later thinkers revised or challenged his reduction of religion to psychic need.

## Philosophy and Theology

Philosophy asks about religious truth, experience, language, symbols, metaphysics, morality, and the relation between faith and reason. It can study arguments for God, the problem of evil, revelation, immortality, and religious pluralism.

Schleiermacher emphasized feeling and religious consciousness, especially the feeling of absolute dependence. This shifted attention from doctrine alone to inner experience and helped shape modern theology.

[[Hegel and Hegelianism (Hegel)|Hegel]] saw religion as symbolic knowledge of the Absolute, important but lower than philosophy's conceptual knowledge. His historical approach deeply influenced 19th-century scholarship even when later scholars rejected his system.

Modern scholars also distinguish insider and outsider approaches. A tradition can be studied through believers' self-understanding, ritual participation, theology, and devotional language, or through external comparison, social analysis, psychology, and historical criticism. Strong work usually needs both sympathy and distance: enough sympathy to understand meaning from within, and enough distance to compare evidence, institutions, and change.

Classification remains useful but risky. Religions may be grouped as prophetic, mystical, sacramental, ethnic, universal, monotheistic, polytheistic, non-theistic, primal, scriptural, or ritual-centred, but every category simplifies living traditions. Classification helps comparison only when it remains flexible and remembers that religions borrow, split, reform, and combine elements across time.

## Key Events & Developments

- Ancient Greece: philosophers criticized, allegorized, compared, or rationalized myths and cults.
- 18th century: Enlightenment and Romantic thinkers debated religion as reason, morality, symbol, history, culture, or feeling.
- 19th century: philology, anthropology, archaeology, psychology, sociology, and historical criticism made religion a formal field of study.
- 20th century: scholars increasingly emphasized plural methods, fieldwork, symbols, institutions, experience, and cross-cultural comparison.

## Interesting Facts

- Religion ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Religion)) can be studied as belief, ritual, experience, institution, art, text, social system, or historical tradition, so no single discipline can exhaust it.
- Prehistoric evidence, such as cave painting or burial practice, is valuable but difficult to interpret because it rarely explains itself.
- Comparative study can reveal patterns among religions, but it can also distort traditions if it ignores local language, history, and practice.
