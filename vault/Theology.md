## Introduction

![[attachments/theology_001.webp|420]]

*Thomas Aquinas reading*

Theology ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theology)) is a philosophically oriented discipline of religious reflection, speculation, interpretation, and apologetics. It traditionally developed within Christianity, but many of its themes also concern Judaism, Islam, and other religions.

Its central themes include God, humanity, creation, the world, sin, salvation, revelation, worship, history, ethics, and eschatology, or last things. Theology asks what religious claims mean and how they can be understood, defended, organized, and related to life.

## Nature of Theology

Theology is difficult to define as a neutral science of religion ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Religious_studies)) because its concept came from Greek thought but gained its mature form in Christianity. In a narrow sense it is not easily transferred to every religion.

In a broader sense, theology applies wherever a tradition reflects systematically on ultimate reality, sacred power, divine action, human destiny, ritual, ethics, or salvation. It can therefore compare themes across religions while still respecting their differences.

Theology differs from simple belief because it seeks understanding. It may organize doctrines, interpret sacred texts, answer critics, clarify symbols, explain practices, and connect faith with reason, history, culture, and philosophy.

## Greek Origins

The word theology comes from Greek theos, meaning God or gods, and logos, meaning reason or discourse. It first appeared in Greek philosophical contexts.

[[Plato and Platonism (Plato)|Plato]] used theology in connection with stories about the gods and wanted myths cleansed of harmful or offensive elements. For him, theology was tied to education, politics, and the search for eternal realities.

[[Aristotle and Aristotelianism|Aristotle]] treated theology as the highest speculative science because it studied the highest reality, the unmoved mover or being as such. This made theology a form of contemplation directed toward ultimate truth.

## Christian Form

![[attachments/theology_002.webp|420]]

*Painting of Augustine of Hippo*

Christianity adopted and transformed Greek philosophical language. In the Hellenistic world, many people were searching for communion with the divine, and philosophical reflection acquired a religious cast.

Christian thinkers used theology to interpret Scripture, Christ, creation, Trinity, incarnation, church, sacraments, sin, grace, and salvation. Theology became both intellectual discipline and spiritual practice.

Origen described stages of the soul's advance toward communion with God and helped shape theology as disciplined reflection. Later Christian theology developed through councils, doctrines, monastic study, scholasticism, Reformation debate, and modern criticism.

## Themes and Tensions

Theology often joins faith and reason, but the relation can be tense. Some theologians stress revelation and obedience; others stress philosophical argument, experience, culture, history, or moral life.

It may be systematic, arranging doctrines into a coherent whole; biblical, interpreting scripture; historical, tracing doctrine; practical, guiding worship and ethics; or apologetic, defending faith against objections.

Because theology speaks about God and the world together, it can include [[Cosmos (Cosmology)|cosmology]], anthropology, history, politics, ethics, education, art, and social order. It rarely remains only private or abstract.

## Cultural Importance

Theology has deeply shaped Western culture. Hebrew prophetic theology helped create a linear view of history directed toward a goal, unlike cyclical views in which events repeat endlessly.

Universities and schools often grew from church institutions, and theological subjects helped organize the study of nature, history, ethics, and human destiny. Modern philosophy also emerged in dialogue with theological problems.

Even in secular culture, theological categories often survive in altered form. Ideas about history, justice, alienation, liberation, human dignity, evil, hope, and ultimate meaning can retain traces of theological origin.

## History of Theology

![[attachments/theology_003.webp|420]]

*Greek theology manuscript*

![[attachments/theology_004.webp|420]]

*Icon of the First Council of Nicaea*

The history of theology moves from Greek philosophy and early Christian reflection through patristic, medieval, Reformation, modern, Protestant, Catholic, Orthodox, Jewish, and Islamic forms.

Major Christian works include Thomas Aquinas' Summa Theologica, John Calvin's Institutes, Martin Luther's writings on Christian liberty, and later works by Karl Barth, Paul Tillich, Karl Rahner, and others.

Modern theology often engages science ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Science)), historical criticism, secularism, pluralism, politics, psychology, language, and culture. It continues to ask how religious truth can be spoken in changing intellectual worlds.

## Key Events & Developments

- Ancient Greece: [[Plato and Platonism (Plato)|Plato]] and [[Aristotle and Aristotelianism|Aristotle]] gave theology philosophical meanings.
- Early Christianity: theology became reflection on Scripture, God, Christ, salvation, and communion with the divine.
- Middle Ages: scholastic theology joined faith, reason, doctrine, and university study.
- Reformation: Protestant and Catholic theology developed through conflict over Scripture, grace, church, and authority.
- Modern period: theology engaged secular philosophy, historical criticism, science, and pluralism.

## Interesting Facts

- Secular philosophy often continues to debate questions first shaped in theological language.
