# Standard Model

## Introduction

![[attachments/subatomic_particles_001.png|420]]

*Standard Model of elementary particles diagram*

The Standard Model of particle physics is the quantum field theory framework that classifies the known elementary particles and describes three of the four known fundamental interactions: electromagnetism, the weak interaction, and the strong interaction. It does not include a complete quantum theory of gravity.

The model organizes matter into fermions and interactions into fields whose quanta include gauge bosons and the Higgs boson. Its predictions have been confirmed with striking precision, including weak neutral currents, the W and Z bosons, gluons, the top quark, the tau neutrino, and the Higgs boson.

## Particle Content

The Standard Model contains twelve spin-1/2 matter fermions. Six are quarks: up, down, charm, strange, top, and bottom. Six are leptons: electron, muon, tau, electron neutrino, muon neutrino, and tau neutrino. These particles are arranged into three generations. Ordinary atoms are made almost entirely from first-generation particles: up quarks, down quarks, and electrons.

Quarks carry colour charge and therefore participate in the strong interaction. They are not observed in isolation because of colour confinement; instead they appear inside hadrons such as protons, neutrons, mesons, and other composite particles.

Leptons do not carry colour charge. Charged leptons participate in electromagnetic and weak interactions, while neutrinos interact only through the weak interaction and gravity, making them difficult to detect.

## Gauge Bosons and Forces

The Standard Model describes interactions through gauge bosons. The photon mediates electromagnetism and is massless. Eight gluons mediate the strong interaction and themselves carry colour charge. The W and Z bosons mediate the weak interaction and are massive, so the weak force is short-ranged.

Electromagnetism binds atoms and molecules, powers electronics, and controls most chemistry. The strong interaction binds quarks into hadrons and, through a residual force, binds protons and neutrons in atomic nuclei. The weak interaction allows particle transformations such as beta decay and is essential in nuclear reactions and stellar processes.

## Higgs Mechanism

The Higgs field and Higgs boson are central to electroweak symmetry breaking. The Higgs mechanism gives mass to the W and Z bosons and contributes to the masses of quarks and charged leptons through Yukawa interactions. The photon and gluons remain massless.

The Higgs boson is a spin-0 scalar particle. Experiments at the Large Hadron Collider reported a new particle near 125 GeV in 2012, and subsequent measurements confirmed it as the Higgs boson expected by the Standard Model.

## Historical Development

The Standard Model developed in stages during the 20th century. Dirac's relativistic electron theory implied antimatter. Yang-Mills gauge theory generalized the mathematics of fields. Glashow, Weinberg, and Salam developed electroweak theory. Quantum chromodynamics became the theory of strong interactions after quark evidence and the discovery of asymptotic freedom. Renormalization work by 't Hooft and others showed that these gauge theories could be predictive.

The experimental story includes the electron, positron, muon, neutrinos, strange particles, quarks, neutral currents, W and Z bosons, gluons, bottom and top quarks, tau neutrino, and Higgs boson. Each discovery strengthened or refined the model.

## Tests and Open Problems

The Standard Model is one of the most successful theories in science, but it is incomplete. It does not explain gravity, dark matter, dark energy, the matter-antimatter asymmetry of the universe, the observed pattern of particle masses, why there are exactly three generations, or the full origin of neutrino masses. These gaps motivate searches for physics beyond the Standard Model.

Experiments continue to test rare decays, neutrino properties, Higgs couplings, CP violation, precision electroweak quantities, and high-energy collider events. So far, no replacement theory has been experimentally established.

## Dark Matter and Dark Energy

Dark matter is inferred from gravitational evidence such as galaxy rotation curves, gravitational lensing, galaxy cluster dynamics, and the cosmic microwave background. It behaves as matter gravitationally, but it does not emit or absorb light in the way ordinary atoms do. The Standard Model has no viable dark-matter particle: neutrinos are too light and too fast-moving to account for the observed cold dark matter, and all known Standard Model particles interact too strongly, decay too quickly, or have the wrong abundance.

This absence is one of the strongest reasons physicists search for physics beyond the Standard Model. Candidate ideas include weakly interacting massive particles, axions, sterile neutrinos, hidden-sector particles, primordial black holes, and modified-gravity alternatives, but none has been confirmed as the dominant dark matter.

Dark energy is the name for the effect driving the observed accelerated expansion of the universe. In cosmology it is often modeled by a cosmological constant or by a field with negative pressure. The Standard Model does not explain the measured value of dark energy. Naive estimates of vacuum energy from quantum fields are enormously larger than the observed cosmological value, creating the cosmological constant problem.

Dark matter and dark energy are therefore different failures of completeness. Dark matter looks like missing gravitating mass that may correspond to new particles, while dark energy looks like a missing explanation for cosmic acceleration and vacuum energy. Both connect particle physics to cosmology, but neither is solved by the Standard Model alone.

## Core Concepts

The Standard Model is a gauge quantum field theory. Particles are treated as excitations of fields, and interactions arise from symmetries and gauge fields. Its internal gauge symmetry is commonly written as SU(3) x SU(2) x U(1).

The theory is encoded compactly in a Lagrangian density, usually written as $\mathcal{L}$. In quantum field theory, the Lagrangian density specifies the fields, their kinetic terms, their interactions, their symmetry structure, and the mass-generating Higgs and Yukawa sectors. Predictions are obtained by using this density in the action and then calculating scattering amplitudes, decay rates, and cross sections.

The model separates matter particles from force carriers. Fermions obey the Pauli exclusion principle and make up matter. Bosons have integer spin and mediate interactions or, in the Higgs case, express the scalar field associated with electroweak symmetry breaking.

The model is both a classification scheme and a dynamical theory. It tells which particles exist, which charges they carry, which interactions affect them, and how scattering and decay probabilities can be calculated.

The central caution is that the Standard Model is not a theory of everything. It is the best established framework for known non-gravitational particle physics, but its unexplained parameters and missing cosmological ingredients, especially dark matter and dark energy, show that it is probably part of a deeper theory.

A guide to the Standard Model should begin with atoms, electrons, nuclei, quarks, leptons, fermions, bosons, charge, spin, and antiparticles. It should then cover gauge symmetry, electromagnetism, weak interaction, strong interaction, quantum chromodynamics, electroweak unification, Higgs mechanism, particle generations, conservation laws, collider tests, and open problems.

## Core Equations

The Standard Model gauge symmetry is summarized as:

$$SU(3)_C\times SU(2)_L\times U(1)_Y$$

Here SU(3) is associated with colour and the strong interaction, while SU(2) and U(1) form the electroweak theory.

The full theory is often summarized by its Lagrangian density:

$$\mathcal{L}_{SM}=\mathcal{L}_{gauge}+\mathcal{L}_{fermion}+\mathcal{L}_{Higgs}+\mathcal{L}_{Yukawa}$$

This compact expression groups the gauge-field dynamics, fermion kinetic and interaction terms, Higgs-field dynamics, and Yukawa couplings that generate fermion masses after electroweak symmetry breaking.

The action is the spacetime integral of the Lagrangian density:

$$S=\int \mathcal{L}\,d^4x$$

The principle of stationary action supplies the field equations, while the quantum theory uses the same structure to compute amplitudes and probabilities.

Relativistic energy and momentum satisfy:

$$E^2=(pc)^2+(mc^2)^2$$

For a particle at rest this reduces to mass-energy equivalence:

$$E=mc^2$$

The Higgs vacuum expectation value sets the electroweak scale:

$$v\approx246\ \mathrm{GeV}$$

The W boson mass is related to the weak coupling and Higgs scale by:

$$m_W=\frac{1}{2}gv$$

The Z boson mass is:

$$m_Z=\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{g^2+g'^2}\,v$$

A simplified Higgs potential is:

$$V(\phi)=-\mu^2\phi^\dagger\phi+\lambda(\phi^\dagger\phi)^2$$

This potential allows spontaneous symmetry breaking when the field takes a nonzero vacuum value.

## Key Events & Developments

- 1928: Paul Dirac's equation implied antimatter.
- 1954: Yang Chen-Ning and Robert Mills generalized gauge theory to non-Abelian groups.
- 1961-1968: Sheldon Glashow, Steven Weinberg, and Abdus Salam developed electroweak theory.
- 1964: Peter Higgs, Francois Englert, Robert Brout, Gerald Guralnik, Carl Hagen, and Tom Kibble developed symmetry-breaking ideas now associated with the Higgs mechanism.
- 1973: Neutral weak currents were observed at CERN.
- 1973: David Gross, Frank Wilczek, and Hugh Politzer discovered asymptotic freedom.
- 1975: The term Standard Model entered particle-physics usage.
- 1983: The W and Z bosons were discovered at CERN.
- 1995: The top quark was discovered at Fermilab.
- 2000: The tau neutrino was observed.
- 2012: The Higgs boson was discovered at the Large Hadron Collider.

## Key People

- Paul Dirac: Developed the relativistic electron equation and predicted antimatter.
- Yang Chen-Ning and Robert Mills: Developed non-Abelian gauge theory.
- Sheldon Glashow, Steven Weinberg, and Abdus Salam: Built electroweak theory.
- Peter Higgs, Francois Englert, Robert Brout, Gerald Guralnik, Carl Hagen, and Tom Kibble: Developed the mechanism behind electroweak symmetry breaking.
- Gerard 't Hooft: Proved key renormalization properties of gauge theories.
- David Gross, Frank Wilczek, and Hugh Politzer: Discovered asymptotic freedom in non-Abelian gauge theories.
- Leon Lederman: Led the Fermilab team that discovered the bottom quark.

## Interesting Facts

- The Standard Model explains ordinary matter using only first-generation particles: up quarks, down quarks, and electrons.
- Quarks cannot be isolated because of colour confinement.
- Gluons carry colour charge, so the strong force carriers interact with one another.
- The Higgs boson has spin 0, unlike the spin-1 gauge bosons and spin-1/2 matter fermions.
- The Standard Model predicted several particles before they were observed.
- Gravity is not included in the Standard Model, even though it is one of the four known fundamental interactions.
- Neutrino masses require an extension or modification of the classic Standard Model.
