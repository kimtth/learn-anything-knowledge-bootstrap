# Ireland

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> Ireland is an island country whose physical geography, Celtic cultural inheritance, English connection, religious divisions, and changing rural and urban economy shaped its modern identity. Its political history includes union, independence, partition, and the continuing relationship between the republic and Northern Ireland.

## Irish Eire

Ireland ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ireland)), or Eire, is a country of western Europe occupying five-sixths of the westernmost island of the British Isles. Its Atlantic position, heavy rainfall, green grasslands, and long geographic isolation helped shape a Gaelic cultural inheritance, strong folklore, and the familiar image of the Emerald Isle.

Modern Ireland also carries persistent political and social concerns: emigration, cultural identity, economic vulnerability, and relations with Northern Ireland, the six counties of Ulster that remained in the United Kingdom. Independence was recent in historical terms. Ireland was part of the United Kingdom from 1800 to 1922, when the Anglo-Irish Treaty created the Irish Free State, and the state later became the Republic of Ireland.

The economy ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economy)) improved strongly at the end of the 20th century through export-led growth, foreign investment, and high technology, earning the name Celtic Tiger. That success was damaged in 2008 by a financial and property crisis that forced a costly bailout by the European Union and the International Monetary Fund.

## Land

Ireland's republic covers most of an island west of Great Britain, separated from it by the North Channel, Irish Sea, and St. George's Channel. It lies on the European continental shelf and is the western outpost of the Atlantic edge of Eurasia. The island stretches about 302 miles from north to south and 171 miles from east to west.

## Relief

![[attachments/ireland_002.webp|420]]

*Cliffs of Moher on Ireland's Atlantic coast*

The landscape is a broad central limestone plain almost ringed by coastal highlands. Low hills, lakes, bogs, and ridges break the central lowland, while mountain ranges include the Blue Stack Mountains, Wicklow Mountains, Knockmealdown and Comeragh mountains, Macgillycuddy's Reeks, and the Twelve Pins. Carrantuohill, in Macgillycuddy's Reeks, is the republic's highest point.

The western coast is rugged and deeply indented, especially where the mountains of Donegal, Mayo, Galway, and Kerry meet the Atlantic. Bantry Bay and Dingle Bay are drowned river valleys. The east coast is less indented but commercially important because its ports face Britain and continental Europe. Glaciation strongly shaped the scenery and left widespread peat bogs.

## Drainage

Drainage differs between short, rapid rivers flowing from coastal mountains and slow inland streams crossing marshes and lakes. The River Shannon rises near Sligo Bay, flows south-southwest, reaches tidewater at Limerick, and drains much of the central lowland. Other important rivers include the Slaney, Liffey, Boyne, Nore, Barrow, Suir, Blackwater, Lee, Bandon, Clare, and Moy.

Underground drainage developed because Carboniferous limestone is porous. The state carried out arterial drainage projects to reduce flooding, lower lake levels, and expand cultivable land, while farm-drainage schemes brought marginal land into production.

## Soils

Irish soils mostly come from glacial drift. Some rocks weather into poor soils, but limestone-rich drift made many areas productive for farming. Bare limestone regions in the west show how important glacial cover was to agriculture.

## Climate

Ireland's climate is western maritime, shaped by the Atlantic Ocean, mild southwesterly winds, and the North Atlantic Current. Temperatures are relatively even across the island, winters are mild, and summers are cool. Rainfall ranges from about 30 inches in the east to more than 100 inches in exposed western districts.

Grassland benefits from steady precipitation and moderate temperatures, supporting livestock as a major economic base. Snow is uncommon except in mountains. Plant and animal life developed after the Ice Age through migration from other areas, but separation from Britain gave Ireland some distinctive ecological features.

## People

Ireland's population has been shaped by Celts, Norsemen, Normans, English, Scots, immigration, and long emigration. Ethnic and racial minorities made up about 12 percent of the population after rapid growth in the early 21st century. New residents came especially from Europe, Africa, and Asia, with Poles forming the largest minority group.

Travellers, sometimes called Tinkers, are an indigenous ethnic minority with shared customs, traditions, and language. The constitution makes Irish the first official language and English the second. Irish declined after the Great Famine and emigration of the 1840s, but it remained central to national consciousness and survives most strongly in Gaeltacht districts.

Religion and identity were long dominated by Roman Catholicism, though the church's social authority weakened with economic change and revelations of clerical child abuse in the early 21st century. The church still retained important roles in schools and hospitals. Regional differences separated the more industrial and prosperous east of the Shannon from poorer western districts where Irish language and traditional culture were best preserved.

Demography was deeply affected by emigration. Life expectancy became similar to nearby countries, but Ireland's population had long been depleted by outward movement. Many Irish-born people lived abroad, and millions of people in other countries had Irish ancestry.

## Economy

Ireland's mixed economy combines private enterprise with state services and development projects. Semistate bodies historically operated transport, broadcasting, electricity, peat, air transport, and health insurance. Privatization and competition increased with the single European market in the 1990s.

The Celtic Tiger grew from low corporate taxes, high technology, foreign direct investment, exports, education, and social programs. When foreign-investment job growth slowed, political and banking leaders relied heavily on mortgages and construction. By 2008, the property and banking boom collapsed, causing recession and leading to a bailout in 2010 with severe austerity conditions.

## Agriculture

Agriculture ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agriculture)) remains important though no longer the main economic base. Most farmland is pasture or hay, and the climate supports grazing almost year-round. Family farms dominate, mixed farming is common, beef cattle are important in the midlands, dairy farming in the south, cereals in the east and southeast, and sheep on hills and mountain slopes.

Livestock products make up most agricultural output, led by beef, milk, pigs, poultry, eggs, sheep, wool, barley, wheat, sugar beets, and potatoes. Farmhouse cheese, organic vegetables, and the bloodstock industry became important specialized sectors. Entry into the EEC in 1973 helped farm incomes recover after earlier weakness.

## Manufacturing

Manufacturing policy was protectionist until after World War II, using tariffs and quotas to support domestic industry. From the mid-1950s, policy shifted toward tax concessions, grants, export competitiveness, and attracting foreign and Irish-owned firms. By the late 20th century, manufacturing employed more workers than agriculture and produced most exports.

New sectors after the 1970s included computer equipment, software, international finance, and other services. Open European markets and World Trade Organization obligations made competitiveness essential.

## Finance

The Irish pound was linked to sterling until 1979, and the euro later became the official currency. The Central Bank of Ireland, founded in 1942, regulates financial institutions and supervises the Irish Stock Exchange, one of the oldest continuously operating exchanges in the world.

The 2008 collapse created severe financial chaos. A state guarantee of bank deposits made the Irish public liable for huge losses. Ireland accepted more than 100 billion dollars in support from the EU, IMF, and bilateral lenders in 2010, under stringent terms.

## Trade

Trade ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trade)) links Ireland most strongly with the United Kingdom, the European Union, the United States, Japan, and Singapore. Exports include electrical machinery, processed foods, chemicals, clothing, textiles, beverages, and computer software. Imports include machinery, transport equipment, chemicals, petroleum products, food, and textiles.

## Services

Tourism ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tourism)) is a major service industry. The Irish Tourist Board and later Failte Ireland promoted hotels, resorts, sports facilities, amenities, and cooperation with the Northern Ireland Tourist Board. Visitors came mainly from Britain, the United States, Europe, and increasingly from the Middle East and China.

Labour relations include strong trade unions affiliated with the Irish Congress of Trade Unions and employer organizations led by the Irish Business and Employers Confederation. Social partnership agreements among government, employers, and unions became central to economic management from the late 1980s, and the Croke Park Agreement helped limit unrest after the 2008 crash through negotiated wage and benefit cuts.

## Telecommunications

[[Telecommunications Systems (Telecommunication)|Telecommunications]] were dominated by state-owned Telecom Eireann until deregulation in 1998. Foreign firms such as Telenor, British Telecom, and AT&T then entered the market. Regulation moved from the Office of the Director of Telecommunications Regulation to the Commission for Communications Regulation. Internet access rose rapidly after 1997, though high-speed service spread more slowly.

The constitution of 1937 created a parliamentary democracy. The president is directly elected for seven years, signs laws, may consult the Council of State, and can refer bills to the Supreme Court. The Oireachtas consists of the Dail Eireann, the Seanad Eireann, and the president. Executive power rests mainly with the taoiseach and government responsible to the Dail.

## Justice

Irish law is based on common law modified by legislation and the constitution. Judges are appointed by the president and serve until retirement unless removed by both houses for incapacity or misbehaviour. Courts include district courts, circuit courts, the High Court, and the Supreme Court, with a special criminal court established in 1972 for terrorism cases.

Politics uses proportional representation by single transferable vote for Dail elections. Seanad members are partly appointed by the taoiseach, partly elected by universities, and partly elected from vocational panels. Major parties include Fianna Fail, Fine Gael, Labour, and Sinn Fein. Women made gains with the elections of Mary Robinson in 1990 and Mary McAleese in 1997 as presidents.

## Security

Security is handled by An Garda Siochana, a nationwide police force created in 1922. Most officers are uniformed and unarmed, while detectives may be armed when needed. Ireland's defense forces include army, reserve, small naval, and air elements, with voluntary enlistment and no conscription.

Neutrality and peacekeeping shape military policy. Irish forces have served in United Nations missions in Lebanon, the Middle East, Afghanistan, Congo, Cyprus, and the Balkans, while Ireland also balances neutrality with European Union defense obligations.

## Housing

Home ownership is high in Ireland. Fewer than one-tenth of housing units were owner-occupied at independence in 1922, but about four-fifths were owner-occupied by the early 21st century. The housing stock is relatively modern, yet shortages and rising prices fed the property bubble that contributed to the 2008 crisis; prices then fell sharply.

## Education

[[Education]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Education)) is highly valued and helped support the Celtic Tiger economy. Primary education is free, compulsory, and mostly denominational. Secondary education includes religious private schools, state comprehensive and community schools, and vocational schools. Educate Together operates growing numbers of multidenominational private schools.

Higher education includes the University of Dublin, or Trinity College, founded in 1592, the National University of Ireland, founded in 1908, universities at Dublin, Cork, Galway, and Maynooth, and later the University of Limerick and Dublin City University. The Higher Education Authority was created in 1972 to address financial and organizational issues.

## Cultural Life

Irish culture is marked by interaction between Irish-language traditions and English-language expression. Irish-language poets such as Micheal O'Siadhail, Gabriel Rosenstock, Michael Hartnett, Nuala Ni Dhomhnaill, Aine Ni Ghlinn, and Cathal O Searcaigh sustained modern Gaelic writing.

Anglo-Irish literature became internationally important through linguistic cross-fertilization. Major writers include Jonathan Swift, William Butler Yeats, Seamus Heaney, James Joyce, John McGahern, Roddy Doyle, John Banville, and Jennifer Johnston. The Irish Writers' Centre and Poetry Ireland promote contemporary prose and verse.

## Theatre

Irish theatre includes Nobel laureates George Bernard Shaw and Samuel Beckett, as well as Oliver Goldsmith, Richard Brinsley Sheridan, Oscar Wilde, John Millington Synge, and Sean O'Casey. Dublin's Abbey Theatre, founded in 1904, stages classic and new Irish works, while the Gate, Peacock, Druid, and amateur companies broaden theatrical life.

Music and dance draw on old traditional forms, the harp, uilleann pipes, fiddle, accordion, and organized preservation by groups such as Comhaltas Ceoltoiri Eireann. Traditional music revived strongly from the late 19th century and again from the 1960s, with performers such as the Clancy Brothers, Planxty, Clannad, the Chieftains, the Dubliners, and the Wolfe Tones.

Film, arts, and sport include internationally known actors such as Gabriel Byrne, Colin Farrell, Brenda Fricker, Brendan Gleeson, Richard Harris, Maureen O'Hara, and Saoirse Ronan. Cultural institutions include the National Museum, National Gallery, Irish Museum of Modern Art, National Library, Royal Irish Academy, Royal Dublin Society, Royal Hibernian Academy, Royal Irish Academy of Music, Arts Council, Aosdana, and Gaelic League.

[[Sports]] are central to public life. The Gaelic Athletic Association, founded in 1884, promotes Gaelic football and hurling. Football, rugby, horse racing, greyhound racing, cycling, and Olympic sport are also important; Steven Roche won the Tour de France and world championship in 1987.

## History

![[attachments/ireland_001.webp|420]]

*Ireland (MODIS)*

Ireland's history reflects its Atlantic position west of Britain, its links with France, Spain, Portugal, Norway, Iceland, Scotland, Wales, and Roman Britain, and its later role in the Atlantic world. Ireland became famous in the early Middle Ages for Christianity, scholarship, and the arts, and later exerted influence through emigrants, clergy, soldiers, and writers.

## Early Ireland

Early occupation began late in European prehistory. Finds once called Larnian were linked to Mesolithic hunter-fishers, but later archaeology suggested that many belonged with Neolithic farming cultures. Evidence from the River Bann and a debated carbon-14 date near Lough Neagh show the difficulty of fixing Ireland's earliest human phases.

## Neolithic Period

The Neolithic Period began about 3000 BC. Farming communities built long-barrow burials, including more than 300 court tombs, mainly in northern Ireland. Portal tombs developed from court tombs and spread more widely. Passage tombs, often in round mounds on hilltops, were grouped in cemeteries such as the Boyne, Loughcrew, Carrowkeel, and Carrowmore, with decorated stones and rich grave goods.

## Bronze Age

The Bronze Age brought wedge tomb builders associated with Beaker pottery, especially in the west, and single-burial traditions with food vessels in the east. Ireland developed a flourishing metal industry, exporting bronze, copper, and gold to Britain and the continent. By about 800 BC, a rich late Bronze Age industry was established, including gold hoards and Nordic connections.

## Iron Age

The Iron Age is difficult to connect directly with language groups, but Celtic arrivals likely occurred then. La Tene culture appears in metalwork and stone sculpture, mostly in the north, and hill forts became characteristic. Irish sagas preserve social images resembling descriptions by classical writers such as Poseidonius and Julius Caesar.

## Early Celtic Ireland

Early Celtic Ireland was organized into many petty kingdoms, or tuatha, each under an elected king. Groups combined into the Five Fifths: Ulster, Meath, Leinster, Munster, and Connaught. Society was built around aristocrats, clients, cattle wealth, law-defined ranks, and corporate kin groups responsible for land and legal obligations.

Rural life had no true urban centres. Cattle raising, wheat, barley, oats, flax, hay, pigs, fishing, hunting, and crafts supported the economy. People lived in post-and-wattle houses, ring forts, lake islands, and artificial crannogs, while packhorses, curraghs, and dugouts carried goods and people.

## Conversion

Christianization began before the full legend of St. Patrick. Palladius was sent in AD 431 to Christians in Ireland, while Patrick's own Confessio described his capture from Roman Britain, slavery in Ireland, ordination, and missionary work. Later writers made Patrick the national apostle, though his exact chronology remains debated.

Irish monasticism grew strongly in the 6th and 7th centuries. Monasteries became centres of religion, scholarship, art, and missionary energy, linking Ireland to Britain and continental Europe.

## Medieval and Tudor Ireland

Anglo-Norman Ireland began after English intervention and land grants to figures such as Richard de Clare and Hugh de Lacy. Henry II asserted overlordship, kept chief towns, and received church recognition. The Treaty of Windsor in 1175 made Rory O'Connor acknowledge Henry, but the arrangement failed as Norman lordships expanded.

English administration developed through an exchequer, chancery, counties, English law, and a parliament representing the Anglo-Irish. Gaelic Ireland remained resilient, especially in the northwest, and English authority weakened outside settled areas.

Tudor policy tried to turn elective chiefs into hereditary nobles loyal to the crown, impose royal supremacy over the church, and plant English settlers on Irish land. Protestantism gained little support outside official circles, while Catholic resistance and plantations drew Gaelic and Anglo-Irish interests closer together.

## Ireland under Elizabeth I

Elizabethan Ireland saw the Acts of Supremacy and Uniformity passed in 1560, but enforcement was cautious because officials feared uniting the Pale, Gaelic Irish, and Spain. Three major rebellions followed: Shane O'Neill's rising, the Desmond rebellion, and the Tyrone-O'Donnell war.

## The Shane O'Neill rebellion

Shane O'Neill was elected O'Neill in 1559 and challenged the Dublin government over succession, recognition, and power in Ulster. He fought the MacDonnells and O'Donnells, sought outside support, and was killed by the MacDonnells in 1567. Later plantation schemes in Munster, Leinster, and Ulster deepened conflict.

## The Tyrone Rebellion and Ulster Plantation

The Tyrone war joined Hugh O'Neill, Hugh Roe O'Donnell, Catholic missionary networks, and limited Spanish and papal support against English expansion. Charles Blount, Lord Mountjoy, eventually restored English control after the failure of Spanish aid.

James I pursued moderation after 1603, but the Flight of the Earls in 1607 opened Ulster to plantation by English and Scottish landlords, tenants, and labourers. This settlement was the most successful British plantation and helped create the later Protestant and Presbyterian shape of Ulster.

## Protestant Ascendancy and Union

The Williamite settlement followed the defeat of James II's supporters by William III's forces and the surrender at Limerick in 1691. Catholic landownership fell sharply, and the Protestant Ascendancy, mostly members of the established Church of Ireland, dominated politics while Catholics, Presbyterians, and other Nonconformists were excluded from full rights.

The 18th century saw exiled Catholic soldiers and priests abroad, limited reforms, Maynooth seminary, and resistance to a more representative Irish Parliament. Inspired by the French Revolution, Wolfe Tone and others formed the Society of United Irishmen in 1791. The failed rebellion of 1798 prompted Britain to merge the Irish and British parliaments in the Act of Union, effective January 1, 1801.

## The 19th and Early 20th Centuries

The Act of Union gave Ireland representation at Westminster but kept Protestant privilege and failed to resolve Catholic grievances. Daniel O'Connell turned Catholic emancipation into a mass movement, won election for County Clare in 1828, and forced the Catholic Emancipation Act of 1829.

The Great Famine followed potato blight in the 1840s. About a million people died from starvation and disease, more emigrated, and emigration continued after the famine. By 1911, Ireland's population was less than half its pre-famine level.

Home Rule and land reform became central after Isaac Butt founded the Home Government Association. Michael Davitt founded the Irish Land League in 1879, and Charles Stewart Parnell joined land agitation to parliamentary obstruction. William Ewart Gladstone's Land Act of 1881 conceded fixity of tenure, fair rents, and free sale, and his conversion to Home Rule transformed British-Irish politics.

## The Irish Free State, 192232

The Irish Free State came into being on December 6, 1922, with dominion status. Northern Ireland opted out, and the Boundary Commission later recommended only minor changes; the existing border remained after the 1925 agreement.

The Anglo-Irish Treaty split Sinn Fein and the IRA. Eamon de Valera rejected the oath to the British crown, while Michael Collins led the provisional government. Civil war followed, costing about 1,000 lives; Collins was killed in Cork in 1922, and William T. Cosgrave led the new state.

Postwar reconstruction required heavy defense and compensation spending, but Cosgrave's government built a centralized civil service, reformed courts, pursued farming policy, and developed hydroelectric projects. De Valera founded Fianna Fail, entered the Dail, and helped make the civil-war split between pro-treaty and anti-treaty forces the main divide in Irish politics.

## The Republic of Ireland

The Republic of Ireland Act, introduced by John A. Costello, took effect in 1949, repealed the External Relations Act, and ended the remaining fiction of Commonwealth membership. Britain recognized Ireland's new status but guaranteed Northern Ireland's constitutional position unless its own parliament consented to change.

The 1950s brought stagnation, emigration, and political change. Sean Lemass succeeded de Valera as taoiseach in 1959 and worked with T.K. Whitaker to implement economic expansion, abandon protectionism, encourage foreign investment, and begin Ireland's long move toward Europe.

European integration transformed Ireland after it joined the EEC on January 1, 1973. Farmers benefited from the Common Agricultural Policy, industry gained wider markets, and European programs changed infrastructure and social development. Later governments under Charles Haughey, Albert Reynolds, John Bruton, Bertie Ahern, Brian Cowen, and others managed growth, coalition politics, scandal, and the financial crash.

## Relations with Northern Ireland

Northern Ireland relations dominated much late 20th-century politics. The Irish government used internment during an IRA border campaign from 1956 to 1962. Lemass exchanged visits with Terence O'Neill in 1965, but violence erupted in Northern Ireland in 1969.

Peace efforts included support for direct rule from Westminster in 1972 and participation in the Sunningdale Agreement of 1973, which recognized that Northern Ireland's status could change only with majority consent and proposed a Council of Ireland. The agreement collapsed in 1974, while violence also struck the republic, including Dublin and Monaghan car bombings that killed 33 people.

The later Good Friday Agreement made consent, power sharing, and cross-border cooperation the core principles of peace. It did not erase conflicting national identities, but it created institutions through which unionists, nationalists, the Irish state, and the British state could manage disagreement. For the republic, Northern Ireland policy therefore shifted from constitutional aspiration alone toward practical cooperation on security, rights, trade, movement, and public administration across the island.
