## Introduction

Mohandas Karamchand Gandhi, later known as Mahatma Gandhi, was born in 1869 in Porbandar, India, and became the central figure in the struggle for Indian independence from British rule. He was a leader shaped by both Hindu and Jain traditions, especially the ideas of ahimsa ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ahimsa)) and nonviolence. His childhood was marked by religious discipline, a strong sense of duty, and a deep desire for self-improvement. These traits later formed the moral basis of his political philosophy.

## Youth

![[attachments/gandhi_001.webp|420]]

*Mahatma Gandhi leaves Presidency Jail in Calcutta*

As a young man, Gandhi was shy, ordinary in school, and not especially successful in academic life. He was married at the age of 13, and his early years showed neither exceptional brilliance nor strong political ambition. Yet he developed a strong inner discipline and a habit of self-examination. His time in England for legal study exposed him to vegetarianism, religion, and reformist ideas, which helped him become more confident and thoughtful. These years were important because they shaped his later commitment to simple living and moral principle.

## South Africa

Gandhi's experience in South Africa became the decisive turning point of his life. He faced racial humiliation, exclusion from public spaces, and legal injustice, and these experiences awakened his sense of political responsibility. In response, he developed satyagraha, a method of resistance based on truth, self-discipline, and nonviolent struggle. This was not only a political tactic but a moral approach to conflict, one that invited suffering rather than inflicting it. His work in South Africa helped him build the practical methods he would later use in India.

## Return to India

![[attachments/gandhi_003.webp|420]]

*Nehru and Gandhi, All India Congress, Mumbai, July 6, 1946*

After returning to India, Gandhi became increasingly active in politics. He organized protests, worked with Indian communities abroad, and built a national movement around nonviolent resistance. His campaigns against colonial rule were carefully designed to mobilize large numbers of people without turning to violence. His leadership transformed the Indian National Congress from a small elite body into a mass organization rooted in villages and towns. This was a major shift in the character of Indian nationalism.

## Civil Disobedience and National Leadership

![[attachments/gandhi_002.webp|420]]

*Mahatma-Gandhi, studio, 1931*

Gandhi's major campaigns in 1920, 1930, and 1942 were central to the independence movement. He used methods such as boycotts, fasting, and mass civil disobedience to challenge British authority and to awaken the moral confidence of ordinary Indians. His movement was especially strong because it linked political action with ethical discipline. Gandhi believed India's political weakness came not only from British rule but also from internal divisions and social injustice. His campaigns therefore aimed at both freedom and reform.

## Religion and Social Reform

Gandhi's life was deeply shaped by religion, but not by formal ritual alone. He believed in the spiritual dignity of every person and worked to remove untouchability, promote rural self-reliance, and encourage simple living. He supported spinning, weaving, and village industries as part of a broader effort to rebuild Indian society from the bottom up. His social reform agenda was inseparable from his political vision: the struggle for independence had to include justice, equality, and moral renewal.

## Partition and Assassination

The final years of Gandhi's life were marked by the painful partition of British India into India and Pakistan in 1947. He opposed the division, but he also tried to heal communal violence by fasting and public moral appeal. His efforts to restore peace were not fully successful, and he was assassinated on January 30, 1948, by Nathuram Godse. This death is a tragic ending to a life devoted to reconciliation, nonviolence, and national freedom.

## Place in History

Gandhi is described as a leader whose influence extended far beyond India. His methods inspired movements for civil rights, anti-colonial struggle, and nonviolent protest around the world. His ideas were not always accepted in their full form by Indian leaders, and some later movements borrowed his name without practicing his principles. Even so, Gandhi remains one of the most important figures of the 20th century, especially because he linked political freedom with ethical discipline and moral courage.
