# Drafting

> [!abstract] Reference navigation

## Introduction

Drafting ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Technical_drawing)), also called engineering drawing, is the graphical language used to describe structures, machines, and their parts so that the people who build them can understand the designer’s intent. In the early stage of a project, both freehand sketches and exact mechanical drawings help the designer think, compare ideas, and communicate with collaborators in production, marketing, and management. A rough sketch can suggest a useful idea, while a precise drawing can confirm whether the idea really works. In this way, drafting is not only a final recording method but also a basic part of the design process.

## Perspective

Perspective drawing tries to show a three-dimensional object in a way that looks like normal human vision. Artists and architects developed this method in the 15th century, and its value is easy to understand because it gives a picture that seems natural to the eye. The lines of sight move toward a central point, and the object appears smaller as it recedes. This makes perspective useful for pictorial views, but it is not the best method for engineering work, because visual likeness is not the same as exact shape.

## Orthographic projection

Orthographic projection ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orthographic_projection)) is the projection system used in engineering and architectural drafting. Here the lines of sight, called projectors, strike the picture plane at right angles instead of converging toward a point. Because the projectors are parallel, the drawing gives a more exact record of the object’s shape and size. This is why orthographic views are preferred when the goal is to define a part clearly enough for manufacture or construction.

## Descriptive geometry

Descriptive geometry ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Descriptive_geometry)) is the mathematical base that explains how orthographic drawings work. The method comes from the system developed by Gaspard Monge, who used two perpendicular reference planes, one vertical and one horizontal, to generate views of an object. By rotating the planes into the same flat surface, the drafter can place the views in a single drawing while keeping their geometric relationships intact. This makes the method powerful for understanding how three-dimensional forms become two-dimensional images.

## Ambiguity

Ambiguity must be avoided because a set of drawings should describe only one object, not several possible ones. Two views can sometimes look correct for more than one shape, so the drafter cannot rely on them alone when the object is complex. One example involves three objects that share the same front and top views. In that case, a third view is needed to remove doubt and to show the real form of the part.

## Hidden lines

Hidden lines ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Engineering_drawing)) show edges or surfaces that cannot be seen directly from a particular viewing direction. Drafters normally use dashed lines for these features, because the reader must know that an edge exists even if it is blocked by another part of the object. This convention becomes especially important in third-angle projection, where the nearby view helps the reader judge which surfaces are visible and which are concealed. The rule may seem simple, but it is essential in complicated drawings.

## Auxiliary views

Auxiliary views are used when a surface does not appear in true size and shape in the main front, top, or side views. If a plane is inclined or oblique, the normal views may distort its real form, so the drafter adds a view taken on another plane that is parallel to the surface. That extra view reveals the true length, true angle, and true outline of the feature. In this way, auxiliary views solve a problem that the principal views cannot handle alone.

## Drafting practice standards

Drafting practice standards developed because technical drawings had to be read by many people in many places without confusion. One major issue was the use of first-angle versus third-angle projection. Third-angle projection became standard in United States industrial practice, while many other countries kept first-angle projection, especially in engineering and architecture. Because the two systems place views differently, standards help prevent costly misunderstanding.

## Equipment

Equipment ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equipment)) in manual drafting was developed to make drawing more accurate, faster, and neater. The familiar tools include the T square, triangle, protractor, compass, parallel straightedge, scale, and drafting machine. Each tool supports a specific task, such as drawing straight lines, measuring angles, or setting dimensions. The drafting machine, which appeared around 1930, combines several functions and allows the drafter to move a straightedge while keeping a chosen angle.

## Computers

[[Computers]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer)) changed drafting by allowing graphic images to be created, stored, changed, and plotted electronically. Beginning in the early 1960s, software made it possible to draw on a screen, save the related data in memory, and send that data to plotting devices that produced lines, symbols, dimensions, and notes. This development made drafting faster and more flexible, especially for large projects that require many revisions. It also reduced the limits of manual redrawing.

Duplication of drawings became much easier once reproduction methods were developed for technical documents. Blueprinting was the first economical process, later followed by diazo copying, xerography, and computer-controlled systems. These methods made it possible to distribute many copies of the same drawing to different workers and departments. That changed the organization of work because more people could use the same technical information at the same time.

## Key Events & Developments

- early technical practice: Drafting develops as a way to make exact plans for buildings, machines, and structures.
- 19th century: Standardized projection methods and engineering education make drafting more systematic.
- 20th century: CAD and other digital tools begin to replace much manual drafting.

## Key People

- Gaspard Monge ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gaspard_Monge)): Associated with descriptive geometry and modern technical drawing.
- William Farish: Linked with the development of isometric and projection methods.
- Engineers and architects: They use drafting to turn ideas into buildable plans.
- Draftsmen: They carry out the detailed work of technical representation.
