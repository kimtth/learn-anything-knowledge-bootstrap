## Introduction

![[attachments/maxwell_001.webp|420]]

*Portrait of James Clerk Maxwell*

James Clerk Maxwell was a Scottish physicist best known for electromagnetic theory ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electromagnetism)). His field equations, based on Michael Faraday's lines of force, prepared the way for relativity, quantum theory, and the modern idea of electromagnetic radiation.

Born into a comfortable Scottish family, Maxwell moved from Edinburgh to Glenlair as a child. His mother died in 1839, and at Edinburgh Academy his curiosity, memory, and interest in geometry became clear.

## Scientific Career

![[attachments/maxwell_003.png|420]]

*Electromagnetic wave diagram*

![[attachments/maxwell_002.webp|420]]

*Maxwell equations*

At 14, Maxwell published a paper on oval curves. After Edinburgh and Cambridge, where William Hopkins praised his physical intuition, Maxwell became a Trinity fellow, professor at Marischal College, husband of Katherine Mary Dewar, and then professor at King's College, London.

From 1860 to 1865 at King's College, Maxwell published classic electromagnetic papers, demonstrated colour photography, joined the Royal Society, studied gas viscosity, worked on electrical units, and confirmed the link between electrical units and light's velocity.

In 1865, Maxwell retired to Glenlair and wrote Treatise on Electricity and Magnetism, published in 1873. He translated Faraday's ideas into mathematics and concluded that light was a transverse electromagnetic wave, later generated experimentally by Heinrich Hertz.

Maxwell also contributed to Saturn's rings, thermodynamics, kinetic theory, gas transport, colour vision, experimental apparatus, information theory, cybernetics, fluid theory, and geometrical optics. His Maxwell-Boltzmann distribution applied probability to molecular velocities.

In 1871, Maxwell became the first Cavendish professor at Cambridge and designed the Cavendish Laboratory. His students were few but influential.

## Maxwell's students

Among Maxwell's students were D. Niven, Ambrose Fleming, Richard Tetley Glazebrook, John Henry Poynting, and Arthur Schuster. He died on November 5, 1879, and was buried quietly at Parton in Scotland. He died in Cambridge and was buried in Scotland.

## Core Equations

Physical models often begin by relating force, energy, waves, and conserved quantities:

$$\vec{F}=m\vec{a}$$

This is Newton's second law: force is the product of mass and acceleration, so a larger mass or a faster change in motion requires a larger net force.

$$E=h\nu$$

This relation says that photon energy is proportional to frequency; higher-frequency radiation carries more energy per photon.

$$E=mc^2$$

This expresses mass-energy equivalence: mass is a form of energy, with the speed of light squared as the conversion factor.
