# Einstein

> [!abstract] Reference navigation

## Introduction

Albert Einstein was born on March 14, 1879, in Ulm, Germany, and died on April 18, 1955, in Princeton. The most influential physicist of the 20th century, he developed the special and general theories of relativity and won the Nobel Prize for Physics ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize_in_Physics)) in 1921 for the photoelectric effect ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Photoelectric_effect)).

Einstein grew up in a secular, middle-class Jewish family; his father Hermann ran an electrochemical factory. Two wonders shaped him: a compass at age five and a geometry book at age 12, which sparked his lifelong questions about [[Light|light]].

## From graduation to the miracle year of scientific theories

After his father's business failed in 1894, Einstein entered the Swiss Federal Polytechnic in Zurich, graduating in 1900, and took a job at the Swiss patent office in Bern. In his 1905 miracle year he published four papers in the Annalen der Physik: on light quanta, on Brownian motion proving atoms exist, on special relativity, and on E = mc2.

## General relativity and teaching career

His 1905 work was ignored until Max Planck praised it, after which he rose to Berlin's Kaiser Wilhelm Institute. In November 1915 he completed general relativity, just before David Hilbert derived the equations independently.

## World renown and Nobel Prize

[[World Wars (World War I)|World War I]] interrupted his work; the pacifist Einstein called nationalism "the measles of mankind." After the 1919 eclipse confirmed light deflection, The Times of London announced "Revolution in Science." His [[Cosmos (Cosmology)|cosmology]] predicted an expanding universe, confirmed by Edwin Hubble in 1929.

## Nazi backlash and coming to America

The Nazi movement branded relativity "Jewish physics"; answering "One Hundred Authors Against Einstein" (1931), he said only one fact was needed. In December 1932 he left Germany for the Institute for Advanced Study at Princeton.

## Personal sorrow, World War II, and the atomic bomb

The 1930s brought his son Eduard's schizophrenia and wife Elsa's death in 1936; he became a U.S. citizen in 1940. Though his equation began the Manhattan Project, he was barred from it, later backing J. Robert Oppenheimer and opposing the hydrogen bomb.

## Increasing professional isolation and death

From 1925 he sought a unified field theory, which set him apart from physicists who embraced quantum mechanics ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_mechanics)). In 1952 he declined the presidency of Israel and died in 1955 with the theory unfinished.

## Assessment
Einstein was too far ahead of his time, since the strong force was unknown until the quark model of the 1970s and '80s. His predictions still win Nobel Prizes, including gravitation waves (1993).

## Core Equations

Physical models often begin by relating force, energy, waves, and conserved quantities:

$$\vec{F}=m\vec{a}$$

This is Newton's second law: force is the product of mass and acceleration, so a larger mass or a faster change in motion requires a larger net force.

$$E=h\nu$$

This relation says that photon energy is proportional to frequency; higher-frequency radiation carries more energy per photon.

$$E=mc^2$$

This expresses mass-energy equivalence: mass is a form of energy, with the speed of light squared as the conversion factor.
