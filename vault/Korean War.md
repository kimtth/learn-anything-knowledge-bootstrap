# Korean War

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> The Korean War began in 1950 when Soviet-supplied North Korea invaded South Korea and became an international conflict involving United Nations forces, the United States, and China. Its 1953 armistice ended large-scale fighting without reunification and fixed a continuing division of the peninsula.

## Introduction

The Korean War ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Korean_War)) was the conflict between the Democratic People's Republic of Korea and the Republic of Korea in which at least 2.5 million people died. It became international in June 1950, when Soviet-supplied North Korea invaded South Korea, the United Nations entered mainly through the United States, and China later joined North Korea.

The war's result was not reunification but continued division. Fighting ended in July 1953, after more than a million combat casualties, with Korea still separated into hostile states. Negotiations in 1954 produced no settlement, and the front line became the de facto boundary.

## Revolution, Division, And Partisan Warfare, 1945-50

![[attachments/korean_war_001.webp|420]]

*Lopez scaling seawall*

The war's origins lay in Japan's collapse in 1945. Korea had been annexed since 1910 and had no native government ready to return. Rival claimants included Marxist guerrillas such as Kim Il-sung and nationalist factions including Syngman Rhee.

The 38th parallel began as a temporary administrative division for disarming Japanese forces. The Soviet Union occupied the north and the United States the south, but the split hardened quickly. Refugees moved south, the United States took the question to the United Nations, and both zones built separate security forces.

Partisan warfare ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Partisan_%28military%29)) spread in the south before full war began. Southern communists resisted the creation of the Republic of Korea in 1948, while the North supported guerrilla incursions and built the Korean People's Army. Thousands of security personnel and civilians died, and atrocities became common on both sides.

## Invasion And Counterinvasion, 1950-51

![[attachments/korean_war_002.webp|420]]

*Korean War, train attack*

Kim Il-sung pressed Joseph Stalin for approval to invade. Stalin first refused but later accepted after the North Korean army became stronger with Soviet weapons and Korean veterans released from China's People's Liberation Army. In the predawn hours of June 25, 1950, North Korea attacked across the 38th parallel.

The invasion reached Seoul on June 28, but it did not destroy the South Korean government or army. Harry S. Truman went to the United Nations rather than Congress, and the Security Council called for military assistance because the Soviet delegate was boycotting the council and could not veto action.

Early U.S. intervention expanded defeat before it stabilized the front. American and South Korean forces retreated through Osan, the Kum River, Taejon, and Taegu. Refugee flows, weak weapons, limited numbers, and uncertain leadership caused confusion, including attacks on civilians such as the Nogun-ri incident.

The Pusan Perimeter stopped North Korea in August and September 1950. Lieutenant General Walton H. Walker, South Korean forces, tanks, artillery, aircraft, naval gunfire, supplies through Pusan, Korean labour, and new U.S., Marine, and Commonwealth units halted the Korean People's Army.

The Inch'on landing reversed the war. Douglas MacArthur chose the difficult port west of Seoul despite tides, mines, islands, and nearby enemy forces. On September 15, 1950, X Corps landed, took Inch'on, entered Seoul, and linked with Eighth Army forces. The North Korean field army disintegrated, though many troops escaped north or became partisans.

The UN advance north followed. The war aim expanded to eliminate the North Korean army and reconstruct Korea as one nation. UN and South Korean forces crossed the 38th parallel, took P'yongyang, and reached the Yalu River, while the Kim government withdrew toward Kanggye.

Chinese intervention changed the war again. [[Mao Zedong]], with Soviet air and weapons support promised by Stalin, sent the Chinese People's Volunteers under Peng Dehuai. Chinese forces struck in October and November 1950, using night attacks, ambushes, concealment, and cuts to supply and withdrawal routes.

The winter retreat drove UN forces back from North Korea. Chinese forces struck the overextended Eighth Army and X Corps; the 1st Marine Division fought south from Chosin Reservoir to Hungnam. Seoul was retaken by Chinese and North Korean forces in January 1951, but Matthew B. Ridgway reorganized the UN command and restored the line near the 38th parallel.

MacArthur's dismissal came after he publicly urged expanding the war to China, challenging Truman's control of policy. Truman removed him in April 1951. Ridgway became commander in chief, and James A. Van Fleet took Eighth Army command as Chinese offensives continued and then lost momentum.

## Talking And Fighting, 1951-53

Armistice negotiations began in July 1951 at Kaesong and later moved to P'anmunjom. The war became a costly positional struggle: neither side still expected to unify Korea by force, but both fought for tactical ground and bargaining advantage.

The Autumn Offensive of 1951 showed the cost of fighting for ridges and salients. UN forces fought around the Iron Triangle, Bloody Ridge, Heartbreak Ridge, the Punchbowl, and other positions. The campaign cost about 60,000 UN casualties, including 22,000 Americans, while communist losses were large but not decisive.

Prisoner repatriation became the hardest negotiation issue. The communists wanted all prisoners returned, while many communist prisoners held by the UN refused repatriation. Fighting continued around named hills such as Old Baldy, Sniper Ridge, Triangle Hill, and others while talks stalled.

The final movement toward armistice followed Stalin's death in March 1953, growing communist war-weariness, and the Eisenhower administration's wish to end the conflict. Sick and disabled prisoners were exchanged, and China accepted voluntary repatriation under neutral supervision.

## Armistice

![[attachments/korean_war_003.webp|420]]

*Memorial Hall to Resist U.S. Aggression and Aid Korea*

The armistice ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Armistice)) nearly failed when Syngman Rhee released 27,000 anti-communist Korean prisoners in June 1953. The United States won his acceptance by promising aid and a mutual-security pact, though South Korea never signed the armistice.

The agreement was signed on July 27, 1953, by Mark W. Clark for the UN Command, Peng Dehuai for China, and Kim Il-sung for North Korea. A demilitarized zone about 4 km wide separated the armies, and commissions supervised cease-fire and prisoner arrangements.

The human cost was enormous. The war caused at least 2.5 million deaths and more than a million combat casualties. It left Korea divided, devastated industrial facilities and homes, and fixed a military boundary that remained the practical border between North and South Korea.

## Key Events & Developments

- 1945: [[Korea]] was divided at the 38th parallel after Japan's defeat.
- 1948: Separate Korean states formed in north and south.
- June 25, 1950: North Korea invaded South Korea.
- September 15, 1950: The Inch'on landing reversed North Korea's advance.
- November 1950: [[China]] entered the war in force.
- April 1951: Truman dismissed MacArthur.
- July 1951: Armistice talks began.
- July 27, 1953: The armistice ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Armistice)) ended major fighting.

## Key People

- Kim Il-sung ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kim_Il-sung)): North Korean leader who pressed for invasion and signed the armistice.
- Syngman Rhee ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syngman_Rhee)): South Korean president who opposed any divided settlement.
- Harry S. Truman ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harry_S._Truman)): U.S. president who committed forces and later dismissed MacArthur.
- Douglas MacArthur ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Douglas_MacArthur)): UN commander who planned Inch'on and pushed north.
- [[Mao Zedong]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mao_Zedong)): Chinese leader who ordered intervention.
- Peng Dehuai ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peng_Dehuai)): Commander of Chinese forces in Korea.
- Matthew B. Ridgway ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matthew_B._Ridgway)): UN commander who stabilized the war after Chinese offensives.

## Interesting Facts

- The Soviet boycott of the UN Security Council allowed UN action to pass without a veto.
- MiG Alley became the main jet-air-combat zone over northwestern Korea.
- The DMZ came from the armistice, not a peace treaty.
