# Human Intelligence (Intelligence, Human)

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> Human intelligence is the capacity to learn from experience, adapt to new situations, reason with abstractions, and use knowledge to act effectively on environments. Competing theories examine it through psychometric measurement, cognitive processes, and the different ways people adapt themselves or their circumstances.

## Introduction

![[attachments/human_intelligence_001.webp|420]]

*Bell-curve diagram of IQ scores*

Human intelligence ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Human_intelligence)) is the mental quality that includes learning from experience, adapting to new situations, handling abstract concepts, and using knowledge to manipulate the environment. Researchers have disagreed about its exact definition: Lewis M. Terman emphasized abstract thinking, while Edward L. Thorndike emphasized learning and good responses. Modern views often treat adaptation as the key to what intelligence is and what it does.

Adaptation may mean changing oneself, changing the environment, or choosing a new environment. A student learns course material, a physician studies an unfamiliar illness, and an artist revises a painting; each uses perception, learning, memory, reasoning, and problem solving. Intelligence is therefore not one mental process but a purposeful combination of processes directed toward effective adaptation.

## Theories of intelligence

The main theories of [[Intelligence and Counterintelligence (Intelligence)|intelligence]] developed through several paradigms: psychometric measurement, cognitive psychology, approaches joining cognition with context, and biological study. Each asks a different question. Psychometrics asks how abilities are organized and measured; cognitive theories ask what mental processes produce intelligent action; contextual theories ask how environment shapes intelligence; biological theories ask how brain systems support it.

## Psychometric theories

Psychometrics studies intelligence through tests, scores, correlations, and factor analysis. Charles Spearman argued for a general factor, g, underlying many mental tasks, along with specific factors for particular abilities. L.L. Thurstone challenged this by identifying primary mental abilities such as verbal comprehension, number ability, spatial ability, memory, reasoning, and perceptual speed.

John B. Carroll's three-stratum model reorganized psychometric evidence. The first stratum contained many narrow abilities; the second contained broad abilities such as learning, retrieval, speed, visual perception, fluid intelligence, and idea production; the third contained the general factor g. Many psychologists regarded the model as powerful because it reanalyzed hundreds of data sets.

Psychometric theories also face problems. It is not proved that one truly general ability covers all mental abilities, and test scores do not fully show what happens in the mind. Cultural fairness is another problem: a test may measure different abilities in different cultures. Patricia M. Greenfield argued that ability assessments do not easily cross cultures because test performance depends partly on familiarity with the test makers' cultural framework.

## Cognitive theories

Cognitive theories focus on the processes behind intelligent performance. Lee Cronbach urged psychologists to connect the study of individual differences with the study of common mental processes. This mattered because a low score on a verbal analogy test might reflect poor reasoning, limited vocabulary, reading difficulty, or some combination of these.

Earl Hunt, Nancy Frost, and Clifford Lunneborg studied simple information-processing tasks, such as how quickly people judged whether letters were physically the same or shared the same name. They found links between speed of retrieving verbal information and scores on verbal tests. Their work suggested that rapid access to memory can support some forms of intelligence.

Robert J. Sternberg argued that intelligence research should study the processes used in real test tasks, not only low-level reaction-time tasks. In analogy solving, for example, people must encode terms, compare relationships, infer missing relations, map patterns, apply the answer, and justify the solution. This component approach showed that a single correct or incorrect answer may hide several different mental operations.

## Cognition and context

The contextual view treats intelligence as interaction between mental processes and environment. What counts as intelligent depends partly on setting, culture, task, and goals. A person may adapt to an environment, shape it, or select a new one, and abilities valued in one culture or occupation may not be the same as those valued in another.

This approach helps explain why intelligence tests can be useful yet incomplete. Tests may predict some school or work outcomes, but they may miss practical adaptation, creativity, tacit knowledge, or culturally specific skills. Intelligence therefore includes both internal processing and the external world in which those processes are used.

## Biological approaches

Biological theories ask how the brain supports intelligent learning and thought. They do not replace psychological explanations, because intelligence cannot be reduced simply to neurons or computer-like operations. Learning a new word, for example, involves awareness of meaning and also a brain trace that can later be accessed.

Research on hemispheres showed that brain regions contribute differently to intellectual tasks. Jerre Levy and others found that the left hemisphere is often stronger in analytical and language tasks, while the right is often stronger in visual-spatial and holistic tasks. Work by Roger W. Sperry and Levy with split-brain patients showed how separated hemispheres could function independently after the corpus callosum was cut.

These findings do not create a simple left-brain/right-brain formula. Hemispheric specialization is complex, and most intelligent behaviour requires interaction among many systems. Biological research is best understood as complementary to psychometric, cognitive, and contextual approaches.

## Measuring intelligence

Early measurement began with Francis Galton, who tried to assess intelligence through sensory and motor tasks. James McKeen Cattell continued this Galtonian approach and helped establish mental testing, but Clark Wissler showed that such tasks did not predict college grades or other measures well. This weakened the idea that simple sensory speed captured intelligence.

The more influential testing tradition came from Alfred Binet and Theodore Simon in France. Asked in 1904 to help identify children needing special education, Binet rejected trivial sensory tests and measured judgment, comprehension, and reasoning. Lewis Terman brought the test to Stanford University, producing the Stanford-Binet, which has been revised many times and used internationally.

Tests such as the Stanford-Binet and Wechsler scales produced overall IQ scores and, in some cases, separate verbal and performance scores. Verbal subtests might test vocabulary, while performance subtests might require arranging pictures or solving nonverbal problems. These tests helped standardize assessment but also kept alive debates about culture, general ability, and what intelligence tests truly measure.

## Key Events & Developments

- 1921: Lewis M. Terman and Edward L. Thorndike presented different definitions of intelligence.
- 1904: Alfred Binet was asked to develop tests for identifying children needing special education.
- 1957: Lee Cronbach called for unity between individual-differences research and experimental psychology.
- 1993: John B. Carroll proposed the three-stratum model in Human Cognitive Abilities.
- 1997: Patricia M. Greenfield argued that ability assessments do not easily cross cultures.
- 2002: Debate over the general factor g continued in work edited by Robert J. Sternberg and Elena Grigorenko.
