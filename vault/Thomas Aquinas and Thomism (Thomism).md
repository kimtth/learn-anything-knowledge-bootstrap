## Introduction

![[attachments/thomism_001.webp|420]]

*Thomas Aquinas reading*

Thomism ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomism)) is the theology and philosophy of Thomas Aquinas and the later interpretations, uses, and invocations of his thought by schools, religious orders, and Catholic thinkers.

Its history is often divided into four periods: the 14th-15th centuries, the 16th century, the period from about 1850 to the Second Vatican Council, and the period after Vatican II.

## Aquinas's Thought

![[attachments/thomism_002.webp|420]]

*Manuscript page of the Summa Theologiae*

Aquinas created an original synthesis of Aristotelian philosophy and Christian theology. He used Aristotle while also respecting Augustine, Neoplatonism, and the Church Fathers.

Central Thomistic ideas include existence as the supreme act of being, God's unique creative act, the distinction between essence and existence in creatures, and the difference between God and created beings.

Thomas taught that the human soul is a subsistent form united with matter to make human nature. He defended the soul's immortality, the role of sense experience in knowledge, and the mind's reflective activity.

He also held that nature has a natural tendency toward God, that grace perfects and elevates nature, and that blessedness consists in knowing and loving God.

## Early Thomism

In the first two centuries after Aquinas's death, Thomism was debated and criticized. Some propositions connected with his use of [[Aristotle and Aristotelianism|Aristotle]], his view of human nature, and the soul-body relation were condemned in 1273 and 1277.

Over time, Dominican scholars and other defenders clarified and developed Aquinas's thought. Thomism became a major intellectual tradition rather than only the work of one theologian.

## Neo-Thomism

From about 1850 to Vatican II, neo-Thomism became highly influential in Roman Catholic thought. It was often presented as the standard Catholic response to modern philosophy.

Some modern Thomists explored links with idealism, phenomenology, existentialism, and other schools. Others used Thomism as apologetics or as an alternative to modern systems.

Yet neo-Thomism could become rigid, logical, and abstract, sometimes losing the religious depth and historical richness of Aquinas's own writings.

## After Vatican II

The Second Vatican Council ended the near-monopoly of neo-Thomism in Catholic theology and encouraged openness to other methods, cultures, and traditions.

This did not end Thomism. It allowed scholars to return to Aquinas himself and apply his basic principles to new questions about church, culture, mission, anthropology, ethics, and modern thought.

## Interesting Facts

- Thomism ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomism)) was controversial before it became a symbol of Catholic orthodoxy.
- Neo-Thomism ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neo-scholasticism)) was not always identical with Aquinas's own flexible and historically rooted thought.
- Vatican II reduced Thomism's monopoly but also encouraged deeper study of Aquinas himself.
