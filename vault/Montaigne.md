# Montaigne

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> Michel de Montaigne was the French writer and thinker whose Essays made self-examination, skepticism, experience, and conversational inquiry a distinctive literary-philosophical form. His life, readership, and long influence show why the essay became a durable vehicle for testing judgment rather than declaring certainty.

## Introduction

![[attachments/montaigne_001.webp|420]]

*Portrait of Michel de Montaigne*

Michel Eyquem de Montaigne, born in 1533 near Bordeaux and dead in 1592, was the French writer whose Essais ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Essays_%28Montaigne%29)) created the modern essay. His book became one of European literature's most intimate self-portraits, comparable to works by [[Augustine]] and Rousseau.

Montaigne lived during the second half of the 16th century, when Renaissance confidence in human possibility was shaken by the Calvinist Reformation, persecution, and the French Wars of Religion from 1562 to 1598. In that violent setting, he wrote about himself because the self was the one concrete place from which he could examine human nature, uncertainty, judgment, and the search for truth.

## Life

Montaigne was born into a family that had risen from commerce into France's administrative nobility. His father Pierre Eyquem arranged a gentle Latin-only education, so Montaigne learned French only when he was about six. He later studied law and entered public service as a magistrate.

At the Parliament of Bordeaux, Montaigne met Etienne de la Boetie in 1557. Their intense friendship became one of his defining experiences, and La Boetie's death in 1563 later shaped the Essays ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Essay)). Montaigne eventually withdrew to his library tower, though he remained politically active as mayor of Bordeaux and mediator during religious conflict.

## The Essays

![[attachments/montaigne_002.webp|420]]

*Essays title page*

The Essays ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Essay)) begin from Montaigne's sense that his age was full of dissimulation, corruption, violence, and hypocrisy. His skepticism came from distrust of appearances and of externally imposed certainties. The title Essais means attempts: the book offers trial, testing, and self-examination rather than a closed system of doctrine.

Montaigne's skepticism did not deny truth. It protected the search for truth from false certainty, abstractions, and unexamined authority. He turned to concrete experience, especially his own body-and-mind self, because that imperfect self was the only reliable starting point. His writing joins digression, quotation, anecdote, confession, and reflection, making the movement of thought itself part of the subject.

The essay form created by Montaigne is informal, personal, and exploratory. It treats knowledge as provisional and human beings as unstable, diverse, and changing. By refusing fanaticism and grand certainty, the Essays defend judgment, tolerance, self-knowledge, and the honest recognition of human weakness.

## Readership

Readers have interpreted Montaigne differently across time. Marie de Gournay responded with intense enthusiasm, but many contemporaries reduced him to a Stoic moralist and read the Essays as maxims rather than personal inquiry. His frank discussion of body, habits, reactions, and feelings shocked some readers.

In the 17th century, secular readers admired Montaigne as an image of the honnete homme, a cultivated gentleman of manners and discreet wisdom. Religious writers such as Francis of Sales and Blaise Pascal attacked his skepticism and self-absorption. In the 18th century, [[Voltaire]] and Denis Diderot treated him as a forerunner of Enlightenment free thought, while [[Jean-Jacques Rousseau]] recognized him as a model for self-portraiture.

## Influence

Montaigne's influence extended to writers including [[Francis bacon|Francis Bacon]], John Webster, William Shakespeare, Lord Byron, William Makepeace Thackeray, Ralph Waldo Emerson, Virginia Woolf, T.S. Eliot, and Aldous Huxley. Modern readers continue to value him because his opposition to intolerance and fanaticism, his awareness of human destructiveness, and his belief in self-assessment, honesty, and compassion remain forceful in violent or absurd times.

## Key Events & Developments

- 1533: Michel de Montaigne was born at Chateau de Montaigne.
- 1557: Montaigne met Etienne de la Boetie at the Parliament of Bordeaux.
- 1563: La Boetie died, leaving a major emotional and intellectual mark on Montaigne.
- 1562-1598: The French Wars of Religion formed the violent background of Montaigne's thought.
- 1592: Montaigne died at Chateau de Montaigne.

## Key People

- Michel de Montaigne ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michel_de_Montaigne)): Creator of the Essays and of a new personal, skeptical prose form.
- Pierre Eyquem: Montaigne's father, whose educational experiment shaped his early life.
- Etienne de la Boetie ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Etienne_de_la_Boetie)): Montaigne's close friend and model for his reflections on friendship.
- Marie de Gournay ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marie_de_Gournay)): Early reader and admirer who recognized the force of Montaigne's mind.
- [[Jean-Jacques Rousseau]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jean-Jacques_Rousseau)): Later writer who treated Montaigne as a model of self-portraiture.

## Interesting Facts

- Essais ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Essays_%28Montaigne%29)) means attempts, matching Montaigne's method of testing ideas rather than proving a system.
- Montaigne learned Latin before French because of his father's educational plan.
- The Essays shocked some readers because they treated personal habits, bodily details, and private reactions as serious material.
- Montaigne's skepticism was meant to protect truth-seeking from fanaticism and false certainty.
