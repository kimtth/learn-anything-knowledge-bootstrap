# Islamic Arts

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> Islamic arts encompass literary, visual, musical, and architectural traditions developed across Arabic, Persian, Turkish, and other Muslim societies. ## Introduction

Islamic arts ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islamic_art)) include the literary, performing, musical, and visual arts created by peoples who adopted Islam from the 7th century onward. The term can mean arts directly connected with Islamic religion, but it is usually wider: it includes arts made by Muslim peoples even when the subject is secular, regional, or inherited from older cultures.

The field is hard to define because it covers Arabic poetry, Persian miniature painting, Turkish music, mosque architecture, court historiography, carpets, ceramics, shadow theatre, and modern novels. The connection between religion and art is not simple. Islam gave special importance to the word because revelation came through the Quran, and that helped make calligraphy central. Yet representational art, though often limited by religious caution, also appeared in palaces, books, painting, ceramics, and later miniature traditions.

Islamic artistic structure often favors repeated motifs, ornamental elaboration, variation within a rule, and a carpetlike accumulation of detail. Arabesque decoration, Kufic and other scripts, Persian poetic layering, musical variation around modes, and manuscript illumination all show a preference for richly patterned surfaces and carefully controlled variation rather than Western ideals of sculptural form or dramatic unity.

This structure also affects how Islamic works are experienced. A poem, melody, facade, manuscript page, or ceramic bowl often rewards close attention to repeated units rather than a single climax. Meaning emerges through rhythm, allusion, symmetry, interruption, and return. The same habit can make a mosque tile field, a ghazal sequence, or a maqam performance feel ordered and inexhaustibly varied at once.

Western influence appeared in modern times through Impressionist and Cubist painting, free verse, novels, drama, film, and mixed musical idioms. Earlier Europe admired Islamic objects brought by Crusaders or made in Sicily and Spain, and Arabic scientific books influenced Western science. A more objective admiration of Islamic architecture, Persian literature, and Indian miniatures grew from the 17th century onward.

Beauty and transience formed a recurring tension. The Quranic reminder that earthly things perish discouraged some large-scale worldly ambition, while the prophetic saying that God is beautiful and loves beauty encouraged artists, artisans, poets, musicians, and mystics to treat artistic practice as a reflection of divine beauty. A full theory joining all Islamic arts remained unwritten, but many works reveal shared habits of pattern, discipline, ornament, memory, and controlled invention.

## Literary Languages and Regions

Persian literature became important after Muslims entered Iran in 640. Persian influence entered Arabic literary taste by the mid-8th century, and many tales moved from or through Iran into Arabic and then Europe. Persian adopted Arabic metre and rhyme but added forms such as epic and gave the lyric a supple elegance that became one of Islamic literature's finest achievements.

South Asian Islamic literature developed in northwestern India and present-day Pakistan from the 11th century, especially in Delhi and Agra. Persian remained the official language of Muslim India until 1835, and Persian models shaped poetry and historiography. Arabic theological, philosophical, and mystical works, Turkish court writing, Urdu literature, Sindhi, Punjabi, Pashto, Balochi, Bengali Muslim literature, and Kashmiri Islamic themes all formed regional strands.

The Indian subcontinent shows how language and power interacted. Persian served courts, administration, learned style, and elite poetry, while Urdu grew in urban military and literary settings and regional languages carried devotional, folk, and mystical forms. Muslim rulers, Turkish military groups, Persian scribes, local converts, Sufi teachers, and village singers all helped make Indo-Islamic literature multilingual.

Turkish literature developed a classical style after the 14th century, strongly shaped by Persian metrics and vocabulary, while Anatolian and Rumelian folk poetry used popular syllabic metres. Yunus Emre helped shape Turkish mystical song, and later modern Turkish literature drew both from this folk tradition and from Western literary forms.

Central Asian Turkic writing includes Uzbek, Tatar, Kyrgyz, Chagatai, and other traditions. Samarkand, Bukhara, and Fergana were Muslim learning centres after 711, and Central Asian scholars and poets contributed to Arabic and Persian culture. Ali Shir Navai and Babur deliberately applied Persian-Arabic prosody to Turkic languages, while Tajik literature remained essentially Persian.

Regional Islamic literatures often translated shared mystical and courtly themes into local idioms. Sindhi and Punjabi poets used popular metres for emotional religious verse, Pashto preserved strong borderland poetry, Balochi developed ballads and religious folk songs, and Bengali Muslims created religious epics and songs from the 14th and 15th centuries. This regional variety shows that Islamic literature was never only Arabic or Persian, even when Arabic and Persian supplied prestige forms.

## External Characteristics

Classical Islamic literature is often intellectual rather than subjectively lyrical in the Western sense. Its genres and rules descend from pre-Islamic Arabic poetry but were expanded by Persian and other writers. Rhyme, metre, learned imagery, and formal skill were central markers of artistry.

Arabic poetry uses monorhyme, normally one rhyme letter through an entire poem. Metres are quantitative, based on long and short syllables, and classical Arabic recognizes 16 basic metres. Persian and Persian-influenced poetry often extended rhyme by adding repeated syllables or word groups after the dominant consonant, while modern poets across the Islamic world later moved toward strophic forms, free verse, and Western models.

## Genres

The main poetic genres are qasidah, ghazal, qitah, robai, and manavi, with prose forms such as maqamah standing beside them. These genres spread across Arabic, Persian, Turkish, Urdu, and other literary cultures, though each language adapted them differently.

## Qasidah

The qasidah, or purpose poem, was created by pre-Islamic Arabs and usually has 20 to more than 100 verses. Its classic pattern begins with a love prologue, moves to a journey description, and ends in praise of a patron, attack on enemies, tribal pride, religious praise, or lament. It became the major form for panegyric across the Muslim world.

## Ghazal

The ghazal ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ghazal)) probably grew from the qasidah's opening love section and became an independent short love poem, ideally between five and twelve verses. It can be secular, mystical, or both at once. Its diction is lighter and more graceful than the qasidah, making its effect closer to chamber music than public ceremonial display.

## Qitah

The qitah uses monorhyme but begins without the double-rhymed opening of qasidah and ghazal, giving the impression of a section cut from a longer poem. It was less solemn and useful for occasional verse, satire, jokes, word games, chronograms, and everyday topics.

## Robai

The robai is a Persian quatrain with a fixed metre and an a a b a rhyme pattern. It may express religious, erotic, skeptical, or aphoristic thought in compact form. Persian-influenced cultures adopted it widely, and Omar Khayyam's robaiyat became famous in Europe through Edward FitzGerald's free adaptations.

## Manavi

The manavi is a poem in rhyming couplets that allowed Persian and Persian-influenced poets to build epics, romances, didactic works, and mystical narratives over thousands of verses. The form usually begins with praise of God, and its metre and diction were chosen according to whether the subject was heroic, romantic, mystical, or instructional.

## Maqamah

The maqamah ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maqama)), meaning gathering or assembly, is the most typical ornate Arabic prose form. It tells simple stories in highly complicated language full of puns, double meanings, learned tricks, and verbal display. It approaches the short story in structure but values virtuoso style more than plot.

Because style itself was a social accomplishment, the maqamah also displayed education. Its readers were expected to enjoy grammatical puzzles, rare words, quotations, riddles, and verbal agility. This helps explain why some later prose in Persian, Turkish, Arabic, and Indo-Muslim settings became highly rhetorical even when treating history, science, biography, or mysticism.

## Imagery

Islamic literary imagery draws heavily on the Quran, Persian legend, garden scenes, astrology, alchemy, medicine, courtly games, music, painting, and calligraphy. Joseph, David, Solomon, Moses, Jesus, Jamshid, Alexander, the nightingale, the rose, the tulip, the cup of wine, the Magian elder, and the cruel beloved all became recurring symbolic figures.

The beloved could be compared to a Turk because Turkish military elites were associated with beauty, power, whiteness, and domination in Iran and India. Poets were expected to know classical allusions, technical vocabularies, rhetoric, puns, chronograms, and elegant causal metaphors. Good writing was judged by refinement within inherited rules as much as by originality.

## Early Arabic Poetry and Prose

Pre-Islamic poetry treated the poet as a shair, a figure inspired by hidden powers and responsible for defending tribal honour. It reflected Bedouin fatalism, exact natural description, rich vocabulary, and strict metre and rhyme. Religious expression was rare, while horses, camels, lions, desert travel, and tribal memory supplied much of its vocabulary and imagery.

Early Arabic prose included rhymed prose, tribal battle tales, proverbs, and musamarah, or nighttime conversation. Musamarah developed by verbal association rather than plot, moving listeners from anecdote to anecdote and pun to pun. Its structure later influenced maqamah literature and helps explain the episodic style of many classical Arabic works.

## The Abbasids

The Abbasid period began a golden age of Arabic literature after 750, when Baghdad became the centre of an expanding intellectual culture. Hellenistic, Iranian, Indian, and older Middle Eastern elements entered Islamic thought. Grammarians standardized High Arabic, while urban poets, artisans, freed slaves, and non-Arabs transformed the old Bedouin forms.

Bashshar ibn Burd, a blind son of a Persian slave, brought new expressiveness to love poetry and satire. Abu al-Atahiyyah developed ascetic poems on the transitory world. The Abbasid milieu expanded Arabic literature from tribal memory into urban love, satire, philosophy, religious reflection, and scholarly prose.

## Philosophy: Averroes and Avicenna

Islamic philosophy flourished in the Abbasid East and the Maghrib and reached medieval Europe partly through Arabized Jewish and Christian intermediaries. Al-Kindi, al-Farabi, and especially Avicenna wrote systematic philosophical, scientific, medical, religious, and mystical works.

Ibn Tufayl reworked an Avicennan theme in Hayy ibn Yaqzan, the story of a self-taught man on a lonely island who reaches philosophical and prophetic knowledge. Averroes, court physician in Marrakesh, became famous as the great Arab commentator on Aristotle and replied to al-Ghazali's attack on philosophy with The Incoherence of the Incoherence.

## Western Islamic Literature and Historiography

Muslim Spain developed strophic poetry such as the muwashshah and zajal. Ibn Zaydun of Cordoba wrote love poems and a celebrated muwashshah about his city, while Ibn Quzman used dialect and Romance elements in songlike zajal that suggests connections with troubadour traditions.

Islamic historiography produced conquest histories, world histories, dynastic chronicles, court annals, and biographical dictionaries. Ibn Khaldun, in the Muqaddimah to his Kitab al-ibar, created a broad analysis of historical development and social forces, using his North African political experience to build what became one of the first sociological studies of history.

Biographical writing was especially important because learned authority depended on persons and transmission. Scholars, poets, theologians, jurists, rulers, and saints were organized by generation, city, profession, or chain of learning. Such works preserved intellectual networks, reputations, patronage, and the movement of books and teachers across the Islamic world.

## Persian, Pashto, and Ottoman Traditions

Omar Khayyam was a mathematician involved in calendar reform under the Seljuqs, but his quatrains made him famous in the West. The robai was flexible enough for skepticism, love, and mystical wisdom, though many quatrains were later misattributed to famous names.

Persian mystical poetry increasingly used Persian rather than Arabic from the 11th century onward. It produced pious instruction, theoretical reflection, and narrative poetry that became central across Iran, Turkey, Central Asia, and India.

Yunus Emre is regarded as a founder of Turkish literature proper, especially through mystical poems linked to Bektashi and other Sufi traditions. Kaygusuz Abdal, Pir Sultan Abdal, and Shah Ismail I, writing as Khatai, show how Turkish religious poetry could mix mystical, political, popular, and even burlesque tones.

Khushhal Khan Khatak, a Pashtun chief who died in 1689, is called the father of Pashto poetry. He adapted Persian literary sophistication to Pashto and wrote powerful lyrics about freedom, imprisonment, and resistance to Mughal power.

Ottoman literature developed in parallel with Iran and India, following Persian models in secular and religious forms. Seyyid Imad al-Din Nesimi left traces in later religious poetry, Sultan Cem represented early classical charm, Baki mastered ornate language, and Fuzuli of Baghdad wrote distinguished Arabic, Persian, and Azeri Turkish works, including a manavi on Majnun and Layla.

## Modern Islamic Literatures

Modern Arabic poetry moved slowly away from ornate classical imitation. Ahmad Shawqi and Hafiz Ibrahim still followed conventional patterns, while Khalil Mutran introduced stronger structure and subjectivity. Ibrahim al-Mazini and Abbas Mahmud al-Aqqad defended Romantic approaches.

Modern Arabic prose changed through journalism, translation, realism, and reform. Taha Husayn attacked received views of pre-Islamic poetry and wrote the autobiographical Al-Ayyam. Mahmud Taymur, Khalil Gibran, Muhammad Husayn Haykal, and al-Aqqad helped develop short stories, novels, biographies, criticism, and prose shaped by social questions.

Urdu and Persian in India continued after Persian lost official status in 1835. Mirza Asadullah Khan Ghalib mastered Urdu lyrics and Persian style, while Sir Muhammad Iqbal used Persian and Urdu to call Muslim peoples toward self-awareness and active individuality through works such as Asrar-e khudi, Payam-e Mashriq, and Javid-nameh.

Postwar literature after [[World Wars (World War II)|World War II]] grew more socially critical. Naguib Mahfouz transformed the Arabic novel and won the Nobel Prize in 1988. Yusuf Idris and Abd al-Rahman al-Sharqawi wrote about poor villagers. Yasar Kemal's Ince Memed became a major Turkish village novel. Orhan Pamuk later brought Turkish fiction to a worldwide audience and won the Nobel Prize in 2006.

Modern fiction absorbed nearly every current of Western thought while keeping local social pressures at its centre. Young Arab, Turkish, Persian, Iraqi, and Syrian writers used realism, Marxist politics, Brechtian drama, Freudian psychology, existentialism, and social protest to examine poverty, class, censorship, village life, nationalism, and political disappointment. The novel became increasingly accepted in Arabic as the 20th century advanced, with Mahfouz especially important in making it a major literary form.

Modern literary change was therefore not just formal borrowing. New genres gave writers tools for public argument about colonialism, independence, class, gender, language, religion, and state power, while older poetic images and mystical vocabularies remained available for reinvention.

Modern poetry in Arabic, Persian, and Turkish broke many older rules. Nazik al-Malaikah helped create modern Arabic free verse; Mahmoud Darwish gave Palestinian political poetry exceptional force; Abd al-Wahhab al-Bayati combined politics and mysticism; Adonis, Tawfiq al-Saigh, and Salah Abd al-Sabur reworked tradition in daring forms. In Iran, Forugh Farrokhzad wrote powerful free verse, while Turkish modernists included Orhan Veli Kanik, Nazim Hikmet, Ataol Behramoglu, Fazil Husnu Daglarca, and Hilmi Yavuz.

## Music

Islamic music ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islamic_music)) began with Islam around 610 CE and fused pre-Islamic Arabian music with Persian, Byzantine, Turkish, Berber, and Moorish contributions. The Arabian element served as a catalyst, but not every Muslim region adopted the same art music; Indonesia, parts of Africa, and many folk traditions kept distinct styles.

Classical Islamic music is highly melodic and rhythmic, with the voice usually more important than instruments. The individual performer is both composer and interpreter and is encouraged to improvise. Melodies are organized by maqamat, or modes, which include scales, preferred notes, melodic formulas, rhythmic patterns, and intonation rules.

Modern Arab music included influential performers and theorists such as Dahud Hussni, Sayyid Darwish, Abd al-Wahhab, Umm Kulthum, Farid al-Atrash, Fayrouz, Michel Muchaqa, and Mohammed Chehab al-Din. The International Congress of Arabian Music in Cairo in 1932 examined scales, modes, rhythms, and forms.

Musical theory treated secular art music separately from folk songs and religious chants. Distance from the Nile-to-Persia axis usually meant less direct participation in classical Islamic music, so Berber, Moorish, Turkish, Indonesian, African, and other local traditions remained distinct. The shared classical system therefore joined subtle modal rules with strong regional difference rather than replacing every local sound.

## Dance

Dance and theatre received less attention than literature, music, and architecture because some Muslim scholars treated human portrayal and public performance with suspicion. Yet folk dance, court dance, Sufi ritual movement, entertainment dance, and mimetic dance all existed across Islamic societies.

Artistic dance was often performed at caliphal courts by trained women and imitated by aristocrats. The kurrag became a song and dance festival at court. From the late 19th century, professional dance lost ground to U.S., Latin American, and western European cabaret forms, while nationalists after [[World Wars (World War II)|World War II]] tried to revive native motifs.

Folk dance was usually entertainment and could be mimetic or narrative. Turkish dances include many local forms and group dances such as the bar. Iranian folk dances included war dances, comic masked dances, erotic dances, and local traditions; state-supported companies after [[World Wars (World War II)|World War II]] tried to preserve and stage them until the revolutionary regime changed cultural life after 1979.

## Theatre

Islamic theatre flourished less as high art than as popular entertainment, including mime, shadow plays, marionettes, public shows, and Shiite passion plays. In Ottoman lands, sultans sponsored vast pageants with theatre, dances, fireworks, mock battles, circus performance, and music.

Ortaoyunu, or the middle show, was a Turkish open-air comic theatre with simple props, all-male performers, stock characters, improvisation, mimicry, satire, and slapstick. Shadow plays such as Karagoz were widespread in Turkish- and Arabic-speaking regions, especially during Ramadan. Shiite passion plays commemorated the suffering and death of Ali's descendants during Muharram.

Modern Muslim theatre was largely imported from western Europe. In Beirut, Marun al-Naqqash adapted Moliere's The Miser in colloquial Arabic in 1848. Arabic theatre moved through adaptation, translation, and original plays, with Tawfiq al-Hakim and Mahmud Taymur becoming important Egyptian dramatists.

Turkish theatre for live actors began in 1839 beyond older ortaoyunu forms. Pera and Gedik Pasa in Istanbul became early centres, with Armenian and other minority actors prominent. Namk Kemal's Fatherland, first produced in 1873, showed nationalist energy, while censorship and prejudice remained obstacles until republican reforms and state support.

Iranian theatre began in the second half of the 19th century through adaptations and translations of European plays. Akhundof wrote comedies criticizing Persian and Caucasian Muslim society, and later playwrights used satire, patriotism, and social reform. Censorship under Reza Shah slowed development, new companies formed after 1942, and the 1979 Islamic regime curtailed activity.

## Visual Arts: Origins

Islamic visual art did not arise from one ethnic or geographic base. Muslim rule transformed regions with older histories, including Spain, North Africa, Egypt, Syria, Mesopotamia, Iran, Anatolia, and India. Early Islam did not bring a complete art of its own, so Islamic visual culture formed through selection, adaptation, and recomposition of Roman, Byzantine, Sasanian, Iranian, Mediterranean, and local traditions.

Pre-Islamic Arabia contributed little to later Islamic art, apart from the unique sanctuary of the haram in Mecca. The conquests caused relatively little destruction, so the new culture inherited sophisticated architecture, decoration, materials, engineering, and iconographic habits. Islamic art therefore began by adopting and transforming advanced non-Arab visual systems.

Visual unity came less from a single ethnic source than from repeated needs: mosques for communal life, palaces for rulers, books for learning and prestige, objects for courtly exchange, and ornament that could mark a work as part of Islamic culture. Because each region kept older forms, Islamic visual art must be read both locally and across the wider Muslim world.

The absence of a single origin made adaptation a strength. Builders and artisans could use Roman vaulting, Byzantine mosaics, Sasanian palatial ideas, Central Asian brickwork, Indian stone carving, Chinese motifs, and local craft habits without treating them as foreign once they served new patrons and functions. Islamic visual culture therefore grew by recomposition rather than simple replacement.

## Architecture

The mosque was the main new architectural function. Early mosques were not only places of prayer but community centres for political, educational, legal, social, and financial affairs. Early examples at Kufah, Basra, and Al-Fustat used open courts, qiblah-side covered areas, colonnades, walls, and many doors.

The hypostyle mosque dominated from 715 to the 10th century. Major examples appeared at Nishapur, Siraf, Kairouan, Cordoba, Samarra, and Cairo. The Mosque of Ahmad ibn Tulun in Cairo, built in 876-879, preserved Iraqi techniques in a masterpiece of composition. Kairouan emphasized a T-shaped axis, while Cordoba expanded repeatedly between 785 and 988 while keeping stylistic unity.

## Palaces

Early Islamic palaces include Umayyad rural complexes in Syria, Palestine, and Transjordan, such as Al-Rusafah, Qasr al-Hayr East and West, Khirbat al-Mafjar, Qasr Amrah, Mshatta, and Qasr al-Tubah. They were not romantic desert retreats but agricultural or trade centres. Their villas, baths, oratories, throne rooms, courtyards, pools, mosaics, sculpture, and painted decoration show princely life adapting Roman, Sasanian, and local forms.

## Decorative Arts and Early Assessment

Early ceramics developed strongly in Iraq, Egypt, northeastern Iran, Samarkand, and Nishapur. Lustre glazing gave clay a metallic shine, possibly first in Iraq or Egypt. Painted underglaze pottery used vegetal arabesques, inscriptions, birds, animals, abstract human figures, geometry, splashes of colour, and empty-field motifs that quickly became recognizably Islamic.

Early Islamic art consciously sought forms distinct from older non-Islamic traditions. Sometimes it adopted Greco-Roman mosaics, Persian and Roman building technology, or inherited styles. Sometimes it invented new features such as mosque forms, calligraphic ornament, nonfigurative decoration, and ceramic techniques. Its originality lies in selecting from a vast inheritance and making those choices serve a new culture.

Calligraphic ornament became one of the clearest signs of this new culture. Inscriptions could be legible sayings, blessings, Quranic phrases, or decorative bands whose visual force mattered as much as their words. Together with vegetal arabesque and geometry, writing made surfaces intellectually and spiritually charged without depending on monumental sculpture.

This mattered because figural imagery had an uneven status. Palaces, books, ceramics, and secular objects could include animals, rulers, musicians, hunters, or lovers, while religious buildings tended to favour writing, geometry, vegetal ornament, and controlled abstraction. The result was not an absence of images but a careful sorting of image, word, place, and function.

## Fatimid, Seljuq, and Western Islamic Art

Fatimid architecture in Cairo used traditional hypostyle plans at Al-Azhar and Al-Hakim but added formal facades, gates, corner towers, local urban mosques, and mausoleums. Al-Aqmar and Al-Salih served local needs in dense city spaces. Fatimid decoration moved toward naturalistic vegetal and animal carving, geometric star patterns, elaborate inscriptions, lustre ceramics, rock crystal, textiles, and luxury objects.

Seljuq architecture diversified across Iran, Anatolia, Iraq, and Central Asia. The Great Mosque of Esfahan, completed around 1130, made the four-eyvan courtyard and dome major features of later mosque design. Seljuq builders also developed madrasahs, caravanserais, bridges, tombs, and varied regional types.

Western Islamic art in Morocco, Spain, and the Maghrib between the 11th and 15th centuries included austere Almoravid and Almohad hypostyle mosques, massive square minarets, city gates with horseshoe arches, military architecture, madrasahs such as Bu Inaniyah at Fes, mausoleums, and the Alhambra, whose refined stucco, geometry, inscriptions, and spatial design stood apart from North African austerity.

## Mamluk, Mongol, and Timurid Arts

Mamluk art in Egypt and Syria created nearly 3,000 known monuments, especially in Cairo. It did not invent many new building types but perfected clusters combining mosque, madrasah, hospital, mausoleum, and urban facade. Sultan Hasan's madrasah in Cairo is a major four-eyvan monument. Mamluk decoration favored carved stone, portals, qiblah walls, bold inscriptions, metalwork, glass mosque lamps, woodwork, and manuscripts.

Il-Khanid Iran recovered after Mongol destruction when Mahmud Ghazan took power in 1295. Tabriz, Soltaniyeh, and other centres revived art and scholarship. Il-Khanid architecture refined eyvans, facades, muqarnas, coloured tile, scale, and state monumentality, especially in Oljeitu's mausoleum at Soltaniyeh and the Ali Shah mosque at Tabriz.

Timurid architecture ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Timurid_architecture)) began with the sanctuary of Ahmad Yasavi in 1390 and flourished at Herat, Samarkand, Mashhad, Khargird, Tayabad, Baku, and Tabriz. The Timurid style used large scale, brilliant tile, planned urban ensembles, and royal shrines to project power and refinement.

Persian painting changed in the Mongol and Timurid periods. Manuscripts of Rashid al-Din's Jami al-tawarikh show Chinese influence and historical narrative. The Great Mongol Shah-nameh dramatically illustrated Iranian epic. Later Timurid painting, especially under Behzad, added more realistic poses, daily-life details, psychological observation, and renewed energy to older formulas.

## Ottoman, Safavid, and Mughal Arts

![[attachments/islamic_arts_001.webp|420]]

*Reza Abbasi - Two Lovers (1630)*

Late imperial Islamic art was centred in the Ottoman, Safavid, and Mughal empires. These states differed culturally but shared a memory of Islamic forms, exchanged artists and gifts, collected each other's works, and saw themselves as belonging to one broad artistic world.

Ottoman architecture drew from Anatolian Islamic buildings and Byzantine forms, especially Hagia Sophia. Tekkes, mosques, mausoleums, madrasahs, and kulliyes developed domed central plans. Sinan, the greatest Ottoman architect, built the Sehzade and Suleyman complexes and the Selim Mosque at Edirne, achieving clarity, logic, controlled domes, and skyline-defining monuments.

Safavid architecture reached a peak at Esfahan under Abbas I. The Maydan-e Shah, now Maydan-e Emam, unified mosque, bazaar, funerary mosque, palace gate, gardens, polo ground, parades, trade, worship, and royal pleasure in one vast urban composition. The Masjed-e Shah, Shaykh Lutf Allah mosque, Ali Qapu, and Chehel Sotun show Safavid control of scale, tile, space, and symbolic planning.

Safavid painting became self-conscious about style. Manuscripts such as the Houghton Shah-nameh, Jami Haft owrang, and Hafez illustrations show grand miniature painting. Behzad, Sultan Muhammad, Mir Sayyid Ali, Aqa Mirak, Aqa Reza, and Reza Abbasi shaped styles ranging from crowded idealized compositions to single figures, portraits, genre scenes, satire, and drawings of daily life. Safavid carpet workshops transformed a nomadic and folk medium into royal and urban art.

Safavid painting also reveals the growing status of individual artists. Names were preserved, older miniatures were collected and copied, and patrons could gather or dismiss workshops according to court taste. Alongside large manuscript projects, single-page drawings and albums encouraged connoisseurship, portrait-like figures, and close attention to style itself.

Mughal art joined Islamic court traditions with South Asian forms and Timurid-Iranian models. Architecture developed from earlier Indian Islamic mosques and mausoleums to Humayun's tomb, Fatehpur Sikri, and the Taj Mahal. Mughal builders perfected red sandstone and marble, while Mughal painters adapted Safavid models to portraits, legends, histories, local events, and genre scenes.

Mughal patronage also made observation a courtly skill. Painters recorded emperors, nobles, animals, battles, ceremonies, and wonders of nature with increasing attention to likeness and incident. Architecture likewise used garden settings, axial planning, marble inlay, domes, and monumental gateways to combine Timurid memory, Indian craftsmanship, and imperial display.

## Modern Visual Arts

Modern Islamic visual culture was shaped by European occupation, imported technology, new structural materials, revival of traditional crafts, and uncertainty over whether artists should preserve artisanal forms or create modern art. Damascus, for example, used steel roofing in the Suq al-Hamidiyah and Eastern Art Nouveau decoration at the Hejaz railway station.

After independence in the 1940s and 1950s, Muslim countries built housing, universities, public complexes, and modern cities. Wealthy Arab states and prerevolutionary Iran transformed urban and rural landscapes, while many projects copied international styles rather than developing a unified new Islamic visual language.

Modern artists and architects therefore faced a difficult choice. They could revive calligraphy, geometry, craft, courtyard planning, and regional materials, or they could join international modernism through concrete, steel, abstraction, photography, film, and new urban forms. The strongest modern works often treated Islamic inheritance as a living vocabulary rather than as decoration copied from the past.

## Form, Faith, and Cultural Exchange

Islamic arts ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islamic_art)) describe artistic traditions produced in societies where Islam was influential, not a single uniform style or the art of one ethnicity. They extend across the Middle East, North Africa, Iberia, sub-Saharan Africa, Central Asia, South Asia, Southeast Asia, and diaspora communities. Local materials, pre-Islamic traditions, trade routes, languages, and political patrons gave each region distinctive forms even when artists shared religious references and technical knowledge.

Calligraphy became especially important because the Qurʾan is understood as divine revelation in Arabic. Beautiful writing could decorate manuscripts, buildings, textiles, ceramics, metalwork, and coins. Calligraphy is not merely decoration: it can make sacred words visible, identify patrons, record dates, state religious formulas, or frame a work’s meaning. Arabic script also developed many styles, from angular Kufic forms to flowing cursive scripts, and spread into languages such as Persian, Ottoman Turkish, and Urdu.

Islamic art is often associated with geometric pattern, vegetal ornament, and arabesque, but this association should not be mistaken for a total ban on images. Figurative painting and sculpture appeared in many secular, courtly, scientific, and literary settings, especially in Persianate, Ottoman, and Mughal worlds. Religious spaces often avoided large human or animal images, yet artists found immense variety in architecture, light, water, colour, inscription, and repeating pattern. The result is an art of visual rhythm as well as representation.

Architecture brought these forms together. Mosques, madrasas, shrines, palaces, caravanserais, baths, gardens, and tombs were shaped by local climate and building traditions. Domes, vaults, courtyards, minarets, iwans, tilework, carved stone, stucco, wood, and water features could organize worship, study, hospitality, government, or remembrance. A building’s meaning often came from how people moved through it, heard sound within it, read inscriptions, and experienced shade, reflection, and enclosure.

Trade and empire carried artists and techniques across wide distances. Ceramic glazes, carpet weaving, paper, metalwork, book illumination, textiles, and architectural forms travelled between workshops and courts. Colonialism, nationalism, museum collecting, tourism, and contemporary design later changed how Islamic arts were made and displayed. Modern artists continue to use Islamic visual traditions to explore identity, memory, migration, faith, politics, and abstraction without simply repeating historical styles.

## Key Events & Developments

- 610 CE Islamic music ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islamic_music)): A new musical art began to form around the advent of Islam.
- 640 Muslim armies: Entered Iran, opening the long Persian role in Islamic literature.
- 711 Central Asian Islam: Samarkand, Bukhara, and Fergana entered the Muslim cultural sphere.
- 750 Abbasid rule: Baghdad became the centre of a golden age of Arabic literature.
- 785-988 Cordoba mosque: Expanded in stages into a major western Islamic monument.
- 1130 Great Mosque of Esfahan: Helped make the four-eyvan plan influential.
- 1258 Mongol conquest: Destruction in Iran and Iraq eventually gave way to Il-Khanid renewal.
- 1295 Mahmud Ghazan: Restored stability and patronage in Mongol Iran.
- 1390 Timurid architecture ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Timurid_architecture)): The sanctuary of Ahmad Yasavi opened a major building age.
- 1848 Marun al-Naqqash: Presented an Arabic adaptation of Moliere in Beirut.
- 1932 Cairo music congress: Studied scales, modes, rhythms, and forms.
- 1988 Naguib Mahfouz: Became the first Arabic Nobel laureate in literature.

## Key People

- Jalal al-Din Rumi ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rumi)): Mystic poet whose work exemplifies layered Persian spiritual expression.
- Omar Khayyam ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Omar_Khayyam)): Mathematician and quatrain poet whose robaiyat shaped Western views of Persian poetry.
- Yunus Emre ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yunus_Emre)): Foundational Turkish mystical poet.
- Averroes ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Averroes)): Philosopher and Aristotle commentator influential in medieval Europe.
- Avicenna ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Avicenna)): Philosopher, physician, and writer of scientific and mystical works.
- Ibn Khaldun ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ibn_Khaldun)): Historian who developed a broad sociological view of history.
- Fuzuli of Baghdad ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fuzuli_%28poet%29)): Poet in Arabic, Persian, and Azeri Turkish.
- Sir Muhammad Iqbal ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muhammad_Iqbal)): Modern poet who reinterpreted mystical ideas as self-development.
- Naguib Mahfouz ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Naguib_Mahfouz)): Master of the Arabic novel and Nobel laureate.
- Umm Kulthum ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Umm_Kulthum)): Major modern Arab singer.
- Sinan ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sinan)): Greatest Ottoman architect.
- Behzad ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Behzad)): Persian painter associated with realism, detail, and psychological observation.
- Abbas I: Safavid ruler who remade Esfahan.

## Interesting Facts

- Calligraphy became central because the divine word was a major artistic medium in Islam.
- Arabesque links geometry, ornament, and spiritual ideas through repeated patterns.
- The maqamah ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maqama)) values verbal virtuosity more than plot.
- The ghazal ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ghazal)) can be secular love poetry and mystical religious poetry at the same time.
- The mosque originally functioned as a whole community centre, not only a prayer building.
- Lustre ceramics gave clay objects a metallic shine.
- Karagoz shadow plays and ortaoyunu comedies show the strength of popular theatre.
- The Maydan-e Emam in Esfahan unites worship, trade, royal pleasure, ceremony, and urban design.
