# Dickens

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> Charles Dickens was a Victorian English novelist whose vivid characters, serial narratives, and social criticism depicted poverty, industrial change, law, family, and urban life.

## Introduction

Charles John Huffam Dickens died on June 9, 1870, at Gad's Hill near Chatham in Kent, and he is usually seen as the greatest English novelist of the Victorian age. His major books include A Christmas Carol ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A_Christmas_Carol)), David Copperfield ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Copperfield)), Bleak House ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bleak_House)), A Tale of Two Cities ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A_Tale_of_Two_Cities)), Great Expectations ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Expectations)), and Our Mutual Friend. His popularity was wider than that of any earlier writer in his lifetime because his books could speak to simple readers and educated readers alike, to the poor and to the queen, and because new technology helped his fame spread quickly across the world.

## Early years

Dickens left Portsmouth while still an infant, and his happiest childhood years were spent in Chatham between 1817 and 1822, a place he often returned to in fiction. From 1822 he lived in [[London]] until he moved permanently to Gad's Hill in 1860. His family belonged to the middle class, but it was a fragile kind of respectability, because his grandfathers came from very different and socially uncertain backgrounds.

## Beginning of literary career

![[attachments/dickens_001.webp|420]]

*Bust of Charles Dickens by Giovanni Fontana*

Dickens was strongly attracted to the theatre and almost became a professional actor in 1832. In 1833 he began publishing stories and descriptive essays in newspapers and magazines, and these pieces were later collected as Sketches by Boz ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sketches_by_Boz)) in 1836. That same month he was asked to supply a comic serial narrative for illustrations, and only seven weeks later the first part of Pickwick Papers ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Pickwick_Papers)) appeared. The result was immediate: within a few months the book was a sensation, and Dickens had become the best-known author in England.

## Pickwick and early novels

Pickwick Papers ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Pickwick_Papers)) gave new life to old comic forms while also introducing many of the major qualities that would run through Dickens's later fiction. These qualities include his attacks on social evils, his deep knowledge of [[London]], his use of pathos and the macabre, his delight in Christmas, his generosity of spirit, and his vivid gift for creating speech and character. The book was written quickly and sometimes unevenly, but it still succeeded as a first novel and helped create a new kind of popular literature.

## Christmas books

![[attachments/dickens_002.webp|420]]

*Dickens Gurney head*

A Christmas Carol ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A_Christmas_Carol)) was written in only a few weeks and became the first of the Christmas books that Dickens created almost by accident. It is often called the one great Christmas myth of modern literature. Dickens's idea of Christmas philosophy went beyond simple seasonal cheer, because he wanted the spirit of Christmas to shape the whole year and his family life as well as his books. That close bond between Dickens and Christmas became part of his public image.

## Renown

Contemporaries saw Dickens as a lively public man who mixed constantly in society and took part in benevolent causes. R. H. Horne described him as an active, practical, genial figure with strong confidence and unusual energy. He was said to be an excellent speaker after dinner, a top shorthand reporter, a strong amateur actor, and later one of the best periodical editors and dramatic readers of the day. Even apart from his fiction, people believed he would have succeeded in almost any career he chose.

## Journalism

Dickens's journalistic ambition found a lasting form in Household Words and later All the Year Round. These weekly magazines mixed fiction, poetry, and essays, and they reached huge circulations. Dickens contributed major serials to them, including Hard Times, A Tale of Two Cities ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A_Tale_of_Two_Cities)), Great Expectations ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Expectations)), and A Child's History of England, as well as essays later gathered in Reprinted Pieces and The Uncommercial Traveller.

## Novels of the 1850s

The novels of the 1850s - Bleak House ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bleak_House)), Hard Times, and Little Dorrit - are described as darker than his earlier fiction. They give a broad and increasingly sombre picture of contemporary society, and at the time some readers treated them as propaganda for temporary issues. They are more than that, because Dickens transformed topical material into a larger artistic vision that still matters beyond the immediate moment.

## Personal unhappiness

By 1855, Dickens's spirits had dropped and his confidence in the future had weakened. His friend John Forster linked the unrest partly to politics, because the Crimean War exposed government weakness and distracted attention from poverty and hunger at home. Dickens himself wrote with growing despair about Parliament and representative government, and he admitted that his political faith had almost disappeared.

## Public readings

Dickens's love affair with his reading public took a new form in his public readings from his own works. He had already given occasional readings for charity since 1853, but the paid series began in 1858 as a way to distract himself from domestic unhappiness. These performances drew on his theatrical talent, his delight in audiences, and the fact that his fiction could be performed very effectively aloud.

## Final novels

In his later fiction, Dickens remained inventive even as his health weakened. A Tale of Two Cities ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A_Tale_of_Two_Cities)) was more experimental than his earlier books, with less emphasis on character, dialogue, and humour, and more on compact narrative. Sydney Carton's self-sacrifice moved Dickens and many readers, while Dr. Manette became a strong serious character. The French Revolution scenes are vivid, though the historical understanding is not deep.

## Farewell readings

Dickens's health was already weak after the hard American tour, and the strain of the fierce Sikes and Nancy reading made it worse. His farewell reading tour was abandoned when, in April 1869, he collapsed. Even then, he began another novel, but he was already exhausted and carrying several heavy burdens at once.

## Assessment

Ralph Waldo Emerson shows how mysterious Dickens seemed even to admirers. Emerson felt that Dickens had too much talent for his genius and said that there was no simple key to such a many-sided artist. Dickens's fiction is linked to his practical, journalistic, oratorical, and histrionic gifts, but those gifts do not explain him fully.

## Key Events & Developments

- 1817-1822: Dickens's happiest childhood years are spent in Chatham, a place that later appears often in his fiction.
- 1833-1836: He begins publishing essays and stories, and Sketches by Boz ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sketches_by_Boz)) and Pickwick Papers ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Pickwick_Papers)) launch his literary career.
- 1843: A Christmas Carol ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A_Christmas_Carol)) creates the model for his Christmas books and becomes his best-known seasonal work.
- 1846: He helps found the Daily News, showing his wish to shape public opinion beyond fiction.
- 1850-1860: Household Words and All the Year Round become the main home of his journalism and major serial fiction.
- 1858: Dickens and Catherine Dickens separate, and his public readings begin as a new emotional and professional outlet.
- 1869: His farewell reading tour ends after he collapses.
- 1870: Dickens dies while The Mystery of Edwin Drood remains unfinished.

## Key People

- Charles John Huffam Dickens ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_John_Huffam_Dickens)): The central figure, a novelist, editor, journalist, and public reader whose fiction defined the Victorian age.
- Catherine Dickens ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catherine_Dickens)): His wife, whose separation from him in 1858 became one of the major private crises of his later life.
- Ellen Ternan ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ellen_Ternan)): The actress whose relationship with Dickens was deeply important in his later years.
- John Forster: Dickens's friend and biographer, and an important witness to his private unhappiness.
- Angela Burdett-Coutts ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Angela_Burdett-Coutts)): His wealthy friend, who supported the reformatory home he helped direct.
- George Cruikshank ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Cruikshank)) and Phiz ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phiz)): The illustrators who helped shape the visual power and public appeal of his fiction.
- R. H. Horne ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/R._H._Horne)) and Ralph Waldo Emerson ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ralph_Waldo_Emerson)): Critical voices who show how strongly Dickens impressed his contemporaries.
- Thackeray ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thackeray)) and Mark Twain ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mark_Twain)): Writers who reflect the scale of Dickens's fame and performance legacy.

## Interesting Facts

- A Christmas Carol ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A_Christmas_Carol)) was written in only a few weeks and became the most powerful of Dickens's Christmas books.
- Dickens could often earn more by reading aloud than by writing new fiction.
- His novels were often adapted for the stage so quickly that many theatres could be performing them at the same time.
- He was both a great entertainer and a serious moral observer of society.
- His public image stayed strong even during personal unhappiness and scandal.
- He treated [[London]] as his dominant fictional landscape and knew the city in an unusually detailed way.
