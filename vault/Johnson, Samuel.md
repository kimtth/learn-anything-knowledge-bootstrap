## Introduction

Samuel Johnson, known as Dr. Johnson, was born at Lichfield, Staffordshire, on September 18, 1709, and died in London on December 13, 1784. He was an English critic, biographer, essayist, poet, lexicographer, conversationalist, and one of the defining literary figures of the 18th century.

Johnson's life joined hardship and achievement. He called literary biographies mournful narratives and considered his own life radically wretched, yet the sickly boy from the Midlands became the foremost writer and talker of his age. Later generations treated him as almost synonymous with late 18th-century English letters.

## Early Life

Michael Johnson, his father, was a bookseller, and Sarah Johnson was his mother. Johnson suffered from scrofula, facial scarring, near blindness in one eye, and noticeable tics that may have been Tourette syndrome. Yet he was physically strong, fond of riding, walking, and swimming, and remembered for force as well as intellect.

His schooling began at Lichfield grammar school in 1717 under the learned but brutal John Hunter. Johnson later entered Pembroke College, Oxford, in 1728, but poverty forced him to leave after 13 months. At Oxford he read William Law's A Serious Call to a Devout and Holy Life, which made concern for his soul central to his life.

Johnson's first publication came in 1731, with a Latin translation of Alexander Pope's Messiah. After failed teaching attempts, he joined Edmund Hector in Birmingham, translated Jeronimo Lobo's Voyage to Abyssinia, and in 1735 married Elizabeth Porter, a widow older than himself whom he found intelligent and attractive.

The Edial school near Lichfield failed, but it connected Johnson with David Garrick, later the greatest English actor of the age. Johnson had begun the tragedy Irene there, and in 1737 he and Garrick left for London, beginning Johnson's long struggle as a professional writer.

## London And Early Writing

The Gentleman's Magazine became Johnson's early base from 1738. He wrote biographies, poetry, political satire, and parliamentary speeches disguised as Debates in the Senate of Magna Lilliputia. These debates were political fictions based on limited information, and Johnson later had misgivings because readers treated them as authentic.

[[London]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/London)), published anonymously in 1738, was Johnson's first major poem. An imitation of Juvenal's third satire, it attacked corruption in the metropolis and contained the famous line, in capitals, that worth rises slowly when depressed by poverty. Pope admired the poem and tried unsuccessfully to find patronage for its author.

The Life of Richard Savage, published in 1744, was Johnson's first major biography. Johnson defended his troubled friend while also judging him severely, concluding that long negligence and irregularity could make knowledge useless, wit ridiculous, and genius contemptible. The work showed Johnson's gift for mingling pathos, satire, moral judgment, and sympathy.

## Maturity And The Dictionary

The Vanity of Human Wishes, published in 1749, was Johnson's most impressive poem and the first work issued under his name. Another Juvenalian imitation, it surveyed the futility of human desires for greatness and happiness and replaced Stoic self-command with the Christian virtue of patience.

The Rambler, issued twice weekly from 1750 to 1752, opened the most productive decade of Johnson's career. He wrote more than 200 essays on daily life, literature, authorship, novels, biography, wisdom, and piety. He valued the series highly, calling his other works wine and water but The Rambler pure wine.

A Dictionary of the English Language appeared in two volumes in 1755. Earlier English dictionaries existed, but none matched Johnson's scale. He supplied etymologies, strong definitions, and examples of usage drawn mainly from writers between the Elizabethan age and his own time, creating a literary anthology and helping establish an English canon.

The Dictionary also displayed Johnson's personality. He knew language could not be fixed permanently, despite his wish for order. His famous letter to Lord Chesterfield, who praised the work only when publication was near, asserted the dignity of the author against delayed aristocratic patronage.

## Essays, Fiction, And Pension

The Literary Magazine carried Johnson's criticism of England's policy in the Seven Years' War and his important book reviews. His attack on Soame Jenyns's study of evil and suffering was a model of biting moral criticism, rejecting easy talk about the poor from those who had not experienced misery.

The Idler, Johnson's final essay series, ran from 1758 to 1760. Lighter than The Rambler, it still contained serious moral fictions and reflections, including praise of autobiography and a satiric self-portrait as Mr. Sober, a consummate idler.

Rasselas, published in 1759, was Johnson's only long fiction and was written in one week to pay for his mother's funeral. Its travelers leave the Happy Valley to seek a wise choice of life, only to discover disappointment in rulers, shepherds, philosophers, scholars, hermits, and daydreams. In 1762, Johnson received a pension of 300 pounds a year.

## Friendship And Household

*Samuel Johnson by Joshua Reynolds*

James Boswell met Johnson in 1763 and later made him the subject of the most famous biography in English. Boswell's detailed journals preserved Johnson's conversation and personality, while Johnson's aphorisms and social presence made him one of the most quoted English writers.

The Club, founded in 1764 by Johnson and Joshua Reynolds, included figures such as Edmund Burke, Oliver Goldsmith, David Garrick, Edward Gibbon, Richard Brinsley Sheridan, [[Adam Smith]], and William Jones. Johnson also kept a crowded household that included Anna Williams, Robert Levett, Elizabeth Desmoulins, Poll Carmichael, and Francis Barber, whom he made his heir.

Henry Thrale and Hester Thrale gave Johnson another home at Streatham from 1765. Their friendship brought travel, hospitality, and emotional renewal, though it ended painfully after Henry's death and Hester's later marriage to Gabriel Piozzi.

## Shakespeare, Politics, And Travel

Johnson's Shakespeare edition appeared in eight volumes in 1765. He edited, annotated, and wrote a preface that is often considered his greatest literary criticism. He defended Shakespeare against rigid Neoclassical demands for unity of time, place, and action, arguing that audiences understand stage convention and that nature outranks critical rules.

Political pamphlets in the early 1770s supported government positions but also reflected Johnson's own views. Taxation No Tyranny attacked the American Continental Congress, but Johnson also asked why the loudest cries for liberty came from drivers of enslaved people, a sign of his consistent opposition to slavery and colonial oppression.

The Hebrides journey of 1773, made with Boswell, satisfied Johnson's long curiosity about unfamiliar places and ways of life. A Journey to the Western Islands of Scotland, published in 1775, combined travel writing, history, and social observation.

## Lives Of The Poets And Last Years

The Lives of the Poets, published from 1779 to 1781, began as short prefaces for an edition of English poetry but became Johnson's last great work. He loved biography, anecdote, and moral judgment, and his lives combined narrative, character summary, and criticism of poems.

Johnson's literary judgments could separate life from art. He disliked some poets personally but did not simply condemn their work; he praised Gray's Elegy, examined Cowley and the Metaphysical poets, and wrote his strongest life on Pope. His method helped shape later ideas of English literature.

Johnson's final years were shadowed by illness, melancholy, deaths of friends, and the break with Hester Thrale. A devout Anglican, he feared judgment and damnation, yet in 1784 experienced a late serenity that he treated as a sign of possible salvation. He died on December 13, 1784, and was buried in Westminster Abbey.

## Achievement And Reputation

Johnson's criticism, Dictionary, Shakespeare edition, and Lives of the Poets helped invent what is now called English Literature as an object of study. He rejected the role of literary dictator and preferred criticism that combined delight, instruction, judgment, and the writer's own power of performance.

His moral range was broader than the arch-Tory caricature. Johnson valued monarchy but often distrusted kings, defended unpopular positions against slavery, colonialism, and mistreatment of Indigenous peoples, and urged better treatment of prisoners, prostitutes, and the poor. In the 19th century his personality dominated attention; in the 20th century his writings regained prominence.
