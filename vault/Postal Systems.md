# Postal Systems

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> Postal systems are usually public or quasi-public institutions that carry addressed letters and parcels with regularity, speed, security, and prepaid rates. Their history explains how varied older communications arrangements developed into standardized state, national, and international systems supporting commerce and everyday connection.

## Introduction

A postal system ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mail)) is an institution, usually controlled by a government or quasi-government agency, that lets a person send a letter, packet, or parcel to an address in the same country or abroad. It promises regularity, speed, security, and a relatively simple system of prepaid fees based mainly on weight and sometimes on service speed.

Modern postal systems ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mail)) feel familiar because senders normally prepay through stamps, franking machines, or printed postage, and the addressee usually pays nothing. Yet these features are historically recent. Earlier societies needed written communication, but it took a long process for varied systems to become the modern pattern of state monopoly service.

Postal networks also created other public functions. A countrywide network of post offices reached remote areas where banks might not operate, so many governments used post offices for savings, money orders, pensions, family allowances, and other social benefits. The post office therefore became both a communication system and a public utility.

## History

Ancient governments needed message-relay systems to rule large territories. Early references appear in Egypt about 2000 BC and in China under the Chou dynasty about a thousand years later. China may have developed the posthouse relay system first, and the Mongol rulers later brought it to a high level.

The Persian Empire under Cyrus in the 6th century BC used mounted messengers and posthouses. Greek historians such as Herodotus and Xenophon praised it. Greek city-states had messengers, but their political division prevented a unified postal system.

The Roman cursus publicus ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cursus_publicus)) was the most developed ancient postal system. It connected the empire through relay stages along major roads and served military and administrative control. At its height, messengers could reportedly cover more than 170 miles in a day and night, a speed Europe did not rival until the 19th century.

The fall of the western Roman Empire did not immediately destroy the system. Some new rulers, including Theodoric of the Ostrogoths, saw its value. Over time, however, medieval communication became more fragmented, and later postal development depended on royal, commercial, ecclesiastical, and urban needs.

## United States

In colonial America, the first official overseas mail reference dates to 1639, but real development came after Benjamin Franklin became deputy postmaster general for the colonies in 1753. Through inspection and reform, he improved frequency, speed, and range of service. In 1775, he became the first U.S. postmaster general.

U.S. postal service expanded rapidly after independence. Revenue grew from $37,935 in 1790 to $1,707,000 by 1829, when the postmaster general became a Cabinet member. Deficits became common as the system expanded across a growing country, but service access and quality improved dramatically.

The Rowland Hill principle of uniform rate influenced U.S. reform. A single uniform rate regardless of distance was adopted in 1863, after an interim two-rate system from 1845, and postage stamps appeared in 1847. Street letter boxes began free collection in 1858, city free delivery began in 1863, and rural free delivery later extended the system beyond towns.

## Great Britain

The British system grew sharply after the 1840 reforms associated with Rowland Hill. By 1870, postal traffic was 10 times its prereform level. Registration, postcards, book and printed-paper rates, samples, and parcel post expanded services beyond ordinary letters.

The post office also became a financial institution. It took over money orders in 1838, opened a savings bank in 1861, introduced postal orders in 1881, and created parcel post in 1883. In the 20th century it became a major agency for paying social security benefits, beginning with old-age pensions in 1908.

The British post office increasingly became a commercial enterprise rather than only a revenue department. It became a public corporation in 1969. The British Telecommunications Act of 1981 separated postal and banking operations from telecommunications and allowed some competition in certain mail categories.

## France

The French Revolution abolished the farming system, in which the right to operate postal service was sold. The system became a national monopoly in 1801, and a general Directorate of Posts attached to the Ministry of Finance was created in 1804.

France modernized through the 19th century with better administration and transport. Its first postage stamp appeared on January 1, 1849, supporting prepayment and simpler rates. Postal money orders began in 1817, registered letters in 1829, parcel post and savings bank services in 1881, and postal checks in 1918.

Transport innovation mattered. Rail began carrying mail in 1841 and was established by 1892. Steamships served more than 100 ports, bicycles and motor vehicles aided collection, and airmail flights began in 1918. Aeropostale pilots such as Antoine de Saint-Exupery and Juan Mermoz helped build France's airmail tradition.

## Germany and Italy

German postal development was slowed by political fragmentation. The Thurn and Taxis service, begun in the 16th century, survived until 1867 because no unified state displaced it. After Prussian-led unification and the German Empire in 1871, a state monopoly for letters and newspapers was created.

Germany later gave its postal administration financial autonomy in 1924, allowing semicommercial operation while still considering national economic and social factors. It developed postal and financial services, passenger transport, social security functions, rail and road networks, and a night airmail service inaugurated in 1961.

[[Italy]] had Roman postal roots but did not gain a modern national system until the Kingdom of Italy was unified. In 1862, the new postal service received a monopoly over letters, printed papers, and newspapers and adopted a uniform tariff. Italy added registration, postal orders, savings bank services, postal checks, parcel post, and later mechanized centres with electronic address reading.

## Former Soviet Union

After the 1917 October Revolution, postal services in the Soviet Union developed greatly, especially in Central Asian republics where post offices grew to 30 or 40 times the 1913 number. Before dissolution in 1991, the Soviet network had about 90,000 post offices, many in rural areas that had little service before 1917.

Airmail was vital because of the Soviet Union's size, carrying about 60 percent of postal traffic. Facsimile and satellite transmission helped distribute central press publications for decentralized printing. Large sorting centres, mechanized public offices, national automation of postal orders, and computers improved mail handling and administration.

## China

China's postal system ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mail)) dates to the Chou dynasty, and Confucius referred to the speed of post-station service in the late 6th century BC. By the late 3rd century BC, couriers changed mounts at staging posts about nine miles apart. Under the Han dynasty, the network expanded with Central Asian contacts.

The T'ang dynasty increased staging posts and allowed correspondence by road or river. The Sung dynasty created a parallel express service for military correspondence, whose couriers could travel 124 miles a day or more in urgent cases. Marco Polo later described the high quality of the Yuan, or Mongol, system to Europe.

Until the end of the 14th century, the Chinese post served official documents. In the 15th century, private post offices appeared for traders, carrying correspondence and payment transfers. The Ch'ing dynasty later had thousands of such private offices alongside official service.

## India, Pakistan, and Japan

In [[India]], Ibn Battutah described an official system of mounted couriers and runners in the 14th century. Under Akbar in the 16th century, the Mughal system reached a high point with about 2,000 miles of post roads. British rule reestablished official service in 1766, opened it to the public in 1774, and created the Imperial Post in 1837.

The modern Indian Post Office began in 1854 when systems were merged under a director general and a uniform rate was introduced. India joined the Universal Postal Union by 1876, added COD, insured service, money orders, railway traveling post offices, and later airmail.

[[Pakistan]] inherited older Muslim and British postal traditions when it became independent in 1947. Because East and West Pakistan were separated by 1,000 miles of Indian territory, aviation and sea links were essential. After Bangladesh separated in 1971, Pakistan improved internal services, especially in rural areas.

[[Japan]] created a comprehensive government postal service after Hisoka Maejima proposed it in 1870. Service began between Tokyo and Osaka in 1871 and expanded nationwide in 1872. In 1873, postal service became a monopoly, private couriers were banned, uniform tariffs were adopted, and stamps and postcards were introduced.

## Developing Countries and Modernization

Efficient postal systems ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mail)) in developing countries matter both domestically and internationally. They connect rural and urban communities, support administration, carry money and benefits, and allow participation in global mail networks. Weak postal service can isolate communities and limit economic and social development.

[[Modernization and Industrialization (Modernization)|Modernization]] often involves mechanization, postal codes, sorting centres, transport networks, commercial accounting, and new customer-oriented policies. Yet postal systems must also preserve public utility functions, especially where private firms would not serve remote or poor areas.
