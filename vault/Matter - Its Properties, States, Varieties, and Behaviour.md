# Matter - Its Properties, States, Varieties, and Behaviour

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> Matter is physical substance with mass and spatial extension, built from atoms, molecules, ions, crystals, and larger structures. Its states, phase changes, composition, and relation to energy explain ordinary materials as well as nuclear processes and modern physical theory.

## What Matter Is

Matter ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matter)) is anything that has mass and occupies space. Everyday matter includes air, water, rocks, living organisms, metals, plastics, and planets. At smaller scales it is made of atoms, which contain a nucleus of protons and neutrons surrounded by electrons. Atoms can join to form molecules, ions, crystals, and larger structures whose properties depend on both their composition and arrangement.

Matter and energy are closely related but not identical in ordinary use. Matter refers to material entities, while energy describes the capacity to cause change, such as motion, heat, light, or chemical transformation. Modern physics shows that mass can be converted into other forms of energy and vice versa in nuclear processes. This does not mean that ordinary objects routinely disappear into energy; it means mass is one form in which energy can be stored.

## States and Phase Changes

The familiar states of matter are solid, liquid, and gas. In a solid, particles remain close together and usually vibrate around fixed positions, giving the material a stable shape. In a liquid, particles remain close but can move past one another, allowing flow while keeping a nearly fixed volume. In a gas, particles are widely separated and move freely, so a gas expands to fill its container and can be compressed.

Phase changes occur when temperature or pressure changes the balance between particle motion and attractive forces. Melting changes a solid into a liquid; freezing does the reverse. Evaporation and boiling change liquid to gas, while condensation changes gas to liquid. Sublimation is a direct change from solid to gas. These changes involve energy transfer: heat may be absorbed to separate particles or released when particles come together.

## Structure and Properties

Materials behave differently because of [[Chemical Bonding|chemical bonding]] and structure. Metals conduct electricity because some electrons can move through the material. Ionic compounds contain electrically charged particles held together by attraction. Covalent substances use shared electrons; some form molecules, while others form large networks such as diamond or quartz. Polymers contain long chains of repeating molecular units, giving plastics, rubbers, and biological materials distinctive properties.

A crystalline solid has particles arranged in a repeating pattern, while an amorphous solid such as glass lacks long-range regular order. Structure affects strength, transparency, electrical conductivity, melting point, and chemical reactivity. Engineers can change material behaviour through alloying, heating, cooling, pressure, coating, and shaping. A material’s useful properties are therefore not determined only by which elements it contains but also by how those elements are organized.

## Beyond Everyday Matter

Plasma is a gas in which enough electrons have separated from atoms to create charged particles. It occurs in stars, lightning, fluorescent lamps, and some industrial processes. Matter can also form gels, foams, liquid crystals, granular materials, and colloids, each combining properties of more familiar states. These forms show that the simple solid-liquid-gas classification is useful but incomplete.

At the smallest known scales, protons and neutrons are made of quarks, while electrons belong to a group called leptons. Quantum mechanics ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_mechanics)) describes their behaviour using probabilities, fields, and discrete energy levels rather than the simple paths familiar from everyday objects. The Higgs field helps explain why some fundamental particles have mass, while dark matter is inferred from gravitational effects even though it has not been directly identified through light.

## Mass, Gravity, and Conservation

Mass measures inertia: how strongly an object resists a change in motion. Mass also determines gravitational interaction. Isaac Newton described gravity as an attraction between masses, while Albert Einstein described gravity as the curvature of spacetime caused by mass and energy. Both descriptions are useful in different conditions, with Einstein’s theory giving more accurate predictions in strong gravitational fields and at high precision.

In chemical reactions, atoms are rearranged but the total amount of matter is effectively conserved. In nuclear reactions, a tiny difference in mass can become a large amount of energy because of the relation $E=mc^2$. Understanding matter therefore requires movement between scales: the same physical laws connect particle interactions, materials, living systems, planets, and stars.
