## Introduction

Chess ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chess)) is a two-player board game in which each side tries to force the enemy king into checkmate ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Checkmate)). It is one of the oldest and most widely played games in the world, with origins in [[India]] around the 6th century and later spread through [[Asia]], the Middle East, and [[Europe]]. By the 15th century it had become known as the royal game, and by the 20th century it had become a global competitive sport.

Chess is both a game of rules and a subject of theory. Its history includes changes in piece design, notation, tournament play, and championship competition, while its theory includes strategic schools, openings, endgame studies, and composed problems.

## The Board

*Chessboard with all pieces in their starting position*

The chessboard has 64 squares, arranged in eight files and eight ranks. The board is placed so that each player has a light square at the right-hand corner. Standard coordinates and algebraic notation make it possible to record individual moves and entire games precisely.

The board’s structure is the basis for all play. Because every square can be named, analysis, instruction, and tournament recording become possible in a shared language.

## Moves

Each player commands an army of 16 pieces: king, queen, rook, bishop, knight, and pawn. White moves first, and the players alternate turns. Although the pieces are unequal in power, they all obey fixed rules that define how the game develops.

The general aim is to attack the enemy king while protecting one’s own. Piece movement, capture, and special rules such as pawn promotion and castling all serve this larger purpose.

## King

The king moves one square in any direction. The king is the most important piece because the game ends if it is captured or cannot escape capture. Its limited mobility makes it both vulnerable and central to the strategy of the game.

## Rook

The rook moves vertically or horizontally any number of unobstructed squares. Each player begins with two rooks on the corners of the first rank. Rooks were once called castles, reflecting older terminology and piece design.

## Bishop

The bishop moves along diagonals and therefore stays on one colour of square for the whole game. Each player begins with two bishops, one on a light diagonal and one on a dark diagonal. This gives bishops a distinctly long-range role in open positions.

## Queen

The queen is the most powerful piece because it combines the movement of rook and bishop. Starting near the centre of the back rank, it has wide influence over files, ranks, and diagonals and often becomes the chief attacking piece in midgame play.

## Knight

The knight moves in an L-shape, two squares in one direction and one square perpendicular to that. It can jump over other pieces, which makes it tactically unusual and often valuable in crowded positions. A knight always lands on the opposite colour from the one it started on.

## Capturing

Most pieces capture by moving to the square occupied by an enemy piece and removing it from the board. This rule shows that chess is a game of direct spatial conflict in which the pieces act as both movers and attackers. Captures help determine material balance and often decide the course of a game.

## Pawns

The pawn is the most numerous and the most unusual piece. It moves forward but captures diagonally, can advance two squares on its first move, may capture en passant, and must promote when it reaches the final rank. Pawn rules are central to the game because they shape structure, timing, and long-term strategy.

Pawn movement also underlies many strategic ideas, because pawns cannot retreat. Their fixed direction makes them both restrictive and powerful, since they define the long-term shape of the position.

## Castling

Castling is the only move that uses two pieces at once. It moves the king two squares toward a rook and brings that rook to the square crossed by the king. Castling is allowed only if neither piece has moved before and if no square the king crosses is attacked.

This special move is important because it both protects the king and activates the rook. It is therefore one of the key opening and middlegame procedures in serious play.

## Notation

Algebraic notation is the standard way to record moves. Castling kingside is written 0-0, queenside 0-0-0, captures use x or :, checks use + or ch, and checkmate is marked with # or ++. The notation also allows analysts to add evaluative symbols such as ! and ? when commenting on games.

Notation makes chess teachable and sharable. It allows every game to be reconstructed, studied, and compared long after it has been played.

## History

Chess traces back to early Indian board games, especially chaturanga and later shatranj. From there the game spread along trading and cultural routes into the Islamic world, Europe, and Asia. Regional variants changed the pieces, the board, and even the method of play, but the core idea of capturing a king remained.

The standard modern game took shape slowly. Rules and set design became more stable by the early 19th century, after which chess entered the age of clubs, tournaments, books, and championship play.

## Competitive Chess

Organized competitive chess rose in the 19th and 20th centuries. National and international events emerged, world championship play grew, and the game took on an increasingly professional character. Women’s chess also rose, especially once the Soviet Union promoted separate women’s tournaments.

Chess became a modern competitive institution. Chess was no longer only a courtly or scholarly pastime but a mass international sport with titles, rankings, and federated competition.

## Chess Theory and the Masters

Chess theory evolved through major players and schools. Wilhelm Steinitz introduced a scientific approach to the game, and Emanuel Lasker refined defensive and psychological ideas. Siegbert Tarrasch emphasized space and mobility, while later players and writers extended and challenged those principles.

The broader point is that chess theory changed as masters found new ways to understand initiative, pawn structure, compensation, and attack. The game developed a living body of strategic knowledge rather than a fixed set of rules.

## Hypermodernism

Hypermodernism arose as a reaction against older rules that treated central pawn occupation as the main opening goal. Players such as Aron Nimzowitsch and Richard Réti argued that the centre could be controlled from a distance rather than occupied directly. Their ideas introduced new openings, new pawn strategies, and a stronger appreciation of flexibility.

Hypermodernism marked a major shift in chess thinking after [[World Wars (World War I)|World War I]]. It did not abolish classical principles, but it showed that the classical centre could be handled in more than one way.

## Studies

Chess studies are composed endgame-like positions with a specific task, such as White to play and win or draw. Studies often contain only a few pieces, but their solutions can be elaborate and instructive. They serve as both art and training, because they reveal hidden tactical and strategic possibilities.

Studies have a long history, and some were created by leading players and composers. They often teach practical themes such as perpetual check, stalemate, underpromotion, and surprising resourcefulness.

## Problems
Chess problems are compositions that ask for a mate or another specific result under artificial conditions. Originality, economy, and a clear thematic idea all matter. Problems became especially sophisticated in the modern period, with recurring named themes and carefully crafted stipulations.

Heterodox problems include helpmates, selfmates, and retractors. These forms show how chess composition became an intellectual art distinct from practical play while still relying on the same pieces and rules.

## Chess as Art

Studies and problems extend the imaginative reach of chess. They test the logic of the rules, expose hidden possibilities, and reward both beauty and precision. In this way the game’s artistic side mirrors its competitive side.

## Key Events & Developments

- ancient India: The game develops from early forms such as chaturanga.
- medieval period: Chess spreads through the Islamic world and Europe, where its rules and symbolism keep changing.
- modern era: Tournament play, standardized rules, and computer analysis turn chess into a global competitive game.
