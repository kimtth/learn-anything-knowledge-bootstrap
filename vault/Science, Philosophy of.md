## Introduction

![[attachments/science_philosophy_001_mw.webp|420]]

*Portrait of Galileo Galilei holding a telescope*

Philosophy of science ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philosophy_of_science)) studies scientific inquiry from a philosophical point of view. It asks about the aims, methods, assumptions, evidence, explanations, laws, theories, values, and social responsibilities of modern science. It also examines metaphysical, epistemological, and ethical issues raised by scientific practice.

The history of science ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Science)) and philosophy was long intertwined. Before the 19th century, the term science did not have its modern meaning, and many philosophers were also natural philosophers. [[Aristotle and Aristotelianism|Aristotle]] worked in biology and logic; [[Descartes and Cartesianism (Descartes)|Descartes]] developed analytic geometry and optics; Leibniz helped invent calculus; and Kant proposed a nebular hypothesis for the solar system.

Great scientists also reflected on method. [[Galileo]] defended mathematics and experiment in studying nature. [[Newton]] explained his method in presenting the system of the world. [[Lavoisier]], [[Maxwell]], [[Darwin]], and [[Einstein]] likewise wrote about the character of scientific inquiry.

## From Natural Philosophy to General Philosophy of Science

Modern philosophy of science separates special philosophy of science from general philosophy of science. Special fields examine problems in physics, biology, psychology, neuroscience, and other sciences. General philosophy of science studies broad questions about confirmation, explanation, theory, law, reduction, progress, objectivity, and rationality.

This general field became especially self-conscious in the early 20th century. It continued older inquiries about logic and method but used the new tools of formal logic and philosophy of language. It asked why scientific inquiry seemed successful while traditional metaphysics often produced endless disagreement.

## Logical Positivism and Logical Empiricism

![[attachments/science_philosophy_002.webp|420]]

*Collage of logical positivist philosophers*

Logical positivism ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logical_positivism)) and logical empiricism arose from early 20th-century work in mathematical logic by Gottlob Frege, Bertrand Russell, and David Hilbert. The Vienna Circle sought criteria of meaningfulness that would distinguish scientific claims from traditional metaphysical questions they considered meaningless.

For logical empiricists, philosophy's task was not to build speculative systems but to clarify the language, logic, and methods of science. They admired science's firm achievements and tried to reconstruct its reasoning through formal methods. Their project shaped English-language philosophy of science for decades.

## Confirmation and Hypothetico-Deductivism

One central problem was confirmation: how evidence supports a scientific hypothesis. The simple hypothetico-deductive view says that a hypothesis is confirmed when predictions deduced from it are observed. Carl Hempel showed why this attractive idea is too simple.

If any evidence confirming a hypothesis also confirms every consequence of that hypothesis, then absurd results follow. A successful prediction from [[Newton]]'s theory would also confirm Newtonian theory plus any arbitrary claim, such as elves causing global warming. This shows that confirmation cannot be reduced to simple prediction alone.

Rudolf Carnap and others tried to give confirmation a quantitative form. Bayesian approaches used probability theory to explain how evidence can raise the probability of a hypothesis. Bayes's theorem shows why surprising evidence can strongly support a hypothesis if that evidence would be very unlikely without it.

## Bayesianism and Its Problems

Bayesian confirmation depends on assigning prior probabilities and probabilities of evidence given hypotheses. This is easy in card games but hard for real scientific theories. There is no obvious list of all possible scientific hypotheses with equal initial probabilities.

Objective Bayesians seek rational rules for assigning prior probabilities. Subjective Bayesians allow different rational investigators to begin with different priors, so long as they obey probability rules and avoid assigning 0 or 1 to ordinary empirical claims. Mathematical convergence theorems suggest that long-run evidence can reduce disagreement.

Critics note that science must make decisions in finite time. John Maynard Keynes's remark that in the long run we are all dead captures the problem. Two rational Bayesians with different priors may remain far apart after all available evidence has been collected.

## Eliminativism, Falsification, and Underdetermination

Some philosophers rejected confirmation and emphasized falsification. Karl Popper argued that scientific hypotheses cannot be conclusively verified but can be tested by risky predictions that might refute them. Scientific rationality then becomes a matter of bold conjectures and severe attempts at refutation.

The difficulty is that experiments test a hypothesis together with background assumptions, instruments, auxiliary theories, and observation claims. Pierre Duhem had already argued that a failed prediction does not identify which part of the total system is wrong. Scientists often adjust auxiliary assumptions rather than abandon a central theory.

W.V.O. Quine radicalized this problem as underdetermination: evidence may not uniquely determine theory. In a weak logical sense, any hypothesis can be preserved by enough adjustments. Stronger versions claim that rational scientific criteria may still leave multiple theories compatible with all evidence.

## Scientific Explanation

Logical empiricists also studied scientific explanation. Hempel's deductive-nomological model treated explanation as a logical argument: from laws of nature and initial conditions, the event to be explained is deduced. Statistical explanations were treated similarly through probability.

This view faced problems. Real explanations are often explanation sketches, not complete arguments. A person may explain a stain by saying he spilled coffee, relying on background knowledge to fill details. Hempel distinguished the partial answer given to a questioner from the complete objective explanation behind it.

Another problem is the idea of a scientific law. Not every true generalization is a law. All houses on one street might be brick, but that is accidental. The statement that all uranium spheres are smaller than one kilometre reflects natural law because large uranium spheres are physically unstable.

## Laws, Necessity, and Asymmetry

Laws of nature seem to express necessity, not just regularity. Logical empiricists were wary of necessity because of David Hume's critique of necessary connections. They tried to explain necessity through lawlike regularity, but they never fully analyzed what makes a true generalization lawlike.

Hempel's model also allowed arguments that are formally correct but not explanatory. The length of a flagpole shadow can be explained from the pole's height, the Sun's angle, and the law of light propagation. But one can also deduce the pole's height from the shadow and the same law, which does not seem to explain why the pole has that height. Explanation therefore has direction and causal structure not captured by deduction alone.

## Theories and Theoretical Terms

Logical empiricists treated scientific theories as axiomatic systems with observational and theoretical vocabularies. Observational terms were thought to be learned directly, while theoretical terms such as electron were connected to observation by correspondence rules.

Some empiricists believed theoretical terms are partly interpreted by these rules. Others defended instrumentalism, treating the theoretical vocabulary as an uninterpreted calculus for generating observational predictions. Instrumentalists could still distinguish science from metaphysics because theoretical terms in science had a functional role in successful prediction.

Later philosophers questioned the simple distinction between observational and theoretical language. Observation itself can depend on theory, instruments, training, and background assumptions. This weakened the old logical-empiricist picture of theories as neatly connected to neutral observation.

## Reduction and Unity of Science

Logical empiricists often hoped for a unified science, where higher-level sciences could be reduced to lower-level ones. Ernest Nagel's model of reduction required deriving one theory from another with bridge principles connecting their vocabularies.

[[Principles of Genetics and Heredity (Genetics)|Genetics]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Genetics)) showed the limits of this dream. Mendelian explanations of independent assortment depend on pairing and separation of chromosomes during meiosis. A detailed molecular account of all chemical events may not answer the same explanatory question. Molecular biology can deepen classical genetics, but not every explanation is replaced by a lower-level one.

The failure of simple reduction weakens the idea that a final theory could derive all explanations of natural phenomena. Later philosophers used models and local explanations to emphasize the piecemeal, plural, and disunified character of actual science.

## Historicist Challenge

In the mid-20th century, Stephen Toulmin, Paul Feyerabend, and Thomas Kuhn challenged logical empiricism by studying scientific history. They argued that actual scientific change did not fit neat accounts of confirmation, method, and rational progress.

Feyerabend pushed the challenge furthest with the slogan anything goes, meaning that no single methodological rule accounts for all celebrated scientific breakthroughs. Copernican astronomy, modern chemistry, Darwinian evolution, and relativity all involved departures from established standards.

This raised hard questions about scientific progress. If progress is not simple accumulation of truth, and if winners and losers in past controversies did not divide neatly into rational and irrational camps, then philosophers needed a more historical account of reason, evidence, and change.

## Thomas Kuhn and Scientific Revolutions

![[attachments/science_philosophy_003.webp|420]]

*Portrait of Thomas Kuhn*

Thomas Kuhn's The Structure of Scientific Revolutions became a central text. Kuhn argued that normal science works within paradigms: shared exemplars, methods, standards, and assumptions. Crises occur when anomalies accumulate, and revolutions replace one paradigm with another.

The Copernican revolution illustrates the problem. In 1543, Copernicanism had virtues and defects, as did traditional astronomy. Later, [[Kepler]]'s ellipses and [[Galileo]]'s telescopic and dynamical arguments shifted the balance. By the mid-17th century, rejecting Copernicanism became unreasonable, but this judgment emerged historically, not from a single timeless rule.

Kuhn's challenge produced opposite reactions. Some philosophers thought the worries had been answered and that philosophy of science could continue as before. Others concluded that rationality and objectivity were illusions. More careful responses tried to explain how values, evidence, problem-solving, and historical context interact.

## Science, Society, and Values

Later philosophy of science increasingly studied social dimensions of inquiry. Scientific work depends on communities, instruments, institutions, funding, testimony, trust, peer criticism, and standards of evidence. Values can influence research questions, classifications, risk judgments, and applications.

This does not mean that science is merely politics or opinion. It means that objectivity may depend on social practices that expose claims to criticism, diversify viewpoints, and test results. Ethical issues also arise because science affects technology, medicine, environment, warfare, and public policy.

## Key Events & Developments

- 17th century: Bacon, [[Descartes and Cartesianism (Descartes)|Descartes]], [[Galileo]], and [[Newton]] shaped modern reflection on scientific method.
- Early 20th century: The Vienna Circle made logical empiricism central to philosophy of science.
- 1920s-1930s: Carnap and Hempel developed formal studies of confirmation and explanation.
- 1962: Thomas Kuhn published The Structure of Scientific Revolutions.
- Late 20th century: Philosophers increasingly studied models, disunity, realism, social values, and scientific practice.
