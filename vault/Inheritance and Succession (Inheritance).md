# Inheritance and Succession (Inheritance)

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> Inheritance, or succession, is the legally regulated transfer of a deceased person's property to heirs. Its rules differ across civil-law and Anglo-American traditions and depend on individual property, wills, probate, executors, administrators, family relationships, and the distribution of estates.

## Introduction

![[attachments/inheritance_001.webp|420]]

*Cuneiform tablet concerning succession and inheritance*

Inheritance ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inheritance)), also called succession, is the devolution of property to an heir or heirs after the owner's death. The word also denotes the property that passes. Modern societies regulate inheritance in detail through law. Civil-law systems usually call the field the law of succession, while Anglo-American law historically distinguished descent of real estate from distribution of personal estate and often uses terms such as probate law, wills, succession, executors, administrators, or decedents' estates.

Inheritance requires individual property. It cannot occur where goods belong primarily to a group or where objects are destroyed, abandoned, or consumed at death because of ritual, fear of spirits, or belief in magical contamination. Inheritance therefore developed alongside durable property, individual ownership, family organization, and rules for transferring rights beyond death.

Two major policy questions dominate inheritance law. One is how far an owner may decide who receives property after death, called freedom of testation. The other is whether estates should be divided among several heirs or preserved undivided for one successor. The answers shape wealth distribution, family power, landholding, and social policy.

## Property, Family, and Testation

Freedom of testation lets property owners adapt inheritance to family circumstances, reward dependents, prefer a disabled child, support charity, or influence family behaviour. It can stimulate economic activity by assuring owners that property can be directed after death. Yet it can also permit spite, arbitrariness, or disinheritance of close relatives.

Limits on testation exist everywhere. Testamentary conditions contrary to public morals, such as a gift conditioned on divorce, may be invalid or treated as unconditional. The rule against perpetuities and similar civil-law doctrines prevent owners from controlling property indefinitely from beyond the grave. Many systems protect surviving spouses, children, or other close kin through forced shares, family provision, community property, dower, homestead rights, or allowances.

Surviving-spouse protection is especially important. In U.S. states, devices such as dower, indefeasible share, community property, homestead rights, and family allowances prevent total disinheritance. The indefeasible share is widespread, but it can be weakened if the deceased transferred property before death; some states therefore let spouse rights reach certain inter vivos transfers.

Dower once gave a surviving spouse a life estate in real property, but it declined because it applied only to real estate and interfered with sale, gift, mortgage, will, and title security. Modern property wealth often lies in personal assets such as savings and shares, so broader spouse-protection devices became more practical.

## Intestacy

Intestacy governs property when a person dies without an effective will. Intestacy laws reflect assumptions about family duty, kinship, marriage, and social policy, but they often preserve older family structures after society changes. The Roman Twelve Tables, English statutes, New York law, the Uniform Probate Code, French law, and Soviet law all show different ways to rank relatives and protect spouses.

England reorganized intestacy through the Administration of Estates Act of 1925, later amended by the Intestates' Estates Act of 1952 and family-provision legislation. Relatives are grouped into classes, with descendants first, then parents, full siblings and their descendants, half siblings and their descendants, grandparents, and uncles or aunts of the full or half blood. A surviving spouse receives personal chattels and statutory shares depending on whether descendants or close relatives survive.

New York uses a parentelic system under the Estates, Powers and Trusts Law of 1966, as amended. Relatives take up to the grandparental parentela. If children or their issue survive, the spouse takes a money amount and half the residue; if no descendants survive, the spouse takes all.

The Uniform Probate Code, approved in 1969 and later amended, influenced U.S. law reform. It gives the surviving spouse larger or smaller shares depending on surviving descendants, parents, whether the descendants are also descendants of the spouse, and length of marriage. Remaining property passes to descendants, parents, siblings and their descendants, grandparents, and descendants of grandparents by representation or side of family.

[[France]] bases intestacy on the Civil Code of 1804, repeatedly amended. Relatives are grouped into classes: descendants; parents, siblings, and sibling descendants; other ascendants; and other collaterals up to a limited degree. The surviving spouse also has rights in marital acquests and may receive life interests or outright shares. Civil Solidarity Partnerships recognized in 1999 extended some succession rules to unmarried cohabiting partners.

Inheritance taxation and intestacy can serve social policy. Some systems limit remote kin so estates pass to the public treasury rather than distant relatives. Progressive taxes or compulsory partition can reduce wealth concentration. Soviet inheritance law showed how succession rules can be repeatedly modified to serve changing ideological and economic goals.

## Wills

A will, or testament, is the legal transaction by which an owner disposes of property for death. In older Anglo-American usage, will referred to real property and testament to personal property, but modern usage usually treats the terms as interchangeable. A will is both the act of disposition and the written instrument expressing it.

Will formalities usually follow one of three forms. The witnessed will, developed in England and prevailing in common-law jurisdictions, requires the testator's signature and witnesses. The holographic will, developed in French customary law and accepted in many civil-law countries and some U.S. states, is handwritten and signed by the testator without witnesses. The notarial will, rooted in late Roman practice, is executed before a legally trained notary.

Emergency oral wills, or nuncupative wills, are sometimes allowed for soldiers, sailors, or people in immediate danger of death, but they are exceptional. Ordinary wills must be written because death prevents the testator from clarifying intent, and the law must protect against fraud, mistake, coercion, obsolete default rules, and administrative confusion.

Drafting difficulty is substantial, especially in the United States, where state laws differ. A will may need to address taxes, debts, creditor priority, abatement of legacies, predeceased beneficiaries, executor powers, administration formalities, and later legal changes. Periodic legal review is often advisable.

## Probate and Administration

Probate determines whether an instrument is a valid will and supports administration of the estate. In England, ecclesiastical court jurisdiction narrowed until the court reforms of the 1870s created the Probate, Divorce and Admiralty Division of the High Court. Executors derive power from the will once probate is granted, while administrators are appointed when needed.

United States probate developed differently because the colonies lacked English-style ecclesiastical courts. Specialized Probate Courts emerged, called Surrogate's Court in New York and Orphan's Court in Pennsylvania. U.S. probate courts often handle estates of deceased persons, minors, and mentally incompetent persons, and historically supervised estate administration more extensively than English courts.

Administration winds up the estate. A will-like document is filed, an executor or interested person petitions for probate, representatives gather assets, pay creditors and taxes, manage disputes, and distribute what remains. If no executor is named, statutes usually prefer the surviving spouse, then relatives in intestate order, then creditors, and finally public officers.

The Uniform Probate Code offered simpler and cheaper administration for ordinary estates and supervised administration for insolvent, disputed, or risky estates. This approach treats simple estates as normal and reserves close judicial administration for exceptional cases, bringing U.S. practice closer to English and civil-law patterns.

## Key Events & Developments

- Roman Twelve Tables: Early Roman intestacy rules reflected the household ruled by the paterfamilias.
- Statute of Frauds: The English witnessed-will tradition developed especially through the 1677 statute.
- Administration of Estates Act: England reorganized intestacy in 1925.
- New York statute: The Estates, Powers and Trusts Law appeared in 1966.
- Uniform Probate Code ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uniform_Probate_Code)): Approved in 1969 and repeatedly amended afterward.
- French Civil Code: Enacted in 1804 and repeatedly changed for succession matters.
- Civil Solidarity Partnerships: French succession rules expanded for cohabiting partners in 1999.

## Key People

- Alfred Bernhard Nobel ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alfred_Nobel)): His will is a famous example of testamentary disposition.
- Johannes van legal tradition: Civil-law notaries and common-law probate judges shaped different inheritance procedures.
- Roman paterfamilias: The household head whose property and family status shaped early succession concepts.

## Interesting Facts

- Inheritance ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inheritance)) cannot exist without durable property that outlives its owner.
- Dower protected spouses but later became a problem for marketable land titles.
- Holographic wills can be valid without witnesses if handwritten and signed where recognized.
- Probate courts in the United States may also handle minors and mentally incompetent persons.
- Inheritance tax can be used as a tool to reduce wealth concentration.
