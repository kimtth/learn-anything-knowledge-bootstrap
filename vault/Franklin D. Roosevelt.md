# Franklin D. Roosevelt

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> Franklin D. Roosevelt was the four-term U.S. president whose New Deal expanded federal action during the Great Depression and whose leadership guided the country through World War II. His political skill and crisis leadership made him one of the most consequential twentieth-century presidents.

## Introduction

Franklin D. Roosevelt, often called FDR, was the 32nd president of the United States and the only president elected to the office four times. He led the nation through the Great Depression and World War II, two of the most severe crises of the 20th century. His presidency is remembered not only for leadership during war and economic collapse, but also for the expansion of federal power through the New Deal ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Deal)). Roosevelt’s style was energetic, persuasive, and highly political, and he became one of the most influential presidents in American history.

## Early life and political rise

![[attachments/franklin_d_roosevelt_003.webp|420]]

*FDR 1944 Color Portrait*

Roosevelt was born on January 30, 1882, in Hyde Park, New York, the only child of James Roosevelt and Sara Delano Roosevelt. He grew up in a privileged family background, but he also developed a strong sense of public duty. His education at Groton and Harvard did not produce exceptional academic results, yet it exposed him to the political ideas of his fifth cousin, Theodore Roosevelt, who championed a larger role for government in the economy.

During his Harvard years, Roosevelt met Eleanor Roosevelt, whom he later married. Her concern for the poor helped deepen his awareness of social hardship. Roosevelt entered politics as a Democrat and rose through New York state government before becoming governor in 1929. His experience in the state government helped shape the reformist outlook that later defined his national leadership.

## The New Deal

Roosevelt’s first major national goal was to confront the Great Depression. In 1932, he won a landslide election against Herbert Hoover, promising decisive action. His administration moved quickly in what became known as the Hundred Days. It closed banks temporarily, passed emergency banking legislation, and used radio broadcasts known as fireside chats ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fireside_chats)) to restore public confidence.

The New Deal included a wide range of programs aimed at relief, recovery, and reform. The Agricultural Adjustment Act tried to raise farm prices by limiting production, while the National Industrial Recovery Act sought to stabilize industry. The Civilian Conservation Corps employed young men on public works, and new financial reforms strengthened banking regulation and deposit insurance. Roosevelt’s policies were experimental, but they reflected his belief that government had to act directly to protect citizens from economic collapse.

## The Second New Deal and domestic reforms

By 1935, Roosevelt recognized that the first phase of recovery was not enough. He pushed a second set of reforms, including the Social Security Act, the Works Progress Administration, and the Wagner Act. Social Security created unemployment insurance, disability protection, and old-age pensions. The WPA employed millions of Americans on roads, buildings, conservation, and cultural projects. The Wagner Act strengthened labor rights and supported unions.

These reforms made the New Deal more permanent and more ambitious. Roosevelt also faced opposition from the Supreme Court, which struck down several key measures. His attempt to enlarge the Court became one of the major political controversies of his presidency, though the plan failed. In time, the Court became more supportive of New Deal legislation.

## Foreign policy and World War II

![[attachments/franklin_d_roosevelt_002.webp|420]]

*Joseph Stalin, Franklin D Roosevelt and Winston Churchill, in Teheran, 1943, edit*

Roosevelt’s domestic agenda did not remove him from international affairs. As war loomed in Europe, he moved the United States closer to support for Britain and its allies. The Lend-Lease Act allowed America to provide aid without immediate cash payment, and Roosevelt’s Atlantic Charter with Winston Churchill expressed a vision of democratic peace and self-determination.

After Japan attacked Pearl Harbor on December 7, 1941, the United States entered World War II. Roosevelt became the chief architect of the Allied coalition against Germany, Italy, and Japan. He worked closely with Churchill and later with Joseph Stalin, though relations with the Soviet Union remained tense. At the Yalta Conference in 1945, Roosevelt sought cooperation for the postwar world, but Soviet actions in Eastern Europe soon showed the limits of that cooperation.

## Declining health and legacy

By the end of his presidency, Roosevelt’s health had seriously declined. He had suffered from arteriosclerosis for years and was visibly weakened by the time of the Yalta Conference. He won a fourth term in 1944, but his health continued to fail. He died on April 12, 1945, at Warm Springs, Georgia, while sitting for a portrait.

Roosevelt’s legacy remains deeply contested. His supporters saw him as the defender of democracy and the leader who rescued the nation from economic collapse and war. His critics viewed him as an overreach of federal power and a threat to free-market principles. Even so, he remains one of the most influential presidents in American history because he reshaped the role of government and the expectations placed on it.
