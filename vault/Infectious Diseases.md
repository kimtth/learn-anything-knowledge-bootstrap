# Infectious Diseases

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> Infectious disease occurs when microorganisms impair health, while infection more broadly includes invasion and replication with the body's tissue response and may be clinically silent. The distinction explains how vaccines can produce controlled immune stimulation without causing the full illness.

## Introduction

Infectious disease ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infection)) is a process in which a microorganism impairs a person's health. Infection ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infection)) is broader: it is the invasion and replication of bacteria, viruses, fungi, protozoans, worms, or other agents, together with the body's tissue reaction to the agents or their toxins. A person can be infected without being clinically ill, a state called subclinical infection.

Vaccination illustrates the distinction between infection and disease. An attenuated measles virus can stimulate a controlled infection that produces immunity without causing measles illness. The aim is to sensitize antibody-producing cells so the body can respond quickly when the true pathogen appears.

Human barriers include skin and mucous membranes lining the nose, mouth, respiratory tract, eye, digestive tract, and genital tract. When these barriers are broken or weakened, microorganisms can cause local disease, enter the bloodstream, spread to distant sites, or produce generalized infection such as septicemia. Examples include boils, pneumonia, meningitis, intestinal disease, conjunctival viral infection, gonorrhea, and systemic smallpox.

## Infectious Agents

[[Bacteria and Other Monerans|Bacteria]] can live inside the body but outside individual cells. Some are aerobic and require oxygen, while anaerobes grow without it. Capsules and toxins often help bacteria cause disease. Pneumococci, staphylococci, and streptococci may live harmlessly in the upper respiratory tract yet become virulent, producing pneumonia, septicemia, meningitis, or complications after influenza. Bacterial infections can often be treated with antibiotics.

Rickettsias are small intracellular parasites named for Howard T. Ricketts, who died of typhus in 1910. They cause fever-and-rash diseases and are usually transmitted by arthropods such as lice, fleas, mites, and ticks. Murine typhus, scrub typhus, spotted fevers, rickettsialpox, Rocky Mountain spotted fever, and Q fever show how humans become infected by entering natural cycles that usually involve animals and arthropod carriers.

[[Viruses]] are nucleic acid fragments inside protein coats and require living cells to replicate. They range from about 25 nanometres for poliovirus to about 250 nanometres for smallpox virus. Vaccination is the most successful protection against viral disease, while some viral infections can be treated with antiviral drugs or interferon. Herpesviruses include varicella-zoster, Epstein-Barr virus, cytomegalovirus, and herpes simplex viruses HSV-1 and HSV-2.

[[Fungi (Fungus)|Fungi]] are larger organisms that may exist as yeasts or molds and live in soil, plants, animals, airborne material, and skin. Human fungal diseases, or mycoses, include histoplasmosis, coccidioidomycosis, and blastomycosis. They may be mild respiratory infections or severe systemic diseases, especially in people weakened by malnutrition, cancer, immunosuppressive drugs, or other immune deficits. Antifungal drugs treat many such infections.

Parasites include protozoans and worms. Malarial protozoans are only about 4 micrometres in diameter, while tapeworms can grow to several metres. Ascaris lumbricoides causes ascariasis, one of the world's most prevalent infections, after eggs in contaminated food hatch in the intestine and larvae travel through liver, heart, and lungs. Whipworms and pinworms are common intestinal parasites that are usually diagnosable and treatable.

## Survival and Spread

Pathogens survive in different ways. Some, such as measles virus, multiply rapidly and move quickly from one host to another. Measles replicates for one or two weeks, enters the bloodstream, spreads through organs, and is shed from respiratory surfaces before the rash appears. Once antibodies rise after the rash, transmission quickly falls.

Latency and persistence allow other agents to remain in hosts for long periods. Some infections recur when immunity weakens or stress, illness, or environmental changes reactivate the agent. Others persist in animal reservoirs, arthropods, soil, water, or asymptomatic human carriers, creating opportunities for later human disease.

Movement into new environments often triggers outbreaks. Pilgrimages and wars can produce dysentery, cholera, and typhoid fever through improvised feeding and sanitation. Isolated populations may suffer especially severe epidemics when a common disease is introduced. A measles epidemic in the Faroe Islands in 1846 infected about 6,000 of 8,000 inhabitants, and a Fiji epidemic in 1875 killed about one-fourth of the population.

Environmental exposure can also bring disease. Camping, swimming, or eating wild plants may expose people to ticks carrying encephalitis viruses, water containing leptospirosis organisms, or parasites on vegetation. Modern urban populations may limit spread when many people are immune, while vaccination reduces the supply of susceptible children and can prevent epidemics from renewing.

## Immunization and Passive Protection

Active immunization uses live attenuated organisms, inactivated organisms, or toxins to stimulate antibody production without causing the target disease. Antibody-producing cells remain sensitized and can respond strongly on later exposure. A natural infection can also create active immunity, which is why one attack of many diseases protects against another.

Passive immunity transfers antibody from one person or animal to another. It provides immediate but temporary protection because the recipient did not produce the antibody or sensitize immune cells. Maternal antibodies cross the placenta and protect infants against diseases such as measles during early life, but that protection disappears, usually leaving children susceptible unless vaccinated.

Human immune serum globulin, or HISG, is purified from human serum for intramuscular injection. It has been used for antibody-deficiency conditions and short-term prevention of hepatitis A, hepatitis B, measles, chickenpox, rubella, and poliomyelitis after exposure. Hepatitis B immune globulin can protect susceptible people after contaminated blood exposure or infants born to infected mothers when paired with vaccination.

Rho-GAM is human anti-RhD immune serum globulin given to Rh-negative mothers after delivery of Rh-positive infants or after miscarriage or abortion. It prevents anti-Rh antibody formation that could cause hemolytic disease in a later newborn.

Antitoxins neutralize toxins already produced by pathogens. Botulism antitoxin can be given after exposure to contaminated food or early symptoms. Diphtheria antitoxin remains a principal treatment along with antibiotics because diphtheria toxin can damage the heart, nervous system, kidneys, and other organs. Horse-derived antitoxins and antivenoms can be effective but carry reaction risks.

$$\text{risk}=\frac{\text{cases}}{\text{population}}$$

## Key Events & Developments

- Measles example: Attenuated virus vaccination shows that infection can produce immunity without clinical disease.
- Ricketts discovery: Howard T. Ricketts died of typhus in 1910 while investigating disease spread.
- Faroe measles epidemic: In 1846, about 6,000 of 8,000 inhabitants became infected.
- Fiji measles epidemic: In 1875, measles killed about one-fourth of Fiji's population.
- Passive immunization: Immune serum globulin and antitoxins developed as short-term protection or toxin treatment.

## Key People

- Howard T. Ricketts ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Howard_T._Ricketts)): Pathologist associated with rickettsial disease research and typhus transmission.
- Public-health vaccinators: Practitioners who use active immunization to reduce susceptible populations and interrupt epidemics.
- Clinical microbiologists: Specialists who identify bacteria, viruses, fungi, and parasites so therapy and prevention can be targeted.

## Interesting Facts

- Infection ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infection)) does not always mean infectious disease.
- Measles can spread before its rash appears.
- Rickettsias often reach humans through ticks, fleas, lice, or mites.
- Tapeworms can reach several metres in length.
- Maternal antibodies temporarily protect many infants before vaccination age.
- Antitoxin works against toxin effects rather than directly killing the organism.
