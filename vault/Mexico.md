# Mexico

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> Mexico is a large and diverse North American country whose mountains, basins, coasts, cities, Indigenous communities, Spanish language, and mineral and industrial resources shape its national life. Its modern development combines economic influence, inequality, urbanization, trade integration, and a long precolonial and colonial history.

## Introduction

Mexico, officially the United Mexican States, is a large and varied country in southern [[North America]]. It combines great economic influence in Latin America with deep inequality, a limited middle class, and sharp contrasts between wealthy investors, urban workers, rural peasants, and isolated indigenous communities.

The country has a strong industrial base ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manufacturing)), large mineral resources, broad services, and the world's largest population of Spanish speakers. More than half of the population lives in the central region, more than three-fourths live in cities, and [[Mexico City]] is one of the world's largest metropolitan areas.

Mexico's modern economy has often moved through boom-and-bust cycles. Social gains have followed periods of growth, but crises have repeatedly damaged living standards for the middle and lower classes. Ties with the [[United States of America (United States)|United States]] and [[Canada]] deepened through NAFTA ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_American_Free_Trade_Agreement)), yet the economy remained fragile.

## Land

![[attachments/mexico_001.webp|420]]

*Relief map of Mexico*

Mexico borders the [[United States of America (United States)|United States]] in the north, the [[Pacific Ocean]] in the west and south, the Gulf of Mexico and Caribbean Sea in the east, and Guatemala and Belize in the southeast. It also controls islands such as the Tres Marias, Cozumel, and Mujeres.

The country is roughly triangular and about 1,850 miles from northwest to southeast. Its width changes greatly, from less than 135 miles at the Isthmus of Tehuantepec to more than 1,200 miles in the north, giving Mexico strong regional contrasts.

## Relief

![[attachments/mexico_003.webp|420]]

*Painting of Popocatepetl volcano in Mexico*

Mexico lies in the circum-Pacific Ring of Fire, where active volcanism and earthquakes shape the land. The interaction of the North American Plate with the Pacific, Cocos, and Caribbean plates produced rugged southern landscapes and major volcanic peaks.

Citlaltepetl, also called Orizaba, rises to 18,406 feet and is the highest point in the country. Popocatepetl, southeast of [[Mexico City]], reaches 17,930 feet. These young volcanic features helped build central and southern Mexico.

## Physiographic Regions

Baja California is an arid, isolated peninsula between the [[Pacific Ocean]] and the Gulf of California. Its granitic core rises above 9,000 feet, and the steep eastern escarpment makes access from the gulf difficult.

The Pacific Coastal Lowlands run from near Mexicali and the Colorado River delta toward Tepic. Terraces, mesas, small basins, deltas, and coastal strips lie below the Sierra Madre Occidental, and irrigation has turned some northern desert areas into productive farmland.

The Mexican Plateau is the largest and most densely populated region. It includes the Mesa del Norte and the smaller but heavily settled Mesa Central, where basins around [[Mexico City]] contain unstable old lake soils that have caused buildings to sink unevenly.

## Central Basins

Iztaccihuatl belongs to the volcanic landscape that frames central Mexico. Around the central basins, drained lakebeds supported settlement and farming, but their weak soils created long-term structural problems for colonial and modern buildings.

The Sierra Madre Occidental forms the western border of the plateau. Its volcanic highlands average about 8,000 to 9,000 feet and are deeply cut by streams that carved major gorges.

## Copper Canyon

Copper Canyon, or Barranca del Cobre, is the most striking gorge complex in southwestern Chihuahua. It shows how westward-flowing rivers have cut through the Sierra Madre Occidental.

## The Eastern Sierra

The Sierra Madre Oriental is a folded mountain range of shales and limestones on the eastern side of the plateau. It runs about 700 miles before merging with the Cordillera Neo-Volcanica, and it contains important copper, lead, and zinc deposits.

The Cordillera Neo-Volcanica, also called the Trans-Volcanic Axis, links the western and eastern ranges. Its cinder cones and peaks cross the country from Cape Corrientes to Xalapa and Veracruz.

## The Volcanic Belt

The volcanic belt forms a mountainous backdrop to Jalisco, Michoacan, Guerrero, Mexico, Morelos, Puebla, and the Federal District. This region includes silver, lead, zinc, copper, and tin deposits.

## Southern Landforms

Orizaba Peak, or Citlaltepetl, is Mexico's highest mountain. Nearby volcanic history includes Paricutin, which emerged from the fields of Michoacan between 1943 and 1952.

South of the volcanic axis, the Balsas Depression is hot and dry. The Gulf Coastal Plain runs about 900 miles from Tamaulipas through Veracruz and Tabasco to the Yucatan Peninsula, while the Southern Highlands include dissected ranges, plateaus, and the Chiapas Highlands.

## Drainage

Mexico has few large rivers or natural lakes because climate and landforms limit drainage. The Lerma River feeds Lake Chapala, and the Santiago River flows from that lake toward the Pacific through the Sierra Madre Occidental.

The Panuco, Moctezuma, and Santa Maria rivers drain eastward to the Gulf of Mexico. In the arid north, the Rio Bravo del Norte, called the Rio Grande in the United States, forms a long international border and receives the Conchos River, which supports irrigation and hydroelectric power.

The Balsas River drains the Balsas Depression and is dammed for hydroelectric power. In the southeast, the Grijalva-Usumacinta system and the Papaloapan River account for a large share of the country's river volume.

## Soils

Tropical southeastern Mexico has reddish and yellow lateritic soils rich in iron oxides and aluminum hydroxides but generally poor in fertility. The dark volcanic soils of the Mesa Central are much richer and have supported farming for centuries.

Overuse in central farming regions has caused sheet erosion and exposed tepetate, a lime hardpan. In the arid north, gray-brown desert soils can become productive under irrigation, but salt buildup can make fields barren.

## Climate

Mexico has many climates because size, latitude, and elevation vary widely. South of the Tropic of Cancer, maritime air from the Gulf of Mexico, Caribbean, and Pacific brings most rainfall from May through August, while hurricanes strike coastal lowlands from August through October.

Northern Mexico is dominated by the Sonoran and Chihuahuan deserts. Vertical climatic zones are also important: tierra caliente below about 3,000 feet, tierra templada to about 6,000 feet, tierra fria to about 11,000 feet, and higher alpine and snow zones.

## Plant and Animal Life

Mexico joins the Nearctic and Neotropical biogeographic realms, so deserts, conifer forests, tropical scrub, and rainforest all appear. The Sonoran and Chihuahuan deserts contain sparse scrub, cacti, succulents, and unusual plants such as the boojum tree.

Forests once covered much of the Sierra Madre Occidental, Mesa Central, and Southern Highlands, but long occupation and poverty-driven land use reduced them. Rainforests in the Gulf Coastal Plain, Chiapas Highlands, and southern Yucatan remain biologically rich but are damaged by farming, logging, ranching, and mining.

## People

Mexico contains cultural regions shaped by environment, ethnicity, and settlement history. A broad contrast separates the Spanish-mestizo north from the Indian-mestizo south, reflecting pre-Columbian settlement patterns and later colonial development.

## Cultural Regions

The traditional regions include the North, Northeast, Northwest, Baja California peninsula, Central, West, Balsas, Gulf Coast, Southern Highlands, and Yucatan. Mining and ranching shaped the north, petroleum and steel helped the northeast, and irrigated agriculture and border industry transformed parts of the northwest.

The Central region is the cultural core, including [[Mexico City]] and surrounding highlands. It was central to the Aztec empire, New Spain, and modern national government, while the West, Gulf Coast, Southern Highlands, and Yucatan preserve distinct regional identities.

## Northwestern Peoples

The Tarahumara and Seri are among indigenous peoples who remained in isolated northwestern settlements. Their presence shows that the Northwest was not only a mining, ranching, and industrial region but also a zone of older Native communities.

## A Changing Northwest

The Sierra Madre Occidental helped isolate settlements in the northwest. Modern roads, industry, and trade links later changed the region, while some remote communities retained older patterns.

## Languages

Spanish is the official national language and the main language of instruction. Fewer than one-tenth of American Indians speak an indigenous language, but more than 50 indigenous languages are spoken by more than 100,000 people.

## Indigenous Languages

Important indigenous languages include Maya in the Yucatan, Huastec in northern Veracruz, and Nahua, Tarascan, Totonac, Otomi, and Mazahua on the Mesa Central. Zapotec, Mixtec, and Mazatec are important in Oaxaca, while Tzeltal and Tzotzil are spoken in Chiapas.

## Religion

Mexico has no official religion because the constitution separates church and state. More than nine-tenths of the population is at least nominally Roman Catholic, and the Basilica of Guadalupe in [[Mexico City]] is a major pilgrimage site.

Protestant groups form a small but growing share of the population, especially among the urban poor. Many indigenous communities practice syncretic religion, blending Catholic saints with older beliefs about ancestors, mountain spirits, and sacred ceremonies such as those involving peyote among the Huichol.

## Economy

Mexico has a developing market economy closely tied to the [[United States of America (United States)|United States]]. Services make up about two-thirds of GDP, manufacturing about one-fifth, and agriculture only a small share despite employing many rural workers.

For much of the 20th century, state-owned or mixed enterprises controlled petroleum, electricity, banks, railways, airlines, and telecommunications. In the 1980s, neoliberal reforms deregulated industries, privatized many state enterprises, welcomed foreign investment, and reduced import restrictions.

## Agriculture

Only about one-fifth of Mexico is potentially arable because much land is too dry or mountainous. Major crops include corn, sugarcane, sorghum, wheat, tomatoes, bananas, chilies, citrus fruits, mangoes, beans, barley, avocados, blue agave, and coffee.

Traditional subsistence farming remains important in indigenous regions, especially the Southern Highlands, where corn, beans, and squash form the Mesoamerican agricultural base. The ejido system grew from the 1910 revolution and the 1917 constitution, which allowed land redistribution and weakened large estates.

## Forestry

Forests once covered nearly two-thirds of Mexico in the mid-1500s, but exploitation, logging, slash-and-burn farming, and pasture expansion greatly reduced them. Tropical forests in the south and east produce hardwoods such as oak and mahogany, fragrant woods such as cedar and rosewood, and chicle from sapodilla trees.

Softwoods grow in the Sierra Madre Oriental and Sierra Madre Occidental above 6,000 feet. Pine forests are especially developed in Chihuahua and Durango.

## Fishing

Mexico has rich marine resources, although fish and seafood are not central to the national diet. Gulf shrimping areas developed commercially from the 1940s, and the Gulf of California became the most important shrimping ground after large-scale exploitation began in the late 1950s.

The Pacific coast of Baja California became the main fishing ground after a commercial fleet formed in the 1960s. Sardines, anchovies, tuna, lobster, and abalone are major catches, and the Campeche Bank contributes to the Gulf catch.

## Manufacturing

Mexico is among the more industrialized countries in Latin America. Manufacturing produces vehicles and parts, processed foods, beverages, pharmaceuticals, cement, steel, paper, chemicals, electronics, consumer goods, and refined petroleum.

Industry once concentrated around [[Mexico City]], but maquiladoras, foreign investment, and NAFTA ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_American_Free_Trade_Agreement)) dispersed employment toward the central and northern regions. Automobile plants in Puebla, Toluca, Guadalajara, and Hermosillo serve export and domestic markets.

## Finance

Finance ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Finance)) is a major service activity, including banks, savings and loan associations, insurance, the stock market, and government bonds. Private banks were nationalized in 1982 and privatized again in the late 1980s.

The Bank of Mexico issues the peso, divided into 100 centavos. The stock exchange plays a smaller role in capital formation than government bonds and bank securities.

## Trade

The [[United States of America (United States)|United States]] is Mexico's most important trading partner, supplying about three-fifths of imports and receiving more than four-fifths of exports. This makes Mexico far more dependent on U.S. trade than the United States is on Mexican trade.

NAFTA ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_American_Free_Trade_Agreement)) increased duty-free trade and helped nonpetroleum exports grow rapidly, but U.S. subsidized agricultural exports hurt Mexican smallholders. Major exports include machinery, transport equipment, steel, electrical equipment, chemicals, food products, petroleum, and petroleum products.

## Services

The service sector ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tertiary_sector)), including commerce, tourism, business services, finance, and government, accounts for about two-thirds of GDP. Tourism draws many visitors from the [[United States of America (United States)|United States]] because of cultural variety, tropical settings, low prices, and accessibility.

Visitors long traveled to [[Mexico City]], colonial towns, and Teotihuacan, but beach resorts became more important. Pollution and crime weakened the capital's image as a tourist destination, while coastal and archaeological sites gained international appeal.

## Beach Resorts

Acapulco, Puerto Vallarta, Ixtapa-Zihuatanejo, Mazatlan, and Puerto Escondido are important Pacific resorts. Cancun, Cozumel, and Cabo San Lucas expanded rapidly after the 1960s through hotels, airports, and other tourist facilities.

Labour patterns show the economy's social divisions: about two-thirds of the labour force works in services, about one-sixth in manufacturing, and less than one-fifth in agriculture. Women's workforce participation rose from the 1970s to the early 21st century, helped by maquiladora demand and household income needs.

## Justice

Mexico's judicial system includes the Supreme Court of Justice, the Electoral Tribunal, the Federal Judicial Council, and many circuit and district courts. Serious cases are usually heard in federal courts by judges rather than juries.

Legal protections exist for defendants, but the justice system is overburdened and widely distrusted. Many crimes go unreported, poor and indigenous defendants face arbitrary arrest and long detention, and prisons are often overcrowded and corrupt.

## Security

Police operate at federal, state, and local levels, but corruption is widely perceived, including the mordida, or bribe. The armed forces include the army, navy, and air force, and military service is mandatory at age 18.

The military has generally stayed out of elections since the 1920s, but it has joined counternarcotics and internal security operations. In 1994, forces were deployed against the Zapatista National Liberation Army in Chiapas, and abuses were reported during security campaigns in indigenous and poor urban areas.

Organized crime became a major test of Mexican state capacity. Drug trafficking, money laundering, local corruption, arms flows, territorial competition, and violence against officials, journalists, migrants, and civilians exposed weaknesses in policing and justice. The drug war also showed how domestic security is tied to the border with the [[United States of America (United States)|United States]], demand for narcotics, firearms markets, migration routes, and the uneven power of federal, state, and municipal institutions.

## Housing

Housing shortage is one of Mexico's serious social problems. Urban growth has outpaced government housing projects, and economic crises have limited construction funds.

Peripheral squatter settlements, or colonias, appear around almost all cities. Rural migrants and poor urban residents build makeshift homes on unused land, often without water, electricity, or sewerage at first, but many communities slowly become livable.

## Education

Mexico expanded education through required schooling for children ages 6 to 18, mandatory preschool after 2004, and adult literacy programs from the 1970s. By the early 21st century, about nine-tenths of Mexicans were literate.

Rural public schools are numerous but often poor and incomplete. Better urban schools attract internal migrants, while private secondary schools and city universities reinforce inequality because wealthier families can buy better education.

## Universities

The National Autonomous University of Mexico, or UNAM, the College of Mexico, and the Monterrey Institute of Technology and Higher Education are among the country's leading higher-education institutions. Mexico City contains a large share of all universities and university students.

## Access to Higher Education

The Autonomous University buildings and mosaics represent the link between education and national cultural expression. Higher education remains concentrated in large cities, so rural and poor students face major barriers.

## The Arts

Rodolfo Usigli, Luisa Josefina Hernandez, and Emilio Carballido are noted dramatists. Mexican art is especially associated with muralism, in which Diego Rivera, Jose Clemente Orozco, and David Alfaro Siqueiros portrayed revolution, modernization, class struggle, and indigenous heritage.

Music and dance give Mexico strong cross-generational identity. Mariachi, ranchero songs, nortec, norteno bands, cumbia, danzon, rock, pop, theatre, lucha libre, cinema, television, radio, and sports all contribute to shared national and regional culture.

## Pre-Columbian Mexico

The earliest inhabitants of Middle America were American Indians of Asian derivation, but exact dates remain debated. More accepted evidence places hunters with mammoth bones in the Valley of Mexico around 9000 BC.

After the last glacial retreat around 7500 BC, people turned from big-game hunting to small game and plant collection. In the Tehuacan Valley, the El Riego and Coxcatlan phases show seasonal camps and temporary villages, while corn cultivation may have begun by about 5000 BC.

## Classic Period

![[attachments/mexico_002.webp|420]]

*Pyramid of the Moon at Teotihuacan in Mexico*

By the end of the Late Formative Period, Maya Lowland culture had polychrome ceramics, corbeled vaults, calendrical systems, and developing art forms. During the Early Classic, Tikal, Uaxactun, and Copan produced important art and architecture.

In the Late Classic, Maya ceremonial centres, stelae, farming systems, mathematics, and astronomy flourished. The classical world weakened after several centuries; Teotihuacan burned around AD 750, and the Toltec later became the main power in the Valley of Mexico.

## Post-Classic Period

The Post-Classic Period brought more militaristic themes and fortified sites. Chichen Itza lost leadership about AD 1200, and a Maya resurgence later centered on the walled city of Mayapan.

In the late Post-Classic, the Aztecs built power from Tenochtitlan, on the site of modern [[Mexico City]]. Their empire became the dominant force in Middle America.

## The rise of the Aztecs

The Aztecs, also called Mexica or Tenochca, traced their origin to Aztlan. Their language, Nahuatl, became a lingua franca across an area nearly as large as modern Mexico after Aztec political power expanded.

The decline of the Toltec capital Tula in the early 12th century opened space for northern groups and new political formations. The Aztec rise connected older central Mexican urban traditions with later imperial rule.

## The Aztec Economy

The Aztec economy rested on intensive agriculture in the Valley of Mexico. Chinampas, raised fields in lake systems, produced multiple harvests and supported dense cities, possibly with the valley population near 2,000,000 in the early 16th century.

Water transport across connected lakes compensated for the lack of wheels and pack animals. Pochteca merchant guilds carried trade and information far beyond the valley, helping economic activity and military planning.

## Conquest of Mexico

Diego Velazquez, governor of Cuba, sent expeditions to the Yucatan and Gulf of Mexico in 1517 and 1518. Hernan Cortes then landed in Mexico, founded Veracruz, and claimed authority to conquer in the name of Charles I of Spain.

Cortes advanced toward Tenochtitlan with about 500 European soldiers. European weapons, horses, dogs, disease, diplomacy, indigenous allies, and Marina, also called La Malinche, helped him defeat Montezuma's empire and take the capital on August 13, 1521.

## Expansion of Spanish rule

After the fall of the Aztec empire, Spanish rule spread quickly across southern Mexico and toward Guatemala and Honduras by 1525. The Maya region of Yucatan resisted longer, and Francisco de Montejo needed nearly 20 years to control its northern end.

Northern expansion was slower because arid lands, thin settlement, and indigenous resistance limited Spanish control. Silver discoveries in Zacatecas, Guanajuato, and San Luis Potosi drew colonists north, while expeditions by Alvar Nunez Cabeza de Vaca and Francisco Vazquez de Coronado fed hopes of wealthy northern kingdoms.

## Colonial Period, 1701–1821

Colonial society changed as more Spanish women emigrated to New Spain, altering elite family life, inheritance, and property management. Although patriarchal, colonial society allowed wives and daughters to inherit property, and some women ran businesses or rural estates.

The 1701 to 1713 War of the Spanish Succession brought the Bourbon dynasty to the Spanish throne. Bourbon reforms, especially under Charles III, created intendancies in 1784, encouraged economic production, and spread Enlightenment ideas about science, mining, agriculture, administration, and political liberalism.

## Independence

The movement for Mexican independence grew from colonial social tensions, Enlightenment ideas, economic grievances, and political changes in Spain. Insurgent and royalist conflict transformed loyalty to the crown into a struggle over power, privilege, land, and the future of New Spain.

Independence ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Independence)) ended Spanish rule but did not solve Mexico's internal divisions. The new country inherited regional inequality, weak finances, military politics, church power, and disputes over monarchy, republicanism, federalism, and central authority.

## The Mexican Empire, 1821–23

The first Mexican Empire lasted from 1821 to 1823. It showed how quickly independence-era unity fractured when leaders disagreed over monarchy, constitutional rule, and the distribution of power.

The [[The History of European Overseas Exploration and Empires (European Exploration)|empire]] collapsed because the new state lacked stable political institutions and broad agreement. Republican experiments followed, but conflicts among military leaders, regional interests, and ideological factions continued.

## Santa Anna and Instability

Antonio Lopez de Santa Anna became a central figure in early national politics. His career reflected the instability of the early 19th century, when military prestige often substituted for durable institutions.

Under shifting governments, Mexico struggled with foreign threats, regional revolts, and disputes between centralists and federalists. The era deepened the political fragmentation that later reformers tried to overcome.

## La Reforma

La Reforma ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/La_Reforma)) sought to reduce the power of the Roman Catholic Church and military privilege while strengthening liberal law. Its principles emphasized civil equality, secular authority, property reform, and constitutional government.

Benito Juarez, a Zapotec from Oaxaca, became the leading liberal statesman. The constitution of 1857 and later reforms challenged old corporate privileges and helped define the modern secular state.

## The French Intervention

Maximilian, a Habsburg archduke, became emperor during the French intervention, but republican resistance under Benito Juarez continued. French withdrawal left the empire exposed, and republican forces besieged Queretaro in 1867.

Maximilian surrendered, was court-martialed, and was executed with two generals on June 19, 1867. Juarez refused foreign pleas for mercy to show that Mexico could defend equality before law and reject foreign monarchy.

## The Revolution Begins

Francisco I. Madero, from a wealthy northern family, became the main anti-reelection leader against Porfirio Diaz. His 1908 book The Presidential Succession in 1910 argued against absolute power and for reviving democracy.

After arrest and house confinement, Madero escaped to San Antonio and issued the Plan of San Luis Potosi. It declared Diaz's presidency illegal, demanded effective suffrage and no reelection, and called for revolt on November 20, 1910.

## The Constitutionalist Revolution

Venustiano Carranza rejected Victoriano Huerta's seizure of power after the 1913 Decena Tragica, when Madero and Jose Maria Pino Suarez were arrested and killed. Carranza called his movement the Constitutionalist Revolution.

Revolutionary alliances fractured after Huerta fell in 1914. Pancho Villa and Emiliano Zapata fought groups linked to Carranza, while Alvaro Obregon defeated Villa at the Battle of Celaya in 1915.

The 1917 constitution made the national government responsible for social, economic, and cultural welfare. Article 3 provided secular, free, compulsory education; Article 27 asserted national ownership of subsoil resources and land reform; and Article 123 guaranteed labour rights and social welfare.

## Postrevolutionary Reconstruction

The Regional Confederation of Mexican Labour, or CROM, received government support under Alvaro Obregon. His administration distributed land, restored ejidos, and used force and political incentives to pacify military rivals after 1920.

Jose Vasconcelos led rural education and cultural programs. Public muralists such as Diego Rivera, Jose Clemente Orozco, and David Alfaro Siqueiros made revolutionary and indigenous history central to national culture.

## The Cardenas Era

Lazaro Cardenas, president from 1934, reorganized the ruling party, pushed land reform, expanded ejidos, nationalized railways, and expropriated foreign oil companies on March 18, 1938. Petroleos Mexicanos, or Pemex, was assigned to manage the petroleum industry.

Manuel Avila Camacho, supported by Cardenas, defeated Juan Andreu Almazan in the bitter 1940 presidential contest. His conciliatory domestic policy slowed the social programs of the revolution, while economic and diplomatic issues became more urgent.

## World War II, 1941–45

[[World Wars (World War II)|World War II]] accelerated Mexico's industrialization and urbanization. A 1941 agreement with the [[United States of America (United States)|United States]] settled disputes, stabilized the peso, supported silver purchases, and expanded military cooperation.

Mexico entered the war in 1942 after Germany sank two tankers. Its main contributions were raw materials, a small air unit in the [[Philippines]], and the Bracero Treaty, which sent hundreds of thousands of temporary farmworkers and railroad workers to the United States.

## Mexico since 1945

After 1945, population growth, industrial expansion, hydraulic projects, transport networks, and civilian presidential succession reshaped the country. Miguel Aleman changed the ruling party's name to the Institutional Revolutionary Party, or PRI, and Adolfo Ruiz Cortines enfranchised women through a 1953 constitutional amendment.

The PRI dominated politics for decades, while economic crises, debt, inflation, protest, repression, and the dirty war damaged public trust. Carlos Salinas de Gortari advanced neoliberal policies and signed NAFTA ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_American_Free_Trade_Agreement)) in 1992, but the Zapatistas rebelled in Chiapas when it took effect on January 1, 1994.

Opposition pressure led to major electoral reforms and the end of PRI presidential rule when Vicente Fox Quesada of PAN won in 2000. Later governments faced disputed elections, energy reform, drug-cartel violence, human-rights concerns, the 2009 H1N1 outbreak, recession, and the return of the PRI under Enrique Pena Nieto in 2012.

## Interesting Facts

- Mexico has the world's largest population of Spanish speakers, far larger than Spain's own population.
- Chinampas in the Valley of Mexico were raised fields, not truly floating gardens, and could produce multiple harvests each year.
- Paricutin appeared suddenly in Michoacan fields between 1943 and 1952, making volcanic change visible within living memory.
- Cancun now attracts more international visitors per year than [[Mexico City]], reflecting the shift toward coastal tourism.
- Mexico's Drug War became a national security crisis after military operations against cartels began in 2006.
