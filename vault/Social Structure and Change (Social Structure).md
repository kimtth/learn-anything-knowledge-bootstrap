## Introduction

![[attachments/social_structure_003.png|420]]

*Diagram of social network characteristics*

Social structure ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_structure)) is the distinctive and relatively stable arrangement of institutions through which people in a society interact and live together. It is often studied with social change, because changes in forces, organizations, and values can alter the structure of society.

The term is used inconsistently. It should refer to regularities in social life, not simply to custom, tradition, role, or norm when those terms are more precise. Studies of social structure try to explain integration, inequality, organizations, social categories such as age groups, and rates such as crime or birth.

This approach is sometimes called formal sociology. It does not focus directly on individual behaviour or face-to-face interaction. Instead, it studies abstract patterns that result from people responding to one another and to their environment. Because of this abstraction, social structure is not usually treated as a behavioural science, though it remains empirical and observational.

Social structure can also mean patterned social relations: the regular and repeated aspects of interaction inside a social entity. The larger the group being studied, the more abstract the concept becomes. The structure of a small group is closer to daily behaviour than the structure of an entire society.

## Structuralism

![[attachments/social_structure_001.webp|420]]

*Portrait of Claude Levi-Strauss*

Structuralism ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Structuralism)), sometimes called French structuralism, is another approach to social structure. It studies underlying and often unconscious regularities in human expression. These deep patterns are not directly visible, but they shape behaviour, society, culture, myths, language, and institutions.

Claude Levi-Strauss developed this approach from structural linguistics, especially the work of Ferdinand de Saussure. Saussure argued that language is structured because its elements are related in regular, rule-bound ways. Speakers follow many of these rules without being aware of them.

For structuralists, the theorist's task is to detect the hidden rules of transformation that connect a deep structure to its visible expressions. Levi-Strauss applied this method to kinship systems, myths, and customs of cooking and eating. The same structure might generate many different social and cultural forms.

These deep structures do not mechanically determine every concrete expression. Their possible expressions are varied and potentially unlimited. For Levi-Strauss, the deepest structures of culture ultimately reflected basic features of the human mind.

Later scholars applied structuralist methods to other materials. Michel Foucault, for example, used a related approach in studying corporal punishment. His work connected changing penal practices to underlying structures of knowledge, power, discipline, and social control.

## Conclusion

![[attachments/social_structure_002.webp|420]]

*Social network diagram*

Social structure ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_structure)) and social change are general concepts used especially in sociology and social and cultural anthropology. They are often treated as opposites: structure suggests lasting patterns, while change suggests alteration. In practice, the relation is more complex.

Structure cannot be understood without actual or possible change, because institutions persist through time only while adapting or resisting change. Social change is also structured because it often follows regular processes and depends on continuity. Together, the two concepts help explain society, its patterns, and its transformations.

## Key Events & Developments

- 20th century: Structuralism developed as a major way to study hidden regularities in culture and society.
- 1950s-1970s: Claude Levi-Strauss's structural anthropology applied linguistic models to kinship, myth, and custom.
- Later 20th century: Structural and poststructural approaches influenced studies of language, power, punishment, and institutions.

## Key People

- Ferdinand de Saussure ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ferdinand_de_Saussure)): Developed structural linguistics, the model for later structuralism.
- Claude Levi-Strauss ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Claude_Levi-Strauss)): Applied structural methods to anthropology, kinship, myths, and cultural customs.
- Michel Foucault ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michel_Foucault)): Used related methods to study punishment, power, and social control.

## Interesting Facts

- Social structure ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_structure)) is more abstract when applied to a whole society than to a small group.
- Formal sociology ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Formal_sociology)) studies patterns such as organizations, categories, and rates rather than individual motives.
- Deep structures ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deep_structure_and_surface_structure)) are inferred from myths, language, texts, or customs because they are not directly observable.
- [[Social Structure and Change (Social Change)|Social change]] is not the opposite of structure; it often has its own structure over time.
