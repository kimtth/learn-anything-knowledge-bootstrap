## Formosa

![[attachments/taiwan_001.png|420]]

*Relief map of Taiwan*

Taiwan, historically called Formosa, is an island about 100 miles off the southeastern coast of mainland China. It is about 245 miles long north to south and 90 miles wide at its broadest point. Taipei is the largest city and seat of the Republic of China government.

The Republic of China, or ROC, also controls islands in the Taiwan group and the Pescadores, or Penghu, archipelago. Taiwan is bordered by the East China Sea, Pacific Ocean, Bashi Channel, and Taiwan Strait.

From the mid-1660s to 1895, Taiwan was administered by imperial China. Japan ruled it as a colony from 1895 to 1945. After World War II it returned to China, and in 1949 it became the main refuge of the Nationalist government after Communist victory on the mainland.

Both the ROC on Taiwan and the People's Republic of China on the mainland claimed authority over China, while agreeing that Taiwan was a province of China. Taipei served as provincial capital until 1967, when the provincial capital moved to Chung-hsing Hsin-ts'un.

## Relief, Drainage, and Soils

Taiwan is part of the western Pacific island system. It is a tilted fault block running north-northeast to south-southwest. The western side rises gently toward the mountains and faces the shallow Taiwan Strait, while the eastern side drops steeply toward the Pacific.

The main population zones lie on western plains, terraced tablelands, and alluvial lowlands. The east coast has little room for settlement except one major rift valley. Important deepwater ports include Chi-lung, or Keelung, and Kao-hsiung.

The Central Range, or Chung-yang Shan-mo, runs near and parallel to the island's axis. Many peaks reach about 10,000 feet, and Yu Shan, at 13,113 feet, is the highest.

Taiwan's rivers rise mostly in the central mountains. They are short and seasonal, with typhoon rains causing floods, especially on the plains. Major rivers include the Tan-shui, Cho-shui, and Kao-p'ing. Alluvial soils on plains and valleys cover about one-fourth of the island and are its main agricultural resource.

## Climate and Natural Life

![[attachments/taiwan_003.webp|420]]

*Mountain landscape in Taiwan*

Taiwan straddles tropical and subtropical zones. Summers are long, from April to November, and winters are mild in lowlands. The Kuroshio, or Japan Current, moderates the climate. Mountain areas receive snow in winter.

Rainfall is heavy, averaging about 102 inches annually, and can exceed 200 inches in summer in some places. The uplands receive more rain than lowlands, the east more than the west, and the north more winter rain than the south. Most typhoons arrive in July, August, and September.

Forests cover more than half the island. Vegetation changes with elevation: bamboo, palms, and tropical evergreen plants in the lowlands; camphor laurel in subtropical forests; cedars, cypress, junipers, rhododendrons, maples, and Japanese cedar in temperate forests; and conifers at higher elevations.

Animal life resembles that of southern China and includes deer, wild boars, bears, monkeys, goats, wildcats, panthers, pheasants, geese, flycatchers, kingfishers, larks, and abundant coastal fish.

## People

The original inhabitants of Taiwan were Malayo-Polynesian Indigenous peoples, often described by language groups such as Ami, Atayal, and Paiwan. Chinese migrants displaced or assimilated many plains communities, while mountain communities resisted until they were subdued by the Japanese.

Indigenous peoples now form a small share of the population, mostly in foothills and highlands. Many retained languages and customs while becoming more integrated into modern Taiwanese society. By 2008, 14 Indigenous groups had official recognition.

Most Taiwanese descend from Chinese immigrants from Fujian and Guangdong. Hokkien from southern Fujian are the largest group, and their language is often called Taiwanese. Hakka communities have a distinct dialect. Mandarin-speaking mainlanders arrived with the Nationalists in the late 1940s and long held strong influence in government.

Religion is plural and rarely divisive. [[Buddha and Buddhism (Buddhism)|Buddhism]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buddhism)), Taoism, Confucian ethics, Indigenous traditions, Protestant Christianity, Roman Catholicism, and Islam all exist. Many Chinese Taiwanese mix practices from more than one tradition.

## Demography, Family, and Society

Taiwan's population tripled in the first half of the 20th century, then growth slowed as health improved, land reform raised rural opportunity, urban life expanded, and family planning spread.

The [[Family and Kinship (Family)|family]] has been the central unit of traditional Taiwanese society. Confucian filial piety and ancestor worship supported patrilineal extended families that saved, invested, shared production, cared for members, diversified occupations, and spread risk across generations.

Urban settlement is concentrated on the western plains. Major urban areas include Taipei with Chi-lung in the north, Kao-hsiung in the south, and Tai-chung in the west.

## Economy

Taiwan's economy changed from agricultural to industrial during the 20th century. After World War II, growth became one of the most rapid among developing economies. Export promotion, broad land and capital ownership, high returns to labour, family saving, and global demand drove expansion.

Mineral resources are modest. Taiwan has small or limited amounts of copper, gold, iron, sulfur, pyrite, coal, petroleum, and natural gas, while limestone, marble, and dolomite are more abundant in the east. Forests are widespread but often hard to exploit economically.

Agriculture ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agriculture)) first benefited from fertile soils, a tropical climate, and labour. Rice is the main crop, often double-cropped in irrigated paddy fields. Sugarcane, tea, bananas, pineapples, litchis, longans, oranges, grapes, strawberries, mushrooms, asparagus, and vegetables are also important.

Forestry and fishing include timber limits, erosion concerns, tuna grounds, domestic fishery production, and high-value exports such as eels and snails. Energy shifted from exhausted coal to imported petroleum, thermal electricity, hydroelectricity, and nuclear power, which expanded in the 1970s and 1980s.

## Industry, Trade, Services, and Transport

![[attachments/taiwan_004.webp|420]]

*TSMC Global R&D Center at night*

In the 1950s and 1960s, Taiwan's industry relied on abundant cheap labour. Light industries produced foodstuffs, textiles, and other consumer goods, first for domestic use and then increasingly for export.

By the 1960s and 1970s, investment moved into heavy industry, including appliances, vehicles, steel, petrochemicals, machinery, and ships. Rising wages and labour shortages later pushed Taiwan toward high-technology fields such as specialty chemicals, pharmaceuticals, precision instruments, electronics, and information processing.

Taiwan depended on imported materials, equipment, and technology, especially from [[Japan]] and the United States, and on export markets, especially the United States. By the late 20th century, manufactured goods made up more than 95 percent of exports, led by electronics, appliances, clothing, footwear, textiles, toys, sporting goods, and metal products.

Services shifted from small shops and restaurants toward department stores, hotels, finance, communications, construction management, computer programming, and internationalized business. Many private firms remained small family businesses, making the economy flexible but sometimes limiting modern accounting, finance, advertising, and scale.

The state has played a strong economic management role since 1945, running electricity, steel, petroleum, infrastructure, financial oversight, and technology transfer, while private firms increasingly charted their own course after the mid-1970s.

Transport includes a highway around the island, east-west mountain roads, a north-south west-coast expressway, buses, motorcycles, railways, domestic flights, seaports, and international airports at Taoyuan and Kao-hsiung.

## Government and Politics

Taiwan has often been ruled by outsiders: imperial Chinese officials, Japanese colonial administrators, and mainland Nationalists. In 1949, the Kuomintang, or KMT, retreated to Taiwan and built an authoritarian party-state under emergency measures and martial law.

The 1947 ROC constitution combined traditional Chinese councils with Western parliamentarism and presidentialism. It created five yuans: Legislative, Executive, Judicial, Examination, and Control. Formally it guaranteed democracy, civil liberties, political participation, and cultural development.

In practice, for decades Taiwan was ruled by a dictator-led system joining security services, the KMT, and technocratic government. The National Assembly long included mainland-elected representatives from 1948, while local and provincial elections had limited power.

Beginning in 1986, the KMT allowed greater liberalization. Martial law was abolished, opposition politics expanded, leadership became more Taiwanese, and the party-state gradually shifted focus from claiming all China to governing Taiwan.

## Education, Health, Welfare, and Culture

![[attachments/taiwan_002.webp|420]]

*Taipei skyline with Taipei 101*

Taiwan has one of Asia's best-educated populations. [[Education]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Education)) is compulsory for nine years: six years of primary school and three of middle school. Senior high, vocational schools, preschool, adult education, special education, and more than 100 higher-education institutions developed strongly.

Japanese rule introduced modern health measures, water and sewage treatment, and medical training. Nationalist governments expanded hospitals and medical services. Life expectancy and infant mortality became similar to Western standards.

Welfare spending remained relatively low because economic growth and family care supported the elderly and unemployed, but declining birth rates and aging raised concern. Social insurance programs expanded gradually.

Taiwanese culture combines traditional Chinese, Indigenous, modern Chinese, Japanese-era, and Western elements. The National Palace Museum in Taipei holds a major collection of Chinese art and books. Popular culture includes Chinese opera, Taiwanese opera, puppet theatre, folk dances, music, calligraphy, ceramics, painting, television, and film.

## History

Chinese people knew of Taiwan by the 3rd century AD, but large Chinese settlement began only in the 17th century after famines in Fujian encouraged emigration. Before that, the island served as a base for Chinese and Japanese pirates.

The Portuguese named it Ilha Formosa, or Beautiful Island, in 1590. The Dutch founded a settlement at An-p'ing in 1624, and the Spanish settled at Chi-lung in 1626. The Dutch later seized the Spanish settlements but were expelled in 1661 by Cheng Ch'eng-kung, or Koxinga.

In 1683, Taiwan fell to the Qing and became part of Fujian Province. Migration from southeastern China expanded settlement, and Tainan became the capital. By 1842 the population reached about 2.5 million, and rice and sugar were major exports.

The Treaty of Tientsin in 1858 opened Tainan and Danshui as treaty ports. Tea became an important export, and trade shifted north toward Taipei and Danshui. Japan showed increasing interest and took Taiwan after the Sino-Japanese War in 1895.

Japan ruled Taiwan until 1945, building infrastructure, modern health systems, education, and colonial administration while subordinating the population. After Japan's defeat, Taiwan was transferred to the Chinese Nationalist government.

Many Taiwanese first welcomed liberation, but Nationalist rule quickly disappointed them. In 1947, protests by Taiwanese urban middle classes were met by massacres that killed thousands and left deep resentment.

In 1949-50, Nationalist troops, officials, and refugees arrived after Communist victory on the mainland. Chiang Kai-shek survived partly because the People's Liberation Army lacked immediate capacity to invade Taiwan and because the Korean War brought U.S. protection.

The United States placed the 7th Fleet between Taiwan and the mainland in 1950 and later signed a mutual defense treaty in 1954 covering Taiwan and the Pescadores. Crises over Quemoy and Matsu in the 1950s reinforced U.S. support and Taiwan's place in Cold War defence systems.

After 1970, international recognition shifted toward the People's Republic of China. Taiwan still grew economically and democratized. Economic ties with mainland China expanded after 1990, and by 2005 the mainland had become Taiwan's most important trading partner.

The rise of the Democratic Progressive Party strained relations with Beijing. Chen Shui-bian won the presidency in 2000 and 2004, while the KMT later regained power with Ma Ying-jeou in 2008. Cross-strait economic talks improved, including a 2010 trade agreement, but independence debates and mainland pressure remained central.
