# Calvin and Calvinism (Calvinism)

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> Calvinism is a Reformed Protestant tradition shaped by John Calvin and later churches, centred on divine sovereignty, grace, scriptural authority, disciplined communal life, and shared church governance. From Geneva it spread through European and global communities, influencing worship, education, politics, social reform, and debates over religious authority.

## Introduction

Calvinism is a family of Protestant theological traditions associated with John Calvin and the wider Reformed movement of the sixteenth century. It emphasizes God’s sovereignty, the authority of scripture, the centrality of grace, disciplined church life, and the idea that all areas of life should be lived responsibly before God. Calvinism is not identical to Calvin’s personal writings; later churches, confessions, ministers, and communities developed the tradition in different directions.

## Core Beliefs

Calvinist theology teaches that human beings cannot earn salvation through moral effort alone. Grace is understood as God’s undeserved initiative in rescuing and renewing people. The doctrine of predestination is often associated with Calvinism: God’s saving purpose is not dependent on human merit or prior achievement. This teaching was meant to stress divine freedom and assurance, though it has also raised difficult questions about free will, responsibility, and the meaning of human action.

Scripture is treated as the primary norm for faith and practice, but Calvinism has historically relied on education, preaching, confessions of faith, and collective interpretation rather than private reading alone. Reformed churches often emphasize a simple worship style centred on preaching, prayer, psalm singing, and sacraments. Baptism and the Lord’s Supper are important signs of covenant life, although Reformed teaching differs from Roman Catholic and Lutheran views about their exact meaning.

## Church and Community

Calvinist churches commonly use presbyterian governance, in which elders and ministers share responsibility for congregations and regional assemblies. This structure differs from episcopal systems led by bishops and from congregational systems in which each local church is fully independent. Church discipline historically aimed to shape moral community, but it could also become intrusive when religious and civic authority were closely joined.

[[Geneva]] became a major Reformed centre because religious refugees, printers, scholars, and ministers gathered there in the 1550s. Calvin’s academy trained pastors who carried Reformed teaching to France, Scotland, the Netherlands, England, parts of central Europe, and later North America. Geneva was not simply a model city; it was also a contested community where religious ideals, political authority, economic change, and social discipline collided.

## Spread and Legacy

Calvinism influenced French Huguenots, Scottish Presbyterians, Dutch Reformed communities, English Puritans, and many churches in North America, Africa, Asia, and the Pacific. Puritanism ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Puritans)) carried Reformed ideas into English debates about worship, monarchy, Parliament, education, and colonial settlement. Reformed traditions often encouraged literacy because believers were expected to study scripture, and they helped build schools, colleges, publishing networks, and voluntary associations.

The tradition’s legacy is complex. It has been connected with democratic participation, education, work discipline, mutual aid, missionary activity, social reform, and political resistance to tyranny. It has also been associated with religious exclusion, moral rigidity, colonial expansion, and conflict with other Christians. Modern Reformed churches interpret Calvinist inheritance in many ways, debating gender, race, public life, ecology, sexuality, economic justice, and relationships with other faiths.

## Key Events & Developments

- 1536: John Calvin published the first edition of *Institutes of the Christian Religion*.
- 1550s: Geneva became an international training and publishing centre for Reformed refugees and ministers.
- 1618–19: the Synod of Dort clarified major Reformed doctrines during conflict in the Dutch churches.

## Key People

- John Calvin ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Calvin)): Reformer and theologian whose writings shaped the Reformed tradition.
- John Knox ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Knox)): Scottish Reformer linked with the establishment of Presbyterianism in Scotland.
- Theodore Beza: Calvin’s successor in Geneva and an important Reformed theologian.

## Interesting Facts

- Predestination was intended to emphasize divine grace, not to make moral action irrelevant.
- Presbyterian governance distributes church authority among ministers and elected elders.
- Reformed traditions became global through migration, missions, education, and local adaptation.
