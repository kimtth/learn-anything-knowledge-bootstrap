# Game Theory

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> Game theory studies strategic choice where each decision-maker's outcome depends on the anticipated choices of others. Its analysis of pure and mixed strategies, conflict, cooperation, bargaining, and equilibrium provides a mathematical language for interdependent decisions in economics and beyond.

## Introduction

Game theory is a branch of applied mathematics that studies how rational decision-makers choose strategies when their outcomes depend on one another. This field began with John von Neumann and Oskar Morgenstern, who argued that economics is not like physics because people anticipate each other's choices. Their insight led to a new mathematical language for conflict, cooperation, competition, and bargaining. The central idea is that a player's best action depends on what others are likely to do, so strategy is always shaped by interdependence.

## Pure and Mixed Strategies

![[attachments/game_theory_001.webp|420]]

*Payoff matrix for a rock-paper-scissors game*

Simple examples show how players choose strategies in situations with clear payoffs. In a voting example, each party must decide whether to support, oppose, or evade an issue. The best choice depends on how the other side is likely to act, and the outcome can be described by a saddlepoint, where the maximum guaranteed result for one player matches the minimum possible loss for the other. This basic idea introduces the distinction between pure strategies, where a single best choice exists, and mixed strategies, where players randomize over options to avoid being exploited.

## Mixed-Strategy Equilibrium

![[attachments/game_theory_003.webp|420]]

*Hand-written note on game theory, from the papers of David Gregory*

A second example shows how a safecracker and a guard choose between two safes. Here no pure strategy is best for both sides, so the solution requires probabilities. The guard chooses a random pattern of protection, and the safecracker chooses a random pattern of attack, each to make the other indifferent to any deviation. This is the core idea of a mixed-strategy equilibrium: each player balances expected gains and losses so that the other cannot improve by changing strategy alone.

## Cooperative and Noncooperative Games

Game theory also addresses cooperative and noncooperative games. In a cooperative game, players can communicate and make agreements, while in a noncooperative game they cannot. It shows that communication can alter outcomes because players may coordinate to improve joint results. The discussion also introduces the role of threats and credible commitments, which can shape bargaining power. This is important because the same situation may produce different outcomes depending on whether players are allowed to cooperate.

## Nash Equilibrium

The theory also covers Nash equilibrium ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nash_equilibrium)) and the idea of a solution to bargaining problems. A Nash solution must be independent of how utilities are measured, cannot be improved for both players at once, should not depend on irrelevant alternatives, and must be symmetrical when players switch roles. An example of dividing $10,000 shows that the result can be mathematically fair in terms of utility even when it seems unequal in money. This illustrates a deeper theme of game theory: the value of an outcome depends on the preferences of the players, not only on the raw numbers involved.

## The Prisoners' Dilemma

![[attachments/game_theory_004.webp|420]]

*Prisoner's Dilemma conceptual matrix*

The classic Prisoners' Dilemma ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prisoner%27s_dilemma)) shows why individually rational choices can lead to a worse collective outcome. Two prisoners, each acting without communication, are tempted to confess because confession is best no matter what the other does. Yet if both confess, both do worse than if both remain silent. This paradox lies at the heart of game theory: the pursuit of self-interest can produce results that are inferior to cooperation. Practical examples include price wars, arms races, and overproduction, all of which resemble the same structure.

## Repeated Games

The analysis extends to repeated play, showing how the dilemma changes when players can look ahead and remember previous choices. If play begins cooperatively, both players may continue to cooperate because defection would trigger retaliation. If play starts at a noncooperative point, both may remain stuck there. The discussion emphasizes that the outcome depends not only on incentives but also on the starting point of interaction and the ability of players to anticipate future consequences.

## Cooperation in Repeated Play

Rational behavior in repeated games can sometimes escape the dilemma. Players who think ahead may choose cooperation because they know that defection would eventually bring punishment. Far-sighted players can move toward better outcomes even in situations that seem trapped by short-term incentives. This makes the theory useful for understanding not only economics but also social and political behavior.

## Game Theory in Biology

Game theory also connects to biology through the hawk-dove model. In this framework, animals can behave aggressively or peacefully, and the stable mix of strategies depends on the costs and rewards of conflict. The example of the bourgeois strategy, in which animals act as if they own a territory when they are already established, shows how evolutionary pressures can favor patterns that are not simply aggressive or purely peaceful. This application reveals that game theory can describe stable behavioral patterns in nature, not just human decisions.

## N-Person Games

The discussion of n-person games shows how the logic of strategic interaction extends beyond two players. In truels and similar situations, each participant must weigh not only his own survival but also the combined effects of others' choices. Rational players may still shoot, even when all survive better by restraint, because each person can improve his own chance by reducing the number of opponents. This illustrates the complexity that emerges when more than two actors interact.

## Voting and Social Choice

The final section brings game theory into voting and social choice. It shows that voters may select strategies that are not sincere, because they anticipate how others will vote. By eliminating dominated strategies step by step, players can reach a sophisticated outcome rather than simply choosing their first preference. Such reasoning can change who wins an election, even when the preference order of the voters remains the same.

## Strategic Reasoning

This analysis points to a broader lesson: strategic reasoning can transform the apparent meaning of an election or a bargaining situation. The outcome is not merely a reflection of preferences; it also depends on what players expect others to do and how they reason about those expectations. In this sense, game theory provides a powerful framework for understanding competition, cooperation, and collective decision-making across economics, politics, biology, and everyday life.

## Key Events & Developments

- 1944: John von Neumann and Oskar Morgenstern publish The Theory of Games and Economic Behavior.
- The theory is extended to bargaining, voting, and equilibrium analysis.
- John Nash develops the concept of equilibrium that bears his name.
- The Prisoners' Dilemma ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prisoner%27s_dilemma)) becomes a central model for cooperation and conflict.

## Key People

- John von Neumann ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_von_Neumann)): Founder of modern game theory.
- Oskar Morgenstern ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oskar_Morgenstern)): Co-author of the foundational work on game theory.
- Albert W. Tucker ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Albert_W._Tucker)): Popularized the Prisoners' Dilemma.
- John Nash: Developed equilibrium concepts that shaped the field.
