# Celtic Literature

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> Celtic literature comprises distinct Irish, Scottish Gaelic, Manx, Welsh, Cornish, and Breton writing traditions, rooted in oral poetry and saga, clerical manuscripts, bardic institutions, and religious change. Its history traces both linguistic suppression and recurrent revival through translation, folklore collection, and modern cultural renewal.

## Introduction

Celtic literature means writings in Gaelic and its related languages, including Irish, Scottish Gaelic, Manx, Welsh, Cornish, and Breton. These literatures form related but distinct cultural histories shaped by oral tradition, monastic learning, bardic institutions, religious change, and modern revival.

The overall pattern is one of ancient vernacular poetry and saga, later clerical and bardic preservation, then decline under social and political pressure, followed in several languages by modern revival. Writings in English by Irish, Scottish, and Welsh authors belong instead to [[English Literature|English literature]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/English_literature)), and French-language works by Breton authors to French literature.

Irish Gaelic is the oldest and richest of the Celtic literary traditions. Celtic reached Ireland no later than the La Tene culture of the 3rd century BC, and the language in its earliest form is called Goidelic. The earliest evidence is archaic sepulchral inscriptions in the ogham alphabet, cut as strokes and notches on stone or wood and ascribed to the 4th and 5th centuries AD, while writing in the Roman alphabet dates from 8th-century Old Irish glosses. Four periods are recognized: early literature composed by the professional fili and by churchmen; medieval literature dominated by hereditary bardic orders; late literature (17th-19th centuries) written by peasants in broken dialects; and the modern revival that continues today. The fili seem to have inherited the role of the learned priestly order, acting as judges, historians, and official poets until Christianity ended their priestly functions.

### Verse

Irish verse developed from alliterative and rhythmical forms into rhymed strophic poetry, with rhyme used from the 7th century and quatrains derived from Latin hymn metres. One of the earliest poems is a eulogy on St. Columba (c. 521-597) ascribed to Dallan Forgaill, chief poet of Ireland. The real glory of Irish verse lay with anonymous poets, such as the author of the famous address to Pangur, a white cat, whose work seems fresher and more vivid than the deliberately obscure style of the court fili. The monastic and eremitic movement of the Irish Church gave a strong impetus to nature poetry.

### Prose

Irish prose is dominated by saga literature, especially the cycle of the Ulaid, the people who gave their name to Ulster, whose figures include Conchobar, the boy warrior Cu Chulainn, queen Medb, and the doomed lovers Noisi and Deirdre. The nearest approach to an Irish epic is the Tain Bo Cuailnge (The Cattle Raid of Cooley), first put together in the 7th or 8th century and striking for its terse vividness. The sagas preserve a picture of primitive society—fighting from chariots, taking heads as trophies, the force of taboos—recorded in manuscripts such as Lebor Na Huidre (The Book of the Dun Cow) and The Book of Leinster.

## Scottish Gaelic

Scottish Gaelic literature survives in manuscripts, anthologies, and oral tradition. Its earliest writing consists of 12th-century marginalia in the Book of Deer, while the most important early literary manuscript is the Book of the Dean of Lismore, an anthology of verse compiled between 1512 and 1526 by Sir James MacGregor and his brother Duncan, holding the earliest extensive collection of heroic Gaelic ballads. Bardic poetry continued through hereditary families such as the MacMhuirichs, whose work spans nearly 500 years from the 13th to the 18th century. Work songs, including waulking songs used in fulling cloth, laments such as Griogal Cridhe, and the first Gaelic book printed in Scotland (1567) round out the tradition.

## Manx

Manx literature is limited by the island's political history. The Gaels lost control of the Isle of Man to the Norse in the 9th century and to the English after 1333, so they could not provide the aristocratic support the bardic institution needed, and no medieval Manx literature survives. The Reformation came slowly: a Manx Book of Common Prayer was completed about 1610 by John Phillips, but the Bible was translated only piecemeal between 1748 and 1773. Apart from translations, including a version of Milton's Paradise Lost ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paradise_Lost)) (1794), most surviving Manx literature is folklore and religious prose.

## Welsh

Welsh literature forms a continuous tradition from about the mid-6th century to the present, though almost all pre-Norman poetry survives only in 12th- to 15th-century manuscripts. The heroic poets Taliesin, who praised Urien of Rheged, and Aneirin, to whom the long elegiac poem Y Gododdin is attributed, set standards for later ages. Their alliteration and internal rhyme developed by the 13th century into the intricate system of consonant correspondence called cynghanedd. The Reformation brought major prose translations of the Bible and prayer book, and the 18th and 19th centuries saw two great revivals in poetry and prose.

### The Reformation and revival

William Salesbury translated most of the New Testament of 1567 and, with Richard Davies, the Welsh Prayer Book, while William Morgan, aided by Edmwnd Prys, produced the Welsh Bible ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bible)) of 1588, whose 1620 revision is still used; these translations founded modern Welsh prose. The 18th-century revival was led by Goronwy Owen, who reintroduced the strict cywydd and awdl metres, and the second revival followed the establishment of the University of Wales (1872-93), which renewed literary standards through figures such as Sir John Morris-Jones and T. Gwynn Jones.

## Cornish

Cornish literature survives mainly in religious drama, with a few early glosses and a 12th-century vocabulary. The most important work is the Ordinalia, a Middle Cornish trilogy of about the late 14th century designed to be acted on three consecutive days, perhaps in a plen-an-gwary (play-field). Other surviving plays include Beunans Meriasek (1504), on the patron saint of Camborne, and Gwreans an Bys (The Creation of the World), written in Late Cornish in 1611. The Protestant Reformation destroyed the mentality that had created and enjoyed these plays.

## Breton

Breton literature falls into Old Breton (8th-11th century), known only from glosses; Middle Breton (11th-17th century), largely religious verse including the mystery plays Buez santez Nonn and Buhez santes Barba and the 1499 dictionary Catholicon; and Modern Breton, said to begin in 1659. The modern revival was sparked by the celebrated but disputed Barzaz Breiz (1839) of Theodore Hersart de La Villemarque, whose questioned authenticity led Francois-Marie Luzel to collect authentic folk songs. Twentieth-century journals such as Gwalarn (1925-44) and writers like Roparz Hemon made Breton writing a conscious effort to keep the language alive against the fear that it would die.

## Conclusion

Celtic literature is a family of vernacular traditions with strong oral roots, deep religious influence, and repeated cycles of suppression and renewal. Its history is less that of a single literary canon than of several related cultures—Irish, Scottish, Manx, Welsh, Cornish, and Breton—struggling to preserve language and memory.

## Key Events & Developments

- 4th-5th centuries AD: Early Irish ogham inscriptions appear.
- 7th-12th centuries: Irish saga and verse are compiled in manuscripts.
- 12th-16th centuries: Scottish Gaelic bardic and anthology traditions flourish.
- 16th century: Welsh translation and Reformation literature expand prose.
- 17th-19th centuries: Cornish and Breton traditions decline and revive in different ways.
- 19th-20th centuries: Breton and Welsh literary revivals strengthen modern vernacular writing.
