## Introduction

Iceland ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iceland)) is a North Atlantic island country on the active geologic boundary between [[North America]] and [[Europe]]. Its landscape combines glaciers, volcanoes, geysers, lava fields, fjords, and geothermal springs, while the Gulf Stream gives this northern country a milder climate than latitude alone would suggest. The capital, Reykjavik, stands on the site of the island's first farmstead and functions as the national centre of government, commerce, higher education, and culture.

Icelandic identity is shaped by isolation and European connection at the same time. The island was settled in the Viking Age by mainly Norse and Celtic people, and its sagas preserve memories of settlement, law, family conflict, and heroic legend. Modern Iceland is ethnically homogeneous, highly literate, technologically advanced, and strongly committed to preserving its language and traditions.

## Land

![[attachments/iceland_003.webp|420]]

*001 Volcano eruption of Litli-Hrútur in Iceland in 2023 Photo by Giles Laurent*

Iceland ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iceland)) has more than 3,000 miles of rugged coastline, bordered by the Greenland Sea, Norwegian Sea, Atlantic Ocean, and Denmark Strait. Glacier ice and cooled lava each cover about one-tenth of the country. Vatnajokull, the largest glacier, covers more than 3,000 square miles and is roughly as extensive as all the glaciers of continental Europe combined.

[[Volcanism]] is central to Iceland's physical character because the island sits on the Mid-Atlantic Ridge. It contains about 200 volcanoes, and since 1500 Icelandic volcanoes have produced an estimated one-third of Earth's total lava flow. Surtsey emerged from a submarine eruption between 1963 and 1967, while eruptions on Heimaey, in the Krafla area, at Hekla, and near Eyjafjallajokull show how geologic activity can reshape settlements, power projects, and air travel.

## Relief, Waters, and Soils

Relief is dominated by a faulted tableland averaging about 1,640 feet above sea level. The highest point is Hvannadals Peak, rising to 6,952 feet on Orfajokull in Vatnajokull. Basalt lava layers, U-shaped glacial valleys, fjords, plateaus, table mountains, and steep volcanic masses give the country a young, sharply cut appearance.

Geothermal activity appears in hot springs, solfataras, and frequent minor earthquakes. Deildartunguhver emits nearly 50 gallons of boiling water per second, and the Torfajokull area represents a major high-temperature field. Since the mid-20th century, many buildings have used reinforced concrete designed to withstand severe shocks.

Drainage reflects heavy rainfall, glacial melt, springs, lava dams, and ice dams. Rivers may be debris-filled glacial streams or clear spring-fed streams; waterfalls are common in the young mountain landscape. Many fjords have excellent natural harbours because glacial erosion deepened their inner basins, while other coasts are sandy and lined with lagoons behind offshore bars.

[[Soils]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soil)) include mineral yellow-brown loess and organic soils. Both can support agriculture, but the cool northern climate slows biological activity, so cultivation often requires heavy fertilization. Much of the best farming lies in lowlands, especially in the south.

## Climate and Life

[[Climate and Weather (Climate)|Climate]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Climate)) is maritime subarctic, shaped by polar and tropical air masses, the Gulf Stream, the East Greenland Current, and North Atlantic weather fronts. Reykjavik averages about 40 F annually, with a January mean near freezing and a July mean around 51 F. Precipitation ranges from dry northern plateaus to very wet southern mountain slopes, and winter gales, fog, dark days, sunshine intervals, and aurora displays are all part of the seasonal pattern.

Vegetation ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vegetation)) lies between tundra and taiga. Only about one-fourth of Iceland has continuous plant cover; bogs, moors, sparse grasslands, and birch remnants are common. Overgrazing and woodcutting reduced earlier birch woodland, but reforestation efforts have continued since the 1950s. The Arctic fox was the only land mammal present at settlement; humans later introduced livestock, rodents, reindeer, and mink. Iceland has no reptiles or amphibians.

## People and Settlement

[[Population]] is highly homogeneous, descended largely from settlers who arrived from about 874 for roughly 60 years. Between 60 and 80 percent of the settlers were Nordic, mainly from Norway, while the remainder were largely Celtic, from Scotland and Ireland. Old Norse evolved into modern Icelandic, and modern Icelanders can still read the medieval sagas with unusual ease.

Religion ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Religion)) has been dominated by Lutheranism since the mid-16th century. About nine-tenths of the population belongs to the state-supported Evangelical Lutheran church, though freedom of religion is recognized. Historically, farming distributed people across inhabitable regions, but fishing, commerce, services, and education concentrated population in towns. Reykjavik and its environs hold about three-fifths of the population, with Akureyri, Reykjanesbaer, Akranes, Isafjordhur, Selfoss, and fishing towns forming important regional centres.

Demographic history includes the first comprehensive census in 1703, when 50,358 people were counted. The population fell by 1801, then grew to nearly 80,000 by 1901, more than 140,000 by 1950, and about a quarter million by the late 1980s. Emigration to North America was significant from 1870 to 1914.

## Economy

Economy ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economy)) has long depended on fishing and fish products, supported by manufacturing, services, tourism, hydropower, and geothermal energy. Fish prices and catches strongly affect growth. After World War II, GDP growth was rapid; after 1987, fish-catch limits slowed expansion; from the late 1990s to mid-2000s, foreign capital and improved catches revived growth before the 2008 financial collapse.

Agriculture ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agriculture)) employs a small share of the population and centres on sheep, dairy farming, grazing, hay, potatoes, turnips, and greenhouse crops warmed by geothermal heat. Fishing became highly mechanized, but overfishing forced quota systems and stock management. Cod recovery, foreign fishing disputes, and the protection of offshore grounds are recurring economic and political concerns.

Manufacturing ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manufacturing)) is led by aluminum smelting, which uses domestic hydroelectricity to process imported alumina. Other exports and industries include ferrosilicon, diatomite, fish-processing equipment, fishing gear, prosthetic devices, software, cement, fertilizer, food, clothing, and books. Geothermal and hydroelectric power also support heating, industry, greenhouse agriculture, and experimental hydrogen-energy projects.

Finance ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Finance)) was deregulated from the 1980s, and capital movements were largely liberalized by the mid-1990s. The resulting boom made banks and securities markets attractive to foreign investors, but the 2008 credit crisis brought bank failure, krona devaluation, inflation, high interest rates, and government intervention. Trade ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trade)) is closely tied to Europe: the EU receives most exports and supplies more than half of imports, while fish products and aluminum dominate exports.

Services include tourism, transportation, communications, public administration, and education. Hot springs, geysers, glaciers, volcanoes, and scenic routes draw foreign visitors, and Icelandair helped make tourism a major earner of foreign exchange. Iceland has no railroads; the Ring Road circles the island, and Keflavik International Airport links the country to Europe and North America.

## Government and Society

[[The Forms Of Government Their Historical Development (Government)|Government]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Government)) is parliamentary and republican under the constitution adopted when Iceland became a republic in 1944. The president is directly elected, but executive power rests mainly with the cabinet, which depends on support in the Althing. The Althing has 63 members chosen by proportional representation, and all citizens aged 18 and older may vote.

Justice is handled by a supreme court and lower courts with appointed judges and no jury system. Local governments administer primary education, municipal services, and social programs. Iceland has no military forces except a small coast guard, joined the [[United Nations]] in 1946, and became a charter member of NATO in 1949.

Welfare includes compulsory health insurance, free inpatient hospital care, subsidized medical services, pensions, unemployment insurance, family benefits, and sickness benefits, financed through taxation. Education is free from primary school through university, compulsory to age 16, and centred on institutions such as the University of Iceland, founded in 1911, and the university at Akureyri, founded in 1987.

## Culture

Cultural life ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Culture)) is unusually rich for a small population. Medieval sagas, the Eddaic tradition, weaving, silverwork, wood carving, theatre, opera, symphony music, museums, publishing, and festivals all remain important. The Reykjavik area contains many of the main cultural institutions, while rural and local traditions preserve crafts, foods, riding, swimming, fishing, and community festivals.

Literature and arts link medieval and modern Iceland. The sagas record the first centuries of settlement, while Halldor Laxness won the Nobel Prize for Literature in 1955. Visual artists such as Johannes Sveinsson Kjarval and Asmundur Sveinsson, and musicians such as Bjork and Sigur Ros, show the international reach of Icelandic culture.

## History

![[attachments/iceland_001.webp|420]]

*Iceland satellite*

Settlement ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Human_settlement)) began around 870. Ingolfr Arnarson is named as the first permanent settler, establishing a farm at Reykjavik in 874 with Hallveig Frodadottir. More than 400 settlers followed, mainly from Norway and Norse settlements in the British Isles. The Althing developed around 930, creating a commonwealth of chieftains, courts, law, and assemblies without a king or central executive.

Christianization came peacefully through an Althing decision in 999 or 1000, after pressure from Olaf I Tryggvason and missionary activity. Bishoprics were founded at Skalholt in 1056 and Holar in 1106, and written law began in 1117-1118. Internal struggles among powerful chieftain families eventually ended the independent commonwealth, and Iceland submitted to the Norwegian king in 1262-1264.

Late medieval Iceland remained separately administered after union with Norway and then with Denmark in 1380. Dried fish trade connected the island to Bergen, English merchants, and later German traders, but ecological decline, woodland depletion, soil erosion, harsher climate, and plague weakened society. Epidemics in 1402-1404 and 1494-1495 each killed about half the population.

Reformation resistance ended when Jon Arason, bishop of Holar, was captured and beheaded in 1550. Monastic lands passed to the Danish crown, and a trade monopoly established in 1602 tied Iceland to Danish merchants for nearly two centuries. Absolutism was acknowledged in 1662, the Althing was abolished in 1800, and the 18th century brought hardship, famine, volcanic disaster, and depopulation.

Independence ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Independence)) revived in the 19th century. Christian VIII restored the Althing as a consultative assembly in 1845, and Jon Sigurdsson argued that sovereignty surrendered to the Danish king should return to Icelanders. A constitution in 1874 gave legislative power in internal affairs, home rule came in 1904, sovereignty as a separate state under the Danish crown came in 1918, and the republic was proclaimed at Thingvellir on June 17, 1944, with Sveinn Bjornsson as first president.

Modern Iceland became a welfare state after World War II, supported by trawler restoration, fish freezing, full employment, air travel, and later high Internet connectivity. Postwar politics revolved around coalitions, NATO defense, relations with Europe, fishing limits, and women's public status. Vigdis Finnbogadottir became president in 1980, and Johanna Sigurardottir became prime minister in 2009.

## Key Events & Developments

- Settlement ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Human_settlement)): Norse-Celtic migration began around 870, and Reykjavik was settled in 874.
- Commonwealth: The Althing and chieftain system governed Iceland from about 930 to 1262.
- Christianization: A peaceful Althing decision made Iceland Christian in 999 or 1000.
- Foreign rule: Submission to Norway in 1262-1264 and the Danish-Norwegian union of 1380 linked Iceland to Scandinavian crowns.
- Reformation: The execution of Jon Arason in 1550 ended organized Catholic resistance.
- Independence ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Independence)): Restored representation, the 1874 constitution, 1904 home rule, 1918 sovereignty, and the 1944 republic formed the modern state.
- Fishing limits: Iceland expanded its exclusive fishing zone from 3 nautical miles in 1950 to 200 nautical miles in 1975; Britain recognized the limit in 1976.
- Financial crisis: The 2008 banking collapse reshaped politics, finance, and debate over European integration.
