# Ferns and Other Lower Vascular Plants (Fern)

> [!abstract] Reference navigation

## Introduction

Ferns ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fern)) are nonflowering vascular plants ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vascular_plant)) that reproduce by spores rather than seeds. They have true roots, stems, and complex leaves, and many species unroll their young leaves from tight coiled structures called fiddleheads. Botanists recognize about 9,000 species, though some estimates run higher because tropical regions still contain many undiscovered forms. Ferns are ancient plants, with some groups dating back to the Carboniferous Period, and their spore-based life cycle long predates seed plants.
Ferns are extremely diverse in size and habitat. They range from tiny plants only a few millimetres tall to large tree ferns, and they live from tropical rainforests to dry, exposed sites. Some grow in water, others as epiphytes on trees, and a few act as weeds or invasive species.

Some fern species also begin ecological succession by colonizing bare rock crevices, bogs, or marshes before forest vegetation returns.

Their success depends partly on small, easily dispersed spores and partly on the ability of many species to exploit moist microhabitats. Even where a landscape seems dry, shaded rock faces, stream margins, tree trunks, and soil pockets can provide enough water for the gametophyte stage and fertilization.

## Distribution and abundance

Ferns are most abundant in the tropics, especially in rainforests, where a few hectares may contain more than 100 species. Many tropical ferns grow on the trunks and branches of trees, while others are restricted to certain rock types or marshy habitats. Ferns become less common in colder and drier environments, though a few species survive in Arctic and Antarctic regions.

It also describes the spread of some ferns as pests. Bracken, for example, can invade abandoned fields, and water ferns such as Salvinia ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salvinia)) can cover lakes and block irrigation channels. Some species have also spread after being introduced to new regions, especially in tropical and subtropical areas.

## Ecology

Ecologically, ferns are most often plants of shaded, damp forests, but some have adapted to exposed sun, rocky ground, or salt marshes. Species on rocks or in dry habitats often have thick tissues, waxy surfaces, hairs, or scales that help them conserve water. In tropical forests, some ferns are epiphytic, living on trees, while others climb up trunks. These forms show that ferns are highly flexible in how they use available space and moisture.

## Spore structure and reproduction

The spore is the main means of fern dispersal. Fern spores vary in shape, size, and wall structure, and these differences help scientists identify species. Most ferns are homosporous, producing one kind of spore, but some are heterosporous, producing small microspores and large megaspores. In heterosporous ferns, the gametophyte is reduced and the embryo depends on stored food within the megaspore.

Spores may have thick or thin walls. Thick-walled spores can survive for years, while thin-walled green spores are short-lived but germinate quickly in moist conditions. When the spore wall cracks, a small gametophyte develops. This stage begins with a rhizoid and a green cell that forms the rest of the plant.

## Gametophyte and embryo

The fern gametophyte is usually a small, flat structure that produces sex organs. In some species it is ribbonlike, filamentous, or tuberlike, showing major variation in form. The embryo develops inside the archegonium and later becomes an independent sporophyte. In most ferns, the embryo develops in a precise pattern, with the first leaf, stem, root, and foot forming from four initial cells.

The young sporophyte remains attached to the gametophyte for a time, drawing nutrients from it until its own roots can support it.

## Stem, roots, and leaves

Fern stems vary greatly. Some are narrow rhizomes, others are thick trunks in tree ferns, and some are climbing or vine-like. Many stems are protected by scales or hairs, and some have a dense covering of old leaf bases that acts as armour. Fern roots are usually thin and wiry, though some tropical species have thick or hairy roots. Tree fern roots are valuable in horticulture, and their use has led to conservation restrictions in some countries.

Fern leaves, or fronds, are especially diverse. They may be simple, once divided, or highly compound. Their veins are usually free, but some groups have netlike venation. The characteristic coiled young leaf, or fiddlehead, is one of the most recognizable signs of fern form.

## Cytogenetics and evolution

Fern cytogenetics centers on chromosomes and breeding systems. Ferns usually have high base chromosome numbers, but heterosporous ferns have lower ones. Scientists have suggested that these high numbers reflect ancient polyploidy, although most living fern species behave functionally like diploids. Ferns also show frequent hybridization, and many hybrids are sterile, though some reproduce by apogamy or other asexual methods.

The evolutionary section stresses that fern origins are still uncertain. The earliest stems and leaves may have evolved through complex changes in vascular structure, and the exact origin of fern leaves is still debated. Modern fern classification has become more stable through molecular studies, even though some genera remain difficult to place.

## Classification
A modern classification is based on the Smith system. It groups ferns into major classes such as Psilotopsida, Equisetopsida, Marattiopsida, and Polypodiopsida. The true ferns, or Polypodiopsida, include many families such as tree ferns, filmy ferns, royal ferns, and polypodies. The classification reflects both traditional morphology and newer molecular evidence.

## Key Events & Developments

- Ferns are ancient plants with a spore-based life cycle that predates seed plants.
- They are most diverse in tropical rainforests and less common in cold or dry regions.
- Some species became invasive in new habitats, especially water ferns and bracken.
- Fern reproduction includes both sexual spores and unusual forms such as apogamy and heterospory.
- Modern classification now relies strongly on molecular data.

## Key People

## Interesting Facts

- Some fern spores can remain viable for decades.
- Tree fern roots are used in horticulture, which has created conservation concerns.
- A few ferns can grow in extreme environments, including water and exposed rock.
- The gametophyte stage can sometimes be more abundant than the visible fern plant itself.
