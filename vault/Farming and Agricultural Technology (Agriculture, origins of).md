# Farming and Agricultural Technology (Agriculture, origins of)

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> The origins of agriculture describe the long process by which people altered ecosystems, cultivated plants, managed animals, and created domesticated species. These practices increased useful biomass and reshaped human settlement, food production, and environments.

agriculture ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agriculture)) is best understood as a long-term human effort to shape ecosystems for food, materials, and other needs. People alter habitats by clearing vegetation, tilling soil, and managing plant and animal populations, which increases the amount of useful biomass and reduces competition among organisms. Over time, some species become domesticated, meaning they depend on human care and selection for survival and reproduction. Plants such as grasses, beans, and potatoes were especially easy to domesticate because they had traits that made them useful to people, while animals that were naturally social and manageable were more likely to become domesticates.
That domestication is not simply a response to hunger or poverty. Evidence suggests that agriculture emerged in places where people already had stable ways of life, and that new crops and animals often supported existing hunting and gathering practices before full farming systems took over. This view places agriculture on a broad continuum of plant and animal management rather than treating it as a sudden invention.

## Southwest Asia

The earliest clear trajectory toward agriculture appears in Southwest Asia, where communities began to harvest wild grains well before fully domesticated crops were established. Sites such as Ohalo II and Netiv Hagdud in Israel show that people were already collecting large amounts of wild barley, legumes, and nuts by about 23,000 BP and 11,500 BP. At Abu Hureyra in Syria, archaeologists find both early foraging practices and evidence of emerging domestication, including wild einkorn, rye, lentils, and vetch. that some of the earliest rye may be domesticated, but the oldest indisputable domesticated grain is einkorn from Nevali Cori in Turkey, dating to about 10,500 BP.

By roughly 10,000 BP, village farming had spread widely across Southwest Asia, and within a few centuries settled agricultural communities were common. The shift was not a single event but a gradual process in which people intensified the gathering of wild cereals, then selected for useful traits, and eventually built permanent settlements. This region therefore stands as a major center for the first clear development of farming villages and domesticated grain crops.

## The Americas

In the Americas, agriculture developed independently in several regions, especially [[South America]], Mesoamerica, and eastern [[North America]]. Indigenous peoples built diverse systems suited to very different climates, from southern Canada to the Amazon lowlands. Although some animals were domesticated, none were suitable as heavy draft animals, so the plow and other traction-based technology did not become central in the Americas.

A major farming system was swidden agriculture, or slash-and-burn cultivation. In this method, people cut and burn vegetation to release nutrients and create temporary fields that could support varied crops. The system was flexible and ecologically diverse, and settlements often moved as soil fertility declined. In the Andes, the Inca used terraces on steep slopes, with foot plows, hoes, and manure from llamas, alpacas, and people to maintain yields. Corn was the most widespread crop, while other cultigens such as potatoes, squash, beans, and amaranth had more localized distributions.

## East Asia

In East Asia, agricultural communities appeared in China before about 8000 BP. Northern China developed millet, hemp, and Chinese cabbage, while southern China developed rice. Water buffalo, pigs, and chickens were also domesticated, though their early history is less well documented. By 8000–7000 BP, communities in the Yangtze basin were using both dry-field cultivation and river-edge flooding, and the invention of paddy fields later made rice production more intensive and productive.
Farming also spread into [[Korea]] and [[Japan]]. In Korea, millet appeared by about 5500 BP, and rice spread from China by 4000 BP. In Japan, a long sequence of Jomon settlements shows early management of plant resources, and by about 4000 BP people had domesticated at least one plant, with rice and millet arriving later from the continent.

## Europe

Agriculture in [[Europe]] spread through a mix of migration and diffusion. The earliest evidence comes from the Mediterranean coast, where people could move and trade by sea. Archaeological sites such as Franchthi Cave in Greece show that some Southwest Asian plants were present early, but it is unclear whether they were locally domesticated or introduced. By about 9000 BP, sheep, goats, pigs, barley, lentils, and several wheats were part of the region’s economy, and cattle appeared by 8000 BP.

As farming moved north and west, people adapted crops and animals to cooler and more varied conditions. In the Mediterranean, fewer changes were needed; in more temperate zones, cattle, pigs, emmer, einkorn, and legumes became especially important. This expansion was driven by population movement, trade, and local adaptation rather than a single origin point.

## Sumer

In Sumer, one of the world’s earliest civilizations, agriculture was highly organized and intensive. The region between the Tigris and Euphrates supported barley as the main crop, along with wheat, flax, dates, apples, plums, and grapes. Sheep and goats were especially important, and large flocks were kept in towns such as Ur. labor systems in which temple personnel, overseers, and farmers worked together to produce, store, and distribute food.

The farming technology of Sumer included oxen pulling light plows, sickles for harvesting, and wagons with solid wheels. Horses arrived later, and only after several centuries did they replace onagers as draft animals. How agriculture supported not only subsistence but also the growth of urban administration and large-scale social organization.

## Egypt

Ancient [[Egypt]] developed agriculture later than Southwest Asia, with domesticated animals and crops introduced from that region. By the first part of the 7th millennium BP, villages in Al-Fayyum kept sheep, goats, and pigs and cultivated emmer, barley, cotton, and flax. The dry climate encouraged pit silos and specialized harvesting tools. By the predynastic period, agriculture had expanded into the Nile valley, and later tomb and artifact evidence shows that animals such as gazelles and deer were also kept under some form of control.
Egypt is a case where agriculture became a sophisticated state enterprise by about 4525 BP. It points to the importance of irrigation, large settlements, and hierarchical social organization in making farming productive in a river valley environment.

## Mesoamerica

In Mesoamerica, archaeological evidence is more difficult to interpret, but several important early domestication signs stand out. At Guila Naquitz, people were using acorns, pine nuts, prickly pear, mesquite seeds, runner beans, and grasses, and some squash seeds were already larger than wild forms. This suggests that domestication was under way by about 10,000 BP. Maize and manioc appear in later levels at San Andres, and cotton and possibly domesticated sunflower also appear in the record.
Maize may have been domesticated elsewhere and then introduced into Mesoamerica, because it appears in a highly developed form rather than in a clear gradual sequence. This uncertainty reflects how difficult it is to reconstruct the earliest phases of agriculture in tropical regions.

## South America

In [[South America]], plant use followed an early and broad pattern. Potatoes were collected by 14,000 BP, and domesticated potatoes appear in coastal sites by 5000 BP. Guinea pigs were economically important by 14,000–8000 BP, and llamas and alpacas were probably domesticated by 7500–6000 BP. Quinoa, cotton, squash, and peanuts also have early records in the Andes and lowlands.

It also argues that tropical lowland systems may have a long history, especially in the Amazon basin, where swidden agriculture may have supported maize, manioc, cacao, chili peppers, bananas, sweet potatoes, and tobacco. The evidence is debated because some plant remains are phytoliths that cannot yet be directly dated, but the region clearly played a major role in agricultural diversity.

## North America

North America had three or four agricultural complexes north of the Rio Grande. The Upper and Lower Sonoran complexes in the Southwest included corn, squash, beans, and sometimes cotton. Corn appears at Bat Cave by about 3200 BP, and beans appear later. These systems were integrated into the diets of highly mobile Archaic cultures, which also managed wild plants and used burning to encourage useful species.

North America shows a transition from plant management to more settled food production. By about 1700 BP, many Southwestern societies shifted toward more permanent farming, and by 900 BP, communities such as the Ancestral Pueblo, Hohokam, and Mogollon were widespread. The discussion shows that agriculture in North America did not follow one uniform path but developed through local adaptation to dry environments.

## China

Agriculture in [[China]] was divided by the Qin Mountains into a northern zone based mainly on wheat and millet and a southern zone based on rice. Other domesticates included soybeans, peach, persimmon, hemp, rapeseed, tea, water chestnut, and silk production. Early settlements in Inner Mongolia and the Loess Plateau date from roughly 9000–8000 BP, and pit-house villages show the early movement toward permanent settlement.

The evidence from sites such as Xinglongwa, Peiligang, and Cishan shows a long process in which communities combined hunting, gathering, storage, and cultivation. China is one of the world’s major centers of agricultural invention, especially in the development of millet, rice, and associated wetland farming systems.

## Korea

In [[Korea]], the Chulmun culture relied mainly on hunting, gathering, and fishing between 8000 and 4000 BP. Millet appears at Tongsamdong by 5500 BP, and rice seems to have arrived from China by 4000 BP. The later Mumun period brought more intensive agriculture, with rice, wheat, soybeans, adzuki beans, and hemp becoming important.

## Japan

In [[Japan]], archaeologists identify an unbroken sequence of cultures from very early times. The world’s oldest pottery appears in Japan, China, and eastern Siberia, and Jomon settlements show early management of wild plants, fruits, and nuts. This suggests that Jomon people were already food producers in some sense, even before full agriculture arrived from the continent.

## India
[[India]] is a major center of crop diversity and an important historical setting for agricultural development. Early texts such as the Rigveda describe plows, yokes, irrigation, and crop rotation, while Megasthenes later gives a vivid account of India’s irrigated fields and double harvests. The Mughal period is also discussed, showing that agriculture had long been adapted to monsoon rainfall and the management of rivers and canals.

## Southeast Asia

In [[Southeast Asia]], crops such as black pepper, sugarcane, banana, nutmeg, taro, arrowroot, coconut, clove, yam, and citrus are native or early staples. Evidence from Khok Phanom Di and Banyan Valley Cave suggests that rice was part of the region’s agricultural history by 4700–4000 BP, although the exact timing remains uncertain. That the highland site of Kuk Swamp in New Guinea may preserve a long history of drainage and wetland management.

## Improvements in agriculture in the West: 200 BCE to 1600 CE
In the West, Roman farming methods, livestock management, and village systems shaped agricultural practice over many centuries. Roman estates used hand tools, plows, composting, and manure, while livestock such as cattle, sheep, goats, pigs, and donkeys were central to farm life. The medieval period brought further changes in Europe, with recovery in some regions and decline in others.

The discussion also highlights regional differences. The [[Netherlands]] improved rotation and manure use, England expanded sheep and enclosure, [[Spain]] faced conflict between shepherds and farmers, and [[France]], [[Germany]], and [[Italy]] each developed local approaches shaped by climate, landholding, and population change.

## Scientific agriculture: the 20th century

The final sections emphasize how rapidly agriculture changed in the 20th century. Mechanization, especially the tractor, transformed farming in industrial countries, and electricity made pumping, milling, and barn work more efficient. New tools such as the power takeoff, rubber tires, and improved tractor design increased output and reduced labor. That pest control, hybrid seeds, irrigation, and scientific breeding became central to modern farming.

## The Green Revolution

The Green Revolution ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Green_Revolution)) brought major gains in yields through new wheat and rice varieties, especially when combined with water, fertilizer, and pesticides. These improvements helped many countries increase food production, but poorer farmers often could not afford the required inputs. The wider lesson is that agricultural technology is not only about machines and seeds; it is also shaped by economics, politics, and the ability of communities to adopt new methods.

## Key Events & Developments

- Early plant and animal management appeared before full domestication in Southwest Asia and elsewhere.
- Village farming emerged in Southwest Asia by about 10,000 BP.
- Independent agricultural centers developed in the Americas, East Asia, Europe, and Africa.
- Irrigation, terraces, and large-scale land management became important in river valleys and upland regions.
- The 20th century brought mechanization, scientific breeding, and the Green Revolution.
