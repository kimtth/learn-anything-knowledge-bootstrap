# Pakistan

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> Pakistan is a predominantly Muslim South Asian country formed in 1947 from British India, with geography centered on the Indus basin and northern mountains. Partition, the loss of East Pakistan as Bangladesh, conflict with India, military rule, religious politics, and democratic instability shape its national history.

## Introduction

Pakistan ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pakistan)) is a populous, multiethnic country of South Asia, historically and culturally linked with India but distinguished since 1947 by its overwhelmingly Muslim population. Its capital is Islamabad, and its largest city is Karachi on the Arabian Sea. Pakistan was created from British India through the demand of Islamic nationalists led by Mohammed Ali Jinnah and the All India Muslim League.

From independence until 1971, Pakistan consisted of West Pakistan in the Indus basin and East Pakistan in the Ganges-Brahmaputra delta. Civil war led East Pakistan to become [[Bangladesh]]. Pakistan's history has been marked by military rule, disputes with India, the Kashmir conflict, nuclear rivalry, religious politics, ethnic tensions, and struggles for stable democratic institutions.

The human cost of partition shaped Pakistan from its first year. As many as 10 million Muslim refugees left India for Pakistan, while a similar number of Hindus and Sikhs left the new Pakistani territories for India. These rapid population transfers transformed cities and provinces, intensified communal memory, and helped make the India-Pakistan rivalry a permanent part of national politics.

## Land and Relief

Pakistan is bounded by [[Iran]], [[Afghanistan]], [[China]], [[India]], and the Arabian Sea. The disputed Kashmir region is divided among Pakistan, India, and China. Pakistan's land includes five major regions: the Himalayan and Karakoram ranges, the Hindu Kush and western mountains, the Balochistan plateau, the submontane plateau, and the Indus River plain.

The country lies where Central Asian, Iranian, and South Asian routes meet. The Khyber and Bolan passes, the Indus corridor, the Makran coast, and the Karakoram Highway all show how mountains can isolate valleys while also creating strategic gateways. This setting made Pakistan a frontier, a trade route, and a security state from its beginning.

## Northern Mountains

![[attachments/pakistan_001.webp|420]]

*K2 in the Karakoram range of northern Pakistan*

The Himalayan and Karakoram ranges form Pakistan's northern rampart. Peaks include K2, or Mount Godwin Austen, at 28,251 feet, and Nanga Parbat. The Indus, Jhelum, Shyok, and other rivers descend from these ranges. The Karakoram Highway, completed in 1970, links Gilgit with Kashgar in China. Glaciers such as Siachen feed the river system.

Northern Pakistan's mountain barrier also shapes precipitation by intercepting monsoon winds from the south. Settlement is sparse except in favoured valleys, where barley, fruit, and especially apricots are grown. Pine timber occurs in some zones but has been reduced by cutting and overgrazing.

## Hindu Kush, Balochistan, and Indus Plain

![[attachments/pakistan_003.webp|420]]

*Indus River valley landscape in Pakistan*

The Hindu Kush branches from the Pamir Knot and sends ranges through Chitral, Dir, and Swat. Passes such as Khyber, Kurram, Tochi, Gomal, and Bolan historically opened routes into the subcontinent. The arid Balochistan plateau contains mountain basins, deserts, and the Makran coast. The Indus River plain, about 200,000 square miles, is Pakistan's fertile core, divided into upper and lower plains, doabs, floodplains, deserts, and the Indus delta.

The upper Indus plain is organized around the Punjab rivers: Indus, Jhelum, Chenab, Ravi, Beas, and Sutlej. The lower plain has fewer tributaries and a more Nile-like character. Flooding remains a perennial problem, especially when monsoon rains swell the Indus and its tributaries in July and August.

## Soils and Climate

Pakistan's [[Soils|soils]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soil)) are mostly dry pedocals with calcium carbonate and low organic matter. Indus basin soils are alluvial and productive under irrigation, though waterlogging and salinity affect large areas. The climate is marked by aridity, erratic monsoon rainfall, continental temperature extremes, and regional variation. Rainfall decreases from the northeast toward the Indus corridor and Balochistan, while coastal areas are moderated by sea breezes.

Climate controls cultivation as strongly as relief. North of the main plains, snow and glacier melt sustain rivers, but most lowland rainfall arrives in short monsoon bursts from early July to mid-September. High temperatures increase evaporation, so areas south and west of the stronger monsoon zone depend heavily on canals, barrages, wells, and riverine strips.

Pakistan's desert areas include Cholistan or Rohi, Pat or Thar, and the formerly unproductive Thal Desert. Irrigation projects such as the Jinnah Barrage and Thal canal system turned some desert land into farmland. The Indus delta, by contrast, is saline, marshy, and vulnerable when high tides coincide with Indus floods.

Irrigation also created serious drainage problems. Waterlogging and salinity damaged both northern and southern Indus plain soils, and the Left Bank Outfall Drain, built with international support in the 1980s and 1990s, failed badly in southeastern Sindh. Its tidal drain let salt water flood farmland, lakes, and fisheries, and storms in 1999 and 2007 made the damage worse.

## People and Languages

Pakistan is a cultural and ethnic melting pot. Punjabis make up roughly half the population; Pashtuns, Sindhis, muhajirs, Balochs, Seraikis, Brahuis, and many tribal groups add regional diversity. The country is linguistically heterogeneous. Urdu and English are important nationally, while Punjabi, Pashto, Sindhi, Balochi, Seraiki, and other languages have strong regional bases.

The population distribution reflects river plains, mountains, and cities. Dense settlement occurs in the irrigated Punjab and Sindh plains, while northern mountains and arid Balochistan are more sparsely populated. Refugee settlement after partition reshaped Karachi, Sindh, and other urban areas, making migration central to modern Pakistani society.

Regional identities remain politically important because language, landholding, migration, and provincial power overlap imperfectly. Punjabis dominate many national institutions, Pashtun tribal societies link Pakistan with Afghanistan, Sindh contains both rural Sindhis and urban muhajirs, and Balochistan combines sparse settlement, tribal leadership, mineral wealth, and recurrent demands for autonomy.

## Religion

Almost all Pakistanis are Muslim, mostly Sunni, with significant Shiite communities and strong Sufi influence. Islamic revivalist politics are represented by groups such as Jamaat-e Islami, founded by Abu al-Ala Mawdudi. Deobandi, Barelwi, Tablighi Jamaat, Wahhabi, Sufi, and pir networks shape religious life. The Ahmadiyah community was constitutionally declared non-Muslim in 1974 and has faced persecution.

Religious authority is not centralized through an ordained priesthood. Mosque leaders may be called imams, formally trained scholars are known as mullahs or mawlana, and the ulama influence law and doctrine. Hereditary Sufi pirs can command devotion, gifts, and political influence through networks of disciples.

## Economy

Pakistan operates a mixed economy. Early private enterprise gave way to nationalization in the 1970s, Islamization under Mohammad Zia ul-Haq in the 1980s, and privatization from the 1990s. Agriculture, manufacturing, trade, and services all matter, but rapid population growth limited gains in per capita income. Remittances from Pakistanis abroad are a major revenue source.

At independence, the economy was heavily agricultural, but it became more diversified over time. Agriculture still contributes a major share of output and employment, while manufacturing and services expanded. Government policy repeatedly shifted between public control, private enterprise, Islamic financial rules, export promotion, import restrictions, and efforts to attract investment. Economic growth has often compared well with many developing countries, but population pressure slowed improvement in per capita output and kept development uneven across regions, classes, and generations.

## Resources, Manufacturing, and Trade

Pakistan has exploitable [[Minerals and Rocks (Mineral)|minerals]] including iron ore, copper, limestone, chromite, gypsum, rock salt, marble, and granite, along with modest petroleum and large natural gas fields. Manufacturing grew first through cotton textiles and agricultural processing, then import substitution, fertilizers, chemicals, light engineering, steel at Pipri, and industrial expansion in Punjab. Trade remains deficit-prone; exports include cotton goods, leather, carpets, rice, and petroleum products, while imports include machinery, chemicals, oil, food, and vehicles.

Energy vulnerability is a recurring economic constraint. Domestic oil production has not met demand, and crude imports expose Pakistan to world oil prices. Natural gas fields are more important domestically, while limestone supports cement. Karachi began as the main industrial centre, but Lahore and other Punjab cities grew rapidly, especially after Karachi's ethnic violence in the late 20th century.

Industrialization also reflected political separation from India. The interruption of early postpartition trade encouraged Pakistan to process its own agricultural raw materials, especially cotton, and later to build import-substitution industries in consumer goods, chemicals, fertilizers, light engineering, and steel. Yet capital equipment, oil, and many raw materials still had to be imported.

## Finance and Services

The State Bank of Pakistan, founded in 1948, controls banking, currency, reserves, and monetary policy. Pakistan has state, private, foreign, scheduled, and Islamic banks, plus stock exchanges and specialist credit institutions. Services, public administration, defense, utilities, construction, tourism, and remittances support the economy. Mohenjo-daro, Taxila, and northern mountain tourism attract visitors.

The service sector ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tertiary_sector)) also reflects Pakistan's colonial inheritance and overseas labour connections. Government employment remains attractive because it offers security, while workers abroad send home large remittances through official banks and informal hawala networks. Organized labour has been politically important despite representing only a minority of total workers, partly because industry was small at independence.

## Government, Justice, and Security

Pakistan's politics have often been more personalized than institutionalized. The military ruled during 1958-1969, 1969-1971, 1977-1988, and 1999-2008. The judiciary includes the Supreme Court, high courts, district courts, and Islamic institutions such as the Federal Shariat Court. Pakistan's military is large, professional, politically influential, and nuclear-armed, with army, air force, navy, paramilitary forces, missile systems, and domestic arms production.

The Supreme Court has original, appellate, and advisory jurisdiction, but judicial independence has been tested during military and presidential crises. In 2007, Pervez Musharraf replaced judges who challenged his legitimacy; lawyer and opposition pressure helped secure their reinstatement in 2009.

Constitutional politics often turned on whether authority belonged to parliament, the president, the courts, provincial leaders, or the army. Colonial administrative habits encouraged strong executives, while rivalry with India strengthened the military's claim to national guardianship. Coups and dismissals therefore did not simply interrupt politics; they became recurring methods for deciding political deadlocks.

## Housing, Education, and Culture

![[attachments/pakistan_004.webp|420]]

*Badshahi Mosque in Lahore, Pakistan*

Housing shortages are severe, with many urban residents in katchi abadis lacking safe water and sanitation. Education expanded after independence, but literacy remains low and gender gaps persist. Public, private, English-medium, Urdu-medium, and madrassa systems create social divisions. Pakistani culture blends South Asian influences, Islamic traditions, regional languages, Hindustani music, ghazal, qawwali, Sufi music, poetry, theatre, cinema, and modern popular music.

The housing crisis includes pukka houses built with durable materials, katchi houses made from mud, bamboo, reeds, or thatch, and semi-pukka houses between the two. Roughly half of urban residents live in informal settlements, and the 2005 northern earthquake destroyed or damaged more than half a million houses, forcing reconstruction through the Earthquake Reconstruction and Rehabilitation Authority.

Pakistan's education system expanded through primary schools, secondary schools, teacher training, universities, technical colleges, and open education, but it remains divided by class and language. The nationalization of private institutions in the 1970s weakened parts of the system, while later privatization and Islamization produced elite English-medium schools, state Urdu-medium schools, and madrassas with sharply different social outcomes.

Important cultural forms include mushairah poetry gatherings, Punjabi folk dance such as bhangra, devotional qawwali, regional heroic poets, Urdu theatre, cinema, and popular music. Figures such as Nusrat Fateh Ali Khan, Mehdi Hassan, Ghulam Ali, Sir Muhammad Iqbal, and modern bands such as Junoon helped connect classical, devotional, poetic, and popular traditions.

## Partition and Founding

The idea of a Muslim state was articulated by Sir Muhammad Iqbal in 1930, and Choudhary Rahmat Ali coined Pakistan in 1933. The All India Muslim League, founded in 1906, grew under Mohammed Ali Jinnah, who advanced the two-nation theory. Partition in 1947 caused massive migration, violence, and long-lasting rivalry with India.

## Early Political Instability

After Jinnah's death, Liaquat Ali Khan, Khwaja Nazimuddin, and other leaders struggled with constitution-making, provincial representation, and East Bengal's demands. The Muslim League collapsed in East Bengal, governor-general powers expanded, and the bureaucracy and army gained influence. In 1958, Iskander Mirza abrogated the constitution and imposed martial law, only to be displaced by Mohammad Ayub Khan.

## Military Rule, Bangladesh, and Bhutto

Ayub Khan introduced Basic Democracies and ruled until crisis gave way to Yahya Khan. The failure to integrate East Pakistan, the rise of the Awami League, and the 1971 war led to Bangladesh's independence. Zulfikar Ali Bhutto then promised a new Pakistan, adopted the 1973 constitution, restored parliamentary government, and became prime minister, but his rule became increasingly authoritarian.

The loss of East Pakistan exposed the weakness of national integration. Punjab's demographic and military weight, Sindhi regionalism, Baloch grievances, Pashtun politics, and muhajir urban identity all complicated state-building. Constitutions often failed to restrain executive power, and the army repeatedly justified intervention by citing external threats, civilian weakness, and the need for order.

East Pakistan's separation also showed the limits of religion as the sole basis of national unity. Bengali language, distance from West Pakistan, disputes over representation, economic grievances, and military coercion outweighed a shared Muslim identity. After 1971, Pakistan had to redefine itself without its eastern majority and with deeper awareness of provincial imbalance.

## Zia, Sharif, Musharraf, and Zardari

Mohammad Zia ul-Haq ruled after the 1977 coup and promoted Islamization. Later civilian politics involved Benazir Bhutto, Nawaz Sharif, presidential dismissals, coalition struggles, Afghanistan policy, and Taliban influence. Pervez Musharraf seized power in 1999, held the constitution in abeyance, and resigned in 2008 under impeachment pressure. Asif Ali Zardari became president amid coalition tensions, judicial disputes, U.S. drone strikes, and the devastating 2010 floods.

Pakistan's foreign relations remained tied to India, Afghanistan, China, the United States, and the Muslim world. Kashmir dominated relations with India, while Afghanistan shaped security policy, refugee flows, militant networks, and Taliban politics. The resumption of limited Kashmir trade in 2008 showed occasional openings, but nuclear weapons and cross-border violence kept tensions high.

External alliances shifted with Cold War strategy, Afghan wars, aid needs, and regional rivalries. The United States mattered for security and finance, China became a durable partner against India, Saudi and Gulf connections influenced labour migration and religious networks, and Afghanistan repeatedly drew Pakistan into frontier politics that blurred domestic and foreign security.

The 2010 floods underlined how physical and political vulnerabilities converged. Heavy monsoon rains swelled the Indus far beyond normal breadth, affecting roughly one-fourth of the country, damaging crops and infrastructure, and creating food, water, health, transport, and relief problems in a society deeply dependent on the river system.

## Key Events & Developments

- 1930: Sir Muhammad Iqbal proposed a Muslim-majority state in northwest India.
- 1933: Choudhary Rahmat Ali coined the name Pakistan.
- 1947: Pakistan became independent amid partition and mass migration.
- 1971: East Pakistan became Bangladesh.
- 1973: A new constitution restored parliamentary government.
- 1998: Pakistan demonstrated nuclear-weapons capability.
- 2010: Indus floods caused a major national disaster.
