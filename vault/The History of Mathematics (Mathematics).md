# The History of Mathematics (Mathematics)

> [!abstract] Reference navigation

Mathematics ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematics)) is the science of structure, order, and relation. It developed from counting, measuring, describing shapes, logical reasoning, and quantitative calculation into increasingly abstract systems of axioms, theorems, functions, spaces, and structures.

Mathematics grew from practical needs in commerce, agriculture, astronomy, surveying, architecture, mechanics, navigation, and administration. It advanced most in societies able to preserve records, support specialists, and build on earlier achievements.

Its history also alternates between calculation and proof. Mesopotamian and Egyptian traditions supplied algorithms for practical problems, while Greek mathematics made deductive demonstration central. Later periods repeatedly joined these two impulses: algebra, calculus, probability, and numerical methods served astronomy and mechanics, but also demanded clearer definitions and proof.

Since the 17th century, mathematics has been indispensable to physics and technology, and it later became central to life sciences, statistics, economics, computing, and many quantitative fields.

Its modern growth has been uneven but cumulative. Many developments since the 15th century were concentrated in Europe and North America, yet they depended on earlier Mesopotamian, Egyptian, Greek, Indian, and Islamic achievements. Mathematical history therefore includes transmission, translation, notation, institutions, and changing ideas about proof, not only discoveries by isolated individuals.

## Mesopotamian Mathematics

Mesopotamian mathematics ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Babylonian_mathematics)) is known from clay tablets that survive from Sumerian, Akkadian, Babylonian, Assyrian, Persian, and Greek periods. By the Old Babylonian dynasty of Hammurabi, mathematical competence was already high.

Old Babylonian scribes used a sexagesimal place-value system, which was more flexible than earlier additive systems. They developed arithmetic procedures, tables, reciprocals, linear and quadratic problem solving, and number theory.

This scribal culture went beyond routine accounting. Clay tablets preserve school exercises, tables, and worked examples, showing habits of training as well as answers. Problems often look practical, but their difficulty suggests that scribes valued mathematics as a disciplined subject in its own right.

Babylonian tablets include what are now called Pythagorean triples. One famous table orders triples by ratios involving diagonal, base, and height, implying knowledge of systematic procedures for generating such numbers.

Babylonian mathematical astronomy used arithmetic progressions to predict lunar eclipses, planetary cycles, conjunctions, oppositions, station points, and first or last visibility. Its sexagesimal methods became essential for astronomical computation.

## Egyptian Mathematics

![[attachments/the_history_of_mathematics_mathematics_001.webp|420]]

*Reconstruction of fragments from the Rhind Mathematical Papyrus*

Egyptian mathematics used a decimal but nonpositional notation with separate symbols for powers of ten. Hieratic script made papyrus calculations more convenient than hieroglyphic notation.

Egyptian multiplication used successive doubling. To multiply, scribes decomposed one factor into sums of powers or convenient multiples, then added corresponding doubled values.

Fractions were usually expressed as unit fractions, such as 1/2 plus 1/14 instead of 4/7. This shaped Egyptian methods for division and practical calculation.

Egyptian geometry solved problems of area, volume, and measurement. The Rhind papyrus gives a circle-area rule by discarding one-ninth of the diameter and squaring the result, producing a good approximation. The Golenishchev papyrus gives the correct volume formula for a truncated square pyramid.

Egyptian calculation remained strongly algorithmic. Problems were usually presented as examples to imitate rather than as general theorems, but the methods handled wages, grain, beer, bread, slopes, storage, and land measurement. This practical style shows a different mathematical culture from Greek proof, yet it preserved reliable procedures for administration and building.

Egyptian geometry also hints at algebraic reasoning without symbolic notation. Some papyrus problems seek an unknown length from area and proportion, solving by numerical inversion and square root extraction. The procedure was not written as an equation, but the underlying reasoning could handle relationships equivalent to simple algebraic formulas.

## Greek Mathematics and Euclid

![[attachments/the_history_of_mathematics_mathematics_002.webp|420]]

*Manuscript page from Euclid's Elements with geometric diagrams*

Greek mathematics turned practical rules into deductive proof. Euclid's Elements preserved much earlier work and organized geometry, number theory, proportion, and construction into an axiomatic structure.

Books I-IV of the Elements treat triangles, parallels, parallelograms, circles, and polygon constructions. Much drew on Hippocrates and Pythagorean traditions, while debates about parallels led Euclid to treat the parallel statement as a postulate.

Book V uses Eudoxus's general theory of proportion, which did not require commensurable magnitudes. This allowed rigorous treatment of similar figures and irrational ratios.

Book X treated irrational magnitudes with great sophistication, classifying incommensurable lines that could not be expressed by simple numerical ratios. This work gave Greek geometry a way to manage quantities that earlier arithmetic could not easily absorb.

Greek mathematicians distinguished theorems, which prove properties, from problems, which construct objects. Euclidean construction used compass and straightedge, but many famous problems required other curves or methods.

The later books of the Elements extended the programme beyond elementary plane figures. They treated similarity, number theory, solid geometry, and regular polyhedra, turning inherited results into a connected body of deductive knowledge. Euclid's decision to make parallels a postulate also made a future problem visible: if the parallel statement could not be proved from simpler assumptions, then geometry's foundations demanded scrutiny.

## Classical Problems and Analysis

The three classical problems were doubling the cube, trisecting the angle, and squaring the circle. They inspired new methods even though they cannot be solved with Euclidean tools alone.

Hippocrates reduced cube duplication to finding two mean proportionals. Menaechmus solved this using conic sections, helping initiate work on parabolas, ellipses, and hyperbolas.

Greek geometers developed [[Analysis (in Mathematics)|analysis]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematical_analysis)): assume a problem solved, derive an equivalent solvable problem, then reverse the steps in synthesis. This became a powerful method for discovering constructions and proofs.

Apollonius of Perga greatly expanded conic theory in his Conics. He studied parabolas, ellipses, and hyperbolas as plane sections of cones and described their properties in forms close to modern equations.

## Archimedes and Applied Geometry

[[Archimedes]] used Eudoxus's method of exhaustion to measure curved areas and volumes. He bounded pi between values using inscribed and circumscribed polygons and proved the area of a circle equals a triangle with radius as height and circumference as base.

Archimedes proved that the sphere has volume two-thirds that of its circumscribing cylinder and surface area two-thirds that of the same cylinder including its bases. He wanted this result shown on his tomb.

His mechanical method used balance ideas and cross sections to discover volumes and centres of gravity. Though later proofs were geometric, the method showed deep connections between geometry and mechanics.

Greek applied mathematics also included [[Principles of Optics (Optics)|optics]], astronomy, mechanics, catoptrics, and burning mirrors. Euclid, Ptolemy, Diocles, and Anthemius all used geometric methods for physical phenomena.

## Islamic Mathematics

Islamic mathematics grew from Greek, Syriac, Persian, and Indian learning. The rapid expansion of Islam connected regions from Spain to Central Asia, while patrons supported translation and research.

The House of Wisdom in Baghdad under al-Mamun translated Euclid, Archimedes, Apollonius, Diophantus, Menelaus, Ptolemy, and other works. Christian scholars often translated from Greek and Syriac under Muslim patronage.

Indian mathematics contributed decimal numerals, sine tables, and astronomical methods. By the 7th century, Hindu numerals had reached northern Mesopotamia and were praised by Severus Sebokht.

Thabit ibn Qurrah, the Banu Musa, Ibn al-Haytham, al-Karaji, al-Biruni, and others developed geometry, mechanics, astronomy, number theory, optics, and algebra.

Omar Khayyam gave the first complete treatment of cubic equations using conic sections and studied Euclid's parallel postulate through a special quadrilateral. He also argued for ratios as ideal numbers, pointing toward broader number systems.

Later Islamic mathematicians such as al-Samawal and Sharaf al-Din al-Tusi advanced algebraic algorithms, decimal fractions, root extraction, equation approximation, and astrolabe design.

## Medieval Europe and Transmission

Before the 11th century, western Europe knew little Greek mathematics. Boethius's Latin works transmitted Pythagorean number theory, and monastic education taught the quadrivium: arithmetic, geometry, astronomy, and music.

Medieval arithmetic served the computus, the calculation of Easter. Geometry remained practical and approximate, following Roman surveying traditions.

From the 11th and 12th centuries, translations from Arabic ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arabic)) brought Greek and Islamic mathematics into Latin Europe. Universities later helped circulate arithmetic, geometry, astronomy, and algebra.

Transmission was not simple copying. Translators, merchants, teachers, astronomers, and instrument makers adapted Hindu-Arabic numerals, Euclidean geometry, algebra, trigonometry, and astronomical tables to Latin schools and practical needs. This made medieval Europe a receiver and reshaper of Greek, Indian, and Islamic traditions.

At Merton College and Paris, scholars studied the latitude of forms, representing qualities such as speed by geometric figures. The Merton rule for uniformly accelerated motion anticipated later kinematics and influenced Galileo indirectly.

Regiomontanus wrote an independent work on plane and spherical trigonometry around 1464 and published sine and tangent tables used for more than two centuries.

## Renaissance Algebra and Perspective

Italian artists and merchants shaped Renaissance mathematics. Brunelleschi, Alberti, and [[Leonardo da Vinci]] used linear perspective before mathematicians treated it formally.

Italian abacus masters and algebraists attacked cubic equations. Scipione del Ferro found a general solution, Niccolo Tartaglia rediscovered it, and Gerolamo Cardano published it in Ars magna in 1545 along with Ferrari's solution of quartics.

Algebraic notation improved. Symbols for unknowns, square roots, plus and minus spread in southern Germany, while Francois Viete consistently distinguished known and unknown quantities and studied relations between coefficients and roots.

Complex numbers gradually became accepted through work connected with cubic equations, especially by Rafael Bombelli. The claim that degree-n equations have n roots was later stated by Albert Girard and proved by Gauss.

## The 17th-Century Revolution

The 17th century linked mathematics with the scientific revolution. Kepler, Galileo, Descartes, Fermat, Pascal, Huygens, Newton, and Leibniz developed symbolic algebra, analytic geometry, numerical methods, probability, mechanics, and calculus.

Communication networks mattered. Marin Mersenne in Paris and John Collins in London coordinated results through letters, while the Royal Society, French Academy, Berlin Academy, and journals made publication more regular.

Before these academies matured, many mathematicians worked alone or in small circles and delayed publication. Invisible colleges of correspondence spread challenge problems, partial results, and methods across borders. By the late 17th century, official memoirs and independent journals such as Acta Eruditorum helped make research more public, cumulative, and competitive.

Simon Stevin's La Disme introduced decimal fractions to Europe, extending Hindu-Arabic positional arithmetic to fractional quantities and aiding surveying, astronomy, mensuration, and accounts.

New numerical methods answered practical demands from navigation, astronomy, trigonometry, and surveying. Decimal notation, improved tables, symbolic algebra, and analytic techniques made calculation faster and more uniform. This helped shift advanced mathematics from isolated geometric demonstrations toward methods that could be repeatedly applied to whole families of problems.

Fermat and [[Descartes and Cartesianism (Descartes)|Descartes]] founded analytic geometry by linking equations with curves. Descartes introduced modern notation for variables and powers and used algebra to solve geometric problems.

Descartes also narrowed acceptable curves to those expressible by finite polynomial equations in two variables. His restriction excluded spirals and cycloids generated by motion, even as other mathematicians were finding tangents and areas for those curves. The debate shows how analytic geometry both expanded mathematics and imposed new standards on what kinds of curves could be treated rigorously.

The study of tangents, areas, quadratures, mechanical curves, and infinitesimals led to calculus. Roberval used motion ideas for tangents to kinematic curves such as the cycloid; Fermat developed tangent methods from maxima and minima.

## Newton and Leibniz

Isaac Newton developed fluxions and fluents in the 1660s, using infinite series and infinitesimals to treat differentiation and integration as inverse operations. In the Principia, he expressed much of the mathematics geometrically through limits and first and last ratios.

Newton's Principia unified Galileo's mechanics and Kepler's astronomy. Its inverse-square gravitation relied on geometric limit arguments, even though later mathematicians reworked mechanics in analytic calculus notation.

Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz developed differential and integral notation in the 1670s, publishing differential calculus in 1684 and integration in 1686. His symbols d and integral notation proved highly effective.

Leibniz attracted a research community, including Johann and Jakob Bernoulli, Varignon, and l'Hospital. His notation, teaching, and publications gave his version of calculus enormous influence.

## 18th-Century Analysis

The 18th century was dominated by academies and analysis. Euler, d'Alembert, and Lagrange worked in St. Petersburg, Paris, Berlin, and other centres.

Euler made the function the central concept of analysis, treating sine and cosine as functions rather than only geometric ratios. His approach expanded calculus, series, differential equations, mechanics, and number theory.

Lagrange advanced the calculus of variations and analytic mechanics. In Mecanique analytique he boasted that the work used no figures, only algebraic operations, making mechanics a branch of analysis.

Lagrange tried to found calculus on algebraic Taylor expansions rather than infinitesimals or limits. Later Cauchy and others replaced this with limit-based rigor.

## Geometry and the Parallel Postulate

Efforts to prove Euclid's parallel postulate continued for centuries. Islamic mathematicians, including Omar Khayyam, studied special quadrilaterals and reductio arguments.

Johann Heinrich Lambert showed that the parallel axiom is equivalent to the triangle angle sum being two right angles and noticed that rejecting it implies an absolute unit of length.

Adrien-Marie Legendre's geometry textbook tried to prove the postulate and spread new ideas. The 18th century failed to develop non-Euclidean geometry, but it prepared the way.

[[Gauss]], Bolyai, Lobachevsky, and later Riemann transformed geometry in the 19th century by showing that alternatives to Euclidean space could be coherent.

## 19th-Century Abstraction

The 19th century produced many abstract theories now central to mathematics. As mathematics separated professionally from physical science, standards of rigour rose and pure mathematics developed in directions not immediately tied to applications.

The French Revolution created the Ecole Polytechnique in 1794, where Gaspard Monge promoted descriptive geometry for engineering, fortifications, machines, and state needs.

This institutional shift mattered because mathematics was increasingly taught for technical administration as well as scholarship. Monge's descriptive geometry turned spatial projection into a practical language for military and civil engineering, while the school gathered high-level mathematicians into a state project. That setting encouraged both applied training and rapid theoretical development.

Projective geometry, elliptic functions, complex analysis, algebraic number theory, group theory, non-Euclidean geometry, topology, differential equations, and set theory all grew during this period.

Number theory developed from Fermat through Euler and Lagrange to [[Gauss]]. Gauss's Disquisitiones Arithmeticae transformed the field and his concern for rigour inspired later mathematicians.

Group theory abstracted operations with identity, inverses, and associativity. Commutative and noncommutative groups became central in algebra, geometry, and later physics.

## Gauss, Riemann, and Modern Concepts

[[Gauss]] was a polymath linking mathematics and science. He worked on number theory, least squares, potential theory, telegraphy, magnetism, surveying, cartography, elliptic functions, and intrinsic curvature.

His work on least squares and errors showed how mathematical method could discipline observation, especially in astronomy and geodesy. This connection between pure theory and measurement became one mark of 19th-century mathematical science.

Gauss showed that surface curvature can be defined intrinsically, without reference to surrounding Euclidean space. This helped make non-Euclidean and differential geometry acceptable.

Bernhard Riemann connected complex functions, harmonic functions, and potential theory; clarified Fourier series and integrability; and proposed a radical foundation for geometry based on manifolds and intrinsic metric structure.

Riemann's ideas shaped complex analysis, differential geometry, topology, mathematical physics, and later general relativity.

Nineteenth-century differential equations also changed. Cauchy insisted that mathematicians prove solutions exist rather than merely manipulate formal expressions, helping create modern standards for analysis.

The same demand for existence and definition affected functions, integrals, and probability. Mathematicians became less willing to rely on diagrams, physical analogy, or formal manipulation alone. The result was a new expectation that a method should specify its domain, justify limiting processes, and show that the objects it used actually exist.

That expectation helped make modern mathematics portable: results could move between geometry, physics, statistics, and logic because their assumptions were stated more exactly.

The theory of elliptic functions showed the power of complex variables. These functions generalized trigonometric ideas and became a model for studying functions with multiple periods and richer analytic structure.

## Measure, Probability, and Sets

Henri Lebesgue's integral generalized integration using measure, transforming analysis and probability. Andrey Kolmogorov later put probability on a rigorous measure-theoretic foundation.

Norbert Wiener showed that among continuous functions, differentiable functions have measure zero, giving mathematical form to Brownian motion and deepening links between analysis and physics.

Georg Cantor founded set theory while studying trigonometric series. He showed that algebraic and rational numbers are countable but real numbers are uncountable, proving that some infinities are larger than others.

Cantor's work transformed the concept of number. Integers could be defined through sets, and set theory became a foundation for much of modern mathematics.

The rise of rigour changed mathematical identity. Proof, definition, axiomatization, abstraction, formal foundations, and consistency became as important as computation, and pure theories sometimes found surprising later applications in physics and technology.

This new identity did not remove mathematics from the world. Least squares, probability, measure, differential equations, and geometry continued to serve astronomy, geodesy, physics, statistics, and later computing. What changed was the expectation that useful mathematics should also survive stricter logical and conceptual examination.

Foundational debates showed that modern mathematics was not only a collection of techniques. [[Set Theory|Set theory]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Set_theory)), formal logic, measure, and probability raised questions about infinity, existence, construction, and consistency. These questions reshaped analysis, geometry, algebra, and statistics, while still feeding back into physics, computing, and applied science.

## Key Events & Developments

- 1854: Riemann lectured on the foundations of geometry.
- Late 19th century: Cantor developed set theory and transfinite numbers.
