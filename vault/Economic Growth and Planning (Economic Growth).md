# Economic Growth and Planning (Economic Growth)

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> Economic growth is the sustained increase in a society's production of goods and services. It depends on resources, labor, capital, technology, institutions, distribution, and policy choices.

## Introduction

Economic growth ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economic_growth)) means the long-term increase of a nation's wealth. In everyday discussion, people may use the term for short-term business performance, but in economic theory it usually means a wider process that continues over many years. Growth is a process of transformation, not as a smooth movement upward. Both modern industrial economies and poorer economies grow in uneven ways, because different parts of the economy change at different speeds.

## The analysis of growth

To explain why one country grows faster than another, economists use the idea of a production function ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Production_function)). This is a mathematical way to relate output, such as GNP, to the inputs used to produce it. Inputs can include labour measured in man-hours, capital stock measured in money, and other factors. The aim is to describe how changes in labour, capital, and technology affect the growth of output.

## Quality improvements in the inputs

Economists have tried to measure inputs more accurately by allowing for changes in quality. Labour is not only a number of hours worked. A worker with more education or better health may produce more in each hour than a worker with less training. Therefore man-hours may need to be converted into efficiency units. If the average worker's education rises over time, the true labour input rises more than the simple count of hours suggests.

## Entrepreneurship

The element is entrepreneurship. When resources move from one sector to another, the movement usually happens under the leadership of an entrepreneurial class. Entrepreneurs decide whether a new product or process will be introduced. Their choices can make two countries with similar levels of investment achieve very different rates of technical progress.

## The play of influences

No single growth input works independently. Capital, labour, knowledge, and technical progress often influence one another. For example, better capital goods can raise productivity even if they require the same amount of steel and labour to produce as older machines. In that case, capital growth and technical progress interact.

## The role of government

Differences in growth rates are often linked to government and entrepreneurship, and the two are connected. In early stages of sustained growth, governments often provide incentives that help entrepreneurship begin. Some governments build transportation, power, and other utilities directly. Others give subsidies or other financial incentives, such as the land grants given to railroad developers in the [[United States of America (United States)|United States]] during the second half of the 19th century.

## The social cost of growth

Some writers challenge the value of economic growth in an advanced industrial society. They point to traffic congestion, air and water pollution, damage to landscapes, and a reduced ability to enjoy the real amenities of life. For them, growth is not only a rise in goods and income; it can also create social and environmental losses.

## Theories of growth

A distinction separates growth theory for already developed countries from theories about countries trapped in poverty. Its main discussion concerns developed capitalist economies. The central issue is how such economies can keep demand and supply growing together, so that they do not fall into long unemployment or severe inflation.

## Role of the entrepreneur

Modern growth theory, begins with Joseph A. Schumpeter. Unlike many Keynesian and pre-Keynesian writers, he gave the central role to the entrepreneur or businessman. The quality of entrepreneurial performance determined whether capital grew quickly or slowly and whether growth brought true innovation, new products, and new production techniques.

## The role of investment

In Keynes's General Theory, investment has a key role because it strongly affects total spending. This may seem surprising because investment is often only one-fifth to one-sixth of total spending. the point through the multiplier, a concept developed in the 1930s.

## Demand and supply

Much growth theory tries to explain how demand and supply grow together. A theory saying capitalism is inherently unstable would not fit much historical experience, because developed economies often did grow for long periods without permanent collapse. Models can be grouped by whether they stress demand adjusting to supply or supply adjusting to demand.

## Economic stagnation

The slowdown in GNP growth and per capita income growth across the capitalist world beginning in the early 1970s is presented as a case where demand and supply did not grow together. Economists tried to explain this long stagnation by focusing on high unemployment and low use of capital stock. These conditions weakened investment and therefore slowed productivity growth.

## Foreign trade

Foreign trade ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_trade)) is important because most economies need imports and must export enough to pay for them. Postwar comparisons made this clear. [[Japan]] and [[Germany]] grew very rapidly after World War II, while the British economy was more sluggish. Economists connected part of this difference to Britain's repeated balance of payments crises and Germany's lack of similar crises.

Mathematical growth theories come last. These models use equations to describe relations among output, capital, investment, consumption, and other variables. The equations connect variables across time. For example, output last year may affect consumption this year, and consumption this year may help determine output and consumption next year.

## Key Events & Developments

- In the long process of economic growth, economies move away from primary production toward manufacturing and services, changing labour patterns, cities, and investment needs.
- From 1870 to 1913, many developed countries grew more slowly than they later did in the postwar expansion.
- The period from 1913 to 1950 was distorted by two major wars and a severe depression, making growth comparisons difficult.
- After [[World Wars (World War II)|World War II]], West Germany, [[Japan]], and [[Italy]] grew rapidly, partly because late modernization and rebuilding allowed them to use advanced technology.
- In the 1930s, John Maynard Keynes changed growth discussion by showing that saving does not automatically become investment.
- In the late 1930s, Alvin H. Hansen argued that the United States faced stagnation because investment opportunities were weakening.
- Beginning in the early 1970s, capitalist economies faced slower growth, higher unemployment, and inflation problems that challenged earlier growth models.

## Key People

- Colin Clark: described development as a movement from primary production to secondary production and then to tertiary production.
- W.W. Rostow ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/W.W._Rostow)): presented growth as stages from traditional society through transition, take-off, and maturity.
- Simon Kuznets ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simon_Kuznets)): studied population, GNP per person, and long-run growth comparisons, especially for the United States and Europe.
- Joseph A. Schumpeter ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph_A._Schumpeter)): made the entrepreneur central to growth, innovation, technical progress, and capitalist change.
- John Maynard Keynes ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Maynard_Keynes)): showed how saving, investment, demand, unemployment, and inflation are connected in developed economies.
- Alvin H. Hansen ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alvin_H._Hansen)): argued in the late 1930s that mature capitalism could face stagnation because investment demand might become too weak.
- R.F. Harrod and E.D. Domar: built a growth analysis in which investment affects both demand and supply through the capital stock.
- J.R. Hicks: developed a supply-determined model where long-run growth is limited by the growth of maximum output.
- J.S. Duesenberry: developed a demand-determined model where productivity and supply respond to stronger demand.
- N. Kaldor ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/N._Kaldor)): described a model where income distribution shifts help maintain full employment.
- [[Adam Smith]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adam_Smith)): is represented through the neoclassical idea of the invisible hand and flexible market adjustment.
- Jan Tinbergen ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jan_Tinbergen)): developed planning models in which government uses policy instruments to reach targets such as full employment and growth.

## Interesting Facts

- Economic growth ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economic_growth)) is not simply more output; it is a transformation of sectors, technology, cities, investment, and demand.
- The shift out of agriculture ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agriculture)) is one of the most visible signs of growth because it moves labour from a low-productivity sector into higher-productivity activities.
- Late starters such as [[Japan]] and [[Italy]] may grow quickly because they can borrow technology that earlier developers had to invent or discover for themselves.
- War damage after [[World Wars (World War II)|World War II]] could act like a late start because rebuilding allowed countries to install newer and more productive capital goods.
- A simple factor-share calculation made capital look surprisingly unimportant after World War II, but this depends on treating inputs as independent.
- The multiplier example shows how a $10 rise in investment can become a $100 rise in income and output when people keep respending most extra income.
- Treats full employment as more than a social goal; some theories say sustained low unemployment can encourage enterprise investment and faster productivity growth.
- A model that ignores foreign trade may be unrealistic for countries whose growth depends heavily on imports, exports, and balance of payments limits.
