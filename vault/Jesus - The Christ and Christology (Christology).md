# Jesus - The Christ and Christology (Christology)

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> Christology studies Christian interpretations of Jesus Christ's person and work, including titles such as Messiah, Son of God, and Lord. Its historical development through Nicaea, Chalcedon, medieval theology, Reformation debates, and modern criticism shaped Christian doctrine, worship, and art.

## Introduction

![[attachments/christology_001.webp|420]]

*Christ Pantocrator mosaic in Hagia Sophia*

Christology ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christology)) is Christian reflection and doctrine about Jesus of Nazareth. It studies Jesus' nature and work, including the Incarnation, Resurrection, human and divine natures, and the relation between those natures. It depends on the New Testament but is not identical with Gospel narrative, because it systematizes what the Gospels, Epistles, creeds, theologians, and liturgy were understood to mean.

## Messiah, Son of Man, Son of God, and Lord

Early Christology began from titles given to Jesus by his followers. The New Testament presents Jesus as the revelation of God and uses names such as Messiah, Son of Man, Son of God, Lord, judge, high priest, Lamb of God, and Servant of God. These titles came from Hebrew Scripture, Jewish expectation, Greek-speaking Christian worship, and late antique language of divine rule.

Logos theology ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logos_%28Christianity%29)) and preexistence gave Christians language for saying that Jesus came from God before earthly life. Philo used logos to describe God's will, reason, and power in action; Christians applied this to Jesus, especially through the opening of John's Gospel. Paul also described Jesus as preexistent, descending in obedience and returning to God. The startling claim was not only that the logos existed, but that the logos became flesh.

The titles ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Title)) created theological questions. Son of Man could stress Jesus' humanity and judgment role; Son of God suggested a special and finally unique relation to the Father; Lord was especially useful among non-Jews because it implied adoration. By the 2nd century, Christian thinkers held together two themes: Jesus as preexistent divine figure who became human, and Jesus as one chosen or adopted by God.

## From Nicaea to Chalcedon

Early controversies tried to explain how Jesus could be both divine and human. Trinitarian theology asked how Son, Father, and Spirit were related within God; Christology asked how Jesus' humanity and divinity were related in one person. Ignatius of Antioch rejected adoptionism and described Jesus in paradoxes: spirit and flesh, created and uncreated, suffering and nonsuffering.

Competing views included docetism, Gnostic dualism, adoptionism, modalism, and logos theology. Irenaeus argued that Jesus had to be human as well as divine to redeem humanity. Tertullian spoke of two substances mixed but not fused. Sabellius treated Father, Son, and Spirit as modes of one God. Origen called the Son eternally begotten of the Father and used the important term homoousios, meaning of the same essence.

The Arian controversy in the early 4th century was the most consequential early dispute. Arius held that the Son came into being by the Father's will and therefore had a beginning, even if before all ages. Athanasius answered that God had to become human so humans could be restored to divine life, so the Son must share the Father's substance. Constantine convened Nicaea in 325, and the Nicene Creed affirmed the Athanasian position.

Nicaea did not end debate. The Council of Ariminum in 359 nearly reversed it, Constantius supported Arianism, and new questions arose over suffering, Mary, and the two natures. Apollinaris made Jesus' humanity yield to divinity; Nestorius stressed two natures so strongly that union seemed only moral or cooperative. The Council of Constantinople in 381 reaffirmed Nicaea, but Arianism remained powerful among several Germanic peoples.

Chalcedon in 451 adopted Leo I's position after the disputed council of 449. It declared Jesus perfect in Godhead and humanity, consubstantial with the Father in divinity and with humanity in human nature, like humans except without sin. The council's formula joined two natures in one person without confusion, change, division, or separation.

## The Chalcedonian Formula

The Chalcedonian formula became a milestone but never won universal acceptance. Its key terms, homoousios and homoiousios, differed by one Greek vowel yet marked a huge doctrinal divide. These disputes involved ordinary believers as well as bishops and theologians, with public demonstrations, political pressure, and attacks on opponents such as Eustathius of Antioch.

## Eastern Orthodox Christology

Eastern Orthodox Christology has been shaped since the 5th century by Chalcedon. Eastern theology emphasized that the union of divine and human in Jesus glorifies humanity and prepares it for deification, or participation in divine life. This emphasis gave special importance to Jesus' divine nature and to the interpretation of early ecumenical councils.

Icons of Christ became a major difference between Eastern and Western development. Icon veneration was attacked during the Iconoclastic Controversy of the 8th and 9th centuries, affirmed by the second Council of Nicaea in 787, and restored permanently in 843 under Theodora. Orthodoxy treated Christ's icon as inseparable from the person represented.

## The Middle Ages

Medieval Christology in the West mostly amplified Nicaea and Chalcedon. Adoptionism revived in Spain near the end of the 8th century but was condemned at Frankfurt in 794 and Rome in 798. Before the 11th century, Jesus' divinity often received more stress than his humanity, and he was imagined as a stern judge manifesting divine righteousness.

Medieval devotion increasingly turned toward Jesus' humanity. Bernard of Clairvaux, Francis of Assisi, Meister Eckhart, and Thomas a Kempis sought union with Jesus through imitation, suffering, and inward devotion. Women mystics such as Catherine of Siena and Julian of Norwich developed vivid images of Christ's body and maternal care.

Atonement theory was the main medieval doctrinal contribution. Earlier views imagined Christ's death as a ransom paid to free humanity from the Devil. Anselm of Canterbury, in Cur Deus homo, argued that divine justice required satisfaction and that only one who was both God and human could offer it. Peter Abelard instead stressed Jesus as teacher, model, and revealer of God's love, awakening love in sinners.

## The Reformation

Reformation Christology largely restated older positions while changing emphasis. Reformers described Jesus' threefold office as prophet, priest, and king, and debated his presence in the Eucharist. Martin Luther argued that the unity of Jesus' natures meant statements about Jesus applied to both natures, so God suffered and Jesus' humanity was omnipresent. John Calvin held that Jesus' human nature died and is now at the Father's right hand, while the Spirit gives spiritual presence in communion.

Luther's theology strongly emphasized the humble human Jesus. The stable birth and the cry of abandonment from the cross showed that God had entered the condition of the poor, condemned, and forsaken. Anabaptists accepted classical dogma but stressed following Jesus' teaching and suffering. Anti-Trinitarians such as Michael Servetus and Faustus Socinus revived Arian and adoptionist themes, rejecting the Trinity and the Incarnation as orthodox teaching understood them.

## Enlightenment Christology

Enlightenment Christology arose when critics applied ordinary historical scrutiny to Scripture. From the mid-17th century, Deists argued that Christianity should be understood as rational natural religion without superstition. They claimed clergy and theologians had obscured the real Jesus and that modern scholarship should recover the historical Jesus beneath miracle claims and dogma.

John Toland, Anthony Collins, Thomas Morgan, Thomas Woolston, and Thomas Chubb challenged prophecy, miracle, and Gospel reliability. Matthew Tindal argued in 1730 that Jesus taught a universal religion of nature. Hermann Samuel Reimarus's fragments, published in the 1770s, rejected supernatural elements, while Carl Friedrich Bahrdt and Thomas Jefferson tried to distill a historical core from Christian interpretation.

## Post-Enlightenment Christology

Post-Enlightenment Christology had to answer the critique of miracles, metaphysics, and revealed morality while official church teachings remained mostly unchanged. Friedrich Schleiermacher emphasized Jesus' unique consciousness of God and ethical self-consciousness, an approach continued by Albrecht Ritschl and Wilhelm Herrmann.

The historical Jesus remained a disputed project. David Friedrich Strauss treated Gospel narratives as myths expressing timeless truths rather than facts. Albert Schweitzer argued in 1906 that scholars had projected a moral-teacher image onto Jesus and ignored his apocalyptic message. Rudolf Bultmann tried to demythologize the New Testament, while E.P. Sanders took a minimalist approach to what history can confidently say.

Roman Catholic Christology also confronted Enlightenment criticism, often through issues first raised in Protestant scholarship. Catholic theology struggled with the tension between historical criticism and dogmatic authority, especially during the Modernist Controversy around the late 19th and early 20th centuries. Karl Rahner and Edward Schillebeeckx accepted the historicity of dogmatic formulations while seeking new interpretations that preserved their core meaning.

## Contemporary Christology

Contemporary Christology since the 20th century falls into overlapping patterns. Some statements restated pre-Enlightenment doctrine, such as the 1934 Synod of Barmen's rejection of Nazi claims about an Aryan Jesus. Other churches used new language for the two natures while broadly preserving Nicaea and Chalcedon, as in the Batak Protestant Christian Church's confession that Christ is true God and true man.

Developing-world Christologies often criticized Western categories and emphasized Jesus as liberator. African theologians such as Kofi Appiah-Kubi described Jesus as healer, spiritual power, and ally against disease and disorder. Asian theology connected Jesus' suffering with human suffering and resurrection with liberation. South American liberation theology, feminist theology, and womanist theology used Jesus to address oppression, women, and African American women's experience.

The modern result is plural rather than settled. Social concerns have always shaped Christian understanding of Jesus, but the centre of theological reflection is no longer only Europe and North America. The future development of non-Western and Western Christologies remained uncertain.

## Iconoclasm

Images of Jesus raised theological conflict because art had to show both divine and human natures. Some early theologians, including Irenaeus and Clement of Alexandria, rejected visual representation of the divine, while Pope Gregory I called images the Bible of the illiterate. Opponents feared idolatry, paganism, magical abuse, and doctrinal distortion toward either Nestorian separation or monophysite fusion.

Icon approval came gradually. The Quinisext Council in 692 supported human-form images of Christ, and Justinian II placed Jesus on imperial coins. Leo III and Constantine V later promoted iconoclasm, but the second Council of Nicaea in 787 legitimized images, and a council in 843 ended the second iconoclastic wave.

## Jesus in Western Art

Western art made Jesus one of its central subjects from the Middle Ages onward, partly because the church was Europe's largest artistic patron. Sculpture most often showed Jesus on the cross or as judge at the Last Judgment. Catholic churches made the crucifix central, while Protestant churches generally preferred the plain cross.

Painted themes focused on birth, death, Mary, the Nativity, the Magi, the Annunciation, the flight into Egypt, miracles, the Last Supper, and the women at the Holy Sepulchre. Raphael's Sistine Madonna emphasized mother and child, while Matthias Grunewald's Isenheim Altarpiece showed a ravaged crucified body meant to console the sick. [[Michelangelo]], [[Rembrandt]], El Greco, and Rubens developed other powerful images.

## Later Christian Art

Later art showed both decline and transformation. From the 17th century, secular patrons and fewer new churches reduced demand for Christian art, but the 19th and 20th centuries still produced many Jesus images. James Tissot tried to place Jesus in a first-century Jewish setting, while Fritz von Uhde placed him in contemporary scenes to express timelessness.

Modern Jesus images became popular and plural. Georges Rouault, Pablo Picasso, and Marc Chagall reworked crucifixion and suffering. Warner Sallman's Head of Christ from 1940 became one of the most reproduced American images, presenting Jesus as gentle and comforting. African and Asian churches produced indigenous images, while Janet McKenzie's Jesus of the People showed a dark-skinned, feminine Jesus in the Jesus 2000 competition.

## Film

Films about Jesus are more numerous than films about any other historical figure, showing his continuing cultural importance. Many early films were church productions or conservative Protestant alternatives to Hollywood entertainment. Film portrayals face three problems: the Gospels are not full biographies, every portrayal takes a position on Jesus' significance, and miracles are difficult to depict without offending some viewers.

Jesus films often add speculative material to fill Gospel gaps, as in King of Kings, The Greatest Story Ever Told, and Jesus of Nazareth. Pier Paolo Pasolini's The Gospel According to St. Matthew followed Matthew more strictly. Mel Gibson's The Passion of the Christ focused on the last twelve hours but drew criticism for alleged anti-Semitism, violence, and conservative theology.

Other film genres set Jesus' story in the present, use redeemer figures, turn the story into musicals, or satirize Christian tradition. Denys Arcand's Jesus of Montreal retold the Passion through actors staging a play. Jesus Christ, Superstar and Godspell made the story musical, while Life of Brian represented satire. These variations show why a universally accepted screen image of Jesus is nearly impossible.

## Key Events & Developments

- 2nd century Early frameworks: Christian thinkers began organizing New Testament titles into doctrinal models.
- 325 Council of Nicaea: The Nicene Creed affirmed the Son as of one substance with the Father.
- 381 Council of Constantinople: Nicaea was reaffirmed and Arianism condemned again.
- 431 Council of Ephesus: Debate over unity and two natures continued.
- 451 Council of Chalcedon: Jesus was defined as one person in two natures.
- 787 Second Council of Nicaea: Icon veneration was affirmed.
- 843 Restoration of icons: Eastern icon veneration was permanently restored.
- 1730 Matthew Tindal: Christianity as Old as the Creation presented Jesus as teacher of natural religion.
- 1906 Albert Schweitzer: The Quest for the Historical Jesus challenged liberal portraits of Jesus.
- 1934 Synod of Barmen: Traditional Christology was used against Nazi racial reinterpretation.
