## Introduction

Gravitation ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gravity)), or gravity, is the universal force of attraction acting between all matter. that although it is by far the weakest of the known natural forces—playing no role in the internal properties of everyday matter—its long reach and universal action let it control the trajectories of bodies in the solar system and the structure and evolution of stars, galaxies, and the whole cosmos. On Earth it gives bodies weight proportional to their mass and accelerates freely falling objects at about 9.8 metres per second squared; at the surface of the Moon the corresponding acceleration is only about 1.6 metres per second squared.

## Development of Gravitational Theory

The history of gravitational thought runs from [[Aristotle and Aristotelianism|Aristotle]], who held that each heavenly body followed a natural motion unaffected by external forces, through [[Kepler|Kepler's]] three quantitative laws of planetary motion and [[Galileo|Galileo's]] discovery that all bodies fall with the same acceleration. The classical work of Isaac Newton in his Principia (1687) and the later theory of Albert Einstein dominate the field. Newton showed that the same force that makes objects fall on Earth also keeps the Moon in orbit—finding the Moon's inward acceleration to be 0.0027 metre per second squared, exactly (1/60)² of surface gravity, since the Moon lies about 60 Earth radii away. His law of universal gravitation became the foundation of modern mechanics and astronomy.

The discussion then turns to the limits of Newton’s theory. The most famous test was the orbit of Mercury, whose perihelion precesses about 43 arc seconds per century faster than Newtonian perturbations could explain. Einstein’s general relativity explained that gravity is not simply a force between masses, but a consequence of the curvature of space and time, and it predicted Mercury’s precession precisely. This theory predicted small deviations from Newtonian physics that transformed the conceptual understanding of gravity, even though Newton’s law remains sufficient for all but the most precise applications.

## Measurement and Effects
Gravity can be measured and its effects studied in several ways. It discusses the role of Kepler’s laws, the measurement of the constant G, and the way gravity varies with mass, distance, and Earth’s shape. It notes that gravity is stronger at the poles than at the Equator because of Earth’s rotation and equatorial bulge, and it explains why measurements of gravity are important for geodesy and geophysics.

The gravitational field of Earth, the Moon, and the planets can be investigated through spacecraft tracking, geophysical surveys, and laboratory experiments. Potential theory, developed after Newton through the work of Poisson and Laplace, represents these fields as spherical harmonics whose coefficients (such as J2 for the equatorial bulge) measure how a body departs from a sphere. Absolute measurements of gravity progressed from Henry Kater’s reversible pendulum of 1817 to modern free-fall instruments using lasers and even atom interferometry, accurate to about one part in 10⁸. These methods have helped scientists map variations in gravity and better understand planetary structure.
The measurement of G, the gravitational constant, is difficult and important. Unlike the acceleration of falling bodies near Earth, G describes the strength of attraction between any two masses. Newton first estimated it by assuming Earth’s mean density to be about 5.5 times that of water, while the accepted value of 6.6726 × 10⁻¹¹ was first directly measured by Henry Cavendish. Measuring it made Newton’s law quantitatively complete and allowed scientists to calculate Earth’s mass and the masses of other celestial bodies, including binary stars and the planets.

## Black Holes and Modern Research

A major modern implication of gravitation is the possibility of black holes ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black_hole)). that if an object is sufficiently dense, light may be unable to escape its gravitational pull. This idea, first suggested in the 18th century, became a central topic of modern astrophysics. It also notes that gravitational radiation, predicted by Einstein’s theory, may be produced by changing massive systems such as binary stars or supernovae, although it has not yet been observed with complete certainty.
These ideas connect to general relativity, where gravity affects light, time, and the geometry of space itself. This explains why tests such as Mercury’s orbital precession and the bending of starlight near the Sun became important evidence for Einstein’s theory.

## Legacy

Gravity is one of the most important concepts in physics because it connects the motion of ordinary objects, the structure of the solar system, and the evolution of the universe. Newton provided the first great quantitative law of gravitation, while Einstein gave a deeper geometric description of the same phenomenon. Together, these theories form the foundation of modern understanding of the physical world.
