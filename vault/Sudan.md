# Sudan

## Introduction

![[attachments/sudan_001.webp|420]]

*Relief map of Sudan*

Sudan is a country in northeastern Africa. Its name comes from the Arabic bilad al-sudan, meaning "land of the blacks," used by medieval Arab geographers for settled African lands south of the Sahara. Before South Sudan seceded in 2011, Sudan was Africa's largest country.

Sudan has long been an arena where African, Mediterranean, Arab, Islamic, and local traditions interacted. Islam and Arabic became dominant in many northern regions, while older African languages and cultures remained especially important in the south. This cultural divide shaped modern conflict.

The country became independent in 1956. From 1955 to 1972, a civil war was fought largely in the south. The Addis Ababa Agreement ended it temporarily, but war resumed in 1983 over southern autonomy, uneven development, and attempts to Islamize law. The 2005 Comprehensive Peace Agreement ended the war and set up the 2011 referendum that led to South Sudan's independence.

The capital, Khartoum, lies near the centre of the country where the Blue Nile and White Nile meet. Khartoum, Omdurman, and Khartoum North form the largest urban area and the country's political and commercial centre.

## Land

Sudan is mainly a land of vast plains and plateaus drained by the Nile and its tributaries. The central plain is bounded by the Nile-Congo watershed and Darfur highlands in the west and by the Ethiopian Plateau and Red Sea Hills in the east.

## Relief

Northern Sudan includes Sahara rock desert, gravel desert, and sand dunes. The western Qawz has undulating dunes merging into desert. The central-southern clay plain includes isolated hills called inselbergs, especially the Nuba Mountains.

The Marrah Mountains rise from the Darfur Plateau and form part of the Nile-Congo watershed. In the northeast, the Red Sea Hills form an uplifted escarpment above a narrow coastal plain with dunes and coral reefs. Eastern uplands form foothills of the Ethiopian highland massif.

## Drainage and Soils

![[attachments/sudan_002.webp|420]]

*Confluence of the Blue Nile and White Nile at Khartoum*

The Nile system dominates Sudan's drainage. The White Nile enters from the southeast, and the Blue Nile rises in Ethiopia and joins it at Khartoum. The Blue Nile supplies much of the floodwater. North of Khartoum the river is simply called the Nile.

Much drainage does not actually reach the Nile. Seasonal streams from hills disappear into plains, and southwestern rivers only sometimes reach the Bahr al-Ghazal system. Soils include desert surfaces, immature semiarid soils, low-fertility Qawz sands, alluvial soils along rivers, and heavy cracking clays such as those of the Gezira south of Khartoum.

## Climate and Vegetation

Sudan is a hot country with strong rainfall gradients. Northern areas receive almost no rain, while southern areas receive 20 to 30 inches annually. Seasons depend on the movement between dry northerly air and moist southerly air. Rainy seasons become longer farther south.

Vegetation follows rainfall belts. The north has desert, followed southward by semidesert, low-rainfall savanna, high-rainfall savanna with floodplains, and mountain vegetation. Dust storms are common in the north and centre, especially before rains.

## People

Sudan's people are ethnically and linguistically diverse. The country is dominated politically and culturally by Muslims, many of whom speak Arabic and identify as Arabs, though many are ethnically mixed. Arab groups include sedentary farmers, urban people, and nomadic pastoralists.

Major Arab groupings include the Jalayin, mainly agriculturalists along the middle Nile, and the Juhaynah, historically nomadic cattle and camel herders such as the Shukriyah, Kababish, and Baqqarah. Non-Arab Muslim groups include Nubians, Beja, Fur, Zaghawa, and others.

Sudan has many languages. Arabic is the main language of government, commerce, and urban life, while English is also an official working language. Major language families include Afro-Asiatic, Nilo-Saharan, and Niger-Congo. Many Sudanese are multilingual, often using Arabic as a second language.

## Religion

Most Sudanese are Sunni Muslims. Religious brotherhoods, or tariqahs, are important. The Qadiriyyah arrived from the Middle East in the 16th century, the Khatmiyyah was founded by Muhammad Uthman al-Mirghani in the early 19th century, and the Mahdiyyah became politically powerful after its revolt against Turco-Egyptian rule.

Small numbers follow traditional African religions, especially in the Nuba Mountains, and Christianity is also present. Traditional religions often recognize a high creator divinity, an earthly and heavenly world, and spirits or ancestors who mediate between humans and God. Christianity flourished in ancient Nubia before declining after Muslim rule and Arab migrations.

## Settlement and Demographic Trends

The Khartoum urban region includes Omdurman and Khartoum North. Omdurman was the Mahdist capital and retains a more traditional atmosphere, while Khartoum North is industrial. Other important cities include Nyala, Port Sudan, and Ubayyid.

Sudan has a young [[Population|population]], with a large share under age 30. Water scarcity strongly shapes settlement: half the population lives on just over 15 percent of the land, while deserts in the north and northwest are nearly uninhabited. Civil war, famine, and conflict drove millions from the south and west toward the capital.

## Economy

Sudan is one of the world's poorer countries. Agriculture and animal husbandry support about one-third of the population and account for a large part of GDP. Oil production began in the late 1990s and quickly became the country's most important export before southern secession changed oil politics.

Major crops include cotton, peanuts, sesame, gum arabic, sorghum, sugarcane, millet, wheat, corn, and barley. Agriculture includes modern irrigated farming, mechanized rain-fed production, traditional rain-fed farming, and livestock raising.

Irrigated agriculture depends on the White and Blue Niles. The Gezira Scheme and Mangil extension between the rivers are major farming areas. Other irrigation comes from the Khashm Al-Qirbah and Al-Rusayris dams. Mechanized rain-fed farming expanded from the mid-1940s but often resembled large-scale shifting cultivation.

Manufacturing remains small. [[Food Processing (Food Preservation)|Food processing]], sugar refining, vegetable oil, soap, cotton ginning, textiles, oil refining, shoes, fertilizers, and cement are important. Many factories operate below capacity.

## Finance, Trade, and Transport

Sudan's banks were nationalized in 1970, with foreign banks later allowed again. The Bank of Sudan issues currency and acts as government banker. After the 1989 coup, Islamic banking became dominant, and the Bank of Sudan announced plans to Islamize the entire banking system in 1990.

Exports include petroleum, livestock, cotton, gum arabic, sorghum, and sesame. Imports include machinery, equipment, manufactured goods, motor vehicles, and wheat. China became Sudan's leading trading partner, with Japan, Saudi Arabia, and Egypt also important.

Transport is a major constraint. Sudan Railways, roads, Nile navigation, Port Sudan, and Khartoum airport carry the main burden. Roads expanded while railways were neglected. The Port Sudan-Khartoum highway is the most important all-weather road, and Port Sudan is the main Red Sea port.

## Government and Society

Since independence, Sudan has had many constitutions and regime changes, including coups in 1985 and 1989. Omar Hassan Ahmad al-Bashir took power through the Revolutionary Command Council in 1989, then became president in a civilian administration after 1993 and won elections in 1996, 2000, and 2010.

The 1998 constitution made Islamic law the basis for laws and regulations. It was replaced in 2005 by an interim constitution under the Comprehensive Peace Agreement. The president, elected for five-year terms, appoints vice presidents and ministers. Legislative power is bicameral, with a National Assembly and Council of States.

Justice includes the Supreme Court, appeals courts, lower courts, and a Constitutional Court. Muslims are subject to Islamic law, and some penalties apply broadly, though remitted penalties are allowed for non-Muslims. Multiparty politics, banned after 1989, returned in 1999, but the National Congress Party remained dominant.

## Security, Health, and Education

The Sudan Armed Forces expanded after 1969 because of the southern rebellion. The government later created militias and instituted drafts for war against the SPLA. UN peacekeepers were deployed because of conflict in Darfur and along the South Sudan border.

Health problems include malaria, measles, tuberculosis, meningitis, whooping cough, infectious hepatitis, undernutrition, and malnutrition, especially in Darfur. Government plans for primary health care were limited by lack of funds.

Modern education was reorganized in the 1970s and again in 1992. Primary schooling begins at age six and became compulsory in 1998. Arabic is the main language of instruction. Higher education includes the University of Khartoum, Omdurman Islamic University, Sudan University of Science and Technology, and others. Literacy remains relatively low, especially in rural areas.

## Cultural Life

Sudanese culture reflects Arab and African inheritances. Oral tradition is vital among nonliterate groups, while Arabic written literature is tied to Islam, scholarship, and religious biographies of holy men. Recitations on anniversaries remain important.

Al-Tayyib Salih is one of Sudan's best-known novelists, especially for Season of Migration to the North. Modern poetry expresses mixed African and Arab identity through writers such as Muhammad al-Mahdi al-Majdhub. Visual arts include painting, weaving, pottery, printmaking, calligraphy, and photography, with Ibrahim al-Salahi internationally recognized.

Music ranges from Quranic recitation to tribal songs and national styles heard in Khartoum. Sudan also has rich archaeological resources, including remains from ancient Nubia, the Nile valley, Islamic states, and later periods.

## Ancient Nubia

![[attachments/sudan_003.webp|420]]

*Pyramids at Meroe in Sudan*

The earliest inhabitants of what is now Sudan lived near Khartoum in Mesolithic times, about 30,000-20,000 BCE. They were hunters and gatherers who made pottery and later ground sandstone objects. By the Neolithic, about 10,000-3,000 BCE, they had domesticated animals.

Nubia was affected by Egypt from the end of the 4th millennium BCE, when Egypt's 1st dynasty conquered Upper Nubia. Egyptian campaigns sought slaves and building materials and disrupted Nubian culture, but African influence also entered Egyptian art, religion, and society.

After Egyptian weakness, immigrants from Libya and other regions settled along the Nile and developed a distinct Nubian civilization. Later Egyptian dynasties again pushed south, but Nubian societies continued to evolve through trade, conquest, Christianity, and later Islam.

## The Nilotic Sudan and the Funj

The Funj founded Sennar under Amarah Dunqas and became powerful by the early 17th century. They were neither Arab nor originally Muslim, and their homeland was probably near the upper Blue Nile. They struggled with Arab tribes for control of the Gezira region.

Under Badi II Abu Daqn, the Funj reached a golden age, extending authority across the White Nile into Kordofan and reducing Nile chieftaincies to tribute. But slave soldiers grew powerful and eventually competed with the Funj aristocracy, weakening the state.

Under Badi IV Abu Shulukh, royal power became more arbitrary and dependent on slave troops. Later military revolts and viceroys such as Abu Likaylik shifted power away from the old Funj elite.

## Turco-Egyptian Rule and the Mahdiyyah

Under Muhammad Ali's regime, taxation became nearly confiscatory, provoking rebellion and repression. Governor Ali Khurshid Agha later reduced taxes, consulted Sudanese leaders, improved trade routes, and developed Khartoum as administrative capital.

Muhammad Ahmad ibn Abd Allah proclaimed himself the Mahdi after observing misgovernment and religious decline. His followers, the ansar, saw him as a divinely guided renewer of Islam. After his death, Abd Allah ibn Muhammad became caliph and led the Mahdist state.

The Mahdiyyah drew support from holy men, tribes, and people angered by Turco-Egyptian rule. It overthrew the existing regime and created an independent Sudanese state from 1884 to 1898 before British-Egyptian conquest.

## The Anglo-Egyptian Condominium

In 1899, the Anglo-Egyptian Condominium gave Sudan separate status under joint British and Egyptian sovereignty, though Britain dominated in practice. The arrangement reflected British imperial strategy and Egyptian financial and military involvement in conquest.

British administrators pacified northern Sudan quickly and introduced modern administration, while southern administration focused more on keeping peace than modernization. Reginald Wingate, governor-general from 1899 to 1916, shaped early condominium rule.

The condominium left deep political divisions. Northern and southern regions developed under different administrative assumptions, and later nationalist politics struggled with questions of unity, religion, language, and regional inequality.

## Independence, Civil War, and the Addis Ababa Agreement

Sudan became independent in 1956. Conflict soon developed between northern leaders seeking Islamic-Arab unity and southerners and secular northerners who opposed this. Civil war began before independence and lasted until 1972.

The Addis Ababa Agreement of February 27, 1972, ended the 17-year conflict. It gave the south regional autonomy, a separate legislature and executive, and integration of Anya Nya soldiers into the army and police. It gave President Nimeiri prestige but did not solve deeper tensions.

Development projects in the 1970s aimed to make Sudan the breadbasket of the Arab world. Arab investment, IMF loans, sugar, wheat, cotton, irrigation, and infrastructure projects expanded, but flawed implementation, corruption, and debt produced economic problems.

## Bashir, Darfur, and South Sudan

In 1989, Omar al-Bashir and the Revolutionary Command Council seized power with support from the National Islamic Front. The regime jailed opponents, banned unions and parties, silenced the press, dismantled the judiciary, reintroduced Islamic law, and prosecuted war in the south.

The Comprehensive Peace Agreement of 2005 ended the long north-south civil war. It created power-sharing, wealth-sharing, security arrangements, and semiautonomous southern administration. A referendum in January 2011 produced an overwhelming vote for independence, and South Sudan became independent on July 9, 2011.

Conflict in Darfur began in 2003 when rebels protested government neglect of the western region and its non-Arab population. The government supported Arab militias known as Janjaweed, which attacked civilians and blocked aid. By 2007, hundreds of thousands were dead and more than two million displaced.

The International Criminal Court issued an arrest warrant for Bashir in 2009 for war crimes and crimes against humanity. Despite this, he won the 2010 presidential election. The Darfur conflict, southern secession, economic strain, and authoritarian rule continued to define Sudan's crisis.
