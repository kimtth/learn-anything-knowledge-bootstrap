## Introduction

Drug use means the use of drugs for psychotropic rather than medical purposes. Common psychotropic drugs include the opiates ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Opioid)) such as opium, morphine, and heroin; hallucinogens such as LSD, mescaline, and psilocybin; barbiturates ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barbiturate)); cocaine; amphetamines ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Substituted_amphetamine)); tranquilizers ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sedative)); and cannabis. Alcohol and tobacco are also sometimes treated as drugs. The term drug abuse is normally applied to excessive and addictive use. Because such drugs can have severe physiological, psychological, and social effects, many governments regulate them.

It is too narrow to think of drugs only as medicines or to confine them entirely to medical practice. Drawing on the sociologist Bernard Barber, one can see that drugs have played many roles across human history, including religious, therapeutic, addictive, political, aesthetic, ideological, and aphrodisiac roles. In other words, drugs are not marginal accidents in human culture; they are deeply woven into human attempts to alter perception, feeling, social belonging, and symbolic meaning.

## Characteristics of Drug Use and Abuse

Psychotropic drugs serve many functions. Some users seek aesthetic intensification, mystical contemplation, or ecstatic absorption. For Arthur Schopenhauer, contemplation was central to aesthetic experience, while Friedrich Nietzsche linked the ecstatic dissolution of individuality to the Dionysian force of music, rhythm, and dance. Drugs have often been associated with these kinds of heightened states. They have also been connected with love and with the search for aphrodisiac effects, whether or not the physiological basis for such beliefs is strong.
Drug use is also a social and developmental problem. Moderate use can appear benign in some settings, but disturbed and vulnerable young people may be especially drawn into drug worlds. Because adolescence is already a crisis period in development, regular interference by powerful chemicals can interrupt the process of working through the problems of growing up. Even when a drug is not immediately deadly, the experience may still be deeply unproductive and costly if it prevents the individual from becoming a fully participating adult. In this way, pharmacological analysis joins with moral and social concern.

## Drug Control and the Controlled Substances Act

A major legal turning point in the [[United States of America (United States)|United States]] came in 1970 with the Comprehensive Drug Abuse Prevention and Control Act, which introduced the Controlled Substances Act ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Controlled_Substances_Act)) (CSA). This law replaced earlier narcotics and dangerous-drug legislation. Under the CSA, drugs were grouped into five schedules according to their medical use, potential for abuse, and likelihood of producing dependence. Schedule I includes substances considered to have no legitimate medical use, such as LSD, heroin, and cannabis. Schedule II includes drugs such as cocaine, opium, and morphine, which have recognized medical uses but also high abuse potential. Schedules III, IV, and V contain drugs with decreasing abuse potential and accepted medical applications.

Alcohol and tobacco are not included in this scheduling system but are regulated by separate agencies. The Drug Enforcement Administration ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drug_Enforcement_Administration)) (DEA) was created in 1973, when President Richard Nixon combined older federal agencies into one centralized organization, and it remains central to law enforcement and drug-control efforts. This legal history is not simply a list of laws but part of a broader attempt by governments to manage substances whose cultural uses are old and persistent but whose harmful effects can be severe.

## Opium, Morphine, Heroin, and Related Synthetics

![[attachments/drug_use_001.webp|420]]

*Opium poppy, source plant for opium*

The opiates ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Opioid)) are unmatched in their ability to relieve pain. Opium is the dried milky exudate from the unripe seed pods of the opium poppy, Papaver somniferum, which grows naturally through much of [[Turkey and Ancient Anatolia (Turkey)|Turkey]]. Of its many alkaloids, the most important pharmacologically are morphine, papaverine, codeine, and thebaine. Around 1804, the German apothecary's assistant F.W.A. Sertürner isolated crystalline morphine as the active analgesic principle. Codeine is less potent and derived from morphine. Heroin, or diacetylmorphine, was developed from morphine by the Bayer Company in 1898 and is far more potent than morphine itself.

These drugs are highly effective but medically imperfect. Tolerance develops rapidly, especially with morphine and heroin, and they are strongly addictive. They also cause respiratory depression, nausea, and vomiting. The search for synthetic substitutes therefore became constant. Meperidine, alphaprodine, methadone, and levorphanol are discussed as significant additions to the family of analgesics. Although some of these synthetics have somewhat more favorable properties, they still remain addictive and therefore fall short of the ideal painkiller. Of the major opiate-related substances, codeine has the least addiction liability.

## Barbiturates

At low doses, barbiturates ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barbiturate)) relieve tension and anxiety without necessarily causing sleep, though drowsiness may occur in the early days of use. They act on higher cortical centres, especially those involved in inhibitory control. For this reason, small amounts may produce greater talkativeness and less restrained social behaviour. At the same time, impairment is present even at low doses. In larger amounts, barbiturates induce sleep. They do not remove pain in the same way as analgesics, but they do alter the person's response to pain by reducing anxiety.
These drugs also carry dangers. Adverse reactions may include nervousness, anxiety, nausea, diarrhea, confusion, euphoria, and delirium. Some tolerance develops, and prolonged use may create habituation and psychic dependence. At high chronic doses, physical dependence can also develop. Sudden withdrawal may produce severe symptoms, sometimes even more serious than those associated with opiate withdrawal. The barbiturate addict resembles the chronic alcoholic, with blackouts, slurred speech, poor coordination, emotional instability, and psychosis.

## Cocaine

![[attachments/drug_use_002.webp|420]]

*Three-dimensional model of a cocaine molecule*

Cocaine ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cocaine)) is an alkaloid derived from the leaves of the coca plant, Erythroxylon coca, native to [[Bolivia]], [[Chile]], and [[Peru]] along the Andes. Cocaine strongly excites the central nervous system. In small doses it can create pleasure, reduce fatigue, increase alertness, enhance the feeling of strength, and suppress hunger. In larger amounts it becomes intoxicating and can produce excitement, confusion, and convulsions. The Incas already knew the euphoric and hallucinatory effects of coca and used coca chewing in religious ceremonial life. Miners in the high Andes also used coca leaves for strength and endurance.

The drug is habit-forming and may be physically addictive in some individuals, though generally less so than opiates. Significant physiological tolerance does not develop, but chronic use is associated with severe personality change, insomnia, appetite loss, emaciation, violence, and antisocial behaviour. A toxic psychosis may emerge, often with paranoid delusions and tactile hallucinations such as the sensation of bugs under the skin. Cocaine in pure form is a white crystalline powder. Snuffing was once common in [[Europe]], but injection produces faster, more intense, and shorter effects.

## Methamphetamine and the Amphetamines

The amphetamines ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Substituted_amphetamine)) fall into three related types: amphetamine proper, one of its isomers, and methamphetamine. These central stimulants were used medically for depression, fatigue, childhood hyperkinetic disorders, obesity, and other conditions. They were also combined with barbiturates to produce mood-elevating effects. Those mood effects contributed heavily to widespread abuse. Amphetamines can produce toxic psychosis with hallucinations and paranoid delusions, even at relatively low doses when no tolerance has developed.

Repeated use increases tolerance dramatically. Although the normal lethal dose for adults is estimated around 900 milligrams, habitual users may tolerate even more. Amphetamine psychosis can be clinically almost indistinguishable from paranoid schizophrenia. Some symptoms, such as apathy, emotional blunting, and reduced initiative, may persist after use stops. [[Japan]] offers a striking example: widespread wartime and postwar use of methamphetamine created such serious social problems that the drug was brought under strict government control in 1954, a history sharply captured in the street phrase Meth is death. Amphetamines produce habituation, drug dependency, tolerance, and toxicity, though not classical physical addiction in the same sense as opiates.

## Tranquilizers

The discovery of tranquilizers ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sedative)) relied heavily on chance. These drugs were unknown to medicine until the middle of the 20th century, when the value of reserpine and chlorpromazine in psychiatry was discovered. Reserpine originally came from Rauwolfia serpentina, a tropical plant, but its many undesirable side effects led to replacement in practice by chlorpromazine and related phenothiazines. These later drugs are relatively inexpensive, widely available, and not physically addictive, and they became important for calming hyperactive or agitated states and for treating psychotic conditions.

However, tranquilizers are not free from problems. Side effects may include jaundice, dermatitis, seizures, and dangerous interactions with alcohol. These major tranquilizers are distinguished from a second group often called minor tranquilizers, especially meprobamate. Although these milder agents were once regarded as very safe, they became associated with habituation, psychological dependence, and, with heavy long use, even physical dependence and difficult withdrawal. This discussion shows that drugs introduced as medical solutions can themselves become sources of abuse.

## Cannabis

Cannabis ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cannabis)), or marijuana, is the general name for Cannabis ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cannabis)) plants when used for their pleasure-giving effects. The plant may grow to about 5 metres, though drug varieties are often shorter and highly branched. The resinous exudate is the most valued part because it contains the highest concentration of tetrahydrocannabinol (THC), the hallucinogenic principle associated with potency. Cannabis and marijuana broadly include flowering tops, fruit, seeds, leaves, stems, and bark, even though these vary greatly in strength.

Cannabis grows across temperate regions, but its resin content differs with geography and climate. A hot, dry, upland climate is considered especially favorable for potency, and careful cultivation, prevention of pollination, and trimming of top leaves may increase resin production. Different preparations include hashish, charas, ghanja, bhang, kef, and dagga. Hashish is described as the most potent common preparation, often at least twice as strong as ordinary marijuana and sometimes many times stronger. It may be eaten or smoked, often through a water pipe.

## Social Meaning and Drug Experience

Throughout, drug use appears not just as chemistry but as a human search for altered states, power, relief, pleasure, identity, or belonging. The same substance may serve religious, aesthetic, erotic, rebellious, or escapist roles, depending on the setting and the person. That is why the tension between human motives and social cost keeps returning. Society may tolerate some forms of intoxication, but it generally does not accept the use of drugs as a total escape from adult responsibilities. This is especially significant for youth, whose expectations, values, and place in the social order are still forming.

## Conclusion

The overall argument is that psychotropic drugs are powerful because they operate simultaneously on body, mind, and society. Their appeal is real and deeply rooted in human history, but their dangers are also real. Some are medically useful but addictive, some are socially tolerated but psychologically risky, and some are heavily controlled because of their perceived harms. Drug use is therefore both a pharmacological problem and a social one, requiring attention to law, medicine, psychology, and culture at the same time.
