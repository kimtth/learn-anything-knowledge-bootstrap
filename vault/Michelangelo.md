# Michelangelo

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> Michelangelo was the Florentine sculptor, painter, architect, and poet whose career joined the High Renaissance and its later tensions. His Medici Chapel, Sistine Chapel frescoes, and Last Judgment show how monumental bodies, religious drama, and ambitious architectural design became defining vehicles of his art.

## Introduction

![[attachments/michelangelo_001_mw.webp|420]]

*Portrait of Michelangelo by Daniele da Volterra*

Michelangelo di Lodovico Buonarroti Simoni was the full name of Michelangelo. He became one of the central figures of the Italian Renaissance ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Italian_Renaissance)), working as a sculptor, painter, architect, and poet.

## Life and Career

Michelangelo died on February 18, 1564, in [[Rome]]. During his life he was already regarded as the greatest living artist, and later generations continued to place him among the greatest artists in Western history.

His fame rests on sculpture, painting, and architecture, but he considered himself mainly a sculptor. He worked in marble throughout his life, while painting and architecture occupied particular periods. The Sistine Chapel ceiling became especially famous in modern times, while many works in other media remained unfinished.

Michelangelo was unusually well documented for his age. Giorgio Vasari included him in a biography of artists in 1550, presenting him as the perfection of art. Michelangelo disliked parts of that image and encouraged Ascanio Condivi to write a rival life in 1553. The survival of letters, sketches, poems, and memories gives rich evidence, though often only Michelangelo's side of disputes is known.

He was born into a Florentine family of reduced minor nobility. After the family returned from Caprese to [[Florence]], he entered the workshop of Domenico Ghirlandaio at about 13, then came under the protection of Lorenzo de' Medici. Access to the Medici collection of ancient sculpture and contact with poets and intellectuals shaped his early imagination.

Early works already show the tension between delicacy and power. The Madonna of the Stairs reflects Florentine relief traditions, while the Battle of the Centaurs points toward his later interest in muscular action. In Bologna, he carved figures for the shrine of St. Dominic with compact seriousness.

In [[Rome]], the Bacchus showed a mobile, unstable nude figure meant to be seen from several sides. The Pieta, completed by 1499, joined the dead Christ and the grieving Mary into one dense marble mass while clarifying contrasts of male and female, clothed and naked, dead and alive.

The David, begun in 1501, transformed a long-abandoned marble block into a public image of ideal human strength and republican identity. Florentine leaders placed it before the Palazzo Vecchio rather than on the cathedral. At the same time Michelangelo made private devotional works, including Madonnas and the Holy Family, where movement, sculptural bodies, and brilliant colour helped prepare early Florentine Mannerism.

After 1504, Michelangelo was drawn to immense projects that often remained unfinished. His planned Battle of Cascina fresco survives only in copies and sketches, and the projected Apostles for the cathedral produced only the unfinished St. Matthew. These figures later fascinated viewers because they seem to struggle out of stone, matching his idea that the sculptor releases a hidden form.

Pope Julius II brought Michelangelo to [[Rome]] for a grand tomb with many statues, but papal costs, politics, and other projects repeatedly interrupted it. After conflict with Julius, Michelangelo painted the Sistine Chapel ceiling from 1508 to 1512. He changed a ceiling originally planned around the Twelve Apostles into a vast program of prophets, sibyls, ancestors of Christ, and nine scenes from Genesis.

The ceiling shows rapid artistic growth. The early scenes are more stable and close to earlier works, while later figures become larger, freer, more complex, and more inwardly tense. Images such as Ezekiel, the Creation of Eve, the Creation of Adam, and Jonah combine weight, movement, grief, and heroic struggle. Some interpretations connect the program with Neoplatonic ideas about humanity's spiritual development before God.

After the ceiling, Michelangelo returned to the Julius II tomb. The [[Moses]] gave marble the massive power of the Sistine prophets, while the bound prisoners or slaves used complicated poses and emotional force unmatched in Renaissance monumental sculpture. The discovery of the ancient Laocoon group in 1506 strengthened his interest in twisting, strained bodies, though he had already moved toward such forms.

Under Pope Leo X and Cardinal Giulio de' Medici, Michelangelo worked again for the Medici in [[Florence]]. These commissions shifted him toward architectural design at San Lorenzo, especially the family chapel and the Laurentian Library.

## The Medici Chapel

The Medici Chapel responded to the deaths of two young family heirs, Giuliano and Lorenzo, in 1516 and 1519. Michelangelo focused on the marble interior until 1527, joining wall design and tomb sculpture into a single restless architectural and sculptural system.

The chapel revises Classical forms in strange and forceful ways. Windows, cornices, and wall details have unusual proportions, while the tombs use curved bases and figures that grow organically from the architecture. The personifications Day, Night, Dawn, and Dusk express the circular movement of time toward death.

The four time figures are more striking than the idealized Medici effigies above them. Day and Dusk are massive and calm, while Dawn strains upward and Night sleeps uneasily. The effigies are not portraits but elevated images of young soldiers, traditionally read as active and thoughtful, turned toward a focus near the entrance rather than simply toward the Madonna.

During the same years Michelangelo designed the Laurentian Library. Its entrance lobby, or ricetto, uses recessed columns, bold wall rhythms, and a dramatic staircase, making it a key example of architectural Mannerism. The long reading room is quieter and more regular, showing that Michelangelo could adapt his style to restrained effects as well as powerful ones.

The political crisis after the 1527 sack of [[Rome]] changed his work. When Florence restored the republic and resisted the returning Medici, Michelangelo designed fortifications. His drawings used low, thick, irregular defensive forms suited to artillery, showing practical understanding as well as formal invention.

After the Medici returned in 1530, Michelangelo resumed unfinished tomb work and made independent sculptures such as the uncertain Apollo or David and the Victory. The Victory's spiraling victor and compressed defeated old man became important models for younger Mannerist sculptors.

In 1534, Michelangelo left [[Florence]] permanently for [[Rome]]. Letters from this period show concern for family continuity, old age, and death, as well as intense attachments to young men such as Tommaso Cavalieri. His preserved poetry includes sonnets and madrigals, often shaped by Petrarchan love poetry and Neoplatonism, where love helps the soul rise toward the divine.

## The Last Judgment

Pope Paul III brought Michelangelo back to fresco painting for the Last Judgment ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Last_Judgment)) on the end wall of the Sistine Chapel, painted from 1533 to 1541. The theme revived a medieval church tradition at a time connected with early Counter-Reformation concerns.

The painting differs from the Sistine ceiling. Its colour is simpler, and the figures are heavier and less sharply articulated. At the centre, Christ raises one arm to save and lowers the other to condemn, while apostles, saints, the saved, the damned, skeletons, and mythic figures fill a single unified scene.

The composition draws on both Christian and classical sources. Skeletons rising from tombs come from medieval precedent, while Charon on the River Styx enters through [[Dante]]'s Christianized use of pagan imagery. Michelangelo admired Luca Signorelli for anatomical drama, and the fresco uses gestures and expressions to create a powerful sense of judgment, fear, and movement.

In his late decades Michelangelo turned increasingly to architecture ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Architecture)). His designs for the Capitoline Square transformed a civic hill into a monumental urban room with angled facades, colossal columns, smaller supports, an oval pavement, and the ancient statue of Marcus Aurelius at the centre.

At St. Peter's, Michelangelo returned to Bramante's centralized plan and sought unity, scale, and dynamic force. He reshaped the lower church with huge curving walls, powerful pilasters, and a rhythm of projections and recessions that shows the instincts of a sculptor. His dome, completed after his death, became a model for later domes, including the Capitol in [[Washington, D.C. (Washington)|Washington, D.C.]].

He also completed parts of the Palazzo Farnese and designed late projects such as the Porta Pia. His final paintings in the Pauline Chapel use more conventional wall narrative, deeper space, and colour possibly influenced by [[Titian]].

Michelangelo's late poems became direct religious prayers. His last sculptures returned to the dead Christ mourned by others. One unfinished group, meant for his own tomb, includes a figure of Joseph of Arimathea or Nicodemus that may be a self-portrait; another final work became an almost dematerialized sketch of two leaning figures.

His later reputation remained immense, but his direct influence was selective. Artists admired his tragic grandeur, anatomical power, unfinished marble, spatial compression, and serpentine poses, yet few could imitate his cosmic scale. Later figures such as Daniele da Volterra, Auguste Rodin, Gian Lorenzo Bernini, and Peter Paul Rubens drew on particular aspects of his art rather than copying it whole.

## Key Events & Developments

- c. 1491 to 1492: Early marble works showed Michelangelo's first major directions.
- 1498 to 1499: The Pieta established Michelangelo as a major sculptor in [[Rome]].
- 1501 to 1504: The David became a public symbol of the Florentine Republic.
- 1508 to 1512: The Sistine Chapel ceiling became a vast biblical and prophetic program.
- 1516 to 1527: The Medici Chapel brought tomb sculpture and architectural invention together.
- 1533 to 1541: The Last Judgment ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Last_Judgment)) gave the Sistine Chapel a unified vision of salvation and damnation.
- 1564: Michelangelo died in [[Rome]], leaving major architectural plans and late sculptures unfinished.

## Key People

- Michelangelo ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michelangelo)): Renaissance sculptor, painter, architect, and poet whose work shaped Western art.
- Lorenzo de' Medici ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lorenzo_de%27_Medici)): Florentine ruler whose household and art collection helped form Michelangelo's early development.

- Tommaso Cavalieri ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tommaso_Cavalieri)): Young Roman aristocrat connected with Michelangelo's later letters and poems.

## Interesting Facts

- Michelangelo was the first Western artist to have a biography published while he was still alive.
- The Pieta joins two figures from one marble block, an unusually difficult sculptural problem.
- The David was carved from a marble block that had been left unfinished for about 40 years.
- The Sistine Chapel ceiling was completed in less than four years, despite its huge scale and technical difficulty.
- Many unfinished works became central to Michelangelo's later reputation because they seem to show figures emerging from stone.
