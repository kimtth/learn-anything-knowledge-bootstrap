## Introduction

Music ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Music)) is the art of combining vocal or instrumental sounds for beauty of form or emotional expression, usually according to cultural standards of rhythm, melody, and often harmony. It includes simple folk songs, complex electronic works, ritual music, popular songs, theatre music, and concert compositions.

Music is both conceptual and auditory. It is humanly shaped, heard in time, and present in every known society. It easily joins with words in song and with movement in dance.

Throughout history, music has served ritual, drama, ceremony, entertainment, therapy, education, advertising, social bonding, and emotional communication. Radio, film, television, musical theatre, recordings, and the Internet have spread music globally.

Although music is universal, its necessity has been debated. Democritus considered music a product of surplus rather than need, but modern psychology of play and symbolic activity has weakened the idea that the arts are only decorative graces.

## What Is Music?

The question what is music is difficult because musical boundaries change. Early 20th-century definitions often described musical tones as regular vibrations with fixed pitch, separating tone from noise. Modern music, however, may deliberately use noise, irregular sound, speech, electronics, and silence.

[[Aristotle and Aristotelianism|Aristotle]] observed that it is not easy to determine music's nature or why people should know it. Philosophers have often discussed music through its effects, its ties to emotion, dance, ritual, drama, or words, rather than through music alone.

Many explanations are contextualist, meaning they explain music through its relation to human environment and use. Much music history is a history of music's role in religious, military, courtly, theatrical, social, and ceremonial settings.

## Historical Conceptions

*Portrait of Johann Sebastian Bach holding a musical score*

Before the 19th century, music theory was often technical: it explained style, performance, church needs, theatre practice, tuning, composition, or reform. Musicians such as Johann Sebastian Bach produced monuments of art rather than abstract treatises on musical meaning.

The 19th century brought composer-critics such as Carl Maria von Weber, Robert Schumann, Hector Berlioz, and Franz Liszt. These musicians wrote about music, criticism, and meaning with literary skill.

The period also produced powerful philosophical accounts. Arthur Schopenhauer and Friedrich Nietzsche treated music as a dynamic, nonspatial art closer to inner process than painting, sculpture, or architecture.

## Dynamism and Emotion

For Schopenhauer, music was not merely a copy of appearances but a copy of the will itself. This made music more immediate and powerful than arts that represent visible forms. It seemed to restore inner emotions while distancing listeners from real pain.

[[Nietzsche]] opposed Apollonian form and rationality to Dionysian ecstasy, intoxication, and energy. For him, music was the supreme Dionysian art and had myth-making power beyond tone painting or imitation of natural sounds.

These views stress dynamism: music as movement, force, process, and emotional analogy. They helped later thinkers see music as symbolic of inner life rather than as decoration or simple entertainment.

## Musical Meaning

The phrase language of music is tempting but problematic. Music arouses emotions, but it does not have precise semantics like ordinary language. Different listeners may hear different meanings in the same work.

Because verbal explanation cannot fully translate musical experience, some theorists prefer terms such as import, significance, pattern, or gestalt instead of meaning. Music can matter deeply without naming objects or propositions clearly.

Philosophers who claim all meaning must be expressible in language sometimes describe nonreferential music as meaningless. This conflicts with listeners' experience and shows the difficulty of applying discursive thought to nonverbal arts.

## Symbolist Theories

Mid-20th-century symbolists made major contributions to musical aesthetics. Susanne K. Langer treated music as a symbolic form, though critics objected that symbols should stand for something definite.

Langer distinguished broader symbol from narrower signal. Music does not signal a fixed object; it presents forms of feeling, motion, tension, release, growth, and transformation.

Leonard Meyer connected musical meaning with expectation, pattern, style, and information. Musical events gain significance when they confirm, delay, frustrate, or transform expectations within a learned style.

Information theory also influenced Abraham Moles, who analyzed sonic objects and measurable elements of musical messages. These approaches treated music as organized sound rather than as a direct imitation of emotion or nature.

## Electronic Music and Sound

![[attachments/the_art_of_music_music_002.webp|420]]

*Moog Model D synthesizer controls and keyboard*

Modern theory and technology expanded the sound world of music beyond traditional instruments. Electronic synthesizers allowed composers to work with a richer repertoire of sounds from many sources.

In electronically synthesized music, the medium can become almost indistinguishable from the message. Composers shape tone colour, duration, texture, noise, pitch, and spatial effect as primary musical material.

This expansion challenges older definitions based on regular vibration, fixed pitch, and instrumental tone. Music can now include sonic objects, electronic signals, recorded sounds, and manipulated noise.

## Performance and Social Context

Musical meanings are both intrinsic and extrinsic. They arise from pattern, form, rhythm, harmony, melody, timbre, and structure, but also from church, theatre, broadcasting, ritual, recording, and social setting.

The modern concert emphasizes formal, autonomous meanings by asking listeners to attend to music for its own sake. Recordings greatly expanded this practice by turning any room with equipment into a recital hall.

Listening apart from ritual, dance, or storytelling is historically unusual but powerful. It changes how music is composed, performed, preserved, criticized, and valued.

Performance practice shapes meaning because tempo, articulation, instrument choice, tuning, venue, audience, and tradition affect how a work is heard. Music is never only a score; it is also an event.

## Key Events & Developments

- Ancient Greece: Philosophers debated music's nature, education value, and emotional power.
- 1854: Eduard Hanslick's The Beautiful in Music became a major formalist statement.
- 1880: Edmund Gurney's The Power of Sound developed a detailed nonreferential view.
- 20th century: Symbolist, psychological, information-theory, and electronic approaches expanded musical aesthetics.
- Recordings: Internationalized music and changed listening, repertoire, and performance culture.
