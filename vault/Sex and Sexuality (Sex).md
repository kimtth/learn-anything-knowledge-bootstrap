# Sex and Sexuality (Sex)

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> Sex is the biological organization of male and female reproductive roles in many species, while sexuality and reproduction can extend beyond direct offspring production. The source explains asexual and sexual reproduction, genetic variation, fertilization, mating, courtship, signaling, sex ratios, and hermaphroditism as evolutionary solutions to continuity and change.

## Introduction

![[attachments/sex_and_sexuality_sex_001.png|420]]

*Diagram of sexual reproduction in fungi*

Sex is the set of features by which members of many species are divided into male and female groups that complement each other in reproduction. Sex, sexuality, and reproduction are closely connected because all serve the continuation of life. Yet reproduction can occur without sexuality, and sex can exist apart from direct reproduction.

The first need of any population is to produce successors because every individual life is limited. Nonsexual reproduction can do this without eggs and sperm. Ferns release microscopic spores, bulbs produce new bulbs, some jellyfish and sea anemones bud off body parts, and single-celled organisms divide again and again. These methods depend on the basic ability of cells to grow and divide.

Nonsexual reproduction can create many individuals quickly, but the new individuals are usually genetically identical to the parent. If the environment changes badly, all may be vulnerable in the same way. Sexual reproduction gives a second advantage: it creates variation. Offspring from two parents are not exact copies of either parent, so a population gains more possible responses to changing conditions.

For many higher animals, sexual reproduction is linked to structural complexity. Nonsexual reproduction is less suited to organisms whose bodies and activities are highly specialized. Sex therefore works as a double assurance: it replaces individuals and also helps the species adapt over time.

This biological role connects directly to the formation of sex cells ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Germ_cell)). Eggs and sperm carry inherited material from different parents. Their union reshuffles heredity, making each generation partly new. This process explains why sexual reproduction is so widespread even though it often requires difficult contact between two individuals.

## Mating

Mating ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mating)) becomes necessary when eggs must be fertilized before or at the time they are shed. Many eggs receive protective coverings that sperm cannot later penetrate. These coverings may be gluey cases in crustaceans and insects, protein membranes in fishes, jelly masses in frogs and salamanders, or calcified shells in birds and reptiles.

The timing of fertilization depends on when the protective layer forms. Frog and toad eggs are surrounded by jelly soon after they leave the body, so male and female must release sperm and eggs together. The male clasps the female, bringing their openings close, and fertilization occurs as eggs pass out.

In many [[Fishes|fishes]], mating may be only a side-by-side coming together that allows eggs and sperm to be shed at nearly the same moment. In other animals, the procedure is more complicated. Crustaceans such as crabs and lobsters must mate in ways that fit the timing of shell conditions and egg protection.

The main biological problem is simple but demanding: sperm must reach the egg while entry is still possible. Different animal groups solve this problem through spawning, external contact, internal transfer, or special mating behaviour. Each method reflects the structure of the egg and the environment where development begins.

## Courtship

Courtship ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Courtship)) begins before mating because members of opposite sexes must first find each other or meet at a suitable breeding place. Sometimes two individuals search independently. Sometimes large breeding groups gather in a staging area after long movement. Both cases require timing and some form of navigation.

Mass assembly can make pairing easier, but it also attracts predators and may become dangerous. Solitary search can be even harder. In the deep ocean, animals are scattered widely, so encounters are rare. In the small angler fish Photocorynus spiniceps, a young male that finds a female attaches to her body and becomes parasitic, so sperm is available whenever she produces eggs.

On land, many insects and mammals use signals to find mates. Male crickets and cicadas call by day or night. Fireflies use light flashes, and each species has its own rhythm, reducing wasteful mixing between species. Female moths release scent into the night air, and males fly toward its point of release.

Mammals often depend strongly on smell, especially because many are active at night and are not highly guided by colour. A female cat in heat can attract males from the neighbourhood through scent and sound. In such cases, elaborate courtship may be limited because the searching individuals are already ready to mate.

Courtship ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Courtship)) becomes more important when the male must persuade the female or when several males compete. A small male spider may dance before a larger female so that she responds sexually rather than as a predator. This shows that courtship can reduce danger as well as prepare mating.

Birds provide many strong examples of courtship display. Bird mating needs close cooperation because wings are poor substitutes for arms, and successful copulation depends on precise behaviour. Courtship may create a bond between male and female, and that bond may be renewed by ritual actions during each breeding season or after short separations.

Recognition is another function of courtship. In gull colonies, individuals may look very similar, yet stable pair selection helps with producing, incubating, and rearing young. Small repeated behaviours help mates recognize each other inside a crowded colony.

Some penguins appear to have difficulty distinguishing sex and individuality. A male may offer a pebble to a possible female as a nest-making contribution. If she accepts, the pair may form; if she rejects it, he may have chosen an unready female or even another male. Other birds use song, dance, feathers, and visual display, with the lyre bird combining sight and sound.

The pattern also broadens from courtship to the distribution of sexes. Separate male and female individuals are common in animals and often occur in roughly equal numbers. This arrangement appears very effective for gene reassortment and recombination, though it may have evolved separately in different animal groups.

Separate sexes are not universal. Many slower or attached animals are hermaphrodites, with both ovaries and testes. Earthworms, slugs, land snails, flatworms, tapeworms, barnacles, sea squirts, and others can produce both eggs and sperm. Even when self-fertilization is possible, cross-fertilization is usually achieved or favoured.

Some animals solve sex separation through timing rather than permanent difference. The oyster may change from male to female and back once or twice a year. This shows that sex can be a flexible life-stage pattern, not only a fixed division between two kinds of individuals.

## Parthenogenesis

![[attachments/sex_and_sexuality_sex_003.webp|420]]

*Milkweed aphids on a plant stem*

Parthenogenesis ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parthenogenesis)) is development from an unfertilized egg. A ripe egg already has the potential for full development, while fertilization normally adds the male nucleus, increases variation, may determine sex, and stimulates development. These functions are separate, so development can sometimes begin without sperm.

Natural parthenogenesis occurs in several animals, including Daphnia, the waterflea. Experimental parthenogenesis can also be produced in many species. Eggs of starfish, sea urchins, worms, and other marine invertebrates may begin development after treatment with weak organic acid. Frog eggs can be stimulated by gentle pricking with a fine glass needle dipped in lymph.

The sex of parthenogenetic offspring depends on the egg's chromosomal constitution. Frog eggs developing in this way become males because each cell has only one X chromosome. In nature, the system may be more complex, but the general relation is that XX individuals are female and those with a single X are male.

The queen honeybee shows a detailed natural system. After her nuptial flight, she stores sperm. During spring and summer, most eggs are fertilized and become females, either workers or new queens depending on food. Later, as the sperm supply runs low, unfertilized eggs can develop as males.

## Hormones

![[attachments/sex_and_sexuality_sex_002.png|420]]

*Diagram of the human endocrine system*

Hormones can influence sex development because the reproductive gland of many developing animals begins in a nearly neutral state. Chemical agents in the blood may override or modify the influence of sex chromosomes, especially at early stages.

In the chick, sex can be experimentally controlled by hormones until about four hours after hatching. A female chick injected with testosterone at hatching can develop into a functional cock. Male hormone at later stages can also cause early comb growth, crowing, and aggressive behaviour in either sex.

Female hormones such as estrogen stimulate early oviduct growth in females and can feminize plumage or suppress comb growth in males. Mammals are especially sensitive because the egg and embryo develop inside the uterus, exposed to chemicals from the mother's blood rather than protected inside a shell.

The embryo later makes its own sex hormones, but usually after anatomical sex is already established. The placenta can also produce large amounts of female hormone and some male hormone during pregnancy. Usually these appear too late to change sex development seriously, but they show that sexuality is shaped by both heredity and chemical environment.

## Key Events & Developments

- Nonsexual reproduction produces successors by spores, budding, bulbs, or cell division, often creating genetically identical individuals.
- Sexual reproduction ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sexual_reproduction)) adds variation by combining heredity from two parents, increasing adaptive possibilities for populations.
- Mating ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mating)) evolves when eggs must be fertilized before protective coverings prevent sperm entry.
- Courtship ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Courtship)) develops as a way to find mates, signal readiness, choose partners, reduce danger, and coordinate difficult mating.
- Parthenogenesis ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parthenogenesis)) shows that egg development can sometimes begin without sperm, naturally or experimentally.

## Key People

- Queen honeybee: Demonstrates how fertilized eggs become females and unfertilized eggs can become males.

## Interesting Facts

- Firefly flash rhythms help members of the same species find each other and avoid interspecies mating.
- Bird courtship often builds pair bonds and recognition, not only immediate mating readiness.
- Hermaphrodites may have both ovaries and testes, yet many still avoid self-fertilization.
- Oysters can change sex from male to female and back during the year.
