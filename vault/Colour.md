## Introduction

Colour is the aspect of an object described in terms of hue, lightness, and saturation. It is studied through physics, physiology, and psychology, but the physical study of colour is largely a study of light and its interaction with matter.

Visible colour requires enough light for the eye to distinguish wavelengths, but it also depends on how the brain interprets signals and compares them with memory.

## Physics of colour

The physics of colour covers additive and subtractive colour mixing, colorimetry, chromaticity diagrams, and the measurement of colour. The physical causes of colour include incandescence, gas excitation, molecular vibrations, charge transfer, metallic reflection, semiconductors, scattering, interference, and diffraction.

Historically, Isaac Newton's prism experiments in 1666 overturned older ideas that colour came from mixtures of black and white.

Additive colour, where lights combine, differs from subtractive colour, where pigments or filters remove parts of the spectrum. Red, green, and blue are additive primaries for light, while cyan, magenta, and yellow are subtractive primaries for inks and dyes. This distinction explains why screens and printed images use different colour systems.

Colorimetry tries to measure colour as humans perceive it. Chromaticity diagrams separate hue and saturation from brightness and make it possible to compare colours numerically. These systems are practical tools for lighting, printing, photography, television, and industrial colour matching.

Measurement is needed because physical spectra and perceived colours do not map one-to-one. Different mixtures of wavelengths can look identical to the eye, a phenomenon known as metamerism. This is why colour standards, controlled illumination, and observer models matter in manufacturing, reproduction, and display technology.

In the tristimulus system, colours are matched against red, green, and blue standards and expressed as numerical values such as X, Y, and Z.

Physical colour depends on the spectrum of light reaching the eye. Some objects emit light, as in incandescence or gas excitation; others reflect or absorb parts of incoming light. Molecular structure, charge transfer, crystal fields, and electronic bands all help determine which wavelengths are absorbed and which are returned to the viewer.

## Perception of colour

![[attachments/colour_002.webp|420]]

*Human cone cell response curves for colour vision*

Colour perception depends not only on light but on the eye, the brain, and context. This includes colour constancy and the ways in which visual systems interpret reflected or emitted light under different illuminants.

The eye responds through cone cells that differ in spectral sensitivity, while the brain compares signals to create the experience of hue. This means colour is not simply a property of objects; it is an interaction among light source, surface, eye, and nervous system. Colour constancy allows objects to look similar under changing illumination.

Metamerism is also important: two lights or surfaces can look the same under one illuminant even though their spectra differ. This matters in printing, textiles, lighting, and display technology, where a colour match may fail when the lighting changes.

## Colour in nature and art

Colour phenomena have many natural and artistic consequences, including sky colour, feathers, eyes, opals, thin-film effects, pigments, and designed colour systems. Colour science links to practical use in art and technology.

Natural colours can arise from many physical processes. The blue sky results from Rayleigh scattering, while some feathers, beetle shells, and opals get colour from structure rather than pigment. Thin films, diffraction gratings, metallic reflection, and semiconductors all create colour in different ways.

In art and technology, colour depends on pigments, dyes, lighting, printing, and display systems. Colour science links physics to design because artists and engineers both need to predict how colours will mix, fade, contrast, or change under different viewing conditions.

The larger point is that colour belongs simultaneously to nature and culture. It can be measured as radiation, produced by matter, processed by the eye, named by language, and used by artists, manufacturers, and scientists. This is why colour study crosses optics, chemistry, biology, psychology, art, and technology.

## Conclusion

Colour is a meeting point of optics, materials science, visual biology, and human interpretation.

This is Newton's second law: force is the product of mass and acceleration, so a larger mass or a faster change in motion requires a larger net force.
