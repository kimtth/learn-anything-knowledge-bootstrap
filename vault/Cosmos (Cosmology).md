# Cosmos (Cosmology)

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> Cosmology investigates the universe as a whole: its origin, large-scale structure, expansion, contents, and possible fate. It connects astronomical observation with physical theories of matter, energy, and gravitation.

## Introduction

Cosmology ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cosmology)) studies the universe as a physical whole, combining astronomy and physics to explain its large-scale structure, distance scale, and history. The visible sky is the starting point: what looks like a glowing band is the Milky Way, a galaxy seen edge on, and the Sun is only one star among many.

The central theme is scale. Stars, galaxies, and clusters are separated by immense distances, so cosmology depends on measurement, geometry, and physical theory. The universe is a subject where ordinary intuition fails and where [[Relativity|relativity]] and observation must work together.

## Experimental evidence for general relativity

[[Einstein]] replaced Newton’s picture of gravity with the idea that mass and energy curve space-time ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spacetime)). Bodies then move according to the geometry created by that curvature, so gravitation becomes part of the structure of the universe rather than a force acting in empty space.

This shift gave cosmology a modern mathematical base. The large-scale universe can be studied through physical laws, and experimental evidence has supported Einstein’s approach, making general relativity the framework for most modern cosmological models.

## Relativistic cosmologies

Einstein’s 1917 cosmology assumed a universe that was homogeneous, isotropic, and static on large scales. Later work relaxed or revised those assumptions and produced families of models in which the universe could be closed, open, or critical, with different long-term fates.
These models show that cosmology is not only about shape but also about evolution. A universe may expand forever, slow to a halt, or collapse again, and those outcomes depend on the relation between density, curvature, and the governing equations.

## The Einstein-de Sitter universe

In 1932, Einstein and de Sitter proposed a model with zero cosmological constant and zero spatial curvature. It is Euclidean in space, begins with expansion, and keeps expanding forever while the speed of recession gradually falls toward zero.
This model is a clean critical case between closed and open universes. It helped clarify how a simple physical description can connect geometry with cosmic destiny, and it remains important as a conceptual reference point.

## Bound and unbound universes and the closure density

Later discussion recasts the universe in terms of whether it is gravitationally bound or unbound. If density is high enough, a closed universe can eventually recollapse; if it is lower, the universe remains open and expands forever; if it is exactly at the critical value, expansion continues forever but slows endlessly.

The key idea is closure density, the dividing line between those outcomes. By linking density, geometry, and expansion, cosmology becomes a study of the universe’s ultimate fate.

## Anisotropy Probe
Measurements from the Wilkinson Microwave Anisotropy Probe study the early universe through the cosmic microwave background. Tiny ripples in that background reveal information about matter, radiation, and the conditions that existed long before galaxies formed.

Because the early universe was opaque, direct observation stops at a physical boundary. Cosmology therefore relies on the microwave background and on theory to reconstruct what happened before light could travel freely.

## Primordial nucleosynthesis

The early radiation-dominated universe was a place where temperature and time can be computed with great precision. Very early on, matter and antimatter were in thermal equilibrium with radiation. As the universe cooled, particles could no longer remain in that balance, and the first nuclei formed.

Primordial nucleosynthesis is therefore one of the great tests of cosmological theory. It connects the first moments after the big bang to the later abundance of matter. It shows that cosmology is not only about galaxies and geometry but also about the earliest chemical history of the universe.

## Inflation

Inflation ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inflation)) is one of the most important ideas in modern cosmology. Alan Guth and others proposed that, during very early high-energy phases, the vacuum may have acted like a false vacuum with strong repulsive gravity. This would have caused the universe to expand exponentially, doubling many times in a very short period.

When the universe later shifted to a true vacuum, the stored energy would have been released and reheated the cosmos. Inflation helps explain several problems at once, including why the universe looks so uniform on large scales. It also links particle physics to cosmology, showing that the smallest physical theories can shape the largest possible structure.

## Key Events & Developments

- 1917: Einstein constructs an early relativistic cosmological model.
- 1932: Einstein and de Sitter propose the zero-curvature model.
- Mid-20th century: observations of expansion and background radiation reshape cosmology.
- Late 20th century: inflation becomes a leading early-universe theory.

## Key People

## Interesting Facts

- The cosmic microwave background records the universe’s surface of last scattering.
- Before recombination, the universe was opaque to light.
- Inflation is driven by a false vacuum with strong repulsive behavior.
