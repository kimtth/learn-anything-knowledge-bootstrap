# Norway

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> Norway is a northern European country of rugged relief, fjords, hydropower, maritime exchange, and petroleum wealth. Its independence after Danish and Swedish rule, distinctive languages and folk traditions, and transformation through offshore oil and gas illuminate the links between geography, resources, and national development.

## Introduction

![[attachments/norway_001.webp|420]]

*Relief map of Norway*

Norway is a northern European country occupying the western half of the Scandinavian peninsula. Nearly half its people live in the far south around Oslo, while about two-thirds of the land is mountainous. Its deeply indented coast has about 50,000 islands and many glacial fjords. Norway's early dependence on fishing, farming, and seafaring led to the Viking era, Atlantic exploration, maritime trade, specialized shipbuilding, and later offshore petroleum.

Norway became independent in 1905 after centuries of domination by Denmark and union with Sweden. Since the 1970s, offshore oil and natural gas have transformed the economy, making Norway one of the world's leading petroleum exporters. It remains socially wealthy, politically stable, and culturally tied to both old folk traditions and international maritime exchange.

## Relief

![[attachments/norway_002.webp|420]]

*Norwegian fjord landscape*

Norway is bordered by the Barents Sea, Norwegian Sea, North Sea, Skagerrak, Sweden, Finland, and Russia. Its bedrock belongs to the Fennoscandian Shield and includes ancient granite and metamorphic rocks. Glaciation, erosion, folding, and river cutting produced fjords, gorges, high plateaus, peneplains, and mountains. The Hardanger Plateau is Europe's largest mountain plateau, and the Finnmark Plateau occupies much of the far north.

## Drainage

The Glama, or Glomma, is Norway's longest river, running about 372 miles through eastern Norway. The Tana-Anarjakka forms part of the Finnish border, and Norway has about 65,000 lakes larger than 4 acres. Mjosa is the largest lake, while many rivers and lakes support hydroelectric power.

## Soils

Norwegian [[Soils|soils]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soil)) reflect ice-age flooding, marine deposits, forest leaching, and glacial material. Rich soils occur below old marine coastal regions, while forested lands often have nutrient-poor soils. The Jaren Plain has exceptionally productive glacier-formed soils, mild winters, long growing seasons, and ample precipitation.

## Climate

Norway's [[Climate and Weather (Climate)|climate]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Climate)) is much warmer than its latitude suggests because the Norwegian Current brings warm water into surrounding seas. Fjords usually remain unfrozen, even in Arctic Finnmark. Western Norway has a marine climate with cool summers, mild winters, and heavy rainfall, while eastern Norway is sheltered and more continental, with warmer summers, colder winters, and less precipitation.

## Plant and Animal Life

Norway has about 2,000 plant species, with spruce and pine forests in broad glacial valleys and birch, ash, rowan, and aspen on steep hillsides. Mountain zones pass from conifers to birch and willow belts. Animal life includes Sami reindeer landscapes, coastal and mountain species, fish, seabirds, and wildlife adapted to forests, fjords, and Arctic conditions.

## People

Most Norwegians are Nordic in heritage, while the Sami are the country's first inhabitants and live mainly in northern Norway. The Sami arrived at least 10,000 years ago and formerly faced official discrimination, but they are now legally recognized as a distinct culture with some autonomy through the Sami Parliament. Only a minority still practice traditional reindeer herding.

## Languages

Norwegian belongs to the North Germanic branch. Bokmal, descended from Danish-influenced Riksmal, dominates commerce, communication, and most schools, while Nynorsk, developed from rural dialects, is used especially in western and interior regions. North Sami, a Uralic language, is spoken as a first language by more than 15,000 people and is official in several municipalities. English is widely spoken.

## Religion and Settlement

About nine-tenths of Norwegians traditionally belonged to the Church of Norway, an Evangelical Lutheran national church, with smaller Pentecostal, Catholic, Lutheran Free Church, Jehovah's Witness, Methodist, Baptist, Muslim, and Buddhist communities. Settlement is concentrated in Ostlandet around Oslo, coastal towns, Stavanger, Bergen, Trondheim, and northern centres, with little sharp boundary between rural and urban populations.

## Economy

Norway's economy ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economy)) depends heavily on petroleum, though it also includes shipping, services, manufacturing, finance, fisheries, agriculture, and public employment. The government created the Government Petroleum Fund in 1990, later renamed the Government Pension Fund Global, to invest budget surpluses abroad and reduce oil-price volatility. Norway's per capita income became among the world's highest.

## Hydroelectricity

Norway has enormous hydroelectric potential because many lakes lie high above sea level, mountains receive heavy precipitation, and rivers descend steeply. Hydropower meets almost all domestic electricity demand, and per capita hydroelectric production is among the world's highest. Renewable energy forms a large share of national energy consumption.

## Electrometallurgy

Cheap hydroelectricity supports Norway's electrometallurgical industries. The country is a major producer of aluminum, iron-based alloys, ilmenite, olivine, nepheline syenite, dimension stone, and coal from Svalbard. Magnesium production declined when Norway could no longer compete internationally.

## Manufacturing

Manufacturing ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manufacturing)) includes metals, engineering, petroleum equipment, supply ships, semisubmersible drilling platforms, and Condeep production platforms for rough offshore seas. Traditional shipbuilding declined after 1980, but petroleum-related equipment grew. Ostlandet, Stavanger, Alesund, and coastal furniture industries are important manufacturing regions.

## Finance and Trade

The Bank of Norway acts as central bank, while public, savings, commercial, insurance, and foreign banks share credit markets. A banking crisis in 1991 led to temporary government ownership of major banks before reprivatization. Foreign trade accounts for a large share of national income, with petroleum exports going mainly to the European Union. Other exports include machinery, transport equipment, metals, fish, and shipping services.

## Services, Labour, and Taxation

The service sector ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tertiary_sector)) employs most workers, and tourism attracts Germans, Swedes, Danes, and others. Public-sector employment is high. Agriculture and fishing are subsidized, remote industries may receive incentives, and taxes are high, including progressive income and wealth taxes and a large value-added tax. Strong unions and employer associations help coordinate wage and economic policy.

## Justice

Norwegian justice uses local conciliation boards before many civil cases reach court. Formal courts include appeals courts and the Supreme Court, the final arbiter. Ombudsmen protect citizens by mediating issues with public administrators.

## Security

Norway's security strategy depends on national forces and NATO. Military service has traditionally been compulsory for fit men, but Norway's territory is too large for defense without allies. Its strategic location on NATO's northern flank led to sophisticated warning systems, mobile forces, and restrictions on foreign troops and nuclear weapons except in war or immediate threat. After the Cold War, spending fell and specialized UN/NATO units gained importance.

## Health, Housing, and Education

Norway has national health insurance, welfare benefits, and improved housing standards. World War II housing shortages eased through state loans, though Oslo prices later surged. Education is compulsory from ages 6 to 16, with optional further schooling, universities, specialized institutions, vocational schools, folk high schools, and adult education. Major universities include Oslo, Bergen, Trondheim, Tromso, Stavanger, the Norwegian University of Life Sciences, and Agder.

## Cultural Life

![[attachments/norway_003.webp|420]]

*Oslo Opera House*

Norwegian culture ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Culture)) preserves legends of haugfolket, underjordiske, vetter, trolls, folk costumes, folk song, and festivals such as Constitution Day, Midsummer's Eve, St. Olaf's Day, and Christmas. Cuisine reflects coast, mountains, and wildlife, including salmon, cod, lutefisk, pinnekjott, shrimp, meatcakes, lefse, geitost, reindeer, moose, and aquavit. The Sami maintain a distinct culture and dress.

## Arts, Sports, and Media

Norway produced major cultural figures including Henrik Ibsen, Edvard Grieg, Bjornstjerne Bjornson, Knut Hamsun, Sigrid Undset, and Sonja Henie. Skiing is central to national identity; Holmenkollen, founded in 1892, is the world's oldest ski festival. Press freedom is protected, and NRK held a broadcasting monopoly from 1933 until private radio and television expanded from the 1980s and 1990s.

## History

Human occupation in Norway began along the coast after the last ice age, with stone tools from about 9500 to 6000 BCE. Rock carvings at Alta from 6200 to 2500 BCE show hunting and fishing life. Farming arrived between 3000 and 2500 BCE, and later settlements developed chieftain graves, farmhouses, hill forts, local assemblies, and regional things.

## The Vikings

The Viking Age, about 800 to 1050, sent Norwegian raiders and settlers west to Ireland, Scotland, England, France, the northern islands, Iceland, Greenland, and Vinland in North America. Surplus population, ships, weapons, military organization, and adventure all contributed. Viking contact with western Europe helped unify and Christianize Norway.

## Christianization and Medieval Kingdom

Harald I Fairhair unified much of Norway after Hafrsfjord, sometime between 872 and 900. Olaf I Tryggvason and Olaf II Haraldsson pushed Christianization, sometimes by force. Olaf II died at Stiklestad in 1030 and became St. Olaf, Norway's patron saint. English bishops, royal power, and church estates helped establish the monarchy.

## Greenland and the Kalmar Union

Erik the Red led Norse settlement of Greenland in 985. The settlements later declined through climate worsening, plague, isolation, piracy, and Inuit pressure, disappearing after about 1410. In Scandinavia, Margaret I and Erik of Pomerania created the Kalmar Union in 1397, after which Norway became politically subordinate to Denmark and remained in union with Denmark until 1814.

## Danish Rule and the Treaty of Kiel

In 1536, Norway was declared a Danish province, though it kept many laws and institutions because its society differed from Denmark's. Natural resources such as fish, timber, iron, and copper increased in importance after 1550. Christian IV founded towns including Kristiansand, Kongsberg, and Christiania. The Treaty of Kiel in 1814 transferred Norway from Denmark to Sweden, prompting the Eidsvoll constitution of May 17.

## Union with Sweden and 19th-Century Norway

After a brief war in 1814, Norway entered union with Sweden while retaining internal self-government and its constitution. The 19th century brought population growth, emigration, shipping expansion, railroads, agricultural modernization, national culture, and figures such as Niels Henrik Abel, Sophus Lie, Fridtjof Nansen, Edvard Munch, Ibsen, and Grieg. Norway gained independence in 1905.

## 20th-Century Norway

Norway stayed neutral in [[World Wars (World War I)|World War I]] but lost much merchant shipping. The Great Depression caused severe unemployment until Labour reforms under Johan Nygaardsvold. In [[World Wars (World War II)|World War II]], Germany invaded on April 9, 1940; King Haakon VII and the government went to London, Vidkun Quisling headed a Nazi puppet regime, and resistance grew through strikes, sabotage, and exile shipping under Nortraship.

## Postwar Norway and Europe

Postwar foreign policy joined NATO in 1949 while restricting foreign bases and nuclear weapons. Norway debated EEC/EU membership for decades, rejected EEC membership in 1972, joined the European Economic Area in 1994, and again rejected EU membership in 1994. Oil wealth, the Government Pension Fund, low unemployment, and centre-right or Labour governments shaped early-21st-century politics.
