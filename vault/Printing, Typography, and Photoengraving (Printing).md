# Printing, Typography, and Photoengraving (Printing)

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> Printing developed from East Asian woodblock work and movable type into Gutenberg's metal-type press, industrial rotary machinery, photographic composition, offset, gravure, screen, and electrostatic processes. Its reproducibility transformed the circulation of knowledge, literacy, publishing, commerce, and public communication.

## Introduction

Printing ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Printing)) traditionally meant applying a colouring agent under pressure to a surface to create text or illustration. Modern reproduction methods no longer always require pressure, ink, or even a traditional press, so printing is better understood as techniques for reproducing texts and images in desired numbers on durable surfaces.

The history of printing is a movement away from its original materials: lead, ink, and the press. For five centuries printing held a near monopoly on storing and transmitting information, but radio, television, film, microfilm, tape, and electronic media later challenged that role.

Printing ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Printing)) still has a vast field. It is used for books, newspapers, textiles, plates, wallpaper, packaging, billboards, and even miniature electronic circuits. Its invention helped create the modern world by multiplying knowledge during an age of commercial expansion, social change, and intellectual transformation.

Its importance lies in repeatability. Once a text or image could be reproduced accurately in many copies, information could circulate beyond the limits of manuscript labour. Printing changed authorship, censorship, education, science, religion, advertising, bureaucracy, and public opinion because identical copies could reach dispersed readers.

## Early Printing in East Asia

The earliest major printing required paper, ink, and relief surfaces bearing text. China had these elements early: papermaking, ink formulas, and carved surfaces such as Buddhist marble pillars and religious seals. Pilgrims could press damp paper against inked relief inscriptions to take impressions.

By perhaps the 6th century, the wood block became a practical printing surface. Text was written on thin paper, pasted face down onto a smooth wood block, and carved so that the characters stood in relief and reverse. The block was inked with a brush, paper was laid on it, and the back was rubbed to make an impression.

The oldest known wood-block printed works include Buddhist incantations in Japan around 764-770, the Diamond Sutra in China in 868, and a 130-volume collection of Chinese classics begun in 932 under Fong Tao. These works show that printing first flourished in religious, scholarly, and state-supported settings.

## Movable Type in China and Korea

Around 1041-1048, Pi Sheng conceived movable type made of baked clay and glue. He placed individual types on an iron plate coated with resin, wax, and paper ash. Heating and cooling fixed the types for printing, after which they could be separated and reused.

Chinese movable type faced limits because the writing system required many characters. Metal movable type later appeared in Korea, where bronze type was used before Gutenberg's European invention. These Asian developments were technically important even though wood-block printing often remained more practical for large character sets.

## Xylography in Europe

Xylography, or printing from carved wood, appeared in Europe no earlier than the late 14th century. Paper made wood impressions easier than parchment, and the method first served decorative initials, religious pictures, and short devotional texts.

By the first half of the 15th century, European wood-block books such as religious works and Latin grammar donats were made by a method similar to Chinese printing. Experiments may have been made with carved alphabet blocks cut into reusable letters, but small roman letters in wood were fragile, irregular, and difficult to reuse.

This problem pushed [[Europe]] toward a new solution: metal movable type. The alphabet required far fewer symbols than Chinese writing, so reusable type could be economical if letters were durable, uniform, and capable of precise composition.

## Gutenberg and Typographic Printing

*Metal movable type arranged in a type case*

Johannes Gutenberg transformed printing by combining movable metal type, suitable ink, type composition, and a press. The typographer's work involved selecting type from a case, arranging it in a composing stick, justifying lines with blank spacing material, and distributing type back after printing.

This system made typographic composition repeatable because separate letters could be arranged, locked, printed, and returned for reuse rather than carved anew for each page.

The Gutenberg press was likely adapted from a binding or wine-style screw press. Type locked into a form was inked, covered with paper, and pressed between a bed and platen. This gave sharper impressions than brushing and allowed both sides of a sheet to be printed.

Early presses soon gained key features: a mobile bed, improved screw threads, and printing in two pulls or turns. These functional characteristics remained central for centuries. Printing spread rapidly because it could reproduce texts more quickly, uniformly, and cheaply than manuscript copying.

## Improvements After Gutenberg

For about 350 years, the screw press improved gradually. Stronger frames, better inking, more accurate beds, and more efficient operation increased output. Yet the basic process remained discontinuous: type was placed on a flat bed, paper was inserted, pressure was applied, and the bed moved again.

The move from flat bed to cylinder and then to continuous paper rolls changed printing from a hand-fed craft into a high-speed industrial process.

Industrial printing required mechanization. Friedrich Koenig and Andreas Bauer introduced powered cylinder presses that greatly increased speed. In 1818 they designed a perfecting machine that printed both sides of a sheet. William Church added grippers in 1824 to control sheet handling.

The next leap was the rotary press. Richard Hoe patented a type-revolving press in the United States in 1844, placing columns of type on a large cylinder and using smaller impression cylinders. Stereotyping solved the problem of type falling from curved forms by creating curved plates from molds.

Roll-fed rotary presses completed the mechanized cycle by using continuous paper reels instead of sheets. This allowed newspaper printing at far higher speeds and connected printing technology with mass literacy, advertising, and the daily press.

## Lithography and Photographic Processes

Lithography, developed by Aloys Senefelder in 1796, is neither relief nor intaglio. It depends on the fact that water and grease do not mix. A greasy design on porous limestone accepts ink after wetting, while blank areas retain water and repel ink.

Senefelder also showed that designs could be transferred to other stones and that zinc could substitute for stone. By 1850, mechanized lithographic presses used cylinders, wetting rollers, and inking rollers. Zinc plates made rotary lithographic presses possible in 1868.

Joseph-Nicephore Niepce discovered in the 1820s that some chemical compounds are sensitive to light while seeking automatic image inscription. This led toward photography, photogravure, and photographic reproduction. William Henry Fox Talbot later contributed to applying photography to printing.

The halftone screen made it possible to reproduce continuous photographic tones by translating them into printable dot patterns. In the 1880s, crossed glass screens replaced earlier cloth screens, improving the reproduction of photographs in letterpress and lithographic printing.

## Gravure and Rotogravure

Gravure is intaglio printing: ink is held in cells or recesses and transferred to paper under pressure. The challenge was to create many tiny cells directly on a cylinder and still allow doctor blades to wipe the surface clean.

J.W. Swan's carbon tissue, invented in 1862-64, could be made photosensitive and applied to metal surfaces. In 1878, Karl Klic copied a screen onto carbon tissue to transfer both image and cell structure to a cylinder. His work led to rotogravure.

Klic and colleagues founded the Rembrandt Intaglio Printing Company in 1895 and used rotogravure for picture reproduction. The process spread after a worker revealed Klic's method in the United States in 1903. Rotogravure became important for magazines, packaging, and high-volume tonal printing.

## Photocomposition and Phototypesetting

Photocomposition developed because it was inefficient to compose heavy lead type just to photograph it for modern plates or cylinders. By 1915, the Photoline assembled transparent letter matrices in a composing stick to film headings.

First-generation phototypesetters adapted metal typesetting machines by replacing metal matrices with photographic ones and adding camera units. Examples included the Fotosetter, Fotomatic, Linofilm, and Monophoto. Because they inherited mechanical limits from lead machines, they did not greatly increase speed.

Second-generation phototypesetters reduced moving parts. Machines used rotating disks or drums carrying photographic matrices and optical devices controlled by flashes, prisms, or mirrors. The Lumitype, invented as the Lithomat, represented this more functional approach.

The Teletypesetter separated keyboarding from casting or typesetting. Perforated tape could be produced on one keyboard and sent to a machine elsewhere, even by telegraph. This separation of functions anticipated later electronic composition workflows.

## Cold Type and Manual Phototypesetting

Cold type described economical text preparation by typewriter-like machines capable of justified lines with variable-width type. Systems such as IBM Multipoint and Justowriter used coded signs, paper tape, magnetic tape, calculators, or computers to control spacing and final output.

Manual phototypesetters produced short texts and titles by exposing photosensitive film through transparent matrices. Machines such as Dantype, Typro, Headliner, Hadego, Starlettograph, Letterphot, Diatyp, and Monotype photoheadliner varied in automation, optical enlargement, and ease of use.

Phototypesetting produced direct positive or negative images on transparent photosensitive material. This suited offset, rotogravure, and other processes that needed film or plates rather than lead type.

## Rotogravure, Offset, and Letterset

Rotogravure prints from engraved cylinder cells. The cylinder is flooded or supplied with fluid ink, wiped by a doctor blade, and pressed against paper by an impression cylinder. Cell depth controls tonal density, and even line art and type must be screened because the screen creates cell walls.

Offset printing uses a single surface whose image areas repel water and accept ink while nonimage areas accept water and repel ink. The image is first transferred from plate to rubber blanket and then from blanket to paper. Offset presses use plate, blanket, and impression cylinders.

Letterset, also called indirect letterpress or dry offset, combines relief typeforms with an offset-style blanket cylinder. It uses no dampening system, and because the blanket reverses the image, typeforms appear straight rather than reversed.

## Screen Printing, Collotype, Flexography, and Electrostatic Printing

Serigraphy, or screen printing, forces ink through a mesh screen with a squeegee. Nonprinting areas are blocked by stencil or filled mesh. Screens may be silk, synthetic fibres, metal gauze, or combinations, and the method can print on many surfaces.

Collotype uses a photosensitive layer on glass or film whose ink and water retention varies with exposure. Related to lithography and gravure, it can reproduce photographs without a screen and is valued for faithful limited editions, though it is slow and the printing surface wears out quickly.

Flexography is based on letterpress principles but uses fluid inks and flexible plates, usually on roll-fed rotary presses. It is economical for wrapping paper, cardboard, plastic film, newspapers, magazines, and coarse-screen colour work.

Electrostatic printing works without contact, typeform, or ink in the traditional sense. Zinc oxide-coated paper is charged in the dark, exposed through a positive image, and then passed through pigmented particles that cling to remaining charge. It was applied to maps and small-book printing.

## Printing Inks and the Scope of Printing

Printing inks include vehicles, colouring matter, and additives that control flow, drying, adhesion, and appearance. Different processes require different inks: letterpress inks differ from gravure fluids, screen inks, offset inks, and electrostatic particles.

Printing technology is therefore a system of type, plates, paper, ink, press, image preparation, drying, and finishing. Each change in one part affects the others. This is why the history of printing includes chemistry, metallurgy, optics, mechanics, electronics, and information handling.

## Key Events & Developments

- 868: The Chinese Diamond Sutra became the earliest known printed book.
