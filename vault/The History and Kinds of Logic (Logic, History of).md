## Introduction

![[attachments/logic_history_001.webp|420]]

*Portrait bust of Aristotle*

Logic ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logic)) is the discipline that studies valid inference, argument, proof, and formal consequence. Its Western history begins in ancient Greece and develops through Aristotelian term logic, Stoic propositional logic, medieval scholastic logic, algebraic logic, predicate logic, set theory, proof theory, model theory, and modern mathematical logic.

The earliest Greek thinkers used arguments before they studied argument itself. Parmenides gave extended reasoning for his monist view, but he did not formulate general rules of inference. Zeno of Elea came closer to logical reflection through paradoxes using reductio ad absurdum, where an opposing view is refuted by deriving absurd consequences.

Early rhetoricians and Sophists such as Gorgias, Hippias, Prodicus, and Protagoras sharpened Greek interest in proof, refutation, and persuasive argument, but systematic logic began with Aristotle.

Logic becomes formal when it asks whether a conclusion follows from the structure of an argument rather than from its subject matter. A formal study separates premises, conclusion, and inferential pattern. This makes it possible to test the same pattern with many different subjects and to distinguish a persuasive statement from an argument whose conclusion is required by its premises.

## Aristotle and the Organon

[[Aristotle and Aristotelianism|Aristotle]] was the first major systematic logician. Although Plato used dialectic as philosophical method, Aristotle created rules and strategies for reasoning and recognized that his work on deduction had no real predecessor.

Aristotle's logical works are known as the Organon, or Tool, because logic served all sciences rather than being one theoretical science among physics, mathematics, and metaphysics. The Organon includes Categories, On Interpretation, Prior Analytics, Posterior Analytics, Topics, and Sophistic Refutations.

The Categories discusses kinds of entities such as substance, quantity, quality, relation, place, time, position, state, action, and passion. On Interpretation treats semantics and proposition structure. Prior Analytics presents syllogistic, while Posterior Analytics studies scientific demonstration.

The Topics and Sophistic Refutations concern dialectical argument, questioning, debate, and fallacies. Aristotle moved from practical interrogative techniques toward the narrower logical study of necessary consequence.

## Categorical Propositions and Syllogisms

Aristotle analyzed propositions such as universal affirmative, universal negative, particular affirmative, particular negative, indefinite, and singular propositions. Medieval logicians later named the main forms A, E, I, and O.

He studied opposition between propositions. A and E propositions are contraries; A and O, E and I, and singular affirmative and negative pairs are contradictories; I and O were later called subcontraries.

A syllogism ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syllogism)) is an argument in which stated premises make a distinct conclusion follow of necessity. In Aristotle's main practice, a syllogism has two premises and a conclusion, each categorical, and three terms: major, minor, and middle.

Aristotle distinguished three figures according to the middle term's position. Later logicians recognized a fourth figure. With four proposition forms and four figures, there are 256 possible patterns, but only some are valid.

Medieval names such as Barbara, Celarent, Darii, Ferio, Cesare, Camestres, Baroco, Bocardo, and others encode valid moods. Their vowels show proposition forms, and consonants give instructions for reducing moods to first-figure syllogisms.

The reduction system was a compact proof technique. Initial letters pointed to the first-figure mood used as a model; s and p marked simple or per accidens conversion; m ordered the premises differently; and c signaled indirect proof by reductio. Baroco and Bocardo were classic cases requiring that indirect route.

Aristotle's logic was a term logic. Even existence was handled through predicate terms and the organization of sciences rather than through a separate existential predicate as in modern logic.

The workflow of an Aristotelian syllogism is simple but restrictive. First, identify the conclusion's subject and predicate as the minor and major terms. Next, find the middle term shared by the two premises. Finally, check whether the arrangement of terms and the quantity and quality of the propositions match a valid mood. The method is powerful for class-inclusion claims, but it is less suited to arguments involving relations, multiple variables, or complex statements joined by “if,” “and,” and “or.”

## Theophrastus, Megarians, and Stoics

Theophrastus, Aristotle's successor, modified syllogistic and modal logic. He treated possibility as what is not impossible and adopted a rule that a modal syllogism's conclusion cannot be stronger than its weakest premise.

The Megarians studied modality and conditionals. Diodorus Cronus's Master Argument denied unrealized possibilities, while Philo of Megara gave a truth condition for conditionals close to modern material implication.

Stoic logic developed propositional logic rather than term logic. Stoics studied conditionals, conjunction, disjunction, words such as since and because, fallacies, paradoxes, and forms of refutation.

Chrysippus formulated five indemonstrable inference schemata, including forms like modus ponens and modus tollens. Stoics used variables for whole propositions and showed how complex propositions could be studied by truth-functional connectives.

The five schemata covered conditional affirmation, conditional denial, incompatibility, exclusive disjunction with one side affirmed, and disjunction with one side denied. Stoics used ordinal placeholders such as the first and the second for propositions, a major shift from Aristotle's letters standing for terms.

The contrast between term logic and propositional logic is foundational. Aristotle primarily asked how classes or predicates are related: for example, whether every member of one class belongs to another. Stoics instead treated whole statements as units and examined how their truth changes when statements are joined. This made it possible to study patterns now familiar as modus ponens and modus tollens, and it supplied an ancestor of modern reasoning with logical connectives.

Late Greek logic was preserved through commentaries and handbooks. Galen, Alexander of Aphrodisias, and others transmitted, interpreted, and sometimes confused earlier doctrines, but their works helped keep ancient logic available.

## Medieval Logic

After Boethius, Latin Europe had little logical innovation until the 11th and 12th centuries. St. Anselm used logical methods in theology, helping set the tone for scholastic reasoning.

Peter Abelard was the first major Latin logician after Boethius. His commentaries and Dialectica treated the copula, negation, modality, future contingents, conditionals, and consequences with far greater sophistication than earlier Latin work.

Medieval logic expanded Aristotle through semantics, supposition theory, obligationes, insolubles, consequences, modal reasoning, and theology. Arabic logic also preserved and developed Greek materials, influencing later work through translation.

The medieval history of logic therefore depends on transmission as well as invention. Greek logical texts survived, moved, and changed through commentary traditions, Arabic scholarship, and Latin translation. When Latin scholastic logic revived, it did not simply restart Aristotle; it inherited a layered body of problems about language, inference, necessity, contradiction, and theological reasoning. This helps explain why medieval logic could be technical and creative even while working inside inherited authorities.

Medieval logicians also made language itself a central object of study. Supposition theory asked how a term stands for things in a particular sentence, while work on consequences and insolubles examined valid inference and self-referential puzzles. These inquiries made medieval logic more than a preservation of syllogisms: they developed tools for analysing meaning, reference, modality, and apparent contradiction.

## Renaissance and Early Modern Logic

Modern logic is often said to begin after the Renaissance ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Renaissance)), but the period was diverse and chaotic. Humanists sometimes rejected scholastic logic as sterile, and figures such as Luther and Erasmus distrusted Aristotelian syllogistic.

Philipp Melanchthon offered a more balanced Lutheran logic, presenting a simplified Aristotelian dialectic. English textbooks by John Wallis, Henry Aldrich, Isaac Watts, John Wesley, Richard Whately, and John Stuart Mill kept nonsymbolic logic active.

Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz created a symbolic logic in the 1680s strikingly close to later algebraic logic, but it remained unpublished until 1903 and had little direct historical influence. His larger dream was a universal characteristic language and calculus of reason.

Gottfried Ploucquet and Johann Heinrich Lambert developed 18th-century symbolic systems inspired by Leibniz. Lambert created an elegant intensional calculus and worked on quantification, concept analysis, and symbolic method.

Immanuel Kant and G.W.F. Hegel contributed greatly to philosophy but little to formal logic. Kant treated logic as nearly complete, while Hegel opposed separating logical form from substance.

## Boole, De Morgan, and Algebraic Logic

![[attachments/logic_history_002.webp|420]]

*Portrait of George Boole*

George Boole and Augustus De Morgan were the most important British logicians of the early 19th century. Boole created a symbolic extensional algebra of classes in The Mathematical Analysis of Logic and Laws of Thought.

Boole used symbols for classes, 1 for the universe, 0 for the null class, multiplication for intersection, and addition for non-overlapping union. His system showed that logic could be treated algebraically according to laws of combination.

For a universal affirmative such as All A's are B's, Boole could write equations such as AB = B, A(1 - B) = 0, or A = vB. His slogan that validity depends on laws of combination rather than symbol interpretation helped make abstract algebraic methods available to logic.

De Morgan emphasized relations, and Boole's algebra soon evolved through changes by Peirce and Jevons, including inclusive union. These developments moved logic toward modern symbolic systems.

Charles Sanders Peirce became the first major American logician. He refined Boole, developed relational logic, used a transitive inclusion relation, worked on quantifiers, and influenced later algebraic and predicate logic.

## Frege, Schroder, Cantor, and Peano

Gottlob Frege's Begriffsschrift of 1879 introduced a rigorous first-order predicate logic with quantifiers and predicates. His notation was difficult and two-dimensional, but his logic was a major break from algebraic class logic.

Ernst Schroder developed algebraic logic in his Lectures on the Algebra of Logic. He systematized class logic, propositional logic, relational logic, inclusion, Boolean operations, and duality.

Georg Cantor developed set theory from work on infinite sets and the real number line. Cantor and Richard Dedekind used one-to-one correspondence to compare sets, and Dedekind defined infinity by correspondence with a proper part.

Giuseppe Peano created influential logical notation, including membership notation, and formulated postulates for natural numbers. These postulates became central to the idea that mathematics might be derived from logic and set theory.

Modern quantification changed what logic could express. A predicate such as “is red” represents a property, while a variable marks an unspecified individual. A universal quantifier says that a statement holds for every member of a domain; an existential quantifier says that at least one member has the property. Frege's system could therefore represent relations and nested claims that syllogistic notation handles awkwardly or not at all.

Peano's notation for quantifiers and his epsilon-like membership sign were adopted by Russell and Whitehead and became more influential than Frege's notation. His work linked symbolic clarity with the logicist hope that arithmetic could be grounded in logical or set-theoretic principles.

The congresses of 1900 in Paris helped give mathematical logic academic legitimacy. David Hilbert's list of problems focused attention on foundations, axioms, consistency, completeness, and logic's role in mathematics.

## Logic Since 1900

Modern symbolic logic unified earlier work by Frege, Peano, Cantor, Russell, Hilbert, and others. Propositional logic studies connectives such as and, or, not, if-then, and if and only if, using truth tables and truth-functions.

Predicate logic analyzes predicates, variables, identity, and quantifiers. Existential and universal quantifiers formalize claims such as something is red and everything is red. First-order logic quantifies over individuals, while higher-order logic also quantifies over properties, relations, and sets.

Bertrand Russell and Alfred North Whitehead's Principia Mathematica tried to show that mathematics rests on logic. It used higher-order logic and defined numbers through classes of equinumerous classes.

Set theory was axiomatized by Ernst Zermelo, later modified by Abraham Fraenkel and others into ZF. Axioms include extensionality, empty set, separation, power set, union, choice, and infinity. Von Neumann, Bernays, and Godel later developed related systems.

The axioms constrain set formation rather than allowing any collection whatsoever. Separation forms subsets only from already existing sets, power set gathers all subsets, union flattens sets of sets, and choice selects one member from each set in a suitable family. This iterative approach avoided earlier paradoxes.

## Completeness, Incompleteness, and Semantics

Completeness has several meanings. Descriptive completeness concerns whether axioms capture intended models; semantic completeness concerns whether all logical truths are derivable; deductive completeness concerns whether every intended truth or its negation can be formally proved.

Kurt Godel proved first-order logic semantically complete, but in 1931 he proved that elementary arithmetic is deductively incomplete. His first incompleteness theorem showed that any sufficiently strong formal arithmetic contains true but unprovable sentences.

Godel's result was relative to a formal system: strengthening a system can prove the original undecidable sentence, but a new undecidable sentence then appears. Later formulations used the diagonal lemma to show how arithmetic can construct sentences that refer to their own provability.

Completeness and incompleteness are not opposites in a single simple sense. Gödel proved that first-order logic is semantically complete: every statement that is logically true in all models can be derived by its proof rules. His incompleteness theorem concerns sufficiently strong formal theories of arithmetic, which cannot decide every arithmetical truth using their own axioms. The distinction shows why the logical framework can be complete while a mathematical theory expressed within it remains incomplete.

Godel used Godel numbers to let arithmetic encode statements about its own syntax. A sentence effectively says of itself that it is unprovable, producing a rigorous analogue of self-reference.

Rudolf Carnap developed logical syntax and semantics, while Alfred Tarski created influential truth definitions and helped found model theory. Tarski and Abraham Robinson developed model-theoretic methods for algebraic systems.

Model theory studies structures satisfying formal languages. Later work classified models as atomic, saturated, stable, and more; infinitary logics and game-theoretic semantics expanded the study of truth beyond ordinary finite formulas.

Infinitary logics allowed infinitely long conjunctions, disjunctions, and later chains of quantifiers or subformulas. Because ordinary Tarski-style truth definitions rely on minimal atomic formulas, these logics encouraged noncompositional truth definitions and selection-game approaches to semantics.

Modern logic also divided into several connected subfields. Proof theory studies formal derivations, consistency, and the strength of axioms. Recursion theory studies computability and effective procedures. Set theory studies infinity, hierarchy, choice, and foundations. Model theory studies the relation between formal languages and structures. Together these fields turned logic into a central part of mathematics as well as philosophy.

The history of logic therefore moves from argument forms in debate to symbolic systems capable of analyzing mathematics itself. Ancient syllogisms, Stoic propositional schemata, medieval semantics, Boolean algebra, Fregean quantification, and Godelian metamathematics all ask what follows from what, but they answer with increasingly precise tools.

## Core Equations

Formal logic expresses structure through symbols, sets, functions, or logical relations:

$$f:X\to Y$$

This notation represents a function that maps every member of one set to a member of another set.

$$A\subseteq B \quad \Rightarrow \quad (x\in A \Rightarrow x\in B)$$

This states the meaning of subset: every member of A is also a member of B.

## Key Events & Developments

- 5th century BCE: Zeno used reductio-style paradoxes.
- 4th century BCE: Aristotle created systematic syllogistic logic.
- 3rd century BCE: Stoics developed propositional inference schemata.
- 1079-1142: Peter Abelard revived and advanced Latin logic.
- 1680s: Leibniz developed unpublished symbolic logic.
- 1847: Boole published The Mathematical Analysis of Logic.
- 1879: Frege published Begriffsschrift.
- 1900: Paris congresses and Hilbert's problems highlighted foundations.
- 1910-1913: Russell and Whitehead published Principia Mathematica.
- 1931: Godel proved the first incompleteness theorem.
