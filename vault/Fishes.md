## Introduction

Fishes ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fish)) are cold-blooded vertebrates of the phylum Chordata ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chordate)), with more than 30,000 species living in fresh water and salt water throughout the world. The word “fish” describes a life-form rather than a strict biological group, because living fishes belong to about five distinct classes, as different from one another as amphibians, reptiles, birds, and mammals. As chordates they share gill slits at some stage, a notochord, a dorsal hollow nerve cord, and a tail. These lines include jawless fishes such as lampreys and hagfishes, cartilaginous fishes such as sharks and rays, and the large and diverse group of bony fishes, which range from the tiny sea horse to the 450-kilogram blue marlin.

Fishes are important to humans for several reasons. They are a moderate but important part of the world’s food supply, they help control disease by eating mosquito larvae that carry malaria, and they are valuable in medical and biological research. The scientific study of fishes is called ichthyology. Overfishing, pollution, and environmental change threaten fish populations and complicate fisheries management.

Their diversity reflects the variety of aquatic habitats. Rivers, lakes, coral reefs, estuaries, open oceans, deep seas, polar waters, and temporary pools impose different demands on buoyancy, respiration, salinity balance, feeding, reproduction, and movement. Fishes therefore show many solutions to life in water rather than one standard body plan.

Fish behaviour is shaped by inherited traits, learning, and environmental stimuli. Fish responses are more limited than human responses and should not be interpreted too anthropomorphically. Fishes use sight, smell, hearing, touch, taste, and the lateral line system to detect their surroundings. Some species can also use electric fields for perception.

The main behavioural activities of fishes are feeding, reproduction, and escaping predators. Schooling can protect fish from enemies, while long-distance swimming and quick bursts of speed help others capture prey or avoid danger. ## Locomotion

Fish locomotion is strongly related to habitat. Surface swimmers are often streamlined and adapted for quick attacks on insects or other fish. Open-water species such as tunas and trouts are built for powerful, fast movement, while fishes in quiet waters are usually better at short bursts than prolonged pursuit. Bottom-living fishes are generally slower swimmers, and many plankton feeders remain streamlined for rapid movement.

The body plan of fishes has been repeatedly modified through evolution. Their muscles, tail, and fin arrangement all contribute to propulsion, while the skeleton supports movement and protects vital organs.

## Reproduction

Most fishes reproduce by laying many small eggs, often outside the body. The eggs of open-water fishes float in the water, whereas many shore and freshwater species place them on the bottom or among plants. Mortality is very high at the egg and young stages, so only a small fraction of the offspring survive to maturity.

Fishes vary in the anatomy of the reproductive organs in both males and females. In some fishes fertilization is internal, and some species give birth to live young. These live-bearers include members of several families of bony fishes and sharks, and their young are relatively large and few in number.

## Skin and body structure

The skin of fishes is a protective covering and is also important in colouration. Many fishes blend into their surroundings, while others use bright colors for territorial advertisement or as recognition marks. Colour is produced in pigment cells called chromatophores: black melanophores placed beneath silvery iridocytes create structural blues and greens by refracting light, while erythrophores holding carotenoid pigments produce yellow, orange, and red. Many fishes can change colour by moving pigment within these cells.

The body musculature of fishes is mainly trunk muscle arranged in rows of chevron-shaped segments, with smaller fin muscles. The tail, or caudal, fin is the most powerful because it is driven by the body muscles. Contractions pass in successive waves from head to tail, bending the body at the vertebral joints and producing the driving strokes that propel the fish forward.

## Osmoregulation and senses

Fishes must maintain water balance in very different environments. Because seawater is saltier than their body fluids, marine fishes tend to lose water by osmosis and so drink large quantities of seawater, retaining most of the water and excreting the salt, mainly as ammonia through the gills. Freshwater fishes face the opposite problem: they absorb water through the skin, drink very little, and have relatively large kidneys that excrete the excess, while special cells in the gills and mouth actively absorb salt against the diffusion gradient.

The senses of fishes are highly developed. Smell is important for many species, especially those with reduced vision. Taste buds occur not only in the mouth but also in the skin and around the head. Sight is also crucial in most fishes, although some species rely more heavily on smell or touch. Hearing is well developed for low-frequency sounds, and the lateral line system lets fishes detect pressure and water movement.
These systems is adaptations to life in water. Vision changes with depth and clarity, smell can guide migration and feeding, and the lateral line detects vibrations from prey, predators, currents, and nearby objects. Together, these senses allow fishes to navigate environments where light, pressure, salinity, and movement differ sharply from place to place.

## Evolution and paleontology

Fish evolution is long, complex, and only partly known from fossils. The broad pattern has been a continuous replacement of older groups by newer, better-adapted forms. New species often evolved improved feeding, breathing, or swimming methods and then displaced older groups in the same habitats.

The earliest known vertebrate fossils are fragments of armour from jawless fishes of the Upper Ordovician in North America, about 450 million years old. Later groups appeared in different periods: chimaeras of the class Holocephali first appeared in the Late Devonian, and fleshy-finned sarcopterygians appeared in Lower Devonian strata of Germany. Only one of the seven known chimaera orders survived past the Cretaceous Period, about 65.5 million years ago. That some ancient lineages, such as the coelacanths and lungfishes, survived in small numbers while the related rhipidistians died out near the Permian.
The history of fishes is also the history of vertebrate innovation. Jaws, paired fins, stronger skeletons, improved respiration, and more efficient swimming all changed what fishes could eat, where they could live, and how they competed. These innovations helped later vertebrate evolution as well.

## Classification

The classification of fishes has changed often, and ichthyologists still disagree about some relationships. The skeleton is especially useful in classification because fossils usually preserve only bones, and living fish skeletons are easy to study. External features, behaviour, physiology, and genetics are also used.

The classification divides fishes into jawless forms, cartilaginous fishes, and bony fishes. It includes major groups such as lampreys, hagfishes, sharks, rays, chimaeras, sturgeons, bowfins, teleosts, lungfishes, and many orders of ray-finned fishes.
