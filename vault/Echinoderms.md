# Echinoderms

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> Echinoderms are marine animals including sea stars, sea urchins, and sea cucumbers, known for radial symmetry, a water vascular system, and important roles in seafloor ecosystems.

## Introduction

Echinoderms ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Echinoderm)) are marine invertebrates of the phylum Echinodermata. Their name means spiny skin, because many forms have a hard, spiny outer covering. The group reaches back to the start of the Cambrian Period, about 542 million to 488 million years ago, and includes both living classes and many extinct fossil groups.

Living echinoderms include sea lilies, feather stars, sea urchins, sea cucumbers, starfishes, brittle stars, serpent stars, basket stars, and sea daisies. They share a calcite skeleton, a water-vascular system, and usually five-part adult symmetry. More than 6,500 living species and about 13,000 fossil species are known.

## General features

![[attachments/echinoderms_001.webp|420]]

*Echinoderm collage 2*

Echinoderm bodies vary greatly. Sea lilies can look like stalked flowers, sea cucumbers are long and soft, sea urchins are round and armoured, and sea stars have arms around a central disk. Their common features are mainly internal and skeletal.

### Size range and diversity of structure

Most echinoderms are small, often under 10 centimetres long or wide. Some sea cucumbers reach about two metres, some starfishes reach about one metre across, and extinct crinoids could have stems more than 20 metres long.

### Distribution and abundance

Echinoderms ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Echinoderm)) live in marine waters worldwide, though the Arctic has relatively few species. Seashores commonly have sea urchins, sand dollars, and sea stars. Species with planktonic larvae may spread widely because currents carry the larvae long distances.

## Importance

Echinoderms ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Echinoderm)) shape seafloor communities and affect human life. They serve as predators, scavengers, sediment processors, food animals, toxin producers, pests, and research organisms.

### Role in nature

As scavengers and predators, echinoderms remove decaying matter and regulate small organisms. Sea urchins can damage seagrass beds, but some also protect coral reefs by grazing seaweed that would otherwise overgrow corals.

Sea cucumbers ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sea_cucumber)) swallow sediment, digest organic matter, and release the rest, changing the seafloor. Burrowing starfishes, sand dollars, and heart urchins mix sediments, sometimes deeper than 30 centimetres. Echinoderm larvae also feed other planktonic organisms.

### Relation to human life

Some sea cucumbers are dried as trepang or beche-de-mer and used in soups, especially in Asia. Sea urchin gonads are eaten in parts of Europe, the Mediterranean region, Japan, and Chile.

Some holothurians produce holothurin, a toxin used by Pacific islanders to poison water and catch fish. Starfishes may damage oyster culture, and crown-of-thorns starfish can harm coral reefs by eating coral polyps.

## Reproduction and life cycle

![[attachments/echinoderms_003.webp|420]]

*Reconstruction of Marsupites testudinarius*

Most echinoderms have separate sexes and reproduce sexually by releasing eggs and sperm into seawater. Some sea cucumbers, starfishes, and brittle stars also reproduce asexually.

### Asexual reproduction

Asexual reproduction usually happens by fragmentation. A body part breaks away and regenerates missing structures. This ability is linked to the broader echinoderm power to replace lost arms or body regions.

### Sexual reproduction

In sexual reproduction, fertilization usually occurs outside the body. Some females release millions of eggs. Other species, especially in Antarctic, Arctic, and deep-sea settings, brood young inside the body, near the mouth, under the body, or in special pouches.

## Development

Development may be indirect or direct. Small eggs with little yolk usually become free-swimming feeding larvae, then metamorphose into juveniles on the seafloor. Larger yolky eggs may develop into nonfeeding larvae or directly into juveniles.

During indirect embryonic development, the egg forms a blastula and then a gastrula. Echinoderms are deuterostomes, like vertebrates, because the blastopore is linked with the future anus rather than the mouth.

## Life activities

Echinoderm activities include feeding, movement, righting, burrowing, defense, habitat choice, and aggregation. Tube feet, spines, arms, tentacles, skeleton, body-wall contraction, and the water-vascular system all support these behaviours.

### Food and feeding habits

Feeding ranges from suspension feeding and browsing to predation, scavenging, deposit feeding, and mud swallowing. Crinoids catch suspended food with arms, cilia, and tube feet. Many brittle stars capture small organisms with arms and tube feet.

Many starfishes prey on shellfish or other animals, and some push the stomach outside the body to begin digestion. Sea urchins scrape algae or collect buried food, while sea cucumbers catch plankton, select bottom particles, or swallow mud for organic matter.

### Locomotion

Locomotion in sea stars and sea urchins uses tube feet and spines, so they can move in several directions without turning around. Brittle stars row or thrash with arms, and sea cucumbers move with tube feet, tentacles, and body contractions.

Crinoids differ by form. Sea lilies are usually attached by holdfasts, though some detach. Feather stars can swim by beating their arms in coordinated sets or crawl by pulling with them.

### Righting response

The righting response lets an overturned echinoderm return to normal position. Tube feet, arms, spines, and body movements restore the feeding surface and mouth orientation needed for movement, feeding, and defense.

### Burrowing

Many echinoderms burrow into rock or soft sediment. Some urchins grind into rock with spines and teeth. Sand dollars stay near the surface, while some heart urchins occur 38 centimetres or more below it.

Sea cucumbers ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sea_cucumber)) burrow with tentacles and body-wall contractions. Some starfishes bury themselves, and several brittle stars leave only arm tips exposed so they can feed while the body remains protected.

## Habitats

Echinoderms ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Echinoderm)) are exclusively marine. Only a few tolerate brackish water, and a few tropical sea cucumbers survive brief drying when stranded by a falling tide. Most avoid strong changes in salinity, temperature, and light.

Habitats include rocky shores, sand, mud, offshore ooze, coral reefs, deep trenches, and waterlogged wood on the deep-sea floor. In the deepest trenches, holothurians may form more than 90 percent by weight of living organisms.

### Predation and defense

Predation affects all major groups. Brittle stars are eaten by fishes and some starfishes; other echinoderms are eaten by fishes, crustaceans, mollusks, sea birds, and humans.

Defense includes spines, hard plates, pedicellariae, toxins, sticky tissues, hiding, burrowing, aggregation, and regeneration after arm loss.

## Aggregation

Aggregation is common in living echinoderms and appears in fossil beds from the Lower Cambrian. Brittle stars, sea urchins, sea cucumbers, crinoids, and some starfishes may gather in large numbers.

Food availability is often the main cause. Brittle stars and crinoids gather where currents bring plankton. Grouping can also help animals resist currents, reproduce successfully, and perhaps reduce predation.

## Form and function of external features

External form is tied to skeleton, tube feet, arms, spines, and symmetry. These structures help movement, feeding, respiration, sensation, attachment, and defense.

### Skeletal composition

The skeleton is made of calcium carbonate plates in the form of calcite. It may form flexible armour, a rigid test, arm supports, stalk pieces, spines, or tiny internal spicules.

### Symmetry and body form

Early echinoderms either lacked clear symmetry or were bilaterally symmetrical. Radial symmetry developed early and became superimposed on the bilateral plan. Most living forms show five-rayed symmetry, though body shape varies widely.

### Skeleton

The echinoderm skeleton lies in the dermis and is built from ossicles. Each ossicle has living stroma and a calcite lattice called stereom, which makes fossil fragments recognizable. Echinoids have a rigid test and Aristotle's lantern; holothurians usually reduce the skeleton to tiny spicules.

## Form and function of internal features

Internal features include the water-vascular system, body wall, coelom, digestive canal, hemal spaces, axial organ, nervous system, sense organs, and reproductive organs. These systems rely on canals, cavities, and repeated body parts rather than a vertebrate-like central plan.

### Water-vascular system

The water-vascular system is a defining echinoderm feature. It is a hydraulic system of canals and reservoirs filled with watery fluid, and it operates tube feet used for movement, feeding, attachment, respiration, and sensing.

### Body wall and body cavity

The body wall usually has cilia that move food particles toward the mouth or remove unwanted particles. The coelom is the main body cavity, and part of it forms the water-vascular system.

### Alimentary and blood systems

The digestive canal is basically a tube, but its form changes with feeding style. The hemal system consists of blood-related spaces rather than a closed vertebrate-like circulation.

### Axial organ

The axial organ is an elongated tissue mass found in all echinoderms except holothurians. It forms a junction between the perivisceral coelom, water-vascular system, and hemal system.

### Nervous system and sense organs

The [[Nerves and Nervous Systems (Nervous System)|nervous system]] is complex but decentralized. A nerve plexus lies within and below the skin, and sensory responses guide light avoidance, touch response, feeding, righting, movement, and habitat choice.

### Reproductive system

The gonads differ by group. Crinoids keep gonads outside the main body cavity in arm or pinnule spaces, probably because the main cavity is reduced. Broadcast spawners produce many gametes, while brooders protect fewer young.

## Paleontology and evolution

Echinoderm evolution is difficult to reconstruct because the group was already diverse by the Lower Cambrian. Its earlier history must have begun before the main fossil record.

### Extinct echinoderms

Extinct echinoderms include asymmetrical carpoids, stalked blastozoans, crinoid forms, early star-shaped forms, edrioasteroids, helicoplacoids, and other fossil groups. A Precambrian fossil from Australia has three-rayed symmetry and resembles an edrioasteroid.

### Extant echinoderms

Relationships among living classes remain debated. Scientists compare larvae, embryology, adult anatomy, skeletal traits, and fossils to connect crinoids, sea stars, brittle stars, sea urchins, sea cucumbers, and sea daisies.

## Classification

Classification ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taxonomy)) changed greatly during the 1970s and 1980s, and disagreement remains. The scheme here uses five subphyla: Homalozoa, Blastozoa, Crinozoa, Asterozoa, and Echinozoa.

### Distinguishing taxonomic features

Homalozoa are asymmetrical. Blastozoa are stalked with simple feeding apparatus. Crinozoa are stalked with complex feeding apparatus. Asterozoa are star-shaped. Echinozoa are globoid to discoid and lack outspread arms or brachioles.

### Annotated classification

The older Pelmatozoa and Eleutherozoa division described life habit rather than evolutionary history. Pelmatozoans attached to the seafloor for part of life, while eleutherozoans moved freely, but these categories mix unrelated forms.

#### Phylum Echinodermata (echinoderms)

Phylum Echinodermata includes worldwide marine invertebrates with calcite skeletons, five-part symmetry in most fossils and all living representatives, and a water-vascular system formed from the coelom.

#### Subphylum Homalozoa (carpoids)

Homalozoa, or carpoids, were asymmetrical fossil echinoderms from the Middle Cambrian to Middle Devonian, about 570 million to 365 million years ago.

##### Class Stylophora

Stylophora lived from the Middle Cambrian to the Upper Ordovician, about 540 million to 460 million years ago. It had one feeding arm, sometimes interpreted as a stem.

##### Class Homostelea

Homostelea is known from the Middle Cambrian, about 540 million years ago. It had no feeding arm and had a stem made mainly of two plate series.

##### Class Homoiostelea

Homoiostelea ranged from the Upper Cambrian to the Lower Devonian, about 510 million to 400 million years ago. It had a feeding arm and a complex stem.

##### Class Ctenocystoidea

Ctenocystoidea lived in the Middle Cambrian, about 540 million years ago. It lacked a stem and feeding arm but had a grill-like feeding apparatus around the mouth.

#### Subphylum Blastozoa (blastozoans)

Blastozoa were stalked echinoderms from the Cambrian to the Permian, about 540 million to 280 million years ago. A globular theca enclosed their soft parts, and simple brachioles gathered food.

##### Class Eocrinoidea

Eocrinoidea lived from the Lower Cambrian to the Silurian, about 570 million to 430 million years ago. Its body usually had a stem, theca, and feeding brachioles.

##### Class Blastoidea

Blastoidea ranged from the Silurian to the Permian, about 430 million to 280 million years ago. It had a stem, many feeding brachioles, and hydrospires in the theca.

##### Class Paracrinoidea

Paracrinoidea is known from the Middle Ordovician, about 460 million years ago. It had a stem, theca, arms with pinnules, and a unique pore system.

##### Class Parablastoidea

Parablastoidea lived from the Lower to Middle Ordovician, about 500 million to 460 million years ago. It resembled Blastoidea but differed in ambulacra and thecal plates.

##### Class Rhombifera

Rhombifera ranged from the Lower Ordovician to the Upper Devonian, about 500 million to 350 million years ago. It had a globular theca and rhomboid respiratory structures.

##### Class Diploporita

Diploporita lived from the Lower Ordovician to the Lower Devonian, about 500 million to 400 million years ago. It had a globular theca and paired respiratory pores.

#### Subphylum Crinozoa

Crinozoa includes fossil and living forms from the Lower Ordovician, about 500 million years ago, to the present. Members have five-part symmetry, a theca, and complex feeding arms.

##### Class Crinoidea (sea lilies and feather stars)

Crinoidea includes sea lilies and feather stars from the Lower Ordovician to the present. Forms may have or lose a stem, and branching arms with pinnules gather suspended food.

#### Subphylum Asterozoa

Asterozoa includes fossil and living forms from the Lower Ordovician to the present. These echinoderms are radially symmetrical and star-shaped.

##### Class Stelleroidea

Stelleroidea represents the star-shaped asterozoan organization, with radial symmetry and arms extending from a central body region.

##### Class Somasteroidea

Somasteroidea lived from the Lower Ordovician to the Upper Devonian, about 500 million to 350 million years ago. It resembled Asteroidea but lacked tube-foot grooves.

##### Class Asteroidea(starfishes or sea stars)

Asteroidea includes sea stars from the Middle Ordovician, about 460 million years ago, to the present. About 1,800 living species have broad hollow arms and tube feet in oral grooves.

##### Class Ophiuroidea(brittle stars or serpent stars)

Ophiuroidea includes brittle stars and serpent stars from the Ordovician to the present. About 2,000 living species have a distinct disk and long solid arms.

##### Class Concentricycloidea(sea daisies)

Concentricycloidea, or sea daisies, have flattened disk-shaped bodies without obvious arms. Their water-vascular canals form a double ring.

#### Subphylum Echinozoa

Echinozoa includes fossil and living forms from the Lower Cambrian to the present. They are globoid, cylindrical, or disk-shaped and lack outspread arms or brachioles.

##### Class Cyclocystoidea

Cyclocystoidea lived from the Middle Ordovician to the Middle Devonian, about 460 million to 375 million years ago. It was small, disk-shaped, and had branching ambulacra.

##### Class Edrioasteroidea

Edrioasteroidea ranged from the Lower Cambrian to the Lower Carboniferous, about 570 million to 340 million years ago. It had five food grooves radiating from a central mouth.

##### Class Edrioblastoidea

Edrioblastoidea is known from the Middle Ordovician, about 375 million years ago. It was stalked, with a spheroidal theca and five food grooves.

##### Class Helicoplacoidea

Helicoplacoidea lived in the Lower Cambrian, about 570 million years ago. It had a pear-shaped or spindle-shaped body with spirally arranged plates.

##### Class Ophiocistioidea

Ophiocistioidea ranged from the Lower Ordovician to the Upper Silurian, about 500 million to 395 million years ago. It had a dome-shaped test and very large plated tube feet.

##### Class Holothuroidea (sea cucumbers)

Holothuroidea includes sea cucumbers from the Ordovician to the present. The body is elongated, the mouth has feeding tentacles, and the skeleton is usually reduced to microscopic spicules.

##### Class Echinoidea (sea urchins, heart urchins, sand dollars)

Echinoidea includes sea urchins, heart urchins, and sand dollars from the Ordovician to the present. They have a complete test of interlocking plates with movable spines and pedicellariae.

### Critical appraisal

Echinoderm classification remains disputed. Some scientists emphasize living anatomy and embryology because fossil forms differ from modern forms, while others give fossils a strong role because extinct skeletons preserve many lost body plans.

## Key Events & Developments

- Precambrian: Echinoderm history likely began before the main fossil record.
- Lower Cambrian: Echinoderms ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Echinoderm)) were already diverse, and early fossil beds show aggregation.
- Ordovician: Major lines leading to crinoids, sea stars, brittle stars, sea cucumbers, and sea urchins appear.
- 1970s and 1980s: Classification ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taxonomy)) changed greatly as scientists reassessed fossil, skeletal, anatomical, and developmental evidence.
