# Chemical Bonding

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> Chemical bonding explains how atoms lower energy by forming ionic, covalent, metallic, and intermolecular interactions that determine molecular shape, polarity, reactivity, and solid-state properties. Lewis structures, valence-bond theory, molecular-orbital theory, and ligand-field models provide complementary accounts of electron distribution and structure.

## Introduction

Chemical bonding ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemical_bond)) is the set of forces that hold atoms together in molecules, ions, crystals, and other stable species. Bonds form because atoms can lower their total energy when they come together, and the energy released in this process is the bonding energy ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bond_energy)). The modern understanding of bonding grew after the discovery of the electron and the development of quantum mechanics ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_mechanics)), but chemists still use simple models because they are practical and often predictive.

Bonding is a central idea in chemistry because it connects atomic structure, molecular shape, and chemical reactivity. No single simple model explains all bonding, so understanding moves through a series of progressively richer approaches, from valence ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Valence_%28chemistry%29)) and Lewis structures ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lewis_structure)) to valence bond theory and molecular orbital theory.

The simplest models remain useful because they predict many formulas, shapes, and properties without requiring full quantum-mechanical calculation.

Different models answer different questions: Lewis structures track valence electrons, valence bond theory emphasizes localized pairs and hybrid orbitals, and molecular orbital theory explains delocalization, magnetism, and spectra. Bonding is therefore best understood as a toolkit rather than a single picture.

## Valence, Ionic Bonding, and Covalent Bonding

![[chemical_bonding_001.webp|420]]

*Diagram of covalent bonding in methane*

The older concept of valence ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Valence_%28chemistry%29)) helped chemists see patterns in the compounds elements form. Hydrogen was used as a probe, and elements such as oxygen, nitrogen, and chlorine were assigned typical valences of 2, 3, and 1. Some elements, especially carbon, show more than one common valence, which made valence useful but incomplete as an explanation.

Early chemistry also separated compounds into electrolytes and nonelectrolytes, which led to the idea that there are two major bond types. Electrolytes conduct electricity in water because they produce ions, while nonelectrolytes do not. This distinction supports the contrast between ionic compounds, where electrons are effectively transferred and ions are formed, and covalent compounds, where atoms share electron pairs.

This distinction maps onto the periodic table. Metals on the left tend to form cations, while nonmetals on the right tend to form anions or share electrons. The noble gases are important because their reluctance to bond became a clue that helped later theories explain why atoms sometimes do not react.

The discovery that krypton and xenon can form compounds changed the phrase inert gas to noble gas, emphasizing chemical aloofness rather than complete impossibility of bonding.

## Molecular Structure and Shape

The reality of atoms and molecules became more convincing as chemists learned to infer molecular structure. Structure has two dimensions: a topological one, which tells which atoms are linked to which, and a geometric one, which tells how atoms are arranged in space. The example of dichloromethane shows why a molecule with a given formula may still have only one real three-dimensional arrangement.

That idea matters because molecular shape affects properties and reactions. Water is a major example: if the molecule were linear instead of angular, the physical behaviour of water would be very different. For large molecules such as proteins, shape is essential to function, so bonding and geometry are not separate topics but parts of the same chemical problem.

## Lewis Structures and Resonance

Lewis structures ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lewis_structure)) give a simple picture of bonding by counting valence electrons and arranging them into bonding pairs and lone pairs. This method works for molecules such as hydrogen chloride, water, and carbon tetrachloride. In this framework, a single bond is a shared electron pair, multiple bonds are double or triple shared pairs, and the octet rule is a guiding principle for many main-group elements.

Lewis structures can be refined by resonance. In molecules such as ozone and benzene, no single Lewis drawing is fully adequate, so the real structure is described as a blend of contributing forms. Resonance spreads bonding character across several atoms and helps explain equal bond lengths, unusual stability, and the distribution of electron density.

Resonance is not rapid switching between drawings but a quantum-mechanical blending that gives the actual molecule one delocalized structure.

## Hypervalence and Electron Deficiency

Lewis theory works well for many molecules, but several important exceptions remain. One class is hypervalent compounds, in which the central atom has more than eight electrons around it. Sulfur hexafluoride is a clear example, and such compounds are common from period 3 onward, even though the old octet rule seems to fail there.

Another class is electron-deficient compounds, especially diborane. In this case, there are not enough electrons to form all the ordinary two-centre bonds that a simple Lewis picture would require. These exceptions show that bonding is richer than the octet rule alone suggests and that a better theory must explain both overfilled and underfilled electron shells.

## The Quantum Mechanics of Bonding

The quantum-mechanical basis of bonding starts from atomic orbitals. These orbitals have different shapes and energies, with s, p, and d orbitals behaving differently at the nucleus and around it. These differences help explain the periodic table, electron configurations, and the ways atoms can bond.

The building-up principle describes how electrons fill orbitals in the lowest-energy arrangement. Hydrogen, helium, lithium, and later atoms show how the Pauli exclusion principle and orbital ordering determine electron configurations. This framework is essential because bonding depends first on the available valence electrons and the orbitals they occupy.

The shapes of atomic orbitals also matter: s orbitals can place electron density at the nucleus, while p and d orbitals have directional lobes that influence bonding geometry.

## Valence Bond Theory

Valence bond theory ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Valence_bond_theory)) explains a bond as the overlap of atomic orbitals from two atoms, with the paired electrons lowered in energy by constructive overlap in the internuclear region. The theory traces to Walter Heitler and Fritz London, with later development by John Slater and Linus Pauling. In this view, bonds are localized between atoms, and the old Lewis electron-pair picture gains a quantum-mechanical basis.

Methane and ethylene show how the theory works in practice. In methane, equivalent bonds are explained through hybridization, while in ethylene a double bond is described as one sigma bond and one pi bond. The same language also explains why double bonds restrict rotation and give molecules torsional rigidity.

## Hybridization

Hybridization mixes atomic orbitals on the same atom to create new orbitals suited to bonding. sp3 hybridization is the origin of the four equivalent bonds in methane, where the carbon atom uses four equivalent orbitals directed toward the corners of a tetrahedron. This resolves the mismatch between a naive orbital picture and the observed equality of the bonds.

sp2 hybridization applies to ethylene and sp arrangements to linear species such as acetylene. These patterns help explain why ethylene is planar and why acetylene is linear. Hybridization is one of the most useful concepts in everyday chemical language, even though it is part of a simplified model.

## Molecular Orbital Theory

Molecular orbital theory ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Molecular_orbital_theory)) is the alternative quantum approach to bonding. In this theory, atomic orbitals combine to form molecular orbitals that extend over the whole molecule. Bonding and antibonding combinations arise from constructive and destructive interference, and electrons occupy the lowest available orbitals according to familiar filling rules.

The theory explains the stability of H2 and the non-existence of He2. It also explains the electronic structure of N2 and O2, including the fact that oxygen is paramagnetic because its electrons occupy two separate antibonding orbitals with parallel spins. MO theory is the main computationally useful framework for chemistry.

## Polarity and Intermolecular Forces

The polarity of a bond depends on differences in electronegativity. Electronegative atoms pull shared electrons toward themselves, producing partial charges and polar bonds. As the electronegativity difference increases, the bond moves along a continuum from nonpolar covalent through polar covalent toward ionic character.

This extends to whole molecules. Carbon dioxide has polar bonds but is nonpolar overall because the bond dipoles cancel in its linear geometry, while water is polar because its angular shape prevents cancellation. This explains why polarity matters for liquid water, solution chemistry, and many biological interactions.

## Intermolecular Interactions

Bonding within molecules is only part of the story. Weaker forces also act between molecules: dipole-dipole attraction, dipole-induced-dipole attraction, London dispersion effects, and hydrogen bonding. These forces explain why some substances condense easily, why some gases liquefy at low temperature, and why certain molecules assemble into characteristic structures.

Hydrogen bonding receives special attention because of its importance in water, proteins, and DNA. The links between peptide groups help determine secondary structures such as helices and pleated sheets, and hydrogen bonds also help pair bases in the DNA double helix. Intermolecular attraction is thus connected directly to the chemistry of life.

## Varieties of Solids

![[chemical_bonding_002.webp|420]]

*Crystal structure of sodium chloride with coordination polyhedra*

Solids fall into several major types. Ionic solids consist of cations and anions packed to minimize energy, and their properties reflect strong electrostatic attraction, high melting points, and brittleness. Molecular solids are made of whole molecules held together by intermolecular forces, while covalent-network solids such as diamond are held together by extended covalent bonding.

Graphite shows how bonding can vary directionally in a solid. It is layered, with strong bonding within sheets and weak forces between sheets, which explains its lubricating properties and anisotropic conductivity. Solid structure follows from the same bonding principles that explain isolated molecules.

## Metals and Bands

Metals are described as lattices of positive ions embedded in a sea of mobile valence electrons. This model explains metallic lustre, malleability, and good electrical and thermal conductivity. Band theory follows, where many atomic orbitals combine into closely spaced molecular orbitals that form energy bands when the number of atoms becomes very large.

Sodium is a good example: the separation between orbitals becomes so small that the set behaves like a continuum. A band gap between the s and p bands helps explain metallic and nonmetallic behaviour. This connects the chemistry of bonding to the physics of solids and electronic conductivity.

## Crystal-Field and Ligand-Field Theory

Complexes of transition metals come next. Crystal-field theory explains the splitting of d orbitals in an octahedral arrangement of ligands, and the balance between crystal-field splitting and pairing energy determines the electron configuration, magnetism, and some spectroscopic properties. This explains why some complexes are high-spin and others low-spin.

Ligand-field theory is a more realistic MO-based version of crystal-field theory. In this view, ligand orbitals and metal d orbitals combine into molecular orbitals whose energies determine the properties of complexes. This approach better accounts for delocalization and can be extended to more complicated bonding situations.

## Special Bonding Cases and Computation

Special bonding cases include three-centre, two-electron bonds in diborane and the bonding in boranes and related clusters. Metal cluster compounds also appear, including species with direct metal-metal bonds and rare quadruple bonds, where one component is a face-to-face d-orbital overlap.

Computational chemistry rounds out the picture. Semiempirical methods rely partly on experimental input, while ab initio methods start from the Schrödinger equation and try to derive molecular structure from first principles. Qualitative models, experimental observation, and numerical calculation all work together in the modern understanding of bonding.

## Conclusion

Chemical bonding is a continuum of related ideas rather than a single doctrine. Lewis structures ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lewis_structure)), VB theory, MO theory, and the theories of solids and complexes all capture different aspects of the same underlying reality: atoms interact in ways that lower energy, and the consequences appear in structure, stability, polarity, magnetism, and reactivity.

## Core Equations

Bond polarity can be related to electronegativity difference, while bond energy reflects reaction energetics:

$$\Delta E_{\text{bond}} = E_{\text{broken}} - E_{\text{formed}}$$

This compares the energy required to break bonds with the energy released when new bonds form.

Ionic bonding can be represented by electron transfer:

$$\mathrm{Na \rightarrow Na^+ + e^-}$$

Read the left side as the reactants or starting materials and the right side as the products; the coefficients show the proportions needed to conserve atoms or charge.

$$\mathrm{Cl + e^- \rightarrow Cl^-}$$

## Key Events & Developments

- 19th century: Valence and early electrolyte theory organize the first broad patterns of bonding.
- early 20th century: Electron theory and quantum mechanics ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_mechanics)) make a deeper theory of bonding possible.
- 1927: Heitler and [[London]] launch modern valence bond theory.
- 1927: Mulliken and Hund introduce molecular orbital theory.
- 1940s: Powell and Sidgwick develop the roots of VSEPR theory.
- 1960s: Gillespie and Nyholm refine VSEPR theory.
