## Introduction

Beirut ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beirut)) is the capital, chief port, and largest city of Lebanon. It is a city of contrasts: cosmopolitan and provincial, liberal and sectarian, Westernized before 1975 and deeply damaged by civil war afterward. It remained the country's commercial and cultural center even after years of destruction.

## Landscape and Climate

![[attachments/beirut_001.webp|420]]

*Beirut Corniche on the Mediterranean coast*

Beirut sits on two hills, Al-Ashrafiyah and Al-Musaytibah, that project into the sea as a triangular peninsula. A narrow coastal plain lies behind it. This setting explains both the city's strategic importance and its limited space for expansion.

## Urban Form and Social Life

Under the Ottoman and French administrations Beirut was planned more systematically, but after independence growth became rapid and often haphazard. The result was a city filled with high-rises, apartments, villas, slums, and traditional houses placed side by side, with the old downtown badly damaged during the civil war.

## Economy and Urban Decline

The civil war shattered the downtown commercial core and forced businesses to move into other parts of the city. Yet economic activity never stopped entirely. Industry, home production, and especially publishing continued to serve Lebanese and wider Arab markets even during the conflict years.

## Early History

Beirut's ancient name, linked to Canaanite Beerot, refers to wells still used for water. The city appears in Egyptian records and became important as the Roman Colonia Julia Augusta Felix Berytus. Under Rome it was a fashionable residential city and later famous for its law school.

## Arab, Crusader, and Mamluk Rule

Muslim rulers rebuilt Beirut as a small fortified town administered from Damascus. Maritime trade revived in the 10th century, especially under the Fatimids, and the Crusaders seized the city in 1110. Beirut then became a crusader fief and a trading outpost linked to Italian merchants.

## Ottoman and Mandate Periods

Under the Ottomans Beirut expanded as a commercial and intellectual center, especially in the 19th century. Missionary schools, printing presses, and new educational institutions helped create a modern Arabic intellectual class, and Beirut became a leading center for journalism and publishing.

## Civil War and Fragmentation

The Lebanese civil war of 1975-90 is the decisive catastrophe of modern Beirut. The city was split by the Green Line, downtown was destroyed, neighborhoods were occupied by refugees and squatters, and basic services became unreliable. Palestinian resistance organizations, sectarian militias, Syrian troops, and Israeli intervention all shaped the conflict.

## Reconstruction

![[attachments/beirut_002.webp|420]]

*Downtown Beirut after reconstruction*

After the war, Beirut undertook major reconstruction of infrastructure, transport, and historic quarters. This is a difficult but hopeful effort to rebuild the economic and cultural heart of Lebanon after years of division.

## Conclusion

Beirut ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beirut)) is a city repeatedly remade by trade, empire, war, and reconstruction. Its defining themes are cosmopolitan culture, sectarian tension, and a remarkable persistence that allows it to survive repeated destruction.
