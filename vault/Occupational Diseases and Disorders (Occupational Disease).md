# Occupational Diseases and Disorders (Occupational Disease)

## Introduction

Occupational diseases ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Occupational_disease)) are illnesses associated with particular occupations or industries. They arise from biological, chemical, physical, and psychological factors in the work environment or encountered during employment. Occupational medicine studies both the effect of work on health and the effect of health on a worker's efficiency. Because these diseases are usually preventable, improved working conditions can reduce disease, accidents, absenteeism, and costs while raising morale and efficiency.

## Historical Overview

Recognition of work-related disease is ancient. A severe lead colic case attributed to Hippocrates may be the first recorded observation. Pliny described mercury poisoning among slaves in mines. Medieval and early modern mining prompted writers such as Georg Agricola and Bernardino Ramazzini to connect occupations with disease, helping establish occupational medicine.

## Occupational Health Services

The ILO and WHO defined occupational health as promoting workers' physical, mental, and social well-being, preventing health damage from work, protecting workers from hazards, and adapting work to people and people to jobs. Employers often bear responsibility for health and safety, while governments set standards. In developing countries, occupational health is often most practical when combined with primary health care.

## Prevention, Placement, and Treatment

Occupational health services ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Occupational_safety_and_health)) are mainly preventive. Preemployment questionnaires or medical exams can match workers to suitable jobs and avoid placing vulnerable people in dangerous conditions. On-site treatment of injuries and poisonings prevents complications, speeds recovery, and reduces time lost. Health education, counseling, and surveillance can address smoking, alcohol or drug abuse, exercise, diet, and early disease detection.

## Chemical Hazards

Hazardous chemicals ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dangerous_goods)) may irritate skin, cause allergic reactions, or enter the body through skin, ingestion, or inhalation. Airborne gases, vapours, aerosols, and dusts are especially hard to control. Toxicity depends on concentration, particle size, solubility, exposure duration, and individual susceptibility. Respiratory absorption can produce local irritation or systemic poisoning.

## Gases

Occupational gases may inflame mucous membranes, as sulfur dioxide, chlorine, and fluorine do, or cause delayed pulmonary edema, as nitrogen oxides and phosgene can. Simple asphyxiants such as methane and carbon dioxide dilute oxygen, while chemical asphyxiants such as carbon monoxide and hydrogen sulfide interfere with oxygen transport or tissue oxygenation. Carbon monoxide is especially dangerous because it is colourless and odourless.

## Metals

Metal poisoning is common in work and home environments. Mercury poisoning is classic: salts can injure the digestive tract and kidneys, vapour can cause tremor, tooth loosening, and erethism, and organic mercury can cause permanent neurological damage. Other industrially hazardous metals include arsenic, beryllium, cadmium, chromium, lead, manganese, nickel, and thallium; some compounds are carcinogenic.

Inhaled dusts can cause pneumoconioses, lung diseases marked by mineral dust deposits and chronic fibrosis. Coal worker's pneumoconiosis, silicosis, and asbestosis are major examples. Symptoms include reduced exercise capacity, breathlessness, cough, and respiratory difficulty. Prevention includes lowering dust with water and exhaust ventilation, protective clothing, respirators, and smoking cessation.

## Temperature

Extreme heat requires sweating and increased surface blood flow, leading to water and salt loss. Heat exhaustion causes thirst, fatigue, giddiness, cramps, and fainting. Heatstroke is more dangerous, raising body temperature above 106 F and potentially causing coma, convulsions, and death. Cold exposure can cause chilblains, frostbite, gangrene, hypothermia, impaired consciousness, and cardiorespiratory failure.

## Noise and Vibration

Excessive noise reduces efficiency and can cause temporary or permanent hearing loss, especially after prolonged exposure above 80 to 90 decibels. Machinery enclosure, ear protection, and audiometry help prevention. Whole-body vibration can cause motion sickness, while hand-tool vibration can cause Raynaud's syndrome or vibration white finger, marked by numbness, blanching, pain, and sometimes tissue damage.

## Interesting Facts

- Occupational diseases ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Occupational_disease)) are essentially preventable.
- Carbon monoxide is dangerous because victims cannot see or smell it.
- Silicosis can accelerate tuberculosis.
- Vibration white finger is common among users of chain saws and pneumatic tools.

## Core Equations

Health and behavioural sciences often quantify risk, dose, change over time, and population variation:

$$C(t)=C_0e^{-kt}$$

This models concentration falling exponentially over time, often used for clearance, decay, or elimination processes.

$$z=\frac{x-\mu}{\sigma}$$

A z-score tells how many standard deviations a value lies above or below the mean.

$$\text{risk}=\frac{\text{cases}}{\text{population}}$$

This ratio turns the preceding idea into a measurable comparison, with the numerator as the quantity of interest and the denominator as the scale used for comparison.
