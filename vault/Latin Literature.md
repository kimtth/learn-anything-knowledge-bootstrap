# Latin Literature

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> Latin literature spans Roman writing from the Republic and Empire through medieval and Renaissance Latin scholarship. It adapted Greek forms into Roman epic, drama, satire, elegy, history, and prose, while preserving Latin as an intellectual language after the fall of Rome.

## Introduction

![[attachments/latin_literature_001.webp|420]]

*Early printed edition of Virgil*

Latin literature ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_literature)) is writing in Latin, mainly from the Roman Republic and Roman Empire, when Latin was a spoken language. After Rome fell, Latin remained the literary language of the western Middle Ages, then gradually yielded to Romance and other modern languages. After the Renaissance, new Latin writing survived mostly in church and academic use.

Ancient Latin literature ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_literature)) began through Greek translation and imitation. Roman writers borrowed forms, metres, myths, and themes, but their best work turned tradition into a Roman kind of originality. Their strength was vivid verbal art, concrete example, and the civilized ideal of humanitas.

Before formal literature, [[Rome]] had improvised farce, festival verses, liturgical formulas, records, speeches, and possible banquet songs. Greek influence came from the south and Etrusco-Hellenic influence from the north. Formal writing began when Livius Andronicus adapted Greek drama in 240 BC and translated the Odyssey ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Odyssey)); Naevius and Ennius then shaped early epic and metre. By the 1st century BC, Greek-trained declamation and elite literary circles had made written Latin more polished and more distant from common speech.

## Golden Age, 70 BCAD 18

The Golden Age ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Golden_Age)) covered the republic's final crisis and the rise of imperial rule under [[Augustus]]. Its Ciceronian phase, 70-42 BC, included active public men such as [[Caesar]], Varro, and above all Cicero, who made Latin prose capable of clear abstract argument.

After Carthage and Corinth were destroyed in 146 BC, peace and prosperity encouraged self-expressive literature. The neoteroi, especially Catullus, rejected heavy Ennian grandeur for refined Alexandrian art. Their influence passed through Cornelius Gallus to [[Virgil]], whose Eclogues mixed pastoral beauty, artifice, and topical meaning.

The Augustan phase gathered around Maecenas, who guided poets toward the new regime without turning them into simple propagandists. Between 29 and 19 BC, [[Virgil]] wrote the Georgics and Aeneid ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aeneid)), Horace produced odes and epistles, Propertius and Tibullus developed elegy, Ovid began to emerge, and Livy started his huge history. Augustan art prized perfected form, sound, arrangement, and the ability to say inherited things better.

## Silver Age, AD 18133

The Silver Age ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ages_of_Man)) followed the first confidence of Augustan renewal. Imperial patronage and fear restricted writers, and Ovid's fate showed how literature could be suppressed. Yet the period produced major figures, including the two Senecas, Lucan, Persius, the two Plinys, Quintilian, Petronius, Statius, Martial, Tacitus, Juvenal, and Suetonius.

Later imperial writing lost some older Roman civic energy as power decentralized under Hadrian and the Antonines. Some Romans again wrote in Greek, while Latin culture turned toward archaism, philology, and learned refinement. Pagan literature overlapped with Christian writing, and by the age of Ausonius, Claudian, and Prudentius, Christian literature was becoming the stronger force.

## Comedy

Roman comedy ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Comedy)) came from Greek New Comedy, especially Menander, but Romans turned imitation of life into theatrical fantasy. Naevius added Roman details and political criticism, but punishment pushed comedy away from direct topical attack.

Plautus created energetic comedy with songs, oaths, slapstick, verbal play, rhythm, and sudden Roman intrusions into Greek settings. Terence made the form more refined, psychological, and elegant, though less forceful. After Pompey built a permanent theatre in 55 BC, Italian-setting plays, farce, mime, pantomime, and spectacle gradually displaced older comedy.

## Tragedy

Livius Andronicus introduced Greek tragedy ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tragedy)) into Latin, followed by Naevius, Ennius, Pacuvius, and Accius. Early Roman tragedy used Greek myth, Roman legend, and sometimes recent history, with grand diction and an onstage chorus tied to the action.

After Accius, tragedy was mostly revived rather than newly written, and later drama became reading material for aristocrats. The younger Seneca's violent rhetorical tragedies were not likely public stage works, but they strongly influenced 16th- and early 17th-century tragedy in France and English revenge drama.

Roman epic ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epic_poetry)) developed from Livius' Odyssey ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Odyssey)), Naevius' Bellum Punicum, and Ennius' Annales to Virgil's Aeneid ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aeneid)). In the Aeneid, Aeneas leaves Dido and private happiness for Rome's future greatness, joining Homeric travel and war with Augustan ideals of sacrifice, religion, Italian strength, and imperial destiny. Didactic poetry ranged from Lucretius' Epicurean De rerum natura to Virgil's rural and political Georgics and Ovid's entertaining Ars amatoria and Fasti.

## Satire

Satura first meant a medley, but Lucilius made literary satire a hexameter genre of self-expression and fearless criticism. Varro introduced Menippean satire, mixing prose and verse, while Horace softened attack into laughter, self-portrait, and moral conversation.

Persius turned satire into pungent verse sermons, and Juvenal fixed its later image through anger, epigram, and sharp social detail. Satiric energy also appears in Seneca's Apocolocyntosis, Petronius' Satyricon, and Phaedrus' beast fables.

Short forms widened Latin expression. Catullus used iambic and lyric metres for abuse, tenderness, and love; Horace adapted Greek lyric in the Odes; and Martial made epigram pointed, witty, and often obscene.

## Elegy

The elegiac couplet became personal through Catullus, whose poems about Lesbia helped create subjective Roman love elegy. Gallus established the genre, though his work is lost, and Tibullus and Propertius refined it into the convention of the soft love poet.

Propertius began with intense passion for Cynthia and later used literary convention more deliberately. Tibullus favored peace, country life, old religion, grace, and quiet humor. Ovid used elegy for wit and performance, carrying the couplet toward smooth balance and sharp verbal point.

Roman [[Rhetoric|rhetoric]] and oratory were central to public careers and education. Cicero, master speaker from 70 to 43 BC, argued in De oratore that an orator needed broad liberal training. Under monarchy, eloquence lost political force but remained the crown of education, later shaped by Quintilian's humane Institutio oratoria.

## History

Roman [[The Study of History (Historiography)|historiography]] began with Quintus Fabius Pictor, who wrote in Greek during the Second Punic War, and with Cato's Latin Origines. [[Caesar]]'s Commentaries used plain narrative as political defense, while Sallust used speeches, motive, and sharp moral judgment to expose ruling-class decay.

Livy began his 40-year history as Augustus rose. He completed the annalistic tradition with vivid scenes and eloquent speeches, though his methods and patriotic bias could be weak. Tacitus stands higher for drama, irony, character, and the atmosphere of tyranny; Ammianus Marcellinus later continued the tradition to AD 378.

Biography, letters, philosophy, encyclopaedic learning, and criticism also mattered. Cicero's letters made private documents literary; Pliny the Younger showed elite and provincial life; Suetonius gathered imperial lives; Cicero carried Greek philosophy into Latin; Pliny's Natural History collected wide learning; and criticism developed through Cicero, Horace, Quintilian, and Tacitus.

## Fiction

Latin fiction was limited before the empire, but two major works survive. Petronius' Satyricon is a picaresque narrative with low-life characters, colloquial Latin, and Trimalchio's famous banquet.

Apuleius' Metamorphoses, or The Golden Ass, follows a man changed into an ass and restored by Isis. Its adventures include Cupid and Psyche and end in religious initiation.

## Richard H.A. Jenkyns

Medieval Latin literature, from about 500 to 1500, used Latin for church, administration, theology, philosophy, science, history, biography, and belles lettres. It was shaped by classical inheritance, vernacular languages, regional styles, the gap between learned and popular Latin, the Carolingian revival, and the 12th-century renewal.

Christian Latin rose from the 3rd to 5th centuries by adapting classical forms. Epic continued through Virgilian imitation, while hagiography, translation, biblical exegesis, hymnody, and Christian history created new genres. Jerome's Vulgate, Ambrose's hymns, [[Augustine]]'s City of God and Confessions, and saints' lives gave lasting models.

From the 6th to 8th centuries, Gregory of Tours, Venantius Fortunatus, Cassiodorus, Boethius, Isidore, Aldhelm, and Bede preserved, reshaped, and expanded Latin learning. The Carolingian renaissance under [[Charlemagne]] from 768 to 814 revived letters through scholars such as Alcuin, Theodulf, Paulinus of Aquileia, and Paul the Deacon, with major prose by Einhard and John Scotus Erigena.

From the later 9th to 14th centuries, liturgy generated the sequence and liturgical drama, including Notker Balbulus' hymns, Marian songs, and feast-day tropes. Secular lyric survives in the Cambridge Songs and Carmina Burana. University and scholastic culture produced Peter Abelard, John of Salisbury, Roger Bacon, Duns Scotus, Robert Grosseteste, and Thomas Aquinas, whose Summa theologiae was written between 1265 and 1272.

## Peter Godman

Renaissance Latin is linked with 14th-century Italy and writers such as [[Dante]], Petrarch, Boccaccio, Leonardo Bruni, Coluccio Salutati, and Aeneas Silvius Piccolomini. Verse returned to classical models, while prose Latin remained important for humanistic, scientific, philosophical, and religious work.

In 15th- and 16th-century Italy, [[Florence]], [[Rome]], and [[Naples]] formed major centers, with figures such as Marsilio Ficino, Pico della Mirandola, Angelo Poliziano, Pietro Bembo, Marco Vida, Lorenzo Valla, and Girolamo Fracastoro. Northern Europe added [[Erasmus]], the greatest northern humanist, along with Johannes Trithemius, Conradus Celtis, Juan Vives, Thomas More, Polydore Virgil, George Buchanan, and John Barclay.

## Frederic James Edward Raby

Until the early 18th century, Latin ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin)) remained the best international medium for historical and scientific work meant for Europe. [[Francis bacon|Francis Bacon]], Hugo Grotius, Rene Descartes, Benedict Spinoza, Sir Isaac Newton, and earlier humanists used Latin because it still crossed national language boundaries.

## Key Events & Developments

- 240 BC: Livius Andronicus adapted Greek drama and helped begin formal Latin literature ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_literature)).
- 70-42 BC: The Ciceronian period gave Latin prose new range through Cicero, [[Caesar]], and Varro.
- 29-19 BC: The main Augustan decade produced Virgil's major poems, Horace's odes, elegiac books, and Livy's history.
- AD 18-133: The Silver Age ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ages_of_Man)) produced strong writers under imperial pressure.
- 500-1500: Medieval Latin served church, learning, administration, and literary culture.
- 768-814: The Carolingian renaissance revived Latin letters under [[Charlemagne]].
- 1265-1272: Thomas Aquinas wrote the Summa theologiae.
