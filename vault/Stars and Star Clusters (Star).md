## Introduction

![[attachments/star_001.png|420]]

*Hertzsprung-Russell diagram*

Stars ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Star)) are massive self-luminous celestial bodies of gas that shine by radiation from internal energy sources. The observable universe contains enormous numbers of stars, but only a small fraction are visible to the naked eye. Many stars occur in pairs, multiple systems, clusters, and associations connected by common origin and gravity.

The Sun is the main comparison point for other stars. It has a mass of about $2 \times 10^{30}$ kg, a radius of about 700,000 km, and luminosity of about $4 \times 10^{23}$ kilowatts. Other stellar masses, radii, and luminosities are often measured in solar units.

The brightest stars in the sky are not necessarily nearby or Sun-like; they are often visible because their intrinsic luminosity is much greater than the Sun's.

Stars vary greatly. Dwarf stars ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dwarf_star)) such as Altair, Alpha Centauri A and B, and Procyon A have dimensions roughly comparable to the Sun. Sirius A and Vega are also dwarfs but brighter because they are hotter. Giants such as Aldebaran, Arcturus, and Capella are much larger, while supergiants can be enormous and extremely luminous.

## Binary and Multiple Stars

![[attachments/star_002.webp|420]]

*Illustration of a circumbinary star system*

Many stars occur as binary systems. Visual binaries can be seen as double stars through a telescope, while spectroscopic binaries are detected through shifting spectral lines, and eclipsing binaries through brightness changes when one star passes in front of another.

True visual binaries share motion through space and sometimes reveal measurable orbital motion. If the distance and orbital period are known, Kepler's third law can be used to calculate the sum of the stellar masses. The system 70 Ophiuchi is an example where individual masses can be derived from orbital motion.

Binary orbits also provide the mass ratio when each star's motion around the common centre of gravity is measured against the background stars.

The star 61 Cygni, a visual double, was the first star whose distance was measured by parallax, by Friedrich Bessel in the 19th century. Binary stars remain central to stellar astronomy because they provide the most direct way to measure stellar masses.

## Extrasolar Systems and Mass Limits

Space-based infrared observations have found warm disks around many young nearby stars, including Vega, Fomalhaut, and Beta Pictoris. These disks contain particles like sand grains and may represent early stages of planetary formation.

Such disks connect stellar astronomy to planet formation, because dust grains and debris around young stars are possible raw material for larger bodies.

Most stellar masses lie between about 0.3 and 3 solar masses. Extremely massive stars can reach hundreds of solar masses, though the Eddington limit restricts nuclear-burning stars. R136a1 is an example of a very massive star, estimated at about 265 solar masses now and perhaps 320 at formation.

At the low end, an ordinary star must have about 0.075 solar mass to reach central temperatures high enough for nuclear energy. Below this, an object becomes a brown dwarf, shining weakly by gravitational contraction and cooling toward extinction. Jupiter is far below this level and is a giant planet rather than a star.

## Hertzsprung-Russell Diagram

The Hertzsprung-Russell diagram plots stellar luminosity against temperature or spectral type. Most stars lie on the main sequence, running from hot, blue, luminous O and B stars through white A stars, orange K stars, and faint red M dwarfs.

The main sequence is continuous: luminosity falls as surface temperature decreases, while masses and radii also decrease more slowly and densities rise. The Sun lies on this sequence as an ordinary star.

Above the main sequence lie giants, which are yellow, orange, or red stars about 100 times as bright as the Sun and much larger in radius. Supergiants include stars of many spectral types and can be so luminous that they are visible at great distances despite being rare and short-lived.

Below the main sequence lies the white dwarf region, about 10 magnitudes fainter than main-sequence stars of similar colour. White dwarfs are late evolutionary remnants. Subgiants occupy a region between the main sequence and giants, often found in binary systems.

## Stellar Populations and Clusters

Population I stars belong mainly to spiral arms and are associated with interstellar gas and dust. They include stars of many ages, from forming stars to white dwarfs. Population II stars, common in old globular clusters, have lower metal content and different colour-magnitude patterns.

Globular cluster diagrams lack supergiants and show a red giant branch, horizontal branch, and main sequence ending around spectral type G. RR Lyrae variables occupy a special region. Open clusters differ because they have wider age ranges and chemical compositions closer to the Sun.

The luminosity function measures how common stars of different luminosities are. Naked-eye stars are mostly brighter than the Sun because bright stars can be seen farther away, but nearby stars within 20 light-years are mostly fainter than the Sun.

## Variable Stars

Variable stars change brightness. Many classes are named after prototype stars, such as RR Lyrae. Intrinsic variables include pulsating variables, explosive or catastrophic variables, and special types such as T Tauri stars, flare stars, pulsars, magnetic variables, X-ray variables, and radio variables.

Pulsating variables change because the star expands and contracts. Cepheids are especially important: they have periods from 1 to 100 days and show a relation between period and luminosity. This makes them essential distance indicators for astronomy.

Confusion about Cepheids decreased when astronomers recognized that different population types have different period-luminosity relations.

Other pulsators include RR Lyrae stars, Delta Scuti stars, Beta Canis Majoris stars, long-period variables, semiregular variables, and irregular red variables. Explosive variables include dwarf novae, novas, and supernovas, where light changes come from violent loss or explosion of stellar material.

## Stellar Atmospheres

The visible layers of a star form its [[Atmosphere|atmosphere]]. In the Sun, temperature, density, and pressure increase inward, but the Sun has no solid surface, so depth is defined conventionally. Visible solar temperatures range roughly from 4,700 to 6,200 K.

Stellar atmospheres are not simple still layers. Granulation on the Sun shows rising hot gas and sinking cooler gas. Gas motions shape spectral lines and must be included in models. Other stars may have stronger motions and extended or expanding atmospheres.

Models of stellar atmospheres are tested by how well they predict observed continuous and line spectra. Deviations appear in stars with strong winds, especially hot O-type stars, where matter flows outward in a stellar wind more violent than the solar wind.

For stars beyond the Sun, atmospheric structure must be inferred by successive approximation because temperature and pressure cannot be measured directly at depth.

## Stellar Energy

Stars shine mainly through nuclear fusion. In Sun-like stars, the proton-proton chain converts hydrogen into helium. Four protons ultimately form one helium nucleus, and about 0.7 percent of the original mass becomes energy, with some carried away by neutrinos.

The Sun must convert about 674 million tons of hydrogen into 670 million tons of helium every second to maintain its luminosity. The missing mass becomes radiation according to $E=mc^2$. The old solar neutrino problem was later explained by neutrino oscillations, not by a failure of the solar model.

Hotter main-sequence stars use the CNO cycle, where carbon, nitrogen, and oxygen act as catalysts to transform hydrogen into helium. Faint red dwarfs use the proton-proton chain almost exclusively, while the Sun gains most energy from proton-proton reactions and a smaller contribution from the CNO cycle.

## Stellar Structure and Evolution

![[attachments/star_003.png|420]]

*Diagram of stellar evolution*

Models of stellar structure calculate pressure, density, temperature, energy generation, opacity, and energy transport from surface to core. They must account for radiation and convection. In the Sun, radiation dominates deep interior transport, while convection carries energy through outer layers.

These models are solved by numerical integration, beginning from boundary conditions at the surface and core and adjusting until mass, radius, and luminosity fit together.

Stars ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Star)) change composition as they age, so modern models cannot assume uniform chemical composition. Mass, luminosity, radius, and lifetime are closely linked. Massive stars are bright and short-lived; low-mass stars are faint and long-lived.

Later stages of stellar evolution lead stars away from the main sequence into giants, supergiants, white dwarfs, novas, supernovas, neutron stars, pulsars, or other remnants depending on mass and conditions. The H-R diagram provides a map of these evolutionary routes.

## Star Formation

Stars form from molecular clouds. Large clouds can produce OB associations of massive stars or bound clusters of less massive stars. Whether a group remains bound depends on star-formation efficiency: if too little cloud mass becomes stars, winds and expanding H II regions remove gas and the group disperses.

Low-mass stars form in T associations around T Tauri stars. These stars arise from loose groups of small cloud cores. Most star formation produces associations rather than bound clusters; bound clusters account for only about 1 to 10 percent of star births.

If at least about 30 percent of a cloud core becomes stars, the remaining group is more likely to stay gravitationally bound after gas is expelled.

The efficiency of star formation is low, often less than 1 percent of a molecular cloud's mass per crossing time. This low efficiency helps explain why interstellar gas remains in the Galaxy after billions of years. Star formation today is only a small remnant of the more intense formation when the Galaxy was young.

A typical cloud core rotates slowly and concentrates mass toward its centre, conditions that shape collapse, fragmentation, and the birth of stars.

## Key Events & Developments

- 19th century: Friedrich Bessel measured the distance to 61 Cygni by parallax.
- 1911: Ejnar Hertzsprung recognized the difference between main-sequence and giant stars.
- 1995: First unambiguous brown dwarf identification was made.
- Late 1990s-early 2000s: Neutrino oscillation evidence helped resolve the solar neutrino problem.
- Modern astronomy: Infrared space observations revealed warm disks around many young stars.

## Key People

- Friedrich W. Bessel ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Friedrich_Wieseler)): First measured a stellar distance by parallax, using 61 Cygni.
- Ejnar Hertzsprung ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ejnar_Hertzsprung)): Helped establish the H-R diagram and the distinction between giants and main-sequence stars.
- Henry Norris Russell ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henry_Norris_Russell)): Associated with the Hertzsprung-Russell diagram.
- Astronomers studying Cepheids: Used period-luminosity relations to measure cosmic distances.

## Interesting Facts

- The Sun is not an exceptional star in mass, size, or luminosity.
- R136a1 is among the most massive known stars.
- Brown dwarfs are failed stars that cannot sustain ordinary hydrogen fusion.
- Cepheid variables are cosmic distance markers because their periods reveal luminosities.
- Star formation ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Star_formation)) is usually inefficient, leaving much gas in the Galaxy.
