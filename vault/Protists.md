# Protists

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> Protists are diverse eukaryotes, mostly microscopic and unicellular, whose mixed photosynthetic, motile, heterotrophic, parasitic, and fungus-like traits resist the old plant-animal divide. The source uses cell structure, ecology, life history, and classification debates to show their importance for eukaryotic diversity and the origins of other kingdoms.

## Introduction

![[attachments/protists_001.webp|420]]

*Paramecium microscope image*

Protists ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Protist)) are eukaryotic organisms that are mostly microscopic and unicellular, but they do not fit cleanly into the old division between plants and animals. They include forms traditionally called [[Protozoa (Protozoan)|protozoa]], algae, and lower fungi, and many share features with more than one group. Some are pigmented and photosynthetic, some are motile and feed by engulfing particles, and others are parasitic or fungus-like.

For a long time, biology divided living things into [[Plants|plants]] and animals. Plants were treated as green, photosynthetic, usually nonmotile organisms with cell walls, while animals were treated as colourless, motile, phagotrophic organisms without cell walls. Microscopy showed that many tiny organisms did not obey these rules. They were often forced into plant or animal categories even when their structure, nutrition, or life cycle made that classification weak.

The idea of a separate kingdom for these organisms was advanced by Ernst Haeckel in 1866, when he proposed Protista ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Protist)) for lower organisms outside the familiar plant-animal split. Herbert F. Copeland later tried to revive the idea, but broad acceptance came only after biologists reconsidered the whole living world. Around 1960, work by R.Y. Stanier, C.B. Van Niel, and others helped separate prokaryotic and eukaryotic life, making room for protists as a major eukaryotic assemblage.

## Paramecium and the Protist Problem

Paramecium caudatum shows why protists challenged older systems. It is a microscopic eukaryote with animal-like motility, but the wider group also includes photosynthetic, fungus-like, parasitic, and macroscopic forms. This mixture made the old labels of minute plants and minute animals too simple for the real diversity seen under the microscope.

Many protist groups were historically claimed by different specialists. Some algae-like flagellates were placed among both [[Algae|algae]] and protozoa, including members of Chrysophyta, Haptophyta, Xanthophyta, Eustigmatophyta, Chlorophyta, Euglenophyta, Cryptophyta, Dinoflagellata, and related groups. Their mixed traits made them hard to classify by colour, movement, or nutrition alone.

Several fungus-like groups also crossed disciplinary boundaries. Mycetozoa, Dictyosteliidea, Acrasidea, Plasmodiophorea, and Labyrinthomorpha were claimed as fungi by many mycologists and as protozoa by many zoologists. Oomycota, Hyphochytridiomycota, and Chytridiomycetes, once treated as lower fungi, came to be widely regarded as true protists rather than true fungi.

## Locomotion

Many protists are recognized by visible locomotory organelles. Most motile forms use flagella or cilia, while some move by gliding, floating, or extending pseudopodia. These same structures can also help feeding, so movement and nutrition are closely linked in many species.

Cilia and flagella are basically similar eukaryotic structures, even though they may look different in motion and arrangement. They contain an axoneme with nine outer pairs of microtubules and one inner pair, surrounded by a membrane continuous with the cell membrane. Below the cell surface, the outer pairs become triplets in the basal body or kinetosome, which anchors the organelle.

Protist flagella are not the same as bacterial flagella. The bacterial structure is made of flagellin, is extracellular, and is moved from its base in the cell membrane. Eukaryotic cilia and flagella are built from tubulin-based microtubules, are much more complex, and are not ancestral to the prokaryotic structure.

Some protists use pseudopodia, temporary extensions of cytoplasm, for both motion and feeding. Amoeboid forms can change shape as they move, while sarcodine groups may use pseudopodia with special internal organization. These locomotory differences became important clues in classification, but they did not by themselves solve protist relationships.

## Ecology

Protists ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Protist)) are worldwide in distribution and occur wherever their needs for water, nutrients, and, for photosynthetic forms, sunlight can be met. Individual species still have preferred niches and microhabitats, and many are sensitive to changes in their surroundings. This makes the group both cosmopolitan and ecologically specialized.

Free-living protists are especially abundant in aquatic habitats such as ponds, streams, rivers, lakes, bays, seas, and oceans. Some live at particular levels in the water column, while others are benthic bottom-dwellers. They also occur in thermal springs, briny pools, cave waters, snow, ice, beach sands, intertidal mud flats, bogs, marshes, swimming pools, and sewage treatment systems.

Protists also occupy terrestrial habitats such as soils, forest litter, desert sands, bark, and leaves. Cysts and spores can be carried high in the atmosphere, and some algal forms may even live and reproduce in air streams. Fossil protists occur through much of the geologic record, with red algal fossils reaching back to the Precambrian, about 1.9 billion years ago.

Symbiotic protists are widespread because they occur wherever their hosts live. Some are harmless or useful partners, while others are parasites of animals, plants, or other organisms. This range from free-living to symbiotic and pathogenic life shows why protists are important in ecology, evolution, and disease.

## Classification

![[attachments/protists_003.webp|420]]

*Euglena microscope image*

Protist classification has three broad possibilities. One approach recognizes a single kingdom Protista with enough shared unity to be useful, while still asking difficult questions about definition, boundaries, and relationships among subgroups. A second approach treats protists as a structural grade between prokaryotes and higher eukaryotic kingdoms, but this leaves uncertainty about what top-level taxa should contain them. A third approach splits protists among several eukaryotic kingdoms and may avoid using Protista as a formal kingdom.

The single-kingdom approach stresses that protists share a practical taxonomic identity, even if their ancestry and internal relationships are complex. Some protists may have bridged gaps in eukaryotic evolution, and some lineages may be connected to the origins of plants, animals, and fungi. At the same time, many living protist groups do not appear to have led directly to any higher kingdom.

The working classification groups phyla and classes into informal sections rather than strict ranked units. This arrangement shows possible closeness among taxa but does not claim that every neighbouring section is closely related. It also shows why separate formal categories for protozoan protists, algal protists, and fungal protists are difficult: many phyla have been assigned to more than one of those older traditions.

## Mixed Algal, Protozoan, and Fungus-like Groups

The classification includes many phyla that were once treated as algae, protozoa, fungi, or combinations of these. Some sections contain only algal forms, some are purely nonpigmented protozoan groups, some combine algal and protozoan phyla, and one includes protozoan and fungal-like groups. This mixing is central to the idea of Protista as a kingdom with unusual boundaries.

Section I includes chromophyte-like groups, with Eustigmatophyta, Bacillariophyta or diatoms, and Phaeophyta or brown algae among the named phyla. These groups connect photosynthetic protists with major algal traditions and show how protist classification includes both microscopic and larger forms.

## Chlorobionts

Chlorobionts are essentially the green algae. They usually have flattened mitochondrial cristae, chlorophylls a and b, cellulosic cell walls in unicellular or multicellular forms, and starch stored inside chloroplasts. Their flagella lack tubular hairs, and the ancestry of Plantae is found in this group, which explains why they have sometimes been classified with plants.

The group includes about 10,000 described species, though relatively few are fossil forms. Ulvophyta and Glaucophyta appear among the named phyla, with Glaucophyta treated as controversial. The group’s importance lies in its link between protist diversity and the later plant kingdom.

## Euglenozoa

Euglenozoa are marked by discoidal mitochondrial cristae, a large nuclear endosome, cortical microtubules beneath the pellicle, and paraflagellar rods. Euglenoids and kinetoplastideans share molecular similarities in cytochrome c and 5S rRNA, while euglenoid plastids are enclosed by three membranes and lack stored starch and cellulosic walls.

Kinetoplastideans contain a large DNA body in the mitochondrion, and the section includes about 1,600 acceptable species. Hemimastigophorea is also listed near this part of the classification, showing the continuing difficulty of placing unusual flagellate forms.

## Rhodophytes (red algae)

Rhodophytes, or red algae, have flattened mitochondrial cristae but lack centrioles, basal bodies, and flagella. Their photosynthetic species contain chlorophyll and accessory phycobilipigments that hide the green colour. They are mainly marine and filamentous, and some reach lengths of one metre or more.

About 5,000 red algal species are described, with around 750 fossil species. Their deep fossil record and distinctive absence of flagella make them a special algal section within protist classification.

## Cryptomonads

Cryptomonads are algal protists with flattened mitochondrial cristae and chloroplasts containing chlorophylls a and c plus some phycobilipigments. They are usually biflagellate and phagotrophic, though a few species are nonpigmented. Their nucleomorph and ejectisomes are distinctive structures, and about 200 species are recognized.

## Dinozoa

Dinozoa are mostly biflagellates with one longitudinal and one transverse flagellum. Photosynthetic forms contain chlorophylls a and c, xanthophylls, and carotenes, while many also feed phagotrophically. Their tubular mitochondrial cristae, cortical alveoli, and condensed chromosomes help distinguish them.

Of about 4,200 known dinozoan species, roughly half are fossil forms. Syndinea are a nonphotosynthetic, marine, endosymbiotic class with unique life cycles and low chromosome numbers, showing that this section includes both photosynthetic and nonphotosynthetic lineages.

## Polymastigotes

Polymastigotes are higher zooflagellates that are nonpigmented, mostly endosymbiotic, and usually multiflagellated. They lack mitochondria but contain hydrogenosomes that perform mitochondrial functions in the cytoplasm. They are anaerobes with unique organelles near the flagellar apparatus, and only about 500 to 600 of the 750 to 800 reported species are considered acceptable.

## Rhizopod sarcodines

Rhizopod sarcodines often possess nonaxopod pseudopodia during at least one life stage, or they show shuttle-type cytoplasmic flow. Many have tubular mitochondrial cristae, and a biflagellate stage is common in many species. Pseudopodia are used for locomotion and holozoic feeding.

This section includes about 44,000 described species, most of them foraminiferans, and a large share are fossil forms. Lobosea includes rhizopod amoebas, parasitic forms, amoeboflagellates, and many testaceous amoebas, while Filosea is also named in the same classification area.

## Actinopod sarcodines

Actinopod sarcodines have axopodia, which are pseudopodia with microtubular cores. They commonly possess elaborate internal skeletal systems, tubular mitochondrial cristae, and, in many forms, a complex central capsule. They are mainly marine, with about 11,000 to 12,000 reported species, more than half of which are extinct.

## Apicomplexans

Apicomplexans are endosymbionts, mostly true parasites, with a special apical complex visible clearly only under the electron microscope. Spores are common in their life cycles, and their hosts include terrestrial, marine, and freshwater animals. Many are pathogenic, and about 5,000 species are recognized.

## Haplosporidia

Haplosporidia are small endoparasites of cells and tissues, mostly in certain marine invertebrates. Their spores are structurally complex but lack polar filaments or tubes, and they have no flagella. Infective sporoplasms contain unusual haplosporosomes, and only about 25 species are described.

## Myxozoa

Myxozoa are coelozoic or histozoic parasites mainly of cold-blooded vertebrates. Their valved spores contain one or more polar capsules and show multinuclear plasmodial and multicellular developmental stages. Their polar filaments anchor the organism in host tissues rather than inoculating sporoplasms into new hosts.

Myxozoans have no flagella, have flattened mitochondrial cristae, and may possess shell valves that are drawn out or sculptured. At least 1,200 species are known, and Paramyxidia is listed among the related phyla.

## Ciliates

Ciliates ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ciliate)) have a dual nuclear apparatus and an infraciliary or cortical system with distinct microtubular and microfibrillar structures. They reproduce by homothetogenic binary fission and also show conjugation. Their pellicle carries many cilia and usually alveoli, structures otherwise rare among protists except in dinoflagellates.

Most ciliates are heterotrophic and phagotrophic, with complex oral apparatuses. They are mostly free-living but may be symbiotic, and they occur in marine, soil, and freshwater habitats. Their classes are distinguished by kinetid structure, and about 8,000 species are described, with some fossil forms.

## Key Events & Developments

- The 4th century BC: [[Aristotle and Aristotelianism|Aristotle]]'s two-kingdom view helped frame the long plant-animal division later challenged by microscopic life.
- 1866: Ernst Haeckel proposed Protista ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Protist)) as a third kingdom for lower organisms outside plants and animals.
- The mid-20th century: Herbert F. Copeland and later systematists revived the protist idea as classification moved beyond the old two-kingdom model.
- About 1960: R.Y. Stanier, C.B. Van Niel, and colleagues helped formalize the prokaryote-eukaryote division that supported a new view of protists.

## Interesting Facts

- Protists ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Protist)) can resemble animals, plants, or fungi, but many do not fit any of those kingdoms cleanly.
- Red algal fossils reach back about 1.9 billion years, while many other protist lineages left little or no fossil record.
- Cilia and flagella in protists are built from microtubules and are not the same as bacterial flagella.
- Ciliates ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ciliate)) have two kinds of nuclei and complex oral structures, making them among the most organized unicellular protists.
