# Hinduism

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> Hinduism is a diverse religious tradition of the Indian subcontinent encompassing doctrine, ritual, social practice, narrative, devotion, and philosophy rather than a single fixed system. Its ancient roots, major textual and philosophical traditions, and long regional influence make it one of the world's largest and oldest living religions.

## Introduction

Hinduism ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hinduism)) is a major world religion that developed on the Indian subcontinent and has spread across many regions of the world. Hinduism is not a single, fixed system but a broad and diverse tradition of belief, ritual, philosophy, and social practice. Its roots reach back to ancient India, and the term “Hinduism” itself is relatively modern, even though the tradition it names is very old.

Some of its texts and practices date to the 2nd millennium BCE or earlier, and if the Indus valley civilization ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indus_Valley_Civilisation)) of the 3rd-2nd millennium BCE was an early source, Hinduism may be the oldest living religion on Earth. From about the 4th century CE it was the dominant presence in Southeast Asia for more than a thousand years, and in the early 21st century it had nearly one billion adherents, including roughly 80 percent of India's population.

Hinduism is best understood as a collection of overlapping strands: doctrine, ritual practice, social organization, narrative, devotion, and philosophy. These strands coexist in tension, and that tension is one of the religion’s defining characteristics.

## The Meaning of Hinduism

The name Hinduism came into use mainly through British and European writing in the 19th century. Earlier, people in India often described their tradition in other terms such as Vedic religion or sanatana dharma ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/San%C4%81tana_Dharma)). This helps explain why the religion is often seen both as an ancient tradition and as a modern label for a very broad set of practices.

The word gained currency through books such as Hinduism ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hinduism)) (1877) by the Oxford Sanskritist Monier Monier-Williams. The root term is far older: Greek and Persian travelers to the Indus valley called its inhabitants Hindu (Greek indoi), and only in the 16th century did Indians slowly adopt the label to distinguish themselves from the Turks, after which its meaning shifted from geographic to religious.

Many Hindus have accepted the word Hinduism while others prefer other descriptions, showing that the tradition has always been internally diverse.

## Doctrinal and Practical Traditions

Hinduism is built on several linked dimensions. In doctrine, it holds together ideas about the divine, the world, dharma, karma, and moksha. In practice, worship through puja and the offering of prasada is central to daily religious life. In society, the caste system and its many local forms have played a major role in shaping social relations.

A classic source for that social order is the Rigveda hymn 10.90, which describes a primordial sacrifice producing four ideal classes, or varnas: Brahmans as priests, Kshatriyas as warriors and nobles, Vaishyas as commoners, and Shudras as servants. Alongside these ideal classes stand thousands of endogamous birth-groups, or jatis, making Hindu society notably plural and hierarchical.

Narrative and devotion are not secondary features. Stories of gods, heroes, and saints create a shared religious imagination, while bhakti, or devotion, gives many Hindus a path of emotional and personal connection with the divine.

One of Hinduism's central organizing ideas is dharma. It can mean cosmic order, religious duty, social obligation, moral law, and the proper conduct of a person according to stage of life, status, family, and circumstance. This flexibility is one reason Hindu practice varies so widely.

Karma ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karma)) and samsara explain moral causation and rebirth, while moksha names liberation from that cycle. Different schools and devotional movements understand liberation differently: union with ultimate reality, release from ignorance, proximity to a chosen deity, or freedom through knowledge, action, or devotion. These connect to the doctrine of atman-brahman: most Hindus hold that brahman, the uncreated and all-embracing reality, is also the self, or atman, of every living being, so that liberation comes from realizing that identity. The cycle of rebirth, samsara, binds the spirit, or jiva, through actions driven by desire.

Hinduism also recognizes the ashramas, or stages of life, and the purusharthas, or aims of life: dharma, artha, kama, and moksha. These frameworks show that Hinduism does not reduce religious life to world-denial; it also organizes household life, prosperity, pleasure, and social responsibility. The four ashramas trace an ideal lifespan from the celibate student, or brahmacharin, through the grihastha householder and the vanaprastha forest-dweller to the sannyasin who renounces the world in search of liberation.

## Sacred Texts and Philosophies

![[attachments/hinduism_003.webp|420]]

*2016 Singapur, Rochor, Świątynia Sri Krishnan (21)*

The Vedas, the Upanishads, the epics, and the Puranas ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Puranas)) are all important. The Vedas are the oldest sacred texts, but later texts such as the Bhagavad Gita and the Puranas became crucial for ordinary religious life. These writings are not merely theological documents; they also shape ritual, ethics, and devotion.

Hindu philosophy gives a major role to schools such as Vedanta, Yoga, and the many forms of Hindu mysticism. It shows that Hindu thought has always moved between philosophical speculation, ritual discipline, and spiritual realization.

The Vedas preserve hymns, ritual formulas, melodies, and sacrificial knowledge. The Brahmanas explain ritual meaning, the Aranyakas move toward contemplation, and the Upanishads explore brahman, atman, knowledge, death, and liberation. This sequence shows the movement from sacrifice to speculation. The Veda is the most ancient body of Indian religious literature and, for the upper castes, an absolute authority, even though most Hindus venerate it from a distance and know little of its contents. The roots of the tradition also reach back visually to figures such as yakshas and nagas, worshipped from about 400 BCE.

The epics, especially the Mahabharata ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mahabharata)) and Ramayana ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ramayana)), made religious teaching narrative and public. The Bhagavad Gita presents paths of action, knowledge, and devotion through Krishna's teaching to Arjuna. The Puranas ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Puranas)) organize myths, genealogies, cosmology, pilgrimage places, and sectarian devotion.

Dated to about 100 CE, the Bhagavadgita sets out three margas, or roads to liberation: the karma-marga of disinterested ritual and social duty, the jnana-marga of meditative knowledge of one's identity with brahman, and the bhakti-marga of love for a personal God. The belief in a single One that is the All has marked Indian spiritual life for more than 3,000 years.

Several philosophical schools developed. Samkhya analyzes purusha and prakriti, Yoga offers disciplined practice, Nyaya and Vaisheshika develop logic and categories, Mimamsa defends Vedic ritual, and Vedanta interprets the Upanishads in different ways. These orthodox schools all acknowledge the authority of the Veda, whereas groups that rejected it outright, especially the Buddhists and Jains, were traditionally regarded as heterodox, though they are now often seen as part of a wider family of Indic traditions.

Vedanta becomes especially important through nondual, qualified nondual, and dualist interpretations associated with teachers such as Shankara, Ramanuja, and Madhva. Their debates show how one scriptural inheritance could support different metaphysical and devotional systems.

## Worship, Pilgrimage, and Everyday Religion

Hinduism is lived in ordinary life as much as in formal theology. Worship, home shrines, festivals, and pilgrimage are part of the everyday practice of millions of Hindus. These practices are often meant to secure well-being, prosperity, protection, and spiritual reward.

A deep tension runs through Hindu religion between external ritual and internal realization. Many rituals are performed for practical benefits, yet devotional traditions insist that true religion is not only transactional but also an expression of love, humility, and surrender to the divine.

Temple worship centres on the presence of the deity in an image, ritual service, offerings, lamps, music, procession, and the return of blessed food or prasada. Home worship adapts many of the same patterns to family life and daily routine. The worship of icons or images, called pratima, murti, or archa, is broadly known as puja, the honouring of the deity, and that prasada, literally grace, echoes the giving and sharing of food offered to an honoured guest.

Pilgrimage links geography to sacred memory. Rivers, mountains, temples, cities, and regional shrines become places where myth, ritual merit, family duty, and communal identity meet. The Ganges, Varanasi, and many local sacred sites are part of this religious landscape.

Festivals renew mythic time through public celebration. Events linked to Vishnu, Shiva, Devi, Rama, Krishna, and local deities combine ritual, performance, food, music, processions, vows, and social gathering. Festivals are central to lived Hinduism.

The bhakti traditions made devotion available through vernacular songs, saints, sects, pilgrimage, and personal relation to a chosen deity. Vaishnava, Shaiva, and Shakta traditions developed distinct theologies while sharing practices of worship, story, and devotion. Bhakti ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bhakti)) verse first appeared in Tamil in south India and then moved northward into other languages, with poet-saints such as Kabir and Ravidas contrasting true religious feeling with priestly pride, caste exclusion, and empty ritual.

## Reform, Nationalism, and Modern Hinduism

Hinduism changed greatly in the modern period. Reform movements such as the Brahmo Samaj and the Arya Samaj challenged caste, image worship, and traditional practices, while movements led by figures such as Ramakrishna, Vivekananda, and Mahatma Gandhi reshaped Hindu identity in relation to nationalism and social reform. The Brahmo Samaj was founded by Ram Mohun Roy in 1828 and the Arya Samaj by Dayanand Sarasvati in 1875; in 1893 Vivekananda carried Vedanta and yoga abroad at the World Parliament of Religions in Chicago.

These developments show that Hinduism is not static. It has continuously adapted to new historical conditions while preserving deep continuities with its ancient past.

Caste is both a social structure and a religious problem. Varna ideals, jati communities, purity rules, marriage patterns, occupation, reform, law, and anti-caste movements all show that Hindu social life has been contested as well as inherited.

Modern Hinduism developed under colonial scholarship, missionary criticism, Indian reform, nationalism, and global migration. Reformers reinterpreted scripture, criticized social abuses, defended Hindu philosophy, and presented Hinduism as a world religion capable of dialogue with science and modern politics.

In the diaspora, Hinduism ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hinduism)) adapted through temples, associations, festivals, family ritual, language preservation, and new forms of teaching. Hinduism is therefore ancient, plural, local, philosophical, devotional, ritual, social, and global at once.

## Gods, Sects, and Devotion

Vishnu ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vishnu)), Shiva, and Devi are major centres of worship, but Hinduism is not reducible to three fixed churches. Local gods, village goddesses, family deities, regional cults, saints, teachers, and philosophical traditions overlap with larger sectarian patterns.

Vaishnavism emphasizes Vishnu and his avatars, especially Rama and Krishna. The Ramayana ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ramayana)), Mahabharata ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mahabharata)), Bhagavad Gita, Puranas, temple traditions, and devotional poetry all helped make Vishnu worship a major religious world. The classic series of ten avatars, or descents, of Vishnu typically includes the fish, tortoise, boar, man-lion, dwarf, the axe-bearing Parashurama, Rama, Krishna, the [[Buddha and Buddhism (Buddha)|Buddha]], and the future deliverer Kalkin.

Shaivism presents Shiva as ascetic, householder, lord of dance, destroyer, yogic master, and supreme reality. Shaiva traditions include temple worship, philosophical schools, tantric currents, pilgrimage, and regional devotional movements.

Shaktism focuses on the Goddess as power, mother, warrior, protection, fertility, and ultimate reality. Devi traditions include fierce and benevolent forms, local goddesses, festivals, myths, and tantric practices. A central image is the Great Goddess Durga, or Devi, as the slayer of the buffalo demon Mahisasura, set within a wider pantheon of paired deities such as Krishna and Radha, Shiva and Parvati, and Rama, whose enemy is the ten-headed demon king Ravana.

Bhakti ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bhakti)) movements used vernacular languages, songs, saints, and emotional devotion to challenge purely priestly or Sanskritic control. In many regions, poets and saints criticized pride, caste exclusion, empty ritual, and religious hypocrisy while still drawing deeply on inherited myth and worship.

## Ritual, Society, and Sacred Geography

Samskaras, or life-cycle rites, mark birth, naming, initiation, marriage, death, and ancestor obligations. These rites connect family life to sacred order and make religion part of ordinary social continuity.

Purity and pollution rules shaped food, touch, marriage, occupation, temple access, and social hierarchy. These practices were historically important but also contested by reformers, anti-caste movements, law, urbanization, and modern democratic politics.

Sacred geography is central. Rivers, mountains, temple towns, pilgrimage circuits, cremation places, and regional shrines make the land itself religiously meaningful. Pilgrimage links mythic time, family duty, merit, community, and travel.

Tantric traditions add another layer through mantra, diagram, initiation, bodily discipline, deity visualization, and ritual power. Tantra was diverse and influential, not simply marginal, because it affected Shaiva, Shakta, Buddhist, and temple traditions.

## Historical Adaptation

Hinduism moved through Vedic religion, epic and Puranic development, classical philosophy, temple culture, devotional movements, Islamic rule, colonial modernity, nationalism, and global diaspora. This long history shows continuity through repeated reinterpretation.

Contact with Buddhism, Jainism, Islam, Sikhism, Christianity, colonial scholarship, and modern secular politics shaped Hindu self-understanding. Hindu traditions absorbed, resisted, debated, and redefined themselves in response to other religious and intellectual worlds.

The modern period made Hindu identity political as well as religious. Reformers sought universal spirituality, national renewal, caste reform, women's education, and social service; other movements emphasized cultural pride, temple revival, or Hindu nationalism.

Hinduism ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hinduism)) is best understood as a layered civilization of practices and meanings. It contains ritual sacrifice and philosophical monism, village goddess worship and global yoga, caste hierarchy and anti-caste reform, household prosperity and renunciant liberation, Sanskrit scripture and vernacular devotion.

## Myth, Narrative, and Time

[[Myth and Mythology (Myth)|Mythology]] matters because Hindu myths are not simply stories; they organize ritual, pilgrimage, theology, kingship, ethics, and devotional imagination. Myths of creation, destruction, descent, battle, marriage, and divine play explain how gods relate to the world.

[[Cosmos (Cosmology)|Cosmology]] in Hindu traditions often works with vast cycles of creation, preservation, destruction, and renewal. Yugas, kalpas, heavens, hells, rebirth, and liberation place human life inside enormous sacred time rather than a single linear history.

The Ramayana ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ramayana)) presents Rama, Sita, Lakshmana, Hanuman, exile, kingship, loyalty, and dharma in narrative form. The Mahabharata ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mahabharata)) presents kinship conflict, war, fate, duty, and moral ambiguity on an even larger scale. These epics are religious, literary, ethical, and cultural foundations.

Krishna devotion shows the range of Hindu religious emotion: child, lover, teacher, king, and supreme deity. Stories of Krishna's childhood, the Gita's teaching, and devotional poetry all helped make him one of the most beloved forms of the divine.

## Renunciation and Practice

Household religion stands in balance with renunciation. Ascetics, yogis, sannyasins, teachers, and monastic orders pursue liberation through discipline, meditation, knowledge, austerity, or devotion. Their presence challenges ordinary social life even while being honoured by it.

Yoga is more than physical exercise. It includes mental discipline, concentration, ethical restraint, bodily control, meditation, and liberation. Different Hindu traditions interpret yoga through philosophy, devotion, tantra, and modern reform.

Guru lineages transmit teaching, initiation, interpretation, and practice. The authority of teachers can be philosophical, ritual, devotional, ascetic, or charismatic. This helps explain how Hindu traditions remain continuous without one central church.

## Modern Global Forms

Modern Hindu movements translated Hindu ideas into universal religion, comparative mysticism, social service, nationalism, and global spirituality. Vivekananda presented Vedanta and yoga internationally, while [[Gandhi]] drew on Hindu, Jain, and other sources to develop nonviolence and political ethics.

Contemporary Hinduism includes temple networks, pilgrimage economies, televised epics, diaspora festivals, yoga movements, nationalist politics, environmental reinterpretations, and debates over gender, caste, conversion, and secular law.

The final image is of Hinduism ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hinduism)) as a tradition with no single founder, creed, or institution, yet with powerful continuities in sacred story, ritual action, social order, pilgrimage, philosophical reflection, and devotion to divine presence.

Hinduism also shows striking regional diversity. Hindu practice in north India, south India, Bengal, Maharashtra, Gujarat, Tamil regions, Nepal, Bali, and diaspora communities can differ sharply in language, deity, ritual calendar, caste pattern, temple style, and devotional literature.

Women appear in Hinduism through goddess worship, household rites, marriage customs, vows, devotional poetry, reform debates, and modern challenges to inherited gender roles. Hindu social and religious life cannot be understood only through male priests or philosophers.

The tradition's continuing vitality comes from this layered structure. Hinduism ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hinduism)) can speak through Sanskrit scripture, vernacular song, temple image, family ritual, ascetic discipline, nationalist politics, philosophical commentary, festival performance, and global yoga culture without becoming a single uniform system.

Priestly ritual and popular devotion remain intertwined. Brahmanical rites preserve Vedic authority, mantras, fire offerings, domestic ceremonies, and temple service, while village festivals and family vows bring religion into agriculture, illness, fertility, protection, and local identity.

Hinduism also shows a strong syncretic capacity. Hindu traditions have absorbed local deities, tribal cults, regional heroes, philosophical schools, tantric practices, reform teachings, and global interpretations. This does not remove conflict, but it helps explain continuity through change.

Sacred sound is important through mantra, recitation, song, and scripture. Sanskrit has high ritual prestige, but vernacular languages made devotion accessible across regions. Tamil, Hindi, Bengali, Marathi, Gujarati, and other languages carried powerful religious literatures.

[[Ethics]] in Hinduism is contextual rather than only rule-based. Dharma depends on role, stage of life, social relation, intention, and circumstance, while broader ideals include nonviolence, truthfulness, generosity, self-control, purity, compassion, and devotion.

A persistent tension runs between hierarchy and spiritual equality. Caste rules created exclusion and order; bhakti saints, reformers, anti-caste leaders, and modern law challenged inherited boundaries in different ways.

Hindu nationalism and reform movements show how religious identity entered modern politics. Some writers universalized Hindu spirituality; others emphasized cultural unity, majority identity, or national revival. Modern Hinduism is debated, not uniform.

Hinduism ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hinduism)) is demanding to summarize because it spans religion, philosophy, myth, ritual, society, politics, sacred geography, reform, and diaspora. A faithful account must keep those strands visible rather than compressing them into one doctrine.

Temple culture also functions as social organization. Temples are ritual centres, landholders, artistic patrons, pilgrimage destinations, festival organizers, and symbols of royal or community identity. Architecture, image worship, music, dance, procession, and gift-giving all meet there.

Image worship is explained through presence rather than simple representation. A consecrated image can be treated as the living focus of divine attention, served with food, lamps, clothing, bathing, music, and procession. This makes worship embodied and reciprocal.

Hindu philosophy ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philosophy)) also includes debate about knowledge, illusion, self, matter, God, language, ritual authority, and liberation. Hindu schools do not simply repeat myth; they argue rigorously about reality and the means of knowing it.

Festival cycles connect myth to public time. Celebrations of Rama, Krishna, Shiva, Devi, harvests, lights, colours, ancestors, and regional deities renew community memory and make religious life visible in streets, homes, temples, and pilgrimage places.

Modern diaspora Hinduism creates new forms because migrants must build temples, teach children, translate rituals, negotiate minority identity, and select which regional practices will represent Hinduism publicly in new countries.

Hinduism ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hinduism)) cannot be reduced to one belief. It is a family of textual, ritual, philosophical, social, regional, and devotional traditions that overlap without becoming identical.

Continuity works without central control: repeated stories, ritual patterns, sacred places, teacher lineages, family practices, philosophical debate, and festival calendars keep the tradition recognizable across many local forms and historical changes.

Law and custom are also part of Hindu history. Texts on dharma, local caste rules, royal authority, family practice, colonial law, and modern constitutional reform all shaped how religious ideas entered marriage, inheritance, ritual status, education, and public life.

Death rites and ancestor obligations show how Hinduism links the household to cosmic order. Cremation, mourning, offerings, pilgrimage, and ritual memory place family duty within wider ideas of rebirth, merit, purity, and liberation.

Modern reform also drew criticism from within Hindu society. Reformers challenged child marriage, caste exclusion, widow restrictions, ritual excess, and social inequality, while others defended inherited forms as sacred continuity.

This makes Hinduism ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hinduism)) a tradition of debate as much as inheritance. Its long history includes competing claims about scripture, devotion, caste, gender, nationalism, temple practice, philosophy, and the relation between worldly duty and spiritual freedom.

## Paths and Practices

Hindu practice runs through several paths. Karma yoga emphasizes disciplined action and duty; jnana yoga emphasizes knowledge and insight; bhakti yoga emphasizes loving devotion; and yogic discipline emphasizes mental control, bodily practice, and liberation.

Renunciation is a major counterpoint to household life. Ascetics, sannyasis, yogis, gurus, and monastic communities embody the search for liberation beyond ordinary social obligations. Yet Hinduism also values family rites, marriage, prosperity, fertility, and continuity.

The role of gurus shows the importance of personal transmission. Teachers interpret scriptures, initiate disciples, organize sects, guide devotion, and adapt inherited teachings for new communities. Authority is therefore textual, ritual, personal, and institutional.

## Myth, Epic, and Theology

Hindu narratives do theological work. The Ramayana ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ramayana)) explores kingship, exile, loyalty, dharma, marriage, devotion, and the ideal ruler through Rama, Sita, Lakshmana, Hanuman, and Ravana. The Mahabharata ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mahabharata)) explores kinship, war, duty, fate, and moral ambiguity on a vast scale.

The Puranas ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Puranas)) make divine worlds accessible through creation myths, genealogies, avatars, temple origins, pilgrimages, vows, cosmology, and stories of gods and devotees. They connect local shrines to universal sacred history.

Krishna devotion includes child, lover, king, teacher, and supreme deity forms. These images allow theology to become emotional, aesthetic, musical, and communal. Shiva and Devi traditions similarly combine cosmic power with intimate worship.

## Modern Global Presence

Modern Hinduism ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hinduism)) includes reformist Vedanta, temple revival, nationalist politics, anti-caste activism, diaspora temples, yoga movements, meditation teachers, and global spiritual organizations. This range shows that Hindu traditions can be both conservative and experimental.

Hinduism repeatedly negotiates unity and plurality. It has no single founder, creed, or central church, yet shared texts, ideas, rituals, pilgrimage networks, myths, and social patterns give it recognizable continuity.

Its final portrait is of a tradition held together by layered practice rather than uniform doctrine. Hinduism ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hinduism)) persists because it can absorb local cults, philosophical debate, social reform, devotional emotion, ritual precision, and global reinterpretation.
