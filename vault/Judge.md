# Judge

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> A judge is a public official who presides over legal proceedings, rules on law and procedure, and decides cases alone or with juries or lay assessors. The office varies between inquisitorial civil-law systems and adversarial common-law systems, as well as across levels of judicial authority.

## Introduction

A judge is a public official with authority to hear, decide, and preside over legal matters in court. In jury trials, the judge supervises jury selection, explains the relevant law to the jurors, and rules on motions before or during trial. The role differs by legal tradition: civil-law systems usually give judges an inquisitorial role in questioning witnesses and finding facts, while common-law systems usually make lawyers responsible for presenting evidence in an adversarial process.

Judicial roles vary greatly. A rural justice of the peace in the [[United States of America (United States)|United States]] may be legally untrained, part-time, and focused on routine traffic cases. A justice of the U.S. Supreme Court is a full-time professional deciding major national legal questions. Both are judges, but their power, training, prestige, and daily work differ sharply.

Lay judges ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lay_judge)) are common, especially at lower trial levels. England relies heavily on part-time magistrates or justices of the peace, who handle more than 95 percent of criminal cases, while professional judges take the most serious crimes and many civil cases. Other systems use lay judges in religious courts, mixed panels, or assessor roles; [[Japan]] moved in the early 21st century to introduce them.

Professional civil-law judges usually enter the judiciary early as a career branch of public service. After law school and an examination, they may be appointed by a minister of justice, start in a lower court, and advance through official promotion. [[Japan]] strongly represents this model, where the judge is essentially a civil servant.

Professional common-law judges usually reach office after years in practice, teaching, or government legal work. In England, appointments are controlled by the lord chancellor and are relatively nonpartisan. In the [[United States of America (United States)|United States]], many judges are appointed with legislative approval, while many state judges are elected.

The Missouri Plan ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Missouri_Plan)) tries to balance judicial independence with public control. A nominating commission screens candidates, the appointing authority chooses from its list, and the judge later faces a retention ballot. In practice, few judges are removed this way.

Common-law judges often have greater independence, prestige, and freedom from supervision than civil-law judges. They may enter high courts without prior judicial experience, and promotion is not fixed. Discipline usually concerns serious misconduct, impeachment, or newer judicial-discipline systems. Appellate reversal is not discipline; it protects litigants and guides lower courts.
