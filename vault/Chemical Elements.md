# Chemical Elements

## Introduction

Chemical elements ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemical_element)) are substances that cannot be decomposed into simpler substances by ordinary chemical processes. They are the fundamental materials of matter, and their study covers their origin, cosmic abundance, geochemical distribution, and arrangement in the periodic table ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Periodic_table)). Compounds must be treated separately because they are built from elements but have different properties.
There are now 118 known elements. Some occur naturally, while others are synthetic and known only from laboratory production. The discussion that follows moves from the stars to the Earth and then to the patterns that govern where each element is found.

Roughly one-fifth of the known elements are absent from nature or occur only in traces, so laboratory synthesis is part of modern element knowledge. Ordinary conditions also divide elements by state: most are solids, several are gases, and only bromine and mercury are liquids, with cesium and gallium melting near room temperature.

## General Observations

![[attachments/chemical_elements_001.webp|420]]

*Periodic table of the chemical elements*

Elements differ widely in physical state. Some are gases, a few are liquids, and the rest are solids under ordinary conditions. Elements combine to form compounds whose number is almost limitless, and a compound such as water has properties completely different from the hydrogen and oxygen that make it up.

The basic scientific point is that the identity of an element is preserved only if the substance cannot be broken down by ordinary chemical means. That definition is what separates elemental substances from compounds and mixtures.

This definition is chemical rather than nuclear. Hydrogen and oxygen remain elements because ordinary chemistry cannot decompose them further, while water is not an element because chemical processes can separate it into those two substances.

Modern chemistry identifies an element by atomic number, the number of protons in the nucleus. Isotopes of the same element can have different neutron numbers and masses, but they remain the same element chemically because their electron structures are governed by the same nuclear charge.

## Origin of the Elements

Stars are the major sites where elements are made. In the Sun and other stars, hydrogen fuses into helium, and in many stars helium can fuse into heavier elements up to iron. The production of still heavier elements generally requires an input of energy rather than releasing it.

This stellar process changes the chemical composition of stars over time and can eject material into interstellar space. This explains why the chemical composition of the universe is not fixed but evolves as stars are born, live, and die.

Interior fusion cannot be observed directly, so changes in brightness, surface temperature, and stellar instability provide indirect evidence. When unstable stars discharge processed matter, later gas clouds and younger stars inherit a composition already altered by earlier generations.

## Cosmic Abundances

Element abundance is a central clue to origin. Hydrogen and helium are by far the most common elements in the universe, while lithium, beryllium, and boron are unusually rare. There is also a strong abundance peak near iron, which is linked to nuclear stability and binding energy.

Some abundance peaks are associated with so-called magic numbers of neutrons, which indicate especially stable nuclei. These patterns are used to infer how heavy elements may have been built up from lighter ones in stellar and nuclear processes.

## The Solar System and Earth

Stars, meteorites, the Moon, and the Earth can be compared to see whether they share a common composition. Lunar samples from Apollo missions and meteorite analyses suggest that, after allowing for geochemical fractionation and loss of volatiles, the broad chemical composition of the Earth, Moon, Sun, and meteorites may be essentially the same.
Isotopic abundances provide further evidence of related origins. Direct observation is possible for the Earth’s crust, oceans, and atmosphere, but not for the full interior, so scientists rely on indirect geophysical evidence for deeper layers.

## Summary of Observations

The main abundance pattern is consistent across objects in space. Hydrogen and helium dominate, there is a sharp dip for lithium, beryllium, and boron, and then there are peaks and valleys linked to nuclear structure and stellar nucleosynthesis. This is the general shape of the elemental story, even though specific objects differ.

Abundance tables are summaries of large amounts of observational data. The best-known compilation is that of Hans Suess and Harold Urey, and later data refine the details without overturning the basic picture.

## Processes Producing Heavier Elements

Heavier elements can be built from lighter ones in environments where nuclear reactions occur. Since the most tightly bound nuclei lie near iron, there is a tendency for matter to move toward nuclei of maximum binding energy. Stars provide one such environment, but there is also room for other sites of nucleosynthesis.

The observed abundance curve is not random. It reflects nuclear physics, stellar evolution, and the release or absorption of energy during fusion and other reactions.

## Geochemical Distribution of the Elements

The focus then shifts from cosmic chemistry to Earth chemistry. Geochemistry studies the distribution of elements in the crust, mantle, atmosphere, hydrosphere, and interior. The field grew through 19th-century chemical analysis and later large-scale surveys.

Frank W. Clarke is especially important for his work on average crustal composition. He compiled many rock analyses and showed that continental crusts are broadly similar. His work helped establish the idea that average elemental composition could be studied scientifically.

The term clarke came to mean the average percentage of an element in the Earth's crust, reflecting Clarke's influence on geochemical measurement. His results endured because later analytical growth changed details more than the broad crustal pattern.

## The Mantle and Interior

The Earth’s deeper interior cannot be sampled directly, so geophysical data must supplement chemistry. Mantle material, volcanic inclusions, and diamond-bearing pipes in South Africa and Siberia all give clues to deeper composition.

The upper mantle is more variable than the deep mantle, and it consists largely of magnesium-iron silicates, especially olivine-like material. This shows how geochemistry uses indirect evidence to infer the composition of inaccessible regions.

Upper-mantle study matters beyond composition because processes there feed volcanic eruptions, earthquakes, crustal alteration, and ore concentration. Peridotite inclusions and diamond-bearing pipes are rare but valuable samples because their minerals imply origin at great depth and pressure.

## Soils

Soil is a thin but important layer made of inorganic matter, organic compounds, water, living organisms, and gases. It forms by weathering but is strongly shaped by environmental factors such as climate, topography, vegetation, biological activity, and time.

The same parent rock can yield very different soils under different conditions. Tropical soils, for example, are often more heavily leached and less fertile than temperate soils because rainfall, heat, and biological activity alter them more intensely.

## Hydrosphere

Seawater is the dominant reservoir in the hydrosphere, and its composition is remarkably uniform. Sodium chloride makes up most dissolved salts, while magnesium, calcium, and potassium salts account for much of the rest. Salinity varies regionally, but the broad chemical pattern remains stable.

Trace elements and gases vary more than the major components. Biological activity affects nutrients such as phosphorus and silica, which are removed from surface water and redistributed at depth. This shows how living systems and chemistry interact in the oceans.

## Atmosphere

The atmosphere is mostly nitrogen and oxygen, with argon forming most of the remainder and carbon dioxide present only in tiny amounts. Pollution and the chemistry of smog involve reactions among hydrocarbons, nitrogen oxides, oxygen, and ozone.

The ozone layer deserves special attention, since it absorbs harmful ultraviolet radiation and helps stabilize Earth’s surface temperature. The geochemical history of the atmosphere suggests that the present atmosphere is very different from the primitive one, in part because of biological activity such as photosynthesis.

## Conclusion

The elements tell a story of origin, distribution, and classification. Their cosmic birth in stars, their changing abundance in the Earth system, and their ordering in the periodic table are all part of one larger picture in which chemistry and physics overlap.

## Core Equations

Atomic identity is fixed by proton number, while isotopes vary by neutron number:

$$A=Z+N$$

Mass number A is the total of protons Z and neutrons N in an atomic nucleus.

Radioactive decay is commonly modeled exponentially:

$$N(t)=N_0e^{-\lambda t}$$

Exponential decay describes a quantity that decreases by a constant fraction per unit time, as in radioactive decay.

## Key Events & Developments

- 1800: The idea of a limited set of elements becomes established.
- 1869: The periodic table provides a new framework for element relationships.
- 1879: The United States Geological Survey is established.
- 1884: Frank W. Clarke becomes chief chemist.
- 1889: Clarke publishes major work on geochemical distributions.
- 1956: Suess and Urey publish the best-known abundance compilation.
- 1960s: Apollo lunar samples greatly improve knowledge of the Moon’s chemistry.

## Interesting Facts

- Hydrogen and helium dominate the elemental universe.
- Iron sits near the peak of nuclear binding energy.
- Some elements are known only because they were made synthetically.
- The Earth, Moon, and meteorites may share a common broad chemical origin.
- Seawater composition is surprisingly uniform over large areas.
