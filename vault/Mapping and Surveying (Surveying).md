# Mapping and Surveying (Surveying)

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> Surveying measures, interprets, and represents Earth's surface to establish positions, boundaries, construction controls, and spatial information. Its methods, including triangulation, photogrammetry, and hydrography, support mapping, infrastructure, land administration, and environmental development.

## Introduction

*Surveyor using a total station in the field*

Surveying ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surveying)) is the practice of making relatively large-scale, accurate measurements of Earth's surface, reducing and interpreting measurement data, and establishing positions, sizes, construction controls, or land boundaries. It supports mapping, transport, building, land apportionment, and communications, and has been essential to construction, environmental development, and scientific implementation since early history.

## History

Surveying likely began in ancient [[Egypt]], where the Great Pyramid of Khufu, built about 2700 BCE, showed precise squareness and north-south orientation. Boundary surveying appeared by about 1400 BCE in the Tigris, Euphrates, and Nile valleys, with marked cords, rods, boundary stones, and simple A-frame levels used to measure fields and restore property corners after floods.

Greek, Roman, and later European instruments steadily expanded surveying capability. The Greeks used coastal log-line measurement, the astrolabe, and possibly the groma, while Romans standardized right-angle layout, used an odometer-like wheel, water levels for aqueduct grades, and the plane table for recording or establishing angles. Arab traders brought the magnetic compass west in the 12th century.

Modern surveying emerged as instruments improved. Willebrord Snell measured a meridian arc by triangulation in 1615, Edmund Gunter developed a surveying chain in 1620, and theodolites gained verniers, micrometers, telescopic sights, and spirit levels by about 1720. The circle-dividing engine around 1775, photogrammetry around 1920, electronic distance measurement and lasers in the 1960s, satellites, and computers transformed accuracy and speed.

## John Lyman

John Lyman is credited as editor for the modern-surveying section, which explains geodetic control, topographic control, traverses, and the need to account for Earth's curvature. Geodetic surveys reduce baselines to sea-level length, classify work by accuracy orders, and use satellite triangulation to tie continental networks into a world datum and refine Earth's spheroid.

Topographic mapping begins with horizontal and vertical control points, described by latitude, longitude, and elevation. Control surveys use a few accurate measurements to support many shorter detail measurements. Traverses connect marked stations by measured courses and angles; if they close on themselves or superior control, small errors can be adjusted mathematically.

## Triangulation

Triangulation provides superior horizontal control by measuring all angles and at least one side of a triangle system, often quadrangles or chains of quadrangles. Large-area surveys use a reference surface because Earth's curvature matters: the geoid approximates mean sea level, while an oblate spheroid such as Clarke's Spheroid of 1866 provides a mathematical model.

Vertical control uses bench marks connected by precise leveling, with elevations referred to a datum, usually mean sea level adjusted across many observations. Orthometric corrections are needed for long, high, north-south level lines. Trigonometric leveling can determine inaccessible elevations from known points by using horizontal positions and vertical angles.

Global positioning moved from celestial observation with theodolites, chronometers, and time signals to satellite methods. Satellite positions, known from worldwide tracking, allow accurate survey fixes, especially through the Doppler effect. Frameworks then connect reference points by triangles, trilateration, or traverses, with electronic distance measurement using light, infrared, or microwave beams to time round-trip signals.

The theodolite measures horizontal and vertical angles with a telescope pivoted on two axes. In first-order work, repeated readings can detect very small angular differences; targets may be lamps, heliographs, poles, or ranging rods. Framework marks on the ground then support detail surveying, in which features are fixed by angles, distances, bearings, plane-table rays, tachymetry, or modern combined theodolite-EDM-computer instruments.

## Photogrammetric

Photogrammetric surveying uses aerial photographs and satellite images for detail mapping, but photographs must be corrected because vertical objects and relief are displaced from their true map positions. Overlapping flight strips create recognizable control points; templates, studs, and ground control can orient and scale photograph networks, a method once useful for mapping deserts and thinly populated regions.

Accurate contour mapping requires stereoscopic treatment of overlapping photographs. Plotting instruments create a three-dimensional model from paired images, allowing an operator or computer to trace features and contours. Analytic instruments can store coordinates digitally, while photomaps, controlled mosaics, and orthophotographs produce faster and cheaper map products, especially in low-relief rural areas.

## Hydrography

Hydrographic surveying maps underwater features, where the surveyor usually moves and the surface cannot be seen. Early work used dead reckoning, compass bearings, speed, time, and lead-line soundings marked in fathoms, but currents and winds made course and distance uncertain. Echo sounding in the early 20th century greatly improved depth measurement by timing sound pulses reflected from the bottom.

Later sonar, television cameras, radar, satellite positioning, inertial guidance, aircraft images, and satellite images made hydrography more complete and continuous. Sounding records can show bottom shape, fish, mud over rock, wrecks, or rock pinnacles, while cameras and stereoscopic photography document underwater structures such as drilling rigs or ancient shipwrecks.

Height determination above sea level uses spirit leveling, vertical angles and distances, atmospheric pressure differences, and satellite or inertial systems. Spirit leveling is most accurate and can keep error below a centimetre over 100 kilometres in precise work. Trigonometric methods are faster in hilly areas, barometers are rougher but useful for separated points, and satellite or inertial systems support unmapped or large-area surveys.

## Key Events & Developments

- 2700 BCE: The Great Pyramid of Khufu demonstrated precise ancient surveying.
- 1400 BCE: Boundary surveying appeared in ancient river valleys.
- 1615: Willebrord Snell measured a meridian arc by triangulation.
- 1620: Edmund Gunter developed a surveying chain.
- 1775: The circle-dividing engine advanced portable angle measurement.
- 1920: Photogrammetry began transforming mapping from aerial photographs.
- 1960s: Electronic distance measurement and lasers changed surveying practice.

## Interesting Facts

- Surveying ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surveying)) has two opposite functions: determining existing positions and establishing new marks or boundaries.
- The Roman odometer dropped a pebble at each wheel revolution to measure distance.
- A corner mirror returns light toward its source from many receiving angles.
- Echo sounding depends on sound traveling through water at about 5,000 feet per second.
