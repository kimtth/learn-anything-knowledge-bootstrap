# Collective Behaviour

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> Collective behaviour examines how people act in groups outside or alongside established institutions, including crowds, movements, panics, fads, and public responses to shared situations.

## Introduction

Collective behaviour ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Collective_behavior)) refers to the spontaneous activities of sizable but loosely organized groups of people. Such episodes tend to arise from an experience shared by the members of a group that produces a sense of common interest and identity. The defining characteristic is the informality of the group's structure, which is the principal source of the frequent unpredictability of collective behaviour. Its scope is broad, encompassing the activities of people in crowds, panics, fads, fashions, crazes, publics, cults, and followings, as well as more organized phenomena such as reform and revolutionary social movements.

## Milling

Prior to most instances of collective behaviour there is a period during which people move about in a somewhat agitated but aimless way. Early students of crowd behaviour noticed the resemblance to the milling of cattle before a stampede and applied the term to this human phenomenon. The characteristic physical restlessness can be seen in an audience waiting for a late-starting program to begin or among citizens who have just received news of an assassination attempt. In the first case people scuffle their feet, leave their seats, and sometimes join spontaneously in rhythmic behaviour such as foot stamping. In the second case people abandon routine activities and begin talking with neighbours, friends, and strangers. Milling also involves scanning others for emotional cues — sweating, nervousness, changes in tone of voice — as participants try to read the shared situation.

## Rumour

### Rumour-creating situations

Rumour ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rumor)) abounds under specific, identifiable circumstances. The U.S. psychologists Gordon W. Allport and Leo Postman offered the foundational generalization that rumour intensity is high when both the interest in an event and its ambiguity are great. The sociologist Tamotsu Shibutani agreed, contending that rumour arises when the demand for news exceeds the supply available through institutional channels. To interest and ambiguity, two further conditions must be added as prerequisites. Rumour flourishes when a group shares the need to act but is reluctant to do so until the situation is better defined. It also thrives when the situation requires that group members act in concert rather than individually — shared stakes demand shared understanding.

### Stages of rumour spreading

Rumour does not spread passively but passes through recognizable transformations. It tends to undergo levelling, in which details are dropped and the story grows shorter and more concise with each retelling. Simultaneously it undergoes sharpening, in which the few details that survive become more vivid and prominent. The third process is assimilation, in which the content is unconsciously reshaped to fit the existing attitudes, expectations, and cultural frameworks of those passing it on. Together these three processes reveal that participants are not simply transmitting information but are collaboratively constructing an interpretation of a situation that is ambiguous but perceived as urgent and consequential.

## Disaster Response

Disasters produce a distinctive sequence of collective behaviour that moves through recognizable stages. During the impact and warning period, communities receive signals of impending danger and face the question of how to respond. The credibility of warnings, prior experience with the type of disaster, and the clarity of official communications heavily influence whether people take protective action or continue normal routines. Ambiguity about the reality or severity of the threat during this stage can itself generate rumour and milling. An equally important early phase is the organization period, during which communities undergo a characteristic fragmentation. Normal social bonds and routines are disrupted, and the community may temporarily lose coherence before a new shared orientation can form.

## Fads

It is tempting to explain fads entirely on the basis of prestige. Prestige is gained by being among the first and most adept at a skill that others covet, and the skill loses its prestige value when it is no longer scarce — an important reason for the abrupt end of many fads. But motives sustaining fads are complex and varied. The exhilaration of joining a community of enthusiasts in an intense shared preoccupation, and the joy of mastering something genuinely novel, are motivators that cannot be reduced to status competition alone.

### Hysterical contagion

Occasionally waves of fear find expression in a rash of false perceptions and symptoms of physical illness, in a phenomenon known as hysterical contagion. In a documented case, a group of women working in a textile factory reported being bitten by a "june bug" on the premises, suffering nausea, dizziness, and faintness. Investigation revealed no insect capable of producing such effects. The episode spread through social networks within the workplace, moving faster among workers who were friends and slowing where social contact was interrupted by shift patterns or job roles. The case illustrates that physical symptoms can be socially transmitted through collective behaviour mechanisms without any physical cause, wherever anxiety, shared expectations, and dense social contact combine.

## Fashion

Fashion ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fashion)) resembles fads and other collective obsessions, but it is institutionalized and regularized — continuous rather than sporadic, and at least partially predictable. Where fads often emerge from the lower echelons of society and thereby constitute a potential challenge to the class structure, fashion generally flows from higher social levels to lower ones, providing a continuous verification of class differences. The upper classes adopt new styles; those styles gradually spread downward; and the upper classes must then adopt still newer styles to maintain their distinctiveness.

## Crazes

The term craze is not analytically separate from fad and fashion, but it carries somewhat different connotations. It frequently refers to a collective focus on important figures in the entertainment or sports world. Fans of personalities such as Rudolph Valentino, Frank Sinatra, James Dean, the Beatles, Michael Jackson, and Pelé idolize these figures, relish and mimic real or imaginary details of their private lives, and often form clubs or societies to share their fascination. In many cases crazes suffer the same fate as fads — they die abruptly when the central figure loses novelty or public standing. In some cases, however, figures such as Sinatra and the Beatles outlast the craze itself and endure as respected public figures long after the initial frenzy has subsided.

## Crowds

A thin line separates crowd activities from collective obsessions such as fads and crazes. Four characteristics mark the distinction. The crowd is more concentrated in time and space — a race riot, a lynching, or a mass religious gathering is bounded by a few hours or days and by a limited physical area, ranging from a city square or stadium to a section of a metropolitan area. The majority of crowd participants share a collaborative goal rather than pursuing parallel individual goals: where fad participants each spin a hoop for their own satisfaction, crowd participants act together toward a shared objective. Because the goal is collaborative, there is more division of labour and cooperative activity within a crowd than in other forms of collective behaviour. Finally, a major concern of a crowd is with some improvement or social change expected to result from its collective action — labour rioters expect management to become more compliant; participants in a massive religious revival expect life in the community to improve.

## Panic

The word panic is often applied to a strictly individual, maladaptive reaction of flight, immobility, or disorganization stemming from intense fear. A student who freezes during an examination, unable to recall known material, is in individual panic. A disaster victim who flees mild danger directly into greater danger provides another example. Individual panic occurs as a unique personal response without necessarily triggering a similar reaction in others. Collective panic, however, is shared behaviour in which a group pattern of orderly conduct is replaced by a group pattern of fear and disorganization. When an entire military unit breaks into disorderly flight, the panic is collective rather than merely personal — the group response itself is transformed.

## Contingencies

How the immediate effects of collective behaviour are translated into long-term social consequences depends on several contingencies. The first is the nature of the response by authorities. Evidence suggests that alarmed and repressive reactions tend to strengthen polarization between collective behaviour participants and the authorities. Moderate responses that acknowledge grievances while maintaining order tend to strengthen the position of mediating voices within the affected population. Inaction or ineffectual response, on the other hand, facilitates efforts toward usurpation of authority, giving more radical elements greater influence over subsequent developments.

## Key Events & Developments

- Robert E. Park coins the term collective behaviour in early 20th-century American sociology, establishing the field's conceptual foundation and distinguishing it from both individual psychology and organized group action.
- The South Sea Bubble in early 18th-century Britain becomes a defining case study of financial craze: speculation drives asset values far above intrinsic worth and ends in catastrophic collapse, with the king of England among the investors who lost money.
- The june bug epidemic in a textile factory — in which dozens of women report imaginary insect bites and suffer genuine physical symptoms — becomes a documented case study of hysterical contagion spreading through workplace social networks.
- The Hula-Hoop craze illustrates the full lifecycle of a recreational fad: explosive adoption following a key innovation, sustained by the capacity for individual variation, followed by abrupt decline when variations are exhausted.
- 20th-century race riots in American cities become major sociological case studies demonstrating how milling, rumour, and the formation of common mood transform routine incidents into large-scale collective violence.
- Systematic studies of disaster communities in mid-20th-century America document the characteristic four-stage sequence: initial fragmentation, unnatural solidarity and altruism, and the eventual brickbat period in which old tensions resurface.

## Key People

- Robert E. Park ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_E._Park)): U.S. sociologist who coined "collective behaviour" and defined it as behaviour driven by a common collective impulse produced through social interaction rather than by rules or authority.
- Gordon W. Allport ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gordon_W._Allport)): U.S. psychologist who with Leo Postman formulated the foundational rule for rumour intensity: rumour thrives when both interest in an event and ambiguity about it are simultaneously high.
- Leo Postman ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leo_Postman)): Co-author with Allport of the interest-ambiguity formula and researcher on the processes of levelling, sharpening, and assimilation through which rumour is transformed as it spreads.
- Tamotsu Shibutani ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tamotsu_Shibutani)): U.S. sociologist who argued that rumour arises when collective demand for news outpaces the supply provided through official institutional channels, framing rumour as a form of collective problem-solving.
- Neil J. Smelser ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neil_J._Smelser)): Major theorist of collective behaviour who developed a systematic framework for analysing the structural conditions under which different collective forms — from fads to revolutions — emerge.

## Interesting Facts

- The term milling is borrowed directly from animal behaviour: early sociologists observed that people in pre-crowd situations moved in the same aimless, agitated circles that cattle trace before a stampede.
- A fad can spread through scientific research communities following the same mechanics as consumer fads: the discovery of a potent new drug triggers a rush to test it in every conceivable context until innovative possibilities are exhausted.
- Fashion ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fashion)) is structurally dependent on class inequality — if all social classes could adopt new styles simultaneously and at equal cost, the mechanism that drives continuous change would cease to operate.
- In the most extreme instances of collective panic, the four distinguishing features combine to produce trampling: people simultaneously attempt to use the same limited escape routes, with lethal results for those at the back of the crowd.
- The king of England personally lost money in the South Sea Bubble — illustrating how even the highest-status endorsements can amplify a financial craze without protecting participants from its eventual collapse.
- Counter-movements that adopt a backlash strategy are often self-defeating: their extremism discredits them publicly and, over the long run, pushes moderate opinion toward the very reforms they oppose.
- Disaster communities exhibit a powerful brickbat period in which post-rescue solidarity dissolves and suppressed tensions are released — yet despite critical opportunities for change, communities overwhelmingly choose to rebuild on their original social and physical foundations.
