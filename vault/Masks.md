# Masks

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> Masks are face coverings that conceal one identity while creating another in ritual, funerary, theatrical, military, therapeutic, and festival settings. Their materials and symbolism reveal how performance, belief, power, and social transformation are expressed across cultures.

## Introduction

![[attachments/masks_001.webp|420]]

*African mask*

A mask is a disguise or concealment, usually worn over or before the face, that hides one identity and establishes another. Across the world since the Stone Age, masks have varied widely in form, use, and symbolism, but they always combine hiding and revealing.

## Forms and Materials

![[attachments/masks_002.webp|420]]

*Japanese Noh mask*

The mask of Quetzalcoatl shows how masks can use precious materials and religious imagery to create a new being. Masks range from simple false faces to full head coverings with moving parts, made from wood, metal, shell, fibre, ivory, clay, horn, stone, feathers, leather, fur, paper, cloth, and corn husks.

## What Masks Represent

![[attachments/masks_003.webp|420]]

*Venetian carnival mask*

In societies such as the Southern Igbo Ekpe, masks often represent spirits, ancestors, beauty, peace, or social authority. A mask is normally understood with its costume and performance, because costume completes the new identity and hides the wearer's ordinary features.

Masks may be anthropomorphic, with human features, or theriomorphic, with animal features. They may be realistic, abstract, portrait-like, ancestral, supernatural, or fanciful. In ritual, the represented spirit must be present in the mask, or petitions and offerings would have no force.

Ceremonial mask making is usually the work of skilled artisans or sculptors. Materials, tools, and the growing form of the mask may be believed to hold spiritual power, so creation follows taboos and rituals that protect the maker and ensure the mask's force.

## Making and Wearing Masks

The Kuskokwim Yupik walrus mask illustrates the belief that animal, human, and spirit forms can join in one object. Many traditions require mask makers to follow inherited forms and imagery, yet a gifted artist can still give the required image vitality, beauty, and expressive strength.

Mask wearing also carries danger and power. The performer follows sanctioned procedures, dances, poses, and often acts with music. Covered by mask and costume, the wearer loses ordinary identity and may become a partner of, or seem psychologically united with, the spirit character.

Spectators treat ritual masks as consecrated objects linked to early times. They connect the present with origins, especially in cultures without written history, and may produce awe, fear, gaiety, catharsis, or near-frenzy. Some masks show harmful spirits used to preserve social and political balance.

Meaning cannot be read from appearance alone. Similar-looking masks in unrelated cultures can have opposite functions, and frightening forms may be protective rather than evil. Aesthetic value can be judged as art, but full artistic meaning depends on cultural use, function, and local standards.

The preservation of masks follows tradition. Some pass through clans, families, societies, or individuals and are repainted or redecorated without changing their symbolism. Others are used once and discarded or burned. Only in the late 19th and early 20th centuries were many studied outside ceremony as art or artifacts.

Masks serve religious, social, funerary, festive, theatrical, military, protective, hunting, and fertility purposes. They may cure disease, discipline wrongdoers, portray myth, protect in war or sport, reenact origins, or allow seasonal release and disguise.

## Oceanic Masks

In New Guinea, New Ireland, and New Caledonia, masks can represent fanciful birds, fishes, and animals with exaggerated features. Materials such as grass, palm fibre, coconut, shells, and wood create images tied to totems, spirits, and social protection.

## New Guinea Painted Masks

Painted masks from New Guinea and related regions often use bark cloth over frames and combine human and animal forms. Priests and shamans may use powerful totem masks to cure disease, punish enemies, find game or fish, predict weather, and exorcise evil spirits.

## Funerary Masks

Funerary masks honour the dead, protect them, guide spirits, or force spirits to depart. Ancient Egyptians placed stylized masks on the dead from the Middle Kingdom to the 1st century CE, while the golden mask of Tutankhamen, made about 1350 BCE, is among the most splendid burial masks.

## Ancient Mexican Burial Masks

Ancient Mexican burial masks often seem to be generic rather than personal portraits. Aztec skull masks could be inlaid with turquoise and lignite, with pyrites in the eye sockets, and may have been hung or worn in rites connected with the dead.

## Mesoamerican Burial Masks

The mask found near Texcoco, Mex., belongs to this broader ancient Mexican tradition of stone, skull, or burial masks. Such works show how death, portraiture, and symbolic identity could be joined without necessarily making an individual likeness.

## Therapeutic, Military, and Hunting Masks

Teotihuacan and other ancient American traditions used masks in burial and commemorative contexts. In Rome, wax imagines could preserve ancestors, while in Europe from the late Middle Ages to the 18th century effigy masks represented nobles and royalty in processions and ceremonies.

Therapeutic masks fight [[Disease|disease]] through ritual. Iroquois False Face healers used grimacing masks to exorcise demons, Chinese and Burmese communities used epidemic masks, and Sinhalese traditions in Sri Lanka developed 19 sickness-demon masks with fierce, fanged forms.

Military and protective masks frighten enemies or shield the body. Greek and Roman warriors used grotesque faces on shields or armour, Japanese samurai used menpo half masks, and modern fencers, catchers, skiers, and hockey goalies use protective masks that may also appear threatening.

Hunting and fertility masks reach into prehistory. Disguise masks may have helped hunters stalk animals or placate slain spirits. Iroquois corn-husk masks gave harvest thanks, Hopi and Zuni katsina dancers embodied rain-bringing spirits, and Bambara antelope masks in Mali symbolized agricultural fertility.

## Greek Theatre Masks

Masks in [[Athens]] belonged to ancient Greek visual and theatrical traditions. Greek drama grew from Dionysian worship, where disguise made the god present, and later used leather or painted-canvas headpieces to show age, rank, occupation, and character at a distance.

## Festival Masks

In Rottweil, Germany, wooden masks appear in pre-Lenten celebrations. Festival masks in Europe and Latin America support license, humour, anonymity, and seasonal change, including Halloween, Mardi Gras, masked balls, winter-driving rites, and folk dances such as the Dance of the Old Men.

## Theatrical Masks

In Guatemala, masked dance-dramas such as Moros y cristianos use masks for storytelling and caricature. Similar comic or satiric festival masks appear among Inuit groups and Igbo peoples, showing how disguise can create temporary comic identity.

Theatrical masks make characters visible in a physical, lasting form within a fleeting performance. Greek theatre used masks to let few actors play many roles, medieval mystery plays used papier-mache devils and demons, and Italian commedia dell'arte spread masked stock characters through Europe from the 15th to 18th centuries.

## Masks in Modern Theatre

Modern Western theatre sometimes revived masks, as in Gerhart Hauptmann's The Sunken Bell, W.B. Yeats's Noh-influenced plays, Eugene O'Neill's The Great God Brown, and later works influenced by Cubism and Surrealism. Yet masks lost their central theatrical role in the 20th-century West.

Japanese Noh preserved a major mask tradition from the 14th century. About 125 named varieties fall into types such as old persons, gods, goddesses, devils, and goblins. Their carved, lacquered, and gilded forms can seem to change expression through slight actor movement.

Tibetan, Sikkimese, Bhutanese, Chinese, Japanese, Javanese, and Balinese performance masks show other theatrical traditions. They appear in exorcism, sacred drama, lion and dragon dances, Kabuki-like painted faces, wayang wong dance dramas, and protective performances derived from shadow plays and epics.

In the 20th century, masks became widely seen as decorative objects and tourist products, while ethnographers and artists continued to value them. African and western Oceanic masks influenced modern artists such as Pablo Picasso, Henri Matisse, and Andre Derain.

## Conclusion

Masks ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mask)) are best understood as cultural objects, ritual instruments, performance partners, art forms, and symbols whose meanings depend on their living social settings.

## Key Events & Developments

- Stone Age: Masks ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mask)) were already used as disguise, ritual objects, or hunting aids.
- About 1350 BCE: The gold funerary mask of Tutankhamen was made.
- 6th century BCE: Greek theatrical masking was linked with Thespis and tragedy.
- 15th century: Italian commedia dell'arte developed a major masked theatre tradition.
- 20th century: Masks became influential in modern art and were also produced for tourism.

## Key People

- Tutankhamen ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tutankhamen)): Egyptian pharaoh whose gold burial mask is a famous funerary example.
- Thespis ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thespis)): Greek poet credited with helping originate tragedy and theatrical masking.
- W.B. Yeats ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/W.B._Yeats)): Revived masks in plays influenced by Japanese Noh.
- Eugene O'Neill ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eugene_O%27Neill)): Used face masks in The Great God Brown to show inner and outer character.
- Pablo Picasso ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pablo_Picasso)): Modern artist inspired by African and Oceanic mask forms.

## Interesting Facts

- Masks ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mask)) can hide a person while revealing a spirit, ancestor, social role, disease demon, or comic identity.
- A frightening mask does not necessarily represent evil; it may protect, heal, or maintain order.
- Some masks are carefully preserved for generations, while others are burned or discarded after one ceremony.
- Japanese Noh masks can appear to change expression through small movements by the performer.
