# Heraldry

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> Heraldry is the system of armorial symbols used to identify people, families, institutions, and states, originating in medieval warfare, tournaments, and ceremony. Its rules of recognizable design and the work of heralds turned shields, banners, and coats of arms into durable systems of social and political representation.

## Introduction

Heraldry ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heraldry)) is the system of symbolic devices used to identify individuals, families, institutions, and states. These devices, called armorial bearings ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Achievement_%28heraldry%29)), developed from the practical needs of medieval warfare and ceremonial display. Their purpose was to make a person or group recognizable at a glance, especially in tournaments, battles, and formal occasions. The strict meaning of heraldry is the office and duty of a herald, while armory is the part dealing specifically with coats of arms.
The word heraldry has broadened over time. In strict terms it refers to the work of heralds and the rules of armorial design, but in common use it has come to mean the whole art and science of coats of arms and related symbols.

## Origins and Development

![[attachments/heraldry_001.webp|420]]

*Hyghalmen Roll Late 1400s*

Heraldry began in the 12th century, when heralds and minstrels were responsible for recognizing banners, shields, and the deeds of knights. As tournaments and warfare became more formal, the need for precise and memorable symbols grew. Simplicity was essential in the early medieval period, because a knight’s identity had to be recognized quickly on the battlefield.

Early heralds combined messenger, announcer, genealogist, and tournament official roles, and their interest in ancestry grew from the need to praise and identify nobles accurately.

Over time, heraldry became more elaborate. Shields, helmets, crests, mantlings, mottoes, supporters, and badges were all added to the basic display. The design of arms therefore became both a practical sign of identity and a highly developed artistic language.
Early heraldry depended on visibility. Simple geometric divisions, bright tinctures, and easily recognized beasts or objects worked well on shields, banners, seals, and horse trappings. As heraldry moved from battle to ceremony, genealogy, and law, designs became more complex and more regulated.

Heralds became officials who could identify arms, proclaim tournaments, record pedigrees, and supervise ceremonial rank. Their knowledge turned practical recognition into a professional discipline, and their records became important historical evidence.

Heraldic records preserved names, offices, marriages, grants, and claims of descent, making them useful for both ceremony and later historical research.

Inheritance also required distinction. Cadency marks, quartering, impalement, and other devices could show birth order, marriage alliances, combined claims, or institutional identity while preserving the recognizable core of a coat of arms.

## Structure of Arms

An achievement has several main parts. The shield is the central element, while the helmet, crest, motto, supporters, and compartment complete the display. Badges and ordinaries such as crosses, chevrons, pales, and fesses are used as recurring geometric and symbolic features.

Heraldry is governed by rules of language and form. A coat of arms is described through blazon, a formal method of naming and ordering its elements. This language was designed to be concise and precise, allowing a herald to describe a design without ambiguity.

Blazon follows a fixed order: field, divisions, charges, tinctures, positions, and additional elements are described in a specialized vocabulary. This makes heraldry a visual language that can be reconstructed from words. A correct blazon is therefore more than a caption; it is the legal and technical identity of the arms.

Rules of tincture are also important. Metals such as gold and silver are normally not placed on metals, and colours are normally not placed on colours, because contrast is essential for recognition. This rule reflects heraldry’s origin in the need for clear identification from a distance.
Charges are the figures placed on the shield. They may be animals, plants, tools, weapons, stars, crosses, or invented creatures. Lions, eagles, fleurs-de-lis, crosses, and chevrons became especially common because they were visually strong and easy to stylize.

Crests, mantling, supporters, mottoes, and compartments added layers of identity beyond the shield. These elements could express rank, office, lineage, alliance, or institutional meaning. A full achievement of arms therefore works like a compact symbolic biography.

Mottoes are not technically part of the arms in most countries and may be war cries, puns, exhortations, or later explanations attached to a family or institution.

## Cadency, Differentiation, and Modern Use

Cadency explains how families distinguish their arms from those of other branches. Marks such as labels, crescents, mullets, and annulets were used to show a younger son or another branch of the same family. These marks were once important but are now largely historical in many countries.

Modern heraldry survives in national and institutional settings. It continues in the British royal family, in Scottish heraldry, and in national offices such as those in Ireland and South Africa. In the United States, arms have been granted and recorded in a more flexible and less noble-centered way than in Europe.

Heraldry also developed legal and administrative institutions. In England, heralds and the College of Arms recorded pedigrees and grants; in Scotland, the Lord Lyon exercised strong authority over arms; and other countries developed their own offices or customs. These institutions show that heraldry is connected to law, inheritance, and public recognition.

Modern heraldry survives in universities, cities, military units, churches, corporations, and states. Its social meaning has changed: it is no longer only a sign of noble lineage, but it remains a compact symbolic language for identity, continuity, and authority.
[[Inheritance and Succession (Inheritance)|Inheritance]] and difference are central to heraldry. Arms could pass through families, be quartered through marriage, be differenced for younger branches, and be combined to show descent or alliance. These rules made heraldry useful for genealogy but also made it complex, because a single shield could record several claims at once.

National traditions differed. English heraldry developed through royal heralds and the College of Arms, Scottish heraldry maintained especially strict legal control through the Lord Lyon, and continental systems often followed different rules about nobility, municipal arms, and corporate identity. Heraldry was never one universal code applied identically everywhere.

Modern heraldic practice also moved beyond aristocratic families. States, provinces, cities, universities, military units, churches, and civic bodies use arms to express history, jurisdiction, and collective identity. This broader use explains why heraldry remains relevant even where hereditary privilege has declined.

Institutional arms keep heraldry active by adapting inherited visual rules to civic, military, educational, and national communities.

Badges and banners extend this system beyond the shield. Badges can identify governments, clans, or properties, while banners and standards display arms or devices in flag form.

## International and Historical Significance

Heraldry ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heraldry)) is more than decoration. It has played an important role in history, law, literature, and the identification of buildings, manuscripts, and paintings. It also shows how symbols can preserve family memory, political authority, and cultural identity across centuries.

Heraldry helps historians date monuments, identify patrons, trace marriages, and interpret manuscripts or seals. Because arms are inherited, combined, differenced, and granted under rules, they can preserve information about family descent, political allegiance, office, and property.
Seals and rolls of arms are especially valuable evidence because they preserve early designs, names, occasions, and claims before later heraldic writing became more fanciful.
Heraldry ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heraldry)) remains attractive because it joins art, law, memory, and identity in a disciplined visual system. Even when its military purpose disappeared, its ability to condense history into symbols kept it useful for states, cities, schools, military units, and families.

## Key Events & Developments

- 12th century: Heraldry began to develop as a system of recognition in warfare and tournaments.
- 15th–16th centuries: Heraldic literature expanded and became more formalized.
- 1954: The Court of Chivalry in England was revived.
- 1962: South Africa established a Bureau of Heraldry.
