# Libraries

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> Libraries preserve, organize, and provide access to recorded knowledge, from books and manuscripts to databases and networked digital resources. Their evolution into information services raises questions of cataloging, copyright, preservation, access, and the changing professional role of librarians.

## Introduction

A library was traditionally a collection of books for reading or study, or the room or building that held it. Since the middle of the 20th century, libraries have become broad information services that may include books, manuscripts, journals, audiovisual media, databases, and remote digital resources.

Computers, telecommunications, and networks created digital libraries ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Digital_library)) and virtual libraries, where users can reach information without entering a building. This shift raised new problems of metadata, copyright, licensing, preservation, and unequal control over resources that libraries do not own outright.

The role of librarians expanded with this change. They still acquire, organize, preserve, and provide access to information, but they also teach users how to search electronic networks, manage local community information, work with computer centers, and preserve fragile print or digital records for future centuries.

## Origins in Archives and Records

The historical account begins with archives and record rooms, because early libraries often preserved the business, legal, religious, and historical records of whole civilizations.

Ancient libraries appeared wherever records were kept. Nippur had rooms of clay tablets in the first half of the 3rd millennium BCE, Ashurbanipal collected about 25,000 tablets in Assyria, and ancient [[China]] developed civil-service libraries and classification systems after earlier destruction of records under Shih huang-ti.

Greek libraries grew from temples, philosophical schools, and private collectors. [[Aristotle and Aristotelianism|Aristotle]]'s organized Peripatetic collection helped shape the Library of Alexandria ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Library_of_Alexandria)), founded under the Ptolemies to collect Greek literature systematically and staffed by scholars such as Callimachus, Eratosthenes, Aristophanes of Byzantium, and Aristarchus of Samothrace.

## The Library of Pergamum

The library of Pergamum ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Library_of_Pergamum)) in Asia Minor rivaled Alexandria under Attalus I Soter and Eumenes II. Parchment was said to have developed there after Egypt restricted papyrus export, and parchment became important because it was more durable than papyrus.

## Roman Libraries

Classical [[Rome]] had many private libraries and later public ones. Julius Caesar planned a public library, Asinius Pollio built one soon after Caesar's death, and emperors such as Tiberius, Vespasian, and Trajan created collections, including the Bibliotheca Ulpia.

## Byzantine Libraries

![[attachments/libraries_003.webp|420]]

*13-11-02-olb-by-RalfR-03*

Constantinople preserved Greek learning through imperial, patriarchal, and scholarly libraries. Although epitomes sometimes replaced originals, the Byzantine libraries transmitted much of the Greek classical inheritance to western Europe.

The [[Islamic World|Islamic world]] built libraries from sacred texts, literacy, translation, and papermaking. Mosques in Jerusalem and Damascus held texts, Umayyad and Abbasid rulers built collections, and by the 10th century Baghdad and Cordoba had major book markets and libraries that later fed learning back into the West.

Medieval European monastic libraries copied and preserved manuscripts in scriptoria. Benedictine and other houses such as Monte Cassino, Bobbio, Fulda, Canterbury, Wearmouth, and Jarrow kept religious, historical, philosophical, and classical works, sometimes lending books to other monasteries or the public.

The Renaissance, printing, commerce, and lay literacy widened book collecting. Humanists such as Petrarch, Boccaccio, Niccolo Niccoli, and Poggio Bracciolini hunted classical manuscripts, while princely collections such as the Medici, Vatican, Escorial, and Matthias Corvinus libraries helped create public and national collections.

The Reformation destroyed many monastic libraries, especially in England and northern Europe, but also encouraged new town and university libraries. Martin Luther urged German towns in 1524 to spend on libraries, while later religious wars moved whole collections as spoils.

## Bibliotheque Nationale

The Bibliotheque Nationale grew from the Bibliotheque du Roi, traced to Charles V. Legal deposit was reaffirmed in 1617, major departments formed in the 18th century, and revolutionary confiscations added huge aristocratic and church collections.

By the 19th century, cataloging and national service became central. Leopold Delisle completed a card catalog and began a printed catalog, while the modern library helped lead French national library service and professional training.

## The British Library

![[attachments/libraries_002.webp|420]]

*George-peabody-library*

The British Library began within the British Museum after the 1753 acceptance of Sir Hans Sloane's collections, joined with the Cotton, Harley, and Royal Library collections. It gained legal deposit and later added collections such as those of George III and Thomas Grenville.

Under Sir Anthony Panizzi, the printed catalog was issued between 1881 and 1905. The British Library Act of 1972 separated the library from the museum, creating reference, lending, and bibliographic services from older national institutions.

## The BLAISE Service

The BLAISE service offered cataloging facilities to participating libraries. British bibliographic services also worked with the Library of Congress on MARC, giving machine-readable access to catalog data and current acquisitions.

The Library of Congress, founded in 1800, lost books in the 1814 burning of the Capitol and rebuilt partly through the purchase of Thomas Jefferson's library. Its classification, catalog cards, MARC work, preservation research, and National Union Catalog strongly influenced world librarianship.

## Russian State Library

![[attachments/libraries_001.webp|420]]

*Austria - Göttweig Abbey - 2015*

The Russian State Library in Moscow, formerly the Lenin Library, functions as Russia's national library. It receives copies of national publications, distributes copies to specialist libraries, supports cooperative catalogs, organizes lending and exchanges, and formerly produced a Marxist-Leninist classification scheme.

Other national libraries include major institutions in [[Belgium]], The Netherlands, [[Italy]], [[Germany]], Austria, [[Australia]], [[China]], [[Japan]], and [[India]]. National libraries usually preserve national literature, issue bibliographies, and receive legal deposit.

University, research, public, special, school, private, and subscription libraries serve different users. Public libraries promote literacy and community information, special libraries answer specialist needs, school libraries support teaching media, private libraries can become major public resources, and subscription libraries influenced reading before free public libraries spread.

## Archives

Archives ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Archive)) preserve papers, documents, photographs, and unique records created through public or private activity. Unlike libraries, which collect selected materials, archives receive organic bodies of documents and try to preserve their original order and integrity.

Modern archive administration gained importance after the French Revolution, when the state accepted responsibility for public records and public access. Public archives preserve official records, while private archives from universities, businesses, churches, and professional bodies are valued for social and economic history.

## The Librarian as Information Specialist

The operational sections show the shift from the librarian as keeper of records to information specialist, adviser, cataloger, classifier, manager, and technology user.

Professional training developed through institutions such as Melvil Dewey's library school at Columbia in 1887, University College London in 1919, and later university or national programs in Europe, China, Africa, and the Caribbean. Training moved from historical bibliography toward indexing, abstracting, information technology, and information transfer.

Library materials changed with technology. Ancient records used clay, stone, palm leaves, bamboo, papyrus, parchment, vellum, and paper; rare books and manuscripts became preservation responsibilities in national, university, and private research libraries.

## Photographs

[[Photography (Photography, History of)|Photography]] created new records for local history, film, television, and research. Microfilm saved space by preserving newspapers and periodicals, while microfiche improved access because users could go directly to a section rather than search a whole roll.

Libraries also collected audiovisual materials such as records, tapes, compact discs, oral histories, videotapes, films, slides, photographs, and artifacts. Magnetic tapes and discs required careful labeling, indexing, equipment, programming, and protection against accidental erasure.

Indexes and abstracts provide access to articles and documents. Electronic indexes, CD-ROMs, OPAC expansion, and databases allowed users to search titles, abstracts, authors, subjects, journal articles, and sometimes full texts.

## Cataloging

Cataloging identifies library items and groups like materials together. Early catalogs were lists or inventories on clay, stone, papyrus, parchment, palm leaves, bamboo, cards, books, or later machine-readable records.

Author catalogs were once dominant because a writer's name gave a work identity, but subject access grew as science and technology produced more team-based journal literature. Researchers often need subjects rather than author names to find relevant work.

## The Sui Dynasty Classification

The Sui Dynasty History classified writings into Confucian classics, historical records, philosophical writings, and miscellaneous works. This example shows that subject grouping and classification were already important in early Chinese bibliography.

Catalog systems include dictionary catalogs, divided catalogs, and classed catalogs. Dictionary catalogs mix author, title, and subject alphabetically; divided catalogs separate subject entries; classed catalogs arrange entries by classification scheme and are useful across languages.

## Computerized Catalogs

Computerized catalogs overcame delays and filing labor in card systems. The MARC II project from 1968 let records enter central data files and move through telecommunications links, while OPACs let users search words, combine subjects, reserve materials, and see loan status.

Catalog standardization produced rules such as British Museum Rules, Vatican Rules, Prussian Instructions, Anglo-American Catalog Rules, and AACR2. Subject-heading lists such as Sears, Library of Congress Subject Headings, and MeSH supported consistent subject access.

## Thesauri

A thesaurus in library science is a controlled list of authorized indexing terms with cross-references. H.P. Luhn at IBM helped shape this usage in the early 1950s, and Jean Aitchison's Thesaurofacet of 1969 became an early major engineering thesaurus.

Controlled vocabulary helps gather synonyms under one consistent term, such as choosing one form for travel in Great Britain. Its weakness is slow adaptation to new language, so computerized systems often combine controlled terms with free vocabulary, abstracts, and summaries.

## Classification

Classification ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taxonomy)) arranges subjects and documents in a helpful sequence. Unlike catalogs, classification schemes can be shared by many libraries and can organize shelf order, subject catalogs, and broad relationships of knowledge.

## The Dewey Decimal system

The Dewey Decimal Classification, devised by Melvil Dewey in 1873 and published in 1876, was the first modern library classification scheme. It used decimals to show hierarchical divisions from broad classes to specific topics.

The chemistry schedule shows hierarchical notation, with 540 for chemistry and allied sciences, 541 for physical and theoretical chemistry, and further decimal subdivisions for more specific topics such as solutions and photochemistry.

Dewey also used mnemonics, allowing many subjects to be subdivided geographically. A historical-geographic number could be added as a decimal to create a recognizable subject-place relationship.

Combining art and geography can produce a number such as 709.73 for history of art in the United States. This example shows how the Dewey system builds meaning through combined schedules.

## The Universal Decimal system

The Universal Decimal Classification ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Universal_Decimal_Classification)), published in 1905, adapted Dewey for a universal bibliography of books and periodical articles. Paul Otlet and Henri-Marie Lafontaine made it more detailed and synthetic, using auxiliary tables and symbols such as the colon.

The Universal Decimal Classification ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Universal_Decimal_Classification)) was translated into many languages and used especially in scientific, technical, European, and Asian libraries. Later classification theory also drew on the Library of Congress system, Bliss, Ranganathan's Colon Classification, and Marxist schemes.

The Library of Congress system used letters and numbers in a pragmatic shelf-based arrangement. Henry E. Bliss emphasized synthesis and knowledge organization, while S.R. Ranganathan introduced facet analysis through personality, energy, matter, space, and time.

## Preservation

Preservation balances current use with future cultural memory. Libraries must serve readers, but they also preserve literature and records so intellectual and cultural heritage can pass to future generations.

Acidic 19th- and 20th-century paper made preservation urgent because books could disintegrate while sitting unused. Large research libraries became especially active because they held the biggest collections and served future scholarship.

## Reformatting

Reformatting preserves brittle books, photographs, nitrate films, and other fragile materials by microfilming or scanning them into digital images on magnetic or optical disc. It can also move materials into multimedia services.

The danger is technological obsolescence: if the equipment or format disappears, preservation can fail. Because the task is enormous, reformatting often requires cooperation among libraries and government funding.

## Deacidification

Deacidification treats paper chemically when the original has artifact value or may lose information through reformatting. Mass deacidification and the use of nonacidic paper by publishers offer ways to make books last centuries rather than decades.

## Circulation

[[Circulation and Circulatory Systems (Circulatory System)|Circulation]] developed slowly because many ancient libraries were reference-only. Monastic libraries sometimes lent books to monks, but lending libraries became popular only in the 18th century and expanded rapidly with public libraries in the 19th century.

Modern user services include open shelves, reference help, interlibrary lending, retrospective searching, current-awareness service, selective dissemination of information, community information, extension programs, cooperative acquisition, cooperative cataloging, and international standards.

Interlibrary cooperation grew because no library could be self-sufficient. Union catalogs, lending networks, the British Library Lending Division, the Farmington Plan, OCLC, IFLA, FID, ICA, UNESCO, and ISO all helped libraries share records, materials, standards, preservation, and access.

## Key Events & Developments

- 3rd millennium BCE: Nippur preserved clay-tablet records in temple rooms.
- 3rd century BCE: The Library of Alexandria ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Library_of_Alexandria)) was planned and built under the Ptolemies.
- 105 CE: Ts'ai Lun announced the invention of paper to the Chinese emperor.
- 1450s: Movable-type printing transformed book production and library growth.
- 1850: England enabled tax-supported free public libraries.
- 1876: Melvil Dewey's Decimal Classification was published.
- 1968: MARC II advanced machine-readable cataloging.
- 1972-73: The British Museum library was reorganized as the British Library.
