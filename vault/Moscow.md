# Moscow

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> Moscow is Russia's capital and a historic center of Muscovite, imperial, Soviet, Orthodox, and post-Soviet power. Its Kremlin, industrial and cultural institutions, expanding urban landscape, and coexistence of religious, communist, and commercial symbols make it central to Russian history.

## Russian Moskva

Moscow, or Moskva, is the capital of Russia and one of the decisive centres of Russian history. First mentioned in chronicles in 1147, it became the capital of Muscovy in the late 13th century and later the political, industrial, cultural, scientific, educational, and Orthodox spiritual centre of Russia. As capital of the Soviet Union until 1991, Moscow and the Kremlin became symbols of communist power; after the Soviet collapse, wealth, political change, and capitalist institutions concentrated strongly in the city.

The city's character mixes old spiritual and imperial symbols with Soviet remains and post-Soviet commerce. Churches, renovated monasteries, Lenin's Mausoleum, Khrushchev-era apartment blocks, Western-style supermarkets, casinos, and luxury cars coexist. Moscow is often described as Russia's heart, unlike St. Petersburg's role as a window on Europe.

## Landscape

Moscow stands on the Moscow River in the shallow valley of the European Russian plain, about 400 miles southeast of St. Petersburg. Glacial deposits, river terraces, floodplains, and low uplands shape the site, though relief is mostly gentle. The old claim that Moscow was built on seven hills is exaggerated, but southwestern uplands rise more than 400 feet above the river. The city's plan developed in concentric rings around the Kremlin and Kitay-gorod, with radial roads and later ring roads shaping expansion.

## Climate

Moscow's [[Climate and Weather (Climate)|climate]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Climate)) is dominated by Atlantic westerlies, with moderate precipitation, long snowy winters, brief springs, warm summers, and short autumns. Snow usually lasts from mid-November to mid-March. January averages about 14 F, but Arctic winds can bring extreme cold, while southern air can briefly raise temperatures above freezing. Pollution improved when coal gave way to natural gas and factories moved out, but vehicle growth and power generators later increased exhaust pollutants.

## The Kremlin

The Kremlin ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kremlin)) remains Moscow's symbolic and political heart. Its red brick walls and 20 towers were built in the late 15th century under Ivan III with Italian builders. The Saviour Tower, designed by Pietro Solario in 1491, faces Red Square and broadcasts its clock chimes nationwide. Inside are cathedrals, palaces, state offices, museums, and guarded government areas. Since 1991, it has served as the official residence of the president of the Russian Federation.

The Kremlin's Cathedral Square contains major churches from the late 15th and early 16th centuries, including the Cathedral of the Assumption. Churches stopped functioning as places of worship after the 1917 Revolution but services resumed in many from 1990. Palaces include the Palace of Facets, Terem Palace, Great Kremlin Palace, Armoury Palace, Arsenal, Senate, and the Soviet-era Palace of Congresses.

## Cathedral

The Kremlin palace ensemble records many political periods. The Palace of Facets was built in 1487-1491, the Terem Palace in 1635-1636, the Great Kremlin Palace in 1838-1849, and the Armoury Palace in 1844-1851. The Armoury Museum preserves tsarist treasures, while later administrative buildings show imperial, Soviet, and post-Soviet uses of the same enclosed centre.

## The Kitay-gorod

Kitay-gorod is Moscow's historic commercial quarter east of the Kremlin. Red Square is its ceremonial centre, with Lenin's Mausoleum, leadership graves in the Kremlin wall, GUM department store, the State Historical Museum, and St. Basil's Cathedral, built in 1554-1560 to commemorate Ivan IV's defeat of Kazan and Astrakhan. The Kremlin and Red Square became a UNESCO World Heritage site in 1990.

The area preserves old churches and merchant buildings, including the Church of the Trinity of Nikitniki, the Church of St. Anne of the Conception, the Epiphany Cathedral, the Romanov boyars' house, the Old English Embassy, and the Monastery of the Sign. Some older riverfront areas were demolished in the 1960s for the Rossiya Hotel, later torn down in 2006.

## Outer Moscow

Outer Moscow contains industrial zones, microrayony apartment districts, standardized high-rises, broad streets, and large green spaces. Khrushchev-era five-story blocks, called khrushchovkas, gave way farther out to prefabricated high-rises. Open wedges include Izmaylovsky Park, Sokolniki Park, forest tracts, and the Exhibition of National Economic Achievements. Former estates such as Kuskovo, Uzkoe, Petrovsky Palace, Ostankino, and Kolomenskoye survive inside the expanding metropolis.

## People

Moscow's population is overwhelmingly Russian, with Ukrainian, Belarusian, Armenian, Azerbaijani, Tatar, and undocumented Asian immigrant communities. Many residents migrated during rapid urban growth. From 1932, the Soviet propiska system restricted residence registration, though people entered through marriage, apartment exchange, or limitchik labour. After Soviet price liberalization, many migrant workers left, but population growth resumed by 1995 and continued through wealth concentration and migration.

## Economy

After the Soviet collapse in 1991, Moscow's economy changed sharply. The city already held disproportionate wealth, but by 2001 its share of Russia's wealth had grown to about one-fourth. Salaries were high, but much personal income came from entrepreneurship and renting apartments or dachas. Finance, research and development, advanced manufacturing, engineering, and services became central, and women made up more than half the workforce.

## Manufacturing

Manufacturing ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manufacturing)) declined in the 1990s as services expanded, but Moscow remained Russia's largest industrial centre. Engineering and metalworking still produced ball bearings, machine tools, precision instruments, watches, textile machinery, aerospace equipment, and other skilled-labour-intensive goods. Many factories were old and specialized; privatization often shifted investment away from plants and toward banking, retail, telecommunications, or foreign holdings.

## Rail

Moscow is the hub of the Russian rail network. Lines radiate toward St. Petersburg, the Volga, Vladivostok through the Trans-Siberian route, Nizhny Novgorod, the Urals, Siberia, Central Asia, the Caucasus, Ukraine, Minsk, Warsaw, Berlin, and the Baltic. The St. Petersburg line opened in 1851. Electrified radial and suburban lines carry heavy commuter traffic, while the Little Ring and Greater Moscow Ring link the radial routes.

## Waterways

Moscow is a major river port. The canalized Moscow River handles smaller craft, while the Moscow Canal, built by forced labour in 1932-1937, connects the city to the Volga through reservoirs and the Klin-Dmitrov Ridge. Linked Volga canals open Moscow to the seas bordering European Russia, and the capital has large freight river ports and a passenger terminal.

## Air

Moscow is the hub of the Russian airline network. Sheremetyevo-2 handles international flights, Domodedovo handles major international traffic to the south, Sheremetyevo-1 and Vnukovo handle mostly domestic routes, and Bykovo is smaller. Passenger numbers increased steadily as air transport expanded.

The Moscow Metro, begun in 1935, is the most important element of urban transport. Its older stations are famous for marble, stained glass, statues, chandeliers, and elaborate architecture. Radial subway lines linked by a ring line follow the city's street pattern, and frequent trains support heavy use. Buses, trolleybuses, and trams remain necessary because stations are far apart. Since the 1990s, private car ownership has increased sharply, causing congestion and parking shortages.

## Government

Moscow and St. Petersburg have city administrations separate from their provinces. Moscow is divided into districts, and its government operates alongside national institutions spread through the inner city: the Kremlin, Duma chambers at Okhotny Ryad, the White House, ministries on the Garden Ring and Novyi Arbat, and foreign embassies. After 1917 the city had 11 sectors; later expansion and suburban growth produced more complex divisions.

## Health

Moscow has extensive health services, including public and private clinics, dental and maternity services, specialty hospitals, and medical research institutions. Quality worsened in the 1990s after the Soviet collapse but improved later with higher budgets, salaries, and equipment purchases. Public facilities remain free, though elites often prefer private care or foreign treatment.

## Education

Moscow has a very large concentration of educational institutions. Schools include language schools, boarding schools, and specialized institutions; universities increased in the 1990s. Moscow State University, founded in 1755, and Peoples' Friendship University of Russia, founded in 1960, are among the best known. Specialized institutions train engineers, chemists, musicians, theatre artists, agricultural experts, and technical specialists.

## Research and Cultural Life

Moscow concentrates research institutes, design bureaus, the Russian Academy of Sciences, and major libraries such as the Russian State Library. State research funding declined after the 1990s, and many researchers emigrated while retaining affiliations. Cultural life includes the Bolshoi Theatre, Maly Theatre, Moscow Academic Art Theatre, Obraztsov Puppet Theatre, museums, libraries, music institutions, and theatre traditions linked to Konstantin Stanislavsky, Vladimir Nemirovich-Danchenko, and Anton Chekhov.

## History

Moscow's first chronicle mention came in 1147, when Yury Dolgoruky hosted an ally there, though archaeology shows earlier settlement. The first fortifications were built in 1156, forming the Kremlin. Moscow grew as a town of Vladimir-Suzdal, recovered after Tatar sacks in 1236-1240 and 1293, and rose through trade, artisanship, Orthodox authority, and princely power.

Moscow expanded through the imperial period. Moscow University was founded in 1755, industry grew, and by 1811 the population reached 275,000. Napoleon I occupied Moscow in 1812 after Borodino, but fire destroyed more than two-thirds of the city and lack of supplies forced retreat. A rebuilding commission launched new planning and construction, and railways, industry, finance, and migration accelerated growth after the emancipation of the serfs in 1861.

## Soviet Moscow

After Bolshevik power was established in 1917, Vladimir Lenin moved the Soviet government to Moscow in 1918. The city became capital of the U.S.S.R. in 1922. Five-year plans brought industrial growth, while limited housing construction caused severe overcrowding. During [[World Wars (World War II)|World War II]], German forces reached within 25 miles of the Kremlin, but a counterattack on December 6, 1941, saved the city. Postwar Moscow grew, hosted the 1980 Summer Olympics, expanded beyond the Ring Road, and became the scene of the failed hard-line coup in 1991.

## Post-Soviet Moscow

Post-Soviet Moscow saw church renovation, foreign investment, Western-style commerce, recovery after the 1998 financial crisis, and rising living standards, but also organized crime, violence, housing shortages, congestion, ethnic tension, and high living costs. Armed conflict occurred during the 1993 parliamentary revolt against Boris Yeltsin. Bombings in 1999, the 2002 theatre hostage crisis, and 2010 Metro suicide bombings showed the city's exposure to political violence and terrorism.
