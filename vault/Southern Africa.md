# Southern Africa

## Introduction

![[attachments/southern_africa_003.webp|420]]

Southern Africa ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Southern_Africa)) is the southernmost region of Africa, including Angola, Botswana, Lesotho, Malawi, Mozambique, Namibia, South Africa, Swaziland, Zambia, and Zimbabwe. Madagascar is excluded because of its distinct language and cultural heritage. The region contains plateaus, uplands, coastal mountains, escarpments, coastal plains, deserts, grasslands, and major river basins.

The interior consists mainly of undulating plateaus across South Africa, Namibia, Botswana, Angola, Zambia, and Zimbabwe. The Kalahari forms the central depression, while the Great Escarpment flanks the plateau. The Drakensberg includes Mount Ntlenyana in Lesotho, and the Namib Desert borders the Atlantic coastal margin.

Drainage mostly flows east toward the [[Indian Ocean]], especially through the Zambezi and Limpopo. The Orange River is the only major river flowing west to the Atlantic. Seasonal climates range from arid and semiarid to temperate and tropical, and drought is common.

Vegetation includes miombo woodland ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miombo)), dry woodland, grassland, scrubland, bushland, desert vegetation, and Mediterranean vegetation along the southern coast. Animals include antelopes, gazelles, zebras, elephants, big cats, and desert-adapted species. Agriculture has restricted many habitats, but parks and transfrontier reserves protect important landscapes.

The first major transfrontier parks, including Kgalagadi and Great Limpopo, show how conservation increasingly crosses national borders in this region.

The region's peoples include Khoisan and Bantu-language speakers, Europeans, and many mixed and migrant communities. Khoisan peoples lived in the region for millennia before many were displaced by Bantu-speaking farmers and later European expansion. European settlement began at the Cape in the mid-17th century.

## Southern Africa Before the 15th Century

Southern Africa is important for human origins. Fossils from Taung, Sterkfontein, Makapansgat, Klasies River Mouth, and Border Cave helped place australopithecines, Homo erectus, and early Homo sapiens in the region's history. Australopithecines lived in Southern African savannas between about three million and one million years ago.

The Acheulean industry, with stone hand axes, choppers, and cleavers, reached Southern Africa about one million years ago and lasted for a very long period. Middle Stone Age technology later diversified, with smaller blades, fire use, and more mobile and complex human populations.

The Khoisan category refers broadly to Khoekhoe herders and San hunter-gatherers, though the term can hide major differences. Pastoralism appeared in the arid west about 2,000 years ago with sheep, goats, pottery, dogs, and trade signs. Herders, foragers, and hunters often coexisted and sometimes shifted livelihoods.

Iron-using farming communities spread into the wetter eastern half of Southern Africa from the 2nd to 5th centuries CE. They cultivated sorghum, millet, and legumes; herded cattle, sheep, and goats; made pottery; used iron tools; and traded salt, iron, pottery, copper, and other goods.

The spread of Bantu languages likely occurred through multiple streams of migration and cultural adoption from populations ultimately connected to Niger-Congo languages. Archaeologists still debate routes, timing, and whether farming, herding, ironworking, pottery, and language spread together or in stages.

## Toutswe, Swahili Trade, and Great Zimbabwe

![[attachments/southern_africa_002.webp|420]]

*Outer stone walls of Great Zimbabwe*

At Toutswe in eastern Botswana, from about the 7th century CE, new ceramics, technology, hilltop capitals, and cattle-based social organization appeared. Large cattle herds supported wealth, bridewealth, loans, exchange, specialization, and political power.

External trade helped create new hierarchies. By the 8th century, Arab traders visited more southern east-coast harbours, and between the 11th and 15th centuries Swahili towns connected inland gold, ivory, copper, and cattle networks to the Indian Ocean world.

Mapungubwe and Great Zimbabwe prospered through cattle, gold, copper, ivory, and coastal trade. Elites built stone walls and were buried with gold, copper ornaments, imported beads, pottery, and cloth, while commoners lived below the hilltop centres. Archaeology confirms African authorship despite earlier racist denial.

Great Zimbabwe ended abruptly in the second half of the 15th century. Successors included Torwa at Khami, the Mutapa state, and later Rozwi power. These states maintained coastal gold and ivory trade, while cereal farming and cattle remained core economic foundations.

Most people lived in small-scale societies organized by kinship and chiefs who claimed seniority, ritual authority, hunting skill, or control of resources. By 1500, many farming communities had reached roughly present ecological zones, though settlement shifts and conflicts continued.

## Atlantic and Indian Ocean Slave Trades

Portuguese expansion reshaped Angola from the 16th century onward. Slave trading, war bands, refugee communities, and coastal competition transformed older kingdoms. The Imbangala helped destroy Ndongo and later founded Kasanje, a major slave-trading entrepot.

The Chokwe emerged from communities fleeing slave-trading violence in the western savanna. Some refugee groups later became slave raiders themselves. The Ovimbundu kingdoms on the Bie Plateau became major suppliers of enslaved people through Benguela in the 18th and early 19th centuries.

On the southeast coast, Portuguese forces seized cities such as Mocambique and Sofala, seeking gold and Indian Ocean trade. They could not fully control the interior, where African warlords, Chikunda soldiers, Yao traders, and rival trade routes shaped the movement of ivory and slaves.

British antislavery patrols pushed slave trading eastward, and by the late 1820s Mozambique's slave exports surpassed Angola's. Demand from Reunion, Mauritius, Cuba, and Brazil increased raiding in Malawi, northeastern Zambia, and regions around Lake Nyasa.

## Nineteenth-Century Transformations

The 19th century brought warfare, state formation, migration, and colonial pressure. Moshoeshoe built a new Sotho kingdom at Thaba Bosiu, using mountain defenses, cattle, diplomacy, horses, firearms, and missionaries. By the mid-19th century, he had gathered about 80,000 followers.

Mzilikazi, once one of Shaka's commanders, left Zululand in 1823 and created the Ndebele kingdom. After conflict with Griqua raiders, Zulu forces, Pedi power, and white farmers, he moved north of the Limpopo and established power in southwestern Zimbabwe. Ndebele society absorbed Sotho and Shona peoples into a ranked military system.

The Kololo, led by Sebetwane, crossed the Zambezi and conquered the Lozi kingdom in western Zambia around 1840. Their rule ended by 1864, but a Sotho language variant survived in the region.

Britain occupied the Cape Colony during the Napoleonic Wars to protect the route to India. British rule expanded trade, labour demands, settler power, and constitutional politics. Settlers pushed for representative government, while Africans and Khoisan faced dispossession and labour pressure.

## Settler States and Imperial Expansion

The Orange Free State became a settler republic tied to wool markets. Conflict with Moshoeshoe's Basuto kingdom led to wars in 1858 and 1865-69. In 1868, after appeals from Sotho leaders, Britain annexed Basutoland, though fertile Caledon River lands remained with the Orange Free State.

Cecil Rhodes became central to British expansion. After making a fortune in diamonds and forming De Beers, he became Cape prime minister in 1890. His British South Africa Company gained concessions in present-day Zimbabwe, Zambia, and Malawi through treaties of doubtful legality and sent the Pioneer Column north in 1890.

Germany annexed South West Africa late in the imperial scramble. Nama-Herero conflicts, trade routes, firearms, cattle loss, land alienation, and debt prepared the ground for German intervention. Hendrik Witbooi resisted, and the Nama and Herero uprising of 1904-07 was crushed in a genocidal campaign.

The South African War of 1899-1902 between Britain and the Afrikaner republics grew from conflicts over imperial power, gold mines, labour, migrant rights, railway and dynamite policies, and fears of regional dominance. The failed Jameson Raid destroyed Rhodes's Cape alliance and strengthened Afrikaner nationalism.

## Colonial Rule and African Responses

After union in 1910, South Africa sought to incorporate Basutoland, Bechuanaland, and Swaziland, but African and humanitarian opposition and British strategic interests blocked this. The territories remained British protectorates but became economically dependent on South African labour markets.

South Africa conquered German South West Africa during World War I and administered it under a League of Nations mandate. Mining and poor Afrikaner settlement were promoted, while African development was neglected. South Africa later defied the United Nations after the mandate was withdrawn in 1946.

Southern Rhodesia became a self-governing colony under British rule in 1923, but high property qualifications excluded most Africans from voting. In Northern Rhodesia and Nyasaland, white settlers were fewer, but racial discrimination still protected settler interests. Copper made Northern Rhodesia economically important by World War II.

African political organizations grew from educated elites, welfare associations, and congress movements. The South African Native National Congress, later the ANC, aimed to represent African grievances, oppose pass laws and land restrictions, and overcome tribal divisions through constitutional methods. The Industrial and Commercial Workers' Union, founded by Clements Kadalie, became a mass movement in the 1920s.

## Apartheid and Regional Decolonization

The victory of the National Party in South Africa in 1948 brought apartheid into full state policy. Apartheid aimed to entrench National Party rule, promote Afrikaner interests, and protect white supremacy. Under Hendrik Verwoerd, labour controls tightened, employment colour bars expanded, and rural reserves became Bantu homelands.

Lesotho, Botswana, and Swaziland were prepared for independence rather than transfer to South Africa. Ntsu Mokhehle founded the Basutoland Congress Party, while Leabua Jonathan led the conservative Basutoland National Party to victory before Lesotho's independence in 1966. Botswana and Swaziland followed with strong monarchical and elite roles.

Britain created the Central African Federation in 1953, joining Southern Rhodesia, Northern Rhodesia, and Nyasaland. Whites expected Southern Rhodesian administrative skill, Northern Rhodesian copper, and Nyasaland labour to support it. Africans feared racist laws and blocked political progress. The federation dissolved in 1963.

Hastings Kamuzu Banda led Malawi to independence, while Kenneth Kaunda led Zambia. Banda built close ties with South Africa, while Kaunda supported resistance movements and UN sanctions against white Rhodesia, paying heavily through closed routes, raids, war disruption, and economic contraction.

## Liberation Struggles

In Angola and Mozambique, Portuguese settler power remained weaker than white rule in South Africa and Rhodesia, but Portugal fought hard to keep its colonies. Frelimo, founded in 1962, launched guerrilla war in northern Mozambique and expanded under Samora Machel after the assassination of Eduardo Mondlane.

[[Portugal]]'s costly colonial wars in Angola and Mozambique contributed to the 1974 coup in Portugal and withdrawal from Africa. Angola became independent in 1975 amid civil war among liberation movements, with Soviet, Cuban, U.S., Zairean, and South African involvement.

In Rhodesia, the Rhodesian Front under Ian Smith unilaterally declared independence in 1965 to preserve white rule. ZANU under Robert Mugabe and ZAPU under Joshua Nkomo became the main liberation movements. Guerrilla war intensified after Portuguese colonies became independent, and Zimbabwe achieved majority rule in 1980.

In South West Africa, SWAPO emerged from Ovambo contract-worker protest and became the main black nationalist organization. It began armed struggle in 1966. UN Resolution 435 in 1978 proposed a cease-fire and UN-supervised elections, but South Africa delayed until war costs and international pressure forced change. Sam Nujoma led SWAPO to victory in 1989, and Namibia became independent in 1990.

## South Africa and the End of Apartheid

By the 1970s, liberation wars in Mozambique, Angola, Namibia, and Zimbabwe changed the regional balance. South Africa under P.W. Botha increased military spending, raided neighbouring states, armed internal dissidents, and tried to destroy ANC bases. Angola and Mozambique suffered especially from South African destabilization.

Inside South Africa, apartheid faced growing crisis. The Black Consciousness movement, labour organization, township resistance, student protest, international sanctions, recession, and war costs weakened the state. The government recognized African trade unions, abolished pass laws, and tried limited reforms, but these politicized society further.

When F.W. de Klerk became president in 1989, he faced African militancy, sanctions, recession, and regional war. On February 2, 1990, he announced the release of Nelson Mandela, the unbanning of the ANC and PAC, and negotiations for a nonracial constitution. In 1993 an interim constitution was agreed, and in April 1994 Mandela was elected president.

## Key Events & Developments

- 3 million-1 million years ago: Australopithecines lived in Southern African savannas.
- 2nd-5th centuries CE: Early Bantu-speaking farming communities spread in eastern Southern Africa.
- 11th-15th centuries: Mapungubwe, Great Zimbabwe, and related centres prospered through trade.
- 1652 onward: European settlement at the Cape began a new colonial era.
- 1899-1902: The South African War reshaped British and Afrikaner power.
- 1948: The National Party came to power in South Africa on an apartheid platform.
- 1963-1964: Central African Federation dissolved; Malawi and Zambia moved to independence.
- 1975: Angola and Mozambique became independent after Portugal's withdrawal.
- 1980: Zimbabwe achieved majority rule.
- 1990: Namibia became independent and Mandela was released.
- 1994: South Africa held democratic elections and Mandela became president.

## Interesting Facts

- Great Zimbabwe and Mapungubwe were once wrongly denied African authorship by early excavators.
- Cattle were both economic wealth and symbolic power in many Southern African societies.
- Basutoland survived settler pressure partly through Moshoeshoe's diplomacy and mountain defenses.
- South Africa destabilized neighbouring states in the 1970s and 1980s to weaken liberation movements.
- Namibia became independent only after decades of UN pressure, SWAPO struggle, and regional war.
