# The History of Latin America

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> Latin American history links pre-Columbian societies, Iberian conquest, Spanish and Portuguese colonial rule, independence, nation building, and modern political change. Latin America ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_America)) usually includes South America, Mexico, Central America, and Caribbean islands whose peoples speak Romance languages. Its history includes pre-Columbian societies, Iberian conquest, Spanish and Portuguese colonization, independence wars, nation building, and developments through World War II and beyond.

The region shares experiences of conquest, colonization, Catholicism, Iberian institutions, slavery, mixed populations, export economies, and 19th-century independence. Yet countries differ greatly in geography, climate, Indigenous populations, timing of conquest, resources, economic roles, and social structure.

Those differences began before European arrival. Sedentary peoples in Mesoamerica and the central Andes had states, tribute systems, craft specialists, social ranks, and agricultural stores that Spaniards could adapt to conquest. Semisedentary and nonsedentary peoples had fewer fixed resources and stronger mobility, so European rule there often produced avoidance, frontier warfare, and slower occupation rather than quick incorporation.

The contrast between central and frontier areas shaped the whole colonial period. Where dense populations and tribute systems already existed, Spanish conquerors could graft towns, encomiendas, parishes, and labour demands onto older institutions. Where people lived in smaller mobile communities, colonial rule depended more on missions, forts, raids, alliances, and gradual settlement. This meant that Mexico and Peru became early imperial cores, while northern Mexico, Paraguay, the pampas, the Amazon basin, and many Caribbean or coastal zones followed slower, more uneven paths.

Some scholars prefer Iberoamerica, since Spanish and Portuguese influences were far more important than French or Italian ones. Still, Latin America remains the common term.

## Iberian Background

The Spanish and Portuguese shared many European traits but also had Iberian features that shaped America. Late-15th-century Iberia was divided among Portugal, Castile, and Aragon, with Castile and Aragon joined by royal marriage.

Iberian society was strongly urban-centred. Provinces focused on cities, where government, church, professional, commercial, craft, and elite families concentrated. The cabildo, or town council, united leading families and organized surrounding territory.

The Iberian [[Family and Kinship (Family)|family]] was multilinear. Women kept maiden names, dowries remained their property, and kinship could be traced through male and female lines. Formal marriage often came late for men, while informal unions and illegitimate children were common and socially managed.

These family patterns affected colonial society, especially relations among European men, Indigenous women, African women, legitimate families, mixed children, household dependents, and patronage networks.

They also made household authority a basic colonial institution. A Spanish or Portuguese household could include legitimate kin, servants, slaves, apprentices, clients, recognized natural children, and informal partners. Protection, inheritance, marriage arrangements, dowries, and godparentage created vertical ties that mattered almost as much as formal law. Colonial hierarchy therefore worked through family strategy as well as race, office, and wealth.

## Spanish America and the Caribbean Phase

![[attachments/the_history_of_latin_america_002.webp|420]]

*Diego Ribeiro world map from 1529 showing early Iberian knowledge of the Americas*

The Spanish were first Europeans to occupy major American areas in early modern times, quickly finding zones with dense Indigenous populations and mineral wealth.

The Caribbean was the first testing ground. Christopher Columbus imagined exploration and trade, but the Spanish settlers who returned to Hispaniola in 1493 brought people, animals, tools, and institutions for permanent occupation.

Santo Domingo, founded in 1496, became a real Spanish city. Secondary towns spread near gold-mining sites, while Indigenous demographic loss in the Caribbean was rapid and catastrophic.

Spanish practices developed there: [[Cities|cities]], cabildos, encomienda, mining, African slavery, Spanish households, religious institutions, local government, and transatlantic commerce.

The Caribbean experience also showed the limits of conquest based on forced Indigenous labour. Gold deposits were small, disease and overwork devastated local populations, and settlers quickly demanded new labour supplies. African slavery, Spanish livestock, sugar experiments, and movement toward the mainland all grew from this first colonial crisis.

Mainland conquest depended on alliances and local divisions as much as Spanish weapons. In Mexico, Hernan Cortes founded Veracruz, allied with Tlaxcala, exploited resentment against the Aztec tribute system, and returned after defeat to besiege Tenochtitlan with reinforcements and lake vessels. In Peru, Francisco Pizarro captured Atahuallpa at Cajamarca during an Inca civil war, then founded Lima on the coast, separating the Spanish centre from the Andean highland population.

## Colonial Society

Encomenderos dominated early Spanish American society by receiving Indigenous labour and tribute rights. Their cities became centres of Spanish control, while mining and agriculture drew on Indigenous and African labour.

Artisans flourished in Spanish cities, often marrying Spanish women and acquiring property. They trained African slaves and Indigenous apprentices, helping create urban mixed groups skilled in Spanish trades.

Spanish women helped stabilize urban society. As wives, widows, and business actors, they managed households, property, servants, African and Indigenous dependents, and children of both Spanish and mixed descent.

Africans became important early. Encomenderos, artisans, and prosperous Spaniards bought African slaves, who often served as skilled workers, intermediaries, household servants, food sellers, and supervisors over Indigenous labourers. Free Black urban communities also emerged.

Indigenous people worked in Spanish cities as servants, labourers, apprentices, mistresses, interpreters, carriers, and intermediaries. Many learned Spanish and entered Hispanic urban life at low status but in many roles.

## Commerce and Mining

Long-distance merchants linked American silver with European cloth, iron, manufactures, and credit. Sevilla-based firms dominated early import-export trade, while local merchants and small tratantes handled regional goods.

[[Extraction And Processing Industries (Mining)|Mining]] drove the Spanish colonial economy. Gold mining was often seasonal and expeditionary, but silver mining became capital-intensive, technically demanding, and central to export revenue.

The crown claimed residual ownership of [[Minerals and Rocks (Mineral)|minerals]] and taxed silver heavily. Mining camps became municipalities with councils, merchants, craftspeople, professionals, and permanent institutions.

Silver exports made Mexico, Peru, and Potosi central to the imperial system and connected Spanish America to Europe, Africa, and Asia.

Mining labour differed sharply by region. In the Andes, Potosi drew on Indigenous labour obligations and older long-distance labour traditions, slowing cultural change among temporary workers. In northern Mexico, where major silver sites lay beyond dense sedentary populations, miners relied more on free agents, permanent workers, and mobile Hispanized groups.

Silver also reshaped government. Because the crown taxed production directly, mining camps attracted officials, notaries, merchants, muleteers, refiners, credit networks, and courts. A remote camp could become a municipality with urban institutions, while nearby estates and villages supplied food, animals, timber, mercury transport, and workers. Mining wealth therefore tied frontier expansion to imperial finance.

## Brazil

![[attachments/the_history_of_latin_america_001.webp|420]]

*Map showing early Treaty of Tordesillas meridian lines near Brazil*

The 1494 Treaty of Tordesillas gave Portugal a claim to Brazil. Portuguese voyagers reached the Brazilian coast in 1500 on the route to India.

At first Brazil was a fringe area, less profitable than Portuguese ventures in Africa and India. It lacked known precious-metal deposits and had semisedentary Tupian populations similar to peoples Spaniards encountered in Paraguay.

Early Portuguese activity centred on brazilwood, a dye wood that gave the country its name. The trade required European axes, saws, specifications, factors, and coastal contacts but did not at first create large cities.

Over time, Brazil developed plantation society, especially sugar in the northeast, heavy reliance on African slavery, Portuguese colonial administration, and later a more integrated colonial economy.

Brazil's early development differed because Portugal's Asian and African interests remained more profitable for decades. Permanent colonization grew when coastal defence, sugar profits, and private donatory captaincies made occupation more urgent. The plantation zone then linked mills, cane fields, ports, merchants, and enslaved Africans into a society closer to the non-Spanish Caribbean than to the Indigenous tribute cores of Mexico and Peru.

## Late Colonial Growth and Regional Differences

After the mid-18th century, population and economic activity grew sharply. Silver production increased, especially in Mexico, while merchant-financiers invested in larger operations.

Local textile production expanded to serve humble Hispanized urban and rural consumers. Large merchants became localized, marrying, investing, living, owning haciendas, and acquiring noble titles.

Racial and cultural mixing advanced beyond simple categories. Labels multiplied, but many people shifted categories or avoided labels if they functioned in Hispanic settings.

[[Argentina]] and the Rio de la Plata moved from periphery to importance after trade liberalization in the 1770s. Buenos Aires became capital of the new Viceroyalty of the Rio de la Plata in 1776, grew rapidly, and began dominating the interior.

This rise reversed older routes. For much of the colonial period the northwest interior looked toward Potosi, while Buenos Aires remained a small and restricted port. Once legal trade expanded, European imports, silver exports, slave routes, hides, and merchant immigration gave the port new leverage over Paraguay, the pampas, and the interior provinces.

[[Venezuela]] developed through cacao exports from Caracas, first to Mexico and later Europe. Its coastal society included Europeans, Africans, and mixed populations, with similarities to Brazil.

The Caribbean became divided. Non-Spanish islands under French and English control became major sugar producers with African majorities, while Spanish Cuba and Puerto Rico remained more balanced and entered sugar expansion later.

Late colonial merchant elites increasingly combined trade, mining finance, landholding, marriage alliances, and noble titles. They were often foreign-born Spaniards or Portuguese by birth but deeply local in residence, investment, and family strategy.

The weakening of rigid ethnic hierarchy did not mean equality. It meant that categories such as Spaniard, Indian, African, mestizo, mulatto, and casta became harder to apply consistently as language, occupation, residence, wealth, and patronage reshaped status.

## Independence

Between 1808 and 1826, nearly all Latin America except Cuba and Puerto Rico left Iberian rule. The timing resulted from long colonial tensions and external shocks, especially Napoleon's invasion of Iberia.

Spanish Bourbon reforms angered many Creoles by favouring peninsular officials, limiting elite privilege, tightening administration, and restricting trade more than local elites wanted.

Enlightenment ideas, North American and French examples, local conspiracies, economic interests, and resentment of peninsular dominance shaped revolutionary language.

In South America, independence moved from north and south. Simon Bolivar led northern campaigns; Jose de San Martin led southern campaigns. Their forces eventually converged in the central Andes.

Antonio Jose de Sucre won the decisive Battle of Ayacucho in 1824, ending major loyalist resistance. By 1826, Spanish South America was independent.

## Mexico and Central America

Mexico's independence came late because powerful Creole and peninsular elites benefited from the imperial system and feared social revolution.

In 1810, Miguel Hidalgo y Costilla launched the Grito de Dolores, appealing to Indigenous and mestizo people in the Bajio. The movement shocked elites because it combined anti-peninsular revolt with popular social anger.

The Mexican path to independence differed from elite-led revolutions elsewhere. It began with popular insurgency and only later became acceptable to conservative elites seeking autonomy without social upheaval.

[[Central America]] followed its own fragmented course, shaped by local elites, weak central authority, regional rivalries, Guatemala's influence, and later separation into several republics after brief union with Mexico.

## Brazilian Independence

Brazilian independence was less violent than Spanish America's. Portuguese rule had been less exclusionary, Brazilian and Portuguese elites overlapped, and the upper classes feared threats to African slavery and social order.

The decisive step was the transfer of the Portuguese court to Rio de Janeiro in 1808. Rio became a centre of administration, economy, culture, and power, making a return to colonial status impossible.

[[Brazil]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brazil)) became independent under monarchy rather than republican revolution, preserving more territorial unity and continuity than many Spanish American republics.

## Constitutions and Nation Building

Early Latin American elites produced many [[Constitution and Constitutional Government (Constitution)|constitutions]], influenced by the Enlightenment. The first constitutions in Venezuela, Chile, and New Granada declared natural rights and representative government with comparatively weak executives.

By the mid-19th century, many leaders moved toward centralization and stronger executives. Early liberal constitutional systems had struggled amid regional, factional, military, and social conflict.

Foreign models shifted from Rousseau toward more conservative thinkers such as Montesquieu and Jeremy Bentham, but local problems also demanded stronger states able to defeat Spain, gain recognition, control disorder, and build authority.

Postindependence societies also struggled with caudillos, regionalism, church-state conflict, army influence, public debt, export dependence, and the difficulty of turning colonial jurisdictions into stable national states.

The 19th century brought liberal attacks on corporate privilege, including church and Indigenous communal landholding in some countries. These reforms could expand markets and citizenship but also dispossess rural communities and deepen social conflict.

The new republics remained politically fragile because sovereignty had to be rebuilt after imperial authority collapsed. Armies that had won independence stayed powerful, provincial elites resisted capitals, and public finances were weak. Liberal programs promised citizenship and markets, but slavery, tribute, church privilege, local patronage, rural hierarchy, and debt burdens often changed slowly or unevenly.

## Early 20th-Century Challenges

By the early 20th century, many Latin American countries had achieved growth and partial stabilization, but they faced population growth, social unrest, external dependence, and tighter incorporation into the world economy.

World War I disrupted trade ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trade)) and capital flows. Export economies lost shipping and markets, European manufactured imports became scarce, and local industries sometimes expanded to replace imports.

[[Argentina]] suffered trade disruption despite recovering meat exports; Mexico and Argentina defended neutrality; Brazil followed the United States in declaring war on Germany.

The crisis also exposed external dependence. Countries tied to one or two export products were vulnerable to shipping shortages, price swings, and loss of European credit, while the scarcity of imported manufactures encouraged new factories but did not remove dependence on foreign machinery and markets.

This pattern shaped later industrial policy debates.

It also made neutrality and alignment practical questions, not only diplomatic ones. Argentina, Mexico, Brazil, and smaller republics had to weigh export markets, immigrant communities, U.S. pressure, shipping access, and domestic politics while the war reordered trade.

## Mexican Revolution and Political Participation

The Mexican Revolution began in 1910 when Francisco Madero challenged Porfirio Diaz and promised political democracy. Diaz's dictatorship collapsed, but Madero unleashed forces no leader could easily control.

Peasants, miners, urban workers, middle sectors, and rival revolutionaries fought for land, labour rights, political voice, and regional power. The 1917 constitution proclaimed reform, but land redistribution became more serious under Lazaro Cardenas in 1934-40.

The revolution eventually produced a system centred on the Institutional Revolutionary Party, or PRI, which co-opted labour and peasant organizations and made Mexico a model of political stability.

The Mexican case mattered beyond Mexico because it joined political revolution with social reform. Its constitution claimed authority over land, subsoil resources, education, labour, and church-state relations, while later governments built mass organizations for workers and peasants. Many Latin Americans admired its reform language even when few countries copied its one-party structure.

It also showed how revolutionary violence could be converted into institutional rule. Regional armies, agrarian demands, labour mobilization, anticlerical politics, and presidential succession were gradually disciplined through party channels. This settlement did not remove inequality, but it gave Mexico unusual continuity while many neighbours alternated between reformist experiments, oligarchic resistance, and military intervention.

Elsewhere, participation broadened through more conventional reform. Argentina's 1912 electoral reform enabled the Radical Civic Union to win power; Chile developed social legislation and a new constitution; Uruguay pioneered welfare policies.

## Good Neighbor Policy and World War II

The United States shifted from intervention toward the Good Neighbor Policy ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Good_Neighbor_policy)). [[Franklin D. Roosevelt]] formally renounced intervention and used diplomacy, aid, military cooperation, and technical programs instead.

The Good Neighbor Policy ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Good_Neighbor_policy)) improved U.S.-Latin American relations and proved more effective for U.S. influence than gunboat intervention. Roosevelt accepted Mexico's 1938 oil expropriation, and Mexico later cooperated loyally in World War II.

World War II again disrupted trade but accelerated import substitution, industrialization, and economic cooperation. Brazil sent an expeditionary force to Italy, and Mexico sent an air squadron to the Philippines.

## Postwar Development and Reform

After 1945, many governments pursued import-substitution industrialization, regional integration, and economic planning. Organizations such as the Latin American Free Trade Association, Central American Common Market, and Andean Pact sought to increase intra-regional trade.

Growth was visible, but results varied. [[Brazil]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brazil)), Panama, Mexico, Venezuela, and Costa Rica performed strongly; Argentina stagnated; and many countries faced population growth above 3 percent annually.

By the 1960s, easy gains from replacing imports were fading. Some countries renewed interest in exports, especially industrial exports. Brazil was most successful, selling automobiles and parts abroad.

## Christian Democracy and Authoritarianism

Christian Democratic parties emerged after World War II, promoting moderate reform inspired by Catholic social teaching. They gained power in Venezuela, El Salvador, and Chile.

In Chile, Eduardo Frei pursued land reform and partial copper nationalization in 1964-70, backed by the United States as an alternative to Cuban-style revolution. He lost momentum, and Salvador Allende won in 1970.

Allende combined Marxist nationalization with populist benefits for workers, provoking hostility among upper and middle classes and the United States. In 1973, Augusto Pinochet overthrew him.

Pinochet became a key example of bureaucratic authoritarianism, a military dictatorship led by professionalized armies and technocrats committed to modernization, order, and repression.
