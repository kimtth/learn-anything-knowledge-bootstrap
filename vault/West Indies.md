## Introduction

The West Indies, called Indias Occidentales in Spanish, Indes Occidentales in French, and West-Indie in Dutch, are a crescent-shaped island group more than 2,000 miles long. They separate the Gulf of Mexico and the Caribbean Sea from the [[Atlantic Ocean]]. From Florida, the islands extend southeast, then south, then west along the northern coast of [[Venezuela]].

The region has three major physiographic divisions. The Greater Antilles ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greater_Antilles)) include Cuba, Jamaica, Hispaniola, and Puerto Rico. The Lesser Antilles ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lesser_Antilles)) include the Virgin Islands, Anguilla, Saint Kitts and Nevis, Guadeloupe, Dominica, Martinique, Saint Lucia, Saint Vincent and the Grenadines, Barbados, and Grenada. Other island groups include The Bahamas, the Turks and Caicos Islands, Trinidad and Tobago, Aruba, Curacao, and Bonaire. Bermuda is often included culturally, though not physiographically.

The Greater Antilles reflect an old folded and faulted mountain chain, with highlands such as Jamaica's Blue Mountains, Cuba's Sierra Maestra, and Duarte Peak in the Dominican Republic. The Lesser Antilles form a double arc: a volcanic inner arc and a lower outer arc of limestone over older rocks. The region has a tropical maritime climate, wet and dry seasons, high humidity, and frequent hurricanes from August to October.

## Land, People, and Economy

![[attachments/west_indies_001.webp|420]]

*West Indies (orthographic projection)*

The original forests of the West Indies were heavily cut for sugar plantations, fuel, and slash-and-burn agriculture, causing erosion and soil impoverishment. Surviving vegetation includes mangrove swamps, semi-deciduous woodland, tropical rainforest, montane forest, and elfin woodland. The fauna resembles a reduced version of nearby South America, with rodents, bats, lizards, parrots, hummingbirds, ibis, flamingos, turtles, shellfish, dolphins, and many fish.

The population is ethnically diverse because of plantation slavery, colonization, migration, and indentured labour. Many people descend from enslaved Africans, European colonists, or mixed ancestry. Spanish-speaking communities include Cuba, Puerto Rico, and the Dominican Republic; French and English creoles blend European, African, and West Indian languages; and Papiamentu is widely spoken on Aruba, Curacao, and Bonaire. South Asians are important in Trinidad and Tobago, and Chinese and European minorities also exist.

Most West Indian economies are free-enterprise market systems, except Cuba's centrally planned economy. They have long depended on a few exports, especially sugar, bananas, citrus, cocoa, spices, minerals, petroleum, and tourism. Agriculture has declined, and many islands are not self-sufficient in food. Tourism is a major source of foreign exchange on some islands but can raise local costs and decline quickly during recessions.

Regional cooperation tries to reduce dependence and small-island vulnerability. The Caribbean Basin Initiative ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caribbean_Basin_Initiative)), begun by the [[United States of America (United States)|United States]] in 1984, gave duty-free access to many products. Other organizations include the Caribbean Community and Common Market, or Caricom, and the African, Caribbean and Pacific Group of States. Political forms range from Cuba's socialist republic to parliamentary democracies, constitutional monarchies, overseas departments, and dependent territories.

## Culture and Pre-Columbian Peoples

![[attachments/west_indies_002.webp|420]]

*Danish West Indies 1904 10 Daler*

The countries of the West Indies share many cultural patterns despite differences in language, ancestry, and government. Caribbean culture blends African, American Indian, European, and Asian influences. Carnival, dance, parties, festivals, reggae, and modern Caribbean literature are central. Writers and thinkers such as Aime Cesaire, V.S. Naipaul, Jean Price-Mars, Edwidge Danticat, C.L.R. James, and Derek Walcott addressed themes important to the region and to African descendants.

Before European colonization, the West Indies had important Indigenous cultures. Archaeologists divide the pre-Columbian peoples into three broad groups. Paleo-Indians, present from about 5000 to 2000 BCE, were hunter-gatherers on the coasts of Cuba, Hispaniola, and Trinidad. Meso-Indians, from about 1000 to 500 BCE, had pottery and tools and spread from South America into Trinidad and the Greater Antilles.

The third group were Neo-Indians. The Taino, an Arawakan-speaking people, entered Trinidad from South America about 300 BCE and spread through the Lesser and Greater Antilles. The Carib migrated after 1000 CE from the Orinoco delta, living mainly in northern Trinidad and the Lesser Antilles. Taino villages were led by hereditary caciques and used conuco cultivation; Carib villages were more flexible but shared many material practices.

## Discovery, Colonialism, and Slavery

European control began with Christopher Columbus's landing in 1492. Spanish settlement concentrated on the Greater Antilles, especially Hispaniola, where Santo Domingo became the first permanent Spanish settlement in the Americas. The Indigenous population largely disappeared through conquest, slavery, and European diseases. Bartolome de Las Casas tried to protect Indigenous peoples, but his proposed labour solution helped open the way for enslaved Africans in the early 16th century.

Spain's attention shifted to mainland empires in Mexico and Peru, while Havana and San Juan became strategic ports for the treasure fleets. During the 17th century, England, France, and the Netherlands took poorly defended islands, especially in the Lesser Antilles. England occupied parts of Saint Kitts and Barbados, later capturing Jamaica. France took Guadeloupe, Martinique, and Saint-Domingue. The Dutch controlled Curacao, Aruba, Bonaire, Sint Eustatius, Saba, and part of Saint Martin.

Plantation slavery linked West Indian colonialism to mercantilism. Sugar, coffee, enslaved labour from West Africa, European manufactured goods, and Atlantic shipping formed triangular and quadrilateral trade networks that enriched European empires. Plantation society became racially hierarchical: whites had full civil rights, free people of colour and free blacks faced disabilities, and enslaved people were treated as property.

## Emancipation, Decolonization, and Dependency

The successful slave rebellion in Saint-Domingue during the 1790s shook West Indian societies. Leaders such as Toussaint Louverture, Henry Christophe, and Jean-Jacques Dessalines helped create independent Haiti in 1804, the first republic in the Americas founded mainly by people of African descent. Britain abolished the slave trade in 1807 and slavery in the British West Indies between 1834 and 1838. France emancipated enslaved people in 1848, the Netherlands in 1863, Puerto Rico in 1873, and Cuba in 1886.

Emancipation brought freedom but not equality. Plantations still controlled much of the best land, restricted voting systems preserved elite power, and many freed people became plantation workers, peasants, migrants, or squatters. Later arrivals of South Asians, Chinese, Portuguese, Syrians, and Lebanese added new groups to already complex colour, class, and cultural hierarchies.

Decolonization changed politics more deeply. Haiti became independent in 1804; the Dominican Republic rejected Haitian rule in 1844 and achieved final independence in 1865; Cuba gained independence after wars with Spain and U.S. intervention in 1898, then formal independence in 1902. After World War II, many British, French, and Dutch territories gained independence, incorporation, association, or continued dependency. The West Indies Federation of 1958-62 failed, leading to waves of British Caribbean independence.

Yet dependence remained central. West Indian economies relied on external markets, foreign companies, capital, technology, remittances, subsidies, and tourism. North American, British, and European interests shaped bauxite, petroleum, sugar, and banana industries. Late 20th-century nationalizations and democratic gains reduced some ties, but the challenge of turning self-determination into broad grassroots development persisted.

## Key Events & Developments

- 5000-2000 BCE: Paleo-Indians lived in parts of Cuba, Hispaniola, and Trinidad.
- c. 300 BCE: Taino peoples entered Trinidad and spread through the Antilles.
- after 1000 CE: Carib peoples migrated from the Orinoco delta into the Lesser Antilles.
- 1492: Christopher Columbus reached the West Indies.
- 1623-25: English colonization began at Saint Kitts and Barbados.
- 1655: England captured Jamaica from Spain.
- 1697: France formally annexed Saint-Domingue.
- 1804: Haiti became independent.
- 1834-38: Slavery ended in the British West Indies.
- 1886: Slavery was fully abolished in Cuba.
- 1958-62: The West Indies Federation attempted and failed to unite British Caribbean territories.
- 1984: The Caribbean Basin Initiative ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caribbean_Basin_Initiative)) began.

## Key People

- Christopher Columbus ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christopher_Columbus)): European explorer whose 1492 landing began Hispanic control of the region.
- Bartolome de Las Casas ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bartolome_de_Las_Casas)): Missionary who opposed Indigenous destruction but helped redirect labour demand toward African slavery.
- Toussaint Louverture ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Toussaint_Louverture)): Leader in the Saint-Domingue revolution.
- Henry Christophe ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henry_Christophe)): Haitian revolutionary and leader connected with independence.
- Jean-Jacques Dessalines ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jean-Jacques_Dessalines)): Leader who helped bring Haiti to independence.
- Hernan Cortes ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hernan_Cortes)): Conqueror whose expedition to Mexico began from the Caribbean world.
- Francisco Pizarro ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Francisco_Pizarro)): Conqueror whose expedition to Peru was linked to early Spanish Caribbean settlement.
- Derek Walcott ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Derek_Walcott)): West Indian writer associated with modern Caribbean literature.
