# Commercial Fishing

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> Commercial fishing harvests aquatic organisms for food and trade using specialized vessels, gear, processing, and distribution systems. Its management balances economic livelihoods with marine ecology and conservation.

## Introduction

Commercial fishing is the taking of fish and other seafood and resources from oceans, rivers, and lakes for the purpose of marketing them. It ranges from small artisanal fisheries ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Subsistence_fishing)) using little or no mechanization to large-scale enterprises with powerful deep-sea vessels and sophisticated equipment. Both plants and animals are taken: alongside fish, fishers harvest crustaceans such as lobsters and shrimp, mollusks such as oysters and squid, and the water plant [[Algae|algae]], used as food, fodder, fertilizer, and industrial raw material.

Fishing is one of humankind's oldest employments, shown by prehistoric heaps of discarded shells called kitchen middens ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Midden)) found from China and Japan to Peru, Brazil, Portugal, and Denmark. From shellfish gathering, people advanced to traps and nets and then out onto the continental shelves ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continental_shelf)), turning local harvesting into a global food industry through better ships, gear, and preservation. Fisheries are classified by type of water, fresh or salt and inshore, mid-water, or deep sea; by their object, as in whaling, salmon fishing, or sponge fishing; and by the method employed, such as harpooning, seining, trawling, and lining.

## History and methods

![[attachments/commercial_fishing_001.webp|420]]

*Elbe in Cuxhaven - GRE-33 Poseidon (ship, 1971) - by-RaBoe 001*

Fishing developed from prehistoric shellfish gathering and simple nets into industrial fisheries with mechanized trawlers, purse seiners, and processing ships. Two broad groups are caught: demersal fish living at or near the bottom, such as cod, haddock, hake, and pollock, and pelagic fish of the open surface waters, such as herring, mackerel, and tuna. Crustaceans, mollusks, seaweed, and even certain mammals and reptiles are also taken.

The history is not only technological but also economic, driven by changing demand, better market access, and the growth of ports able to support larger fleets. Herring were caught in huge numbers in northern Europe in the Middle Ages, and cod fishing on the Grand Banks of Newfoundland began even before John Cabot's voyage there in 1497; large-fleet whaling followed in the 17th century.

Before mechanization arrived late in the 19th century, sailing vessels evolved to suit each fishery. The Grand Banks schooners, sailing from New England, Nova Scotia, and Newfoundland, fished cod on trips of up to six months, salting the catch for export to Europe, Africa, and the Caribbean, while individual men set and hauled longlines by hand from small wooden dories. Commercial fishing depends on the interaction between gear, species behaviour, and local marine conditions.

Preservation transformed the industry. Salting, drying, smoking, icing, canning, freezing, and processing at sea changed where fish could be sold and how long fleets could stay away from port, turning perishable local catches into regional and global commodities.

Commercial fisheries also depend on ocean productivity. The richest grounds are continental shelves, upwelling zones, and cold nutrient-rich banks, so fishing grounds are unevenly distributed, shaped by currents, plankton, temperature, depth, and the feeding habits of target species.

## Gear, vessels, and fisheries

An international classification recognizes 16 categories of fishing method, from collecting by hand through line fishing, trapping, dredging and trawling, seining, gillnetting, and harvesting with machines. The simplest, gathering by hand at ebb tide or by divers, needs no gear, while long-handled spears, harpoons, clamps, and tongs extend the fisher's reach. Traps range from simple octopus pots used on the Italian coast to large pound nets that guide tuna, herring, and salmon through baffles, and even fish wheels on the Columbia and Rhône act as mechanized lift nets.

Each gear type suits a target and environment. Trawls, large cone-shaped bags dragged along the seabed or through mid-water, are the most important gear of northwest Europe and second only to purse seines in world catch; longlines carry hundreds of baited hooks for cod, halibut, tuna, and shark; gill nets catch fish in their meshes; and pots and traps take shellfish such as lobster and crab.

A fishing fleet is an industrial system. Until the mid-20th century boats were of local design, but vessel type is now dictated by fishing method rather than home port, and the spread of 200-mile fishing limits reshaped fleets worldwide. Steel is standard above 25 metres, fibreglass is common on smaller boats, and ferrocement serves artisanal fisheries; commercial fishing remains one of the highest-risk industries in the world.

Fishers locate stocks using knowledge of seasons, currents, and bird activity together with sonar, echo sounders, aircraft, and electronic navigation. Tuna purse seiners may even carry a light helicopter to spot shoals, combining traditional ecological knowledge with technical detection systems.

Processing at sea changed the industry. Factory trawlers with freezing and refrigeration equipment let fleets stay out longer and work farther from port; wet-fish trawlers, by contrast, store fresh fish on ice and return within about four days. These innovations expanded catches but increased pressure on wild stocks.

Stern trawlers now make up almost all new trawlers, hauling the net up a stern ramp, while beam trawlers tow two nets from side booms and are the most powerful fishing vessels of their size in the world. Purse seines encircle shoals of herring, mackerel, sardine, and tuna and then close the bottom like a purse.

Ports and markets complete the system. Landing crews, auctioning, processing, cold storage, transport, and export determine a catch's value, so a modern fishery includes vessels, crews, gear makers, processors, traders, regulators, and consumers.

## Aquaculture

Fish farming has become a major complement to capture fisheries. Carp, trout, oysters, mussels, shrimp, and seaweed are all cultivated, and aquaculture's share of aquatic food has risen steadily in recent decades.

Aquaculture ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aquaculture)) matters because capture fisheries alone cannot meet demand in many regions. It is a response to scarcity, market pressure, and the limits of wild stocks, with each species requiring its own farming methods and environment.

The farming of finfish, shellfish, crustaceans, and seaweed each demands different water conditions, feeding systems, disease controls, and harvesting methods, so aquaculture is not one technique but a family of production systems.

Fish farming also creates new problems. Dense cultivation can spread disease, require feed, alter coastal environments, and affect wild populations when farmed animals escape, making aquaculture both a solution to supply limits and a new source of ecological and economic questions.

## Conclusion

Commercial fishing ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commercial_fishing)) is a technologically adaptive industry that depends on marine ecology, vessel design, market infrastructure, and resource management. Fishing is never only about catching fish; it is also about preservation, transport, labour, and the management of ocean resources.

Because the industry works at the boundary between nature and economy, its success depends on the condition of fish stocks and the efficiency of the whole production chain, which is why capture fisheries and aquaculture must be considered together.

The larger warning is that commercial success can undermine ecological stability: better vessels and gear can increase catches faster than fish can replace themselves. Sustainable management therefore depends on quotas, closed seasons, protected areas, gear limits, and international cooperation.

## Key Events & Developments

- Prehistory: shellfish gathering and simple fishing begin.
- Middle Ages: bulk fisheries expand in Europe.
- Late 19th-20th centuries: mechanized industrial fishing transforms the industry.
- Modern era: aquaculture becomes a major source of seafood.
- 19th century: improved preservation and transport widen markets.
- 20th century: processing ships and larger fleets increase reach at sea.
- Recent decades: farming becomes a larger share of aquatic food production.

## Key People

- John Cabot ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Cabot)): Italian explorer who reached the Grand Banks cod grounds in 1497.
- Grand Banks schooner crews: New England, Nova Scotia, and Newfoundland fishermen who salted cod for export.
- Portuguese dory fishermen: Sailed annually to the Grand Banks into the late 20th century.
