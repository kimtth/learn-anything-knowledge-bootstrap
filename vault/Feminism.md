# Feminism

> [!abstract] Reference navigation

## Introduction

![[attachments/feminism_001.webp|420]]

*Woman suffrage procession in Washington DC in 1913*

Feminism ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Feminism)) is the belief that women and men should have equal social, economic, and political rights. Although the movement began mainly in the West, it has developed worldwide through institutions and activists working for women’s rights. that women were long confined to the domestic sphere, while public life, property ownership, education, and political office were mostly reserved for men. In medieval Europe, women were often denied property rights and education, and even in the late 19th century some societies still imposed severe restrictions on them.

The history of feminism is presented as a long struggle against inequality, but also as a movement that changed over time. Early protests were rare, and the first arguments for women’s equality emerged slowly. Only later, especially during the Enlightenment and the modern suffrage era, did feminism become a recognizable social and political force.

## History of feminism
The history of feminism begins with the ancient world, where organized protest against women’s inferior status was unusual. One notable exception was in 3rd century BCE Rome, when women gathered at the Capitoline Hill to protest laws limiting their use of luxury goods. This event stands out because it shows that resistance to women’s subordination was not completely absent even in early societies.

In the Renaissance, women’s defenders wrote long arguments that attacked prejudice and praised women’s intelligence and achievement. Moderata Fonte and other writers who challenged the idea that women were naturally weak or immoral. These debates became more intense in England in the late 16th century, where pamphlets and replies debated the true nature of womanhood.

## Influence of the Enlightenment

The feminist voice became a coherent movement only during the Enlightenment. At that time, women began to demand that ideals of liberty, equality, and natural rights be applied to both sexes. Yet many Enlightenment thinkers, including [[Jean-Jacques Rousseau]], still portrayed women as inferior. That the French Declaration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen did not include women, which exposed the gap between universal claims and actual practice.

By the 18th century, writers such as Mary Wollstonecraft challenged these ideas directly. In her work A Vindication of the Rights of Woman, she argued that women were not naturally weaker in mind or character, but were denied education and opportunities. Her argument helped lay the foundation for modern feminism.

## The suffrage movement

The movement became more organized in the 19th century. A landmark was the Seneca Falls Convention of 1848, where Lucretia Mott, Martha Wright, Mary Ann McClintock, Jane Hunt, and Elizabeth Cady Stanton gathered to demand women’s rights. Stanton drafted the Declaration of Sentiments, modeled on the Declaration of Independence, and included the radical demand for the vote.

The convention was a major turning point, but it also had clear limits. The early movement often reflected the concerns of middle- and upper-class women more than those of ordinary workers or Black women. Sojourner Truth gave a powerful challenge to this narrow focus in her speech “Ain’t I a Woman?” which reminded listeners that Black women faced burdens not shared by white middle-class women.

## First and second waves

The first wave of feminism was mainly concerned with legal and political rights, especially the right to vote. The second wave emerged in the 1960s and 1970s, when women demanded not only equality before the law but also changes in family life, work, sexuality, and reproduction. This second wave included demands for abortion rights, equal employment, and the end of gender discrimination.
Second-wave feminism became global in scope. At the 1975 United Nations Decade for Women conference in Mexico City, women from many countries pushed for attention to development and equality, though they often faced resistance from the West. Later conferences in Copenhagen, Cairo, and Beijing revealed disagreements over priorities, especially between women from the developed and developing worlds.

## Third wave of feminism

The third wave emerged in the mid-1990s, led by younger women often called Generation X. These activists inherited the legal rights won by earlier feminists, but they criticized the older movement for being too narrow or incomplete. They were shaped by the rise of media, diversity, and new forms of communication, and they often used irony, humor, and cultural critique to challenge sexism.
Third-wave feminism was more inclusive of women of colour, and it questioned fixed ideas of gender, beauty, sexuality, and femininity. It drew on postmodern ideas, used popular culture, and promoted a wider range of identities and experiences. At the same time, the third wave faced criticism from both older feminists and postfeminist critics who argued that the movement had become too fragmented or outdated.

## Manifestations and controversies

Third-wave feminists built on institutions created by earlier generations, including women’s studies programs, feminist organizations, and magazines such as Ms. they often subverted sexist images rather than simply rejecting them. They used language, performance, and media to redefine womanhood in stronger and more self-directed terms. Figures such as Madonna, Queen Latifah, and Mary J. Blige became popular symbols of this new expression of feminism.
The third wave was not free of conflict. Some critics argued that it had lost focus, while others said its very diversity made unity impossible. Even so, the movement remained powerful because it recognized that women’s experiences differ by race, class, sexuality, and culture.
