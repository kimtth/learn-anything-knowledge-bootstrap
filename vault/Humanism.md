# Humanism

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> Humanism most often denotes the Renaissance program of education and inquiry that cultivated human capacities through classical language, history, moral action, and learning. Originating in northern Italy, it shaped literature, art, politics, education, religion, and science across Europe while drawing on antiquity.

## Introduction

Humanism ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Humanism)) is a term applied to many beliefs, methods, and philosophies that place central emphasis on the human realm. Most often it refers to Renaissance humanism ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Renaissance_humanism)), a system of education and inquiry that developed in northern Italy during the 13th and 14th centuries and later spread through Europe and England. Its influence was so broad that it helped define the Renaissance as a period of renewal and reawakening.

Humanism ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Humanism)) had earlier roots and later effects beyond the Renaissance. It sought philosophical foundations in antiquity, shaped literature, art, politics, education, religion, and science, and continued to influence modern thought even after the original program weakened. Its main concern was not humanity in the abstract but the cultivation of human powers through language, history, moral action, and classical learning.

## Origin and meaning of the term humanism

The word humanism, first used as humanismus by 19th-century German scholars, described the Renaissance emphasis on classical education. Renaissance educators known as umanisti studied and taught Classical literature. Their work centered on the studia humanitatis ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Studia_Humanitatis)): grammar, poetry, rhetoric, history, and moral philosophy.

The term humanitas was the deeper ideal behind the movement. It meant the full development of human virtue, including understanding, eloquence, practical judgment, moral responsibility, and active participation in civic life. Humanism aimed to form people who could speak, write, judge, govern, and act well in the world.

Modern uses of humanism can be confusing. The word may refer to the humanities, to human-centered modern doctrines, to secular humanism, to Christian humanism, or to pragmatic humanism. The Renaissance meaning is more specific: a cultural and educational movement rooted in classical studies, rhetorical skill, moral philosophy, and active public life.

## Classicism

Early humanists returned to the classics not with distant reverence but with a sense of living contact. Petrarch wrote letters to Cicero and Livy, while Coluccio Salutati imagined speaking with Cicero through his letters. Niccolo Machiavelli later described entering his study in evening dress to converse with ancient writers and ask them the reasons for their actions.

For humanists, classical reading was active and questioning. Possessing a text was not enough; readers had to analyze, challenge, and draw practical wisdom from it. The ancients offered examples of eloquence, action, political judgment, poetry, and moral transformation. Humanists therefore associated classical study not only with the past but also with future reform.

## Realism

Humanist realism ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Realism_%28arts%29)) rejected inherited assumptions and emphasized direct experience, history, and the objective study of human affairs. Humanists read, taught, and wrote history because they believed historical method would enlarge present judgment. Machiavelli treated people as they were rather than as they ought to be, making history a basis for political science.

This realism also examined human uncertainty, folly, and immorality. Petrarch explored his own doubts and divided motives, while Boccaccio's Decameron surveyed human vices and disorders. Later writers such as [[Erasmus]], Thomas More, Castiglione, Rabelais, and [[Montaigne]] continued critical social observation without demanding an impossible purity.

Humanists affirmed earthly life, fame, wealth, bodily health, and a balance between mind and body. Their realism also turned toward the Roman Catholic Church, questioning it as a political institution rather than simply attacking theology. Reform, not destruction, was usually the aim.

## Critical scrutiny and active virtue

Humanistic critical scrutiny produced philology, textual comparison, historical criticism, and attention to detail. The humanist reader asked whether texts were authentic, whether words had been understood correctly, and how language shaped public life. This method gave humanism cultural power because it joined classical learning with clear expression, often in the vernacular.

The ideal of active virtue linked learning to action. Humanists argued that virtue had to serve public life through strength, generosity, political judgment, and civic responsibility. Matteo Palmieri insisted that someone with nothing to give could not be generous and that solitude could not produce justice, strength, or political experience. Castiglione, Machiavelli, Rabelais, and English humanists later developed the same theme.

## Early Italian humanism

Brunetto Latini helped create the early humanistic mixture of Ciceronian rhetoric, civic liberty, political education, Aristotelian realism, individual genius, and vernacular expression. He influenced [[Dante]] and later Florentine leaders from Salutati to Machiavelli. His work helped prepare the humanistic revolution that led toward the Renaissance, Enlightenment, and modern democratic state.

[[Dante]] seemed poised to continue Brunetto's role in Florence, but exile changed his path. Petrarch became the great figure of early humanism through his classical devotion, self-examination, and literary authority. Boccaccio added realism, narrative range, and defense of poetry. Together these figures gave humanism its combination of antiquity, inwardness, civic concern, and literary power.

## Education and Greek learning

Humanist [[Education|education]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Education)) aimed at the formation of the whole person. Schools such as Vittorino da Feltre's La Giocosa taught literature, rhetoric, moral discipline, music, drawing, astronomy, mathematics, physical exercise, and social duty. The goal was mental and bodily development joined to virtue and public usefulness.

Greek learning expanded through Manuel Chrysoloras and later Byzantine scholars who came to Italy during the 15th century, especially around the Council of Ferrara-Florence and after the fall of Constantinople in 1453. George Gemistus Plethon, George of Trebizond, Theodore Gaza, Johannes Argyropoulos, and Bessarion brought manuscripts, translations, Platonism, Aristotelian studies, and theological debate.

## Leon Battista Alberti

Leon Battista Alberti showed the range of mature Italian humanism. Educated in law, mathematics, and humanistic letters, he wrote poetry, essays, biography, moral works, rhetorical and grammatical treatises, cartography, ciphers, and major art theory. His works on painting, sculpture, and architecture developed perspective, proportion, human space, and the role of mathematics as a unifying principle.

Alberti's career joined letters, science, art, and public service. His architecture, including the Tempio Malatestiano and the facade of Santa Maria Novella, embodied the humanist belief that art could organize human experience through classical form, mathematical order, and realistic observation.

## Philosophical humanism and the Platonic Academy

Some humanists tried to join [[Rhetoric|rhetoric]] and philosophy, but philosophical humanism declined because it failed to establish a systematic relation between words and things. By the 16th century, Italian humanism became mainly literary, while philosophy developed separately. This division between literary and philosophical studies became a durable feature of Western culture.

The Florentine Platonic Academy emphasized Plato's theory of Ideas and religious transcendence more than Socratic dialectic. Marsilio Ficino sought to reconcile Plato with Christ in a pious philosophy. Influenced by Plethon, Bessarion, and Nicholas of Cusa, this Platonism gave Renaissance thought a spiritual and idealist direction, though it did not fully recover Plato's analytic method.

## Tasso's Aristotelianism

The Aristotelian Renaissance of the 15th and 16th centuries shaped literary theory, especially through Aristotle's Poetics. Torquato Tasso's Gerusalemme liberata of 1581 reflected the original humanist belief that poetry could unite particulars and universals, seek truth, and provide moral education. Tasso reinterpreted Aristotelian virtue through a Christian perspective.

## Northern humanism

Northern humanism drew from Italian sources but selected freely across the whole tradition. Desiderius Erasmus, whose fame rivaled Petrarch's, shared the Italian love of language and dislike of medieval institutional complexity. Influenced by Lorenzo Valla, he advanced Christian philology, criticized pretension, defended humane learning, and gave philology priority over speculative philosophy.

Erasmus's Latin eloquence shaped European style. His De copia taught abundance in words and ideas, while Praise of Folly used satire to expose human and institutional foolishness. His Christian defense of free will drew a powerful answer from Martin Luther, showing how humanist moderation collided with Reformation conflict.

## Chapman, Jonson, and Shakespeare

English humanism entered poetry and drama through classical rhetoric, moral education, and political reflection. George Chapman, translator of Homer, treated poetry as moral wisdom. Ben Jonson saw the poet as a teacher of virtue, nature, manners, and things divine and human, and honored Cicero's civic eloquence in Catiline.

William Shakespeare absorbed classical poetic and rhetorical practice through grammar-school training. He imitated Ovid and Plautus, used Livy and Plutarch, and transformed classical biography into drama in Julius Caesar, Antony and Cleopatra, and Coriolanus. He also questioned humanist confidence, satirizing classical idolization in Troilus and Cressida and exploring tensions between Christian doctrine and political action.

## Realism, classicism, and art

Humanists praised artistic realism ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Realism_%28arts%29)) as a way to recover Nature's creative power. Giotto was admired for moving away from Byzantine style toward natural and ancient Roman technique, while Boccaccio used Giotto's realism to justify his own literary naturalism. Realism was not an end in itself but a means of reconnecting art with Nature.

Renaissance art shared humanism's classicism, anthropocentricity, and individualism. Classical sculpture, Roman painting, proportion, myth, and idealized human beauty shaped artistic theory. Alberti linked mathematics, architecture, and human form, while portraits of figures such as Federico da Montefeltro and Lorenzo de' Medici stressed individual dignity without hiding physical particularity.

## Urbino, art, and science

The palace of Federico da Montefeltro at Urbino expressed humanistic iconography. Its study, religious spaces, and rooms of earthly beauty suggested that humanistic learning could reconcile Christianity and classical or pagan beauty. A portrait of Federico and his son Guidobaldo made the book the point where warrior, ruler, church, state, father, and son converged.

Humanism also shaped science ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Science)) because Renaissance art included mathematics, music, painting, sculpture, architecture, and natural inquiry under the broad idea of ars. Humanist recovery of ancient texts, attention to observation, and respect for mathematical order helped create a setting in which later science could grow, even if humanism itself did not become modern science.

## Later legacy and decline

Humanism influenced later figures such as [[Goethe]], Schiller, [[Hegel and Hegelianism (Hegel)|Hegel]], and Thomas Jefferson, who revived Cicero's republican vision. Yet by the end of the 20th century, the original humanistic program had largely become a historical object. The expansion of printed knowledge and new disciplines made comprehensive humanitas increasingly difficult.

Humanism ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Humanism)) also declined because its own strengths became weaknesses. Its emphasis on rhetoric and philology made it teachable and powerful, but it lacked a strong analytic method for self-criticism and renewal. Without a clear center, it split into many specialties and could not defend itself well against scientists, fundamentalists, materialists, and other critics.

## Conclusion

Humanism ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Humanism)) lacked permanence as a unified movement, but it created the climate for modern thought. Literature, philosophy, art, religion, social science, and natural science all drew nourishment from its methods, eloquence, and moral program. Modern awareness of alienation, freedom, individuality, and human possibility owes much to humanistic sources.

The humanistic movement was heroic in breadth, energy, and aspiration. It created a fertile context for human development, gave genius powerful media in thought, speech, and image, and supported lives remembered with admiration. Its weaknesses and conflicts do not erase its central concern with human continuity and human value.
