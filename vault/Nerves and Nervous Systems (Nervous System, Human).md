# Nerves and Nervous Systems (Nervous System, Human)

## Introduction

The human nervous system ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nervous_system)) conducts stimuli from sensory receptors to the brain and spinal cord and sends impulses back to the body. Like other higher vertebrates, it has a central nervous system, made of the brain and spinal cord, and a peripheral nervous system, made of nerves carrying impulses to and from the central system. The human brain is unusually large and highly developed.

Almost all neurons are generated before birth and are usually not replaced afterward. The nervous system first appears morphologically about 18 days after conception as the neural plate and functionally in the second prenatal month, when touch near the upper lip can produce a withdrawal response. Producing roughly one trillion neurons and about 100 trillion synapses requires genetic instruction plus local chemical, cellular, and environmental signals during development.

Development is rapid enough to show why genes alone cannot specify every detail. An average of about 2.5 million neurons must be generated each minute during prenatal life, and each potential neuron must connect with selected neurons or target tissues at precise membrane locations. Growth therefore depends on genetic programs interacting with local molecular signals, cell contact, and later functional activity.

## Anatomy of the Human Nervous System

*Diagram of the human nervous system showing brain, spinal cord, and peripheral nerves*

The central nervous system consists of the brain and spinal cord, both derived from the embryonic neural tube. They are protected by meninges and bathed in cerebrospinal fluid. The brain lies inside the neurocranium, while the spinal cord runs through the vertebral canal. The brain weighs about 1,500 grams and includes the cerebrum, brainstem, and cerebellum.

## Cerebrum

The cerebrum is the largest and uppermost part of the brain. It handles sensory integration, voluntary movement, speech, abstract thought, and higher intellectual functions. Its outer cerebral cortex is gray matter, while underlying white matter contains myelinated fibres connecting the hemispheres and linking regions inside each hemisphere. The corpus callosum connects the two hemispheres, and the internal capsule carries fibres to and from the cortex.

The cerebrum also contains basal ganglia, subcortical neuronal masses involved in movement organization. Cortical connections are not random: commissural fibres link corresponding hemispheric regions, association fibres connect areas within one hemisphere, and projection fibres run between cortex, brainstem, and spinal cord. This architecture lets local cortical processing become whole-body action and perception.

Each cerebral hemisphere mainly controls and receives sensory information from the opposite side of the body. Touch and vision are largely contralateral, hearing comes from both sides, and smell and taste pathways remain mostly ipsilateral. The hemispheres are not identical in function, and the dominant hemisphere is especially important for language.

## Cerebral Lobes

Each hemisphere is divided into frontal, parietal, temporal, and occipital lobes. The frontal lobe includes the precentral gyrus, the primary motor area; stimulation there produces contralateral voluntary movement, and injury can cause paralysis. The inferior frontal lobe includes Broca area, important for expressive speech.

The parietal lobe includes the postcentral gyrus, which receives touch and body-sensation input from the opposite side. The superior and inferior parietal lobules are association areas that help integrate sensory information. The temporal lobe is important for hearing and language comprehension, while the occipital lobe contains the primary visual cortex.

## Brainstem

The brainstem includes the diencephalon, midbrain, pons, and medulla oblongata, linking the cerebrum to the spinal cord. It contains relay centres, cranial nerve nuclei, motor and sensory pathways, and reticular formation structures. Many vital autonomic and motor functions depend on brainstem organization.

## Epithalamus

The epithalamus is represented mainly by the pineal gland, which lies near the third ventricle. The pineal gland produces melatonin and daylight-sensitive enzymes. Its rhythmic activity suggests a biological-clock role.

## Thalamus

The thalamus relays and distributes most sensory and motor signals to specific cerebral cortical regions. Sensory pathways are organized through thalamic relay nuclei, including the ventrobasal complex for somatic sensation, the lateral geniculate body for vision, and the medial geniculate body for hearing. Thalamic organization preserves distinctions between deep and superficial sensation and maps visual and auditory fields.

The thalamus is not a simple switchboard. Different relay nuclei preserve the organization of incoming information, so visual fields, auditory signals, and body sensations remain mapped as they move toward cortex. This ordered routing helps explain how specialized cortical areas can receive already sorted input while still participating in larger networks.

## Hypothalamus

The hypothalamus lies below the thalamus and controls endocrine, autonomic, temperature, hunger, reproductive, and emotional functions. It secretes oxytocin and vasopressin and releases factors that regulate the anterior pituitary. It also participates in sympathetic and parasympathetic control, food intake, reproductive cycles, and emotional expression.

The hypothalamus is therefore a major bridge between nervous and endocrine control. Its neurosecretory cells influence the pituitary through hormones and releasing factors, while other regions coordinate temperature, feeding, reproductive cycles, visceral responses, and emotional behaviour. It helps turn sensory and internal information into whole-body physiological states.

## Subthalamus

The subthalamus is represented mainly by the subthalamic nucleus, located near the internal capsule and globus pallidus-related fibres. Damage to this nucleus can produce hemiballismus, a violent involuntary flinging movement of the limbs.

## Midbrain

The midbrain contains oculomotor and trochlear nerve nuclei, the substantia nigra, red nucleus, and superior and inferior colliculi. The substantia nigra produces dopamine and influences basal ganglia output, making it central to movement control. The superior colliculus participates in visual reflexes and tracking, while the inferior colliculus processes auditory input.

## Pons and Medulla Oblongata

The pons contains pontine nuclei, reticular formation, cranial nerve nuclei, corticopontine fibres, and the middle cerebellar peduncle, linking each cerebral hemisphere to the opposite cerebellar hemisphere. The medulla oblongata is a conical continuation of the spinal cord and contains major crossings of motor and sensory pathways, plus routes for cerebrospinal fluid through the fourth ventricle.

The pons and medulla also show how anatomy and function are layered. Cranial nerve nuclei, reticular formation cells, ascending sensory tracts, descending motor tracts, and cerebellar connections pass through compact spaces. Damage in these regions can disturb eye movement, facial movement, hearing, balance, swallowing, breathing-related pathways, or long tracts between cortex and spinal cord.

## Cerebellum

The cerebellum, or little brain, overlies the pons and medulla and coordinates voluntary movement, balance, muscle tone, and motor learning. It has two hemispheres, a vermis, gray cortical folia, white matter, and deep nuclei. Its archicerebellum relates to the vestibular system, paleocerebellum to muscle tone, and neocerebellum to voluntary movement coordination. Purkinje cells relay cortical output to deep cerebellar nuclei.

## The Autonomic Nervous System

The autonomic nervous system regulates visceral functions needed for homeostasis. It acts mainly outside voluntary control but changes with stress, fear, sex, sleep-wake cycles, and internal demands. It controls cardiac muscle, smooth muscle, glands, visceral sensory feedback, hormone release, energy metabolism, cardiovascular function, and gastrointestinal activity.

## Sympathetic Nervous System

The sympathetic nervous system produces localized responses such as sweating and cardiovascular reflexes, but under stress it activates the whole body for danger. Preganglionic neurons arise from thoracic and upper lumbar spinal segments, forming the thoracolumbar outflow. Sympathetic ganglia include paravertebral chains and prevertebral ganglia serving the head, trunk, limbs, blood vessels, sweat glands, visceral organs, and adrenal chromaffin cells.

## Sympathetic Neurotransmitters and Receptors

Sympathetic neurotransmission is slower and more diffuse than typical synaptic signalling because autonomic nerve endings often release transmitters across wider junctions. Preganglionic neurons usually release acetylcholine, while most postganglionic neurons release norepinephrine, except sweat-gland fibres that release acetylcholine. Multiple cotransmitters and receptors create more complex chemical coding.

Autonomic junctions differ from ordinary synapses in shape as well as chemistry. Nerve terminals often end as swellings near smooth or cardiac muscle, and the gap to effector cells can be far wider than in a typical chemical synapse. Effector cells compensate through electrical coupling, so activation of a few cells can spread into a coordinated response.

## Parasympathetic Nervous System

The parasympathetic nervous system has craniosacral outflow from the brainstem and sacral spinal cord. Its ganglia lie near or inside target organs. Parasympathetic fibres control pupil constriction, lens focusing, lacrimal and salivary secretion, airway and gastrointestinal activity, bladder function, and many visceral processes. Both preganglionic and postganglionic parasympathetic neurons use acetylcholine, often with cotransmitters.

## Enteric Nervous System

The enteric nervous system is embedded in the gastrointestinal wall and controls motility and secretion. It receives modulatory sympathetic and parasympathetic commands but can maintain basic gut functions independently. Parasympathetic activity generally promotes digestion, while sympathetic activity inhibits it by closing sphincters and suppressing enteric motor neurons.

## Functions of the Human Nervous System

The human nervous system differs from that of other mammals mainly through the great enlargement of the cerebral hemispheres. Knowledge of function comes from disease observations, animal experiments, neuroimaging, and study of nerve-tract degeneration. Many connections remain incompletely understood, but research has clarified sensory processing, movement, pain, perception, language, memory, emotion, and behaviour.

## Receptors

Receptors are biological transducers that convert external and internal energy into electrical impulses. Exteroceptors report sight, hearing, smell, taste, and touch; interoceptors monitor bladder, gut, blood pressure, and blood chemistry; proprioceptors report posture and movement. Receptors may respond to steady states, changing states, chemicals, temperature, mechanical force, or harmful stimuli.

## Movement

Movement depends on coordinated contraction and relaxation of muscles through neuromuscular junctions. Smooth muscle handles many visceral actions such as breathing, swallowing, urination, and defecation, while skeletal muscle relates the body to the environment through locomotion and signalling. Movement must be correct in force, speed, and position, requiring constant feedback from proprioceptors, muscle spindles, tendon organs, vestibular receptors, and other sensory systems.

## Sensory Receptors for Movement

Tendon organs signal muscular tension because they lie in series with muscle fibres near tendon attachments. Muscle spindles are more complex and detect stretch; they are central to reflexes such as the knee jerk. Sensory feedback from muscles, tendons, and joints allows movement to adjust to changing conditions and supports the learning of new motor skills.

## Basic Organization of Movement

Stretch reflexes involve primary afferent fibres exciting motor neurons of a stretched muscle while inhibiting antagonist muscles. Other delayed spinal and transcortical reflexes help restore balance. Reciprocal innervation withdraws a limb from noxious stimuli while extending the opposite limb to support weight.

Movement is organized at several levels. Some visceral movements can be handled by autonomic neurons within organs, trunk and limb reflexes can be organized in the spinal cord, and skilled voluntary movement requires brainstem, cerebellar, thalamic, and cortical circuits. Even an intended movement prepares widespread postural activity before the visible action begins.

## Posture

Posture is the position and carriage of the body and limbs. It counters gravity by maintaining extension of the lower limbs and upright back, but the muscles involved change as the centre of gravity shifts. Extensor thrust reflexes make a weight-bearing leg act as a rigid pillar, and automatic postural fixation stabilizes body parts before and during voluntary movement.

## Cerebellum in Movement Control

The cerebellum supplies feedback and feed-forward control for skilled movement. It receives information from trunk, limbs, eyes, ears, and vestibular apparatus and exchanges information with thalamus and motor cortex. Corollary discharge compares intended movement with sensory results, while learned movement patterns may leave efference copies that guide repeated actions.

## The Vestibular System

The vestibular system detects acceleration and supports balance. It is paired in the inner ears and contains hair cells whose cilia change electrical potential when displaced. The saccule and utricle sense linear acceleration and gravity through otoliths, while three semicircular canals in each ear sense angular acceleration. Vestibular information organizes postural responses to head movement and gravity.

## Functions of the Autonomic System

Autonomic function is regulated by brain cell groups that process visceral information and issue instructions through sympathetic and parasympathetic outflows. The eye requires autonomic control of iris muscle, ciliary muscle, blood vessels, and lacrimal secretion. The light reflex constricts both pupils through retina, pretectum, Edinger-Westphal nucleus, ciliary ganglion, and pupillary sphincter pathways.

## Pain

Pain is not simply excessive stimulation of ordinary sensory fibres. Modern evidence supports stimulus-specific pathways in which noxious mechanical, thermal, or chemical stimuli activate small myelinated and unmyelinated fibres. Mechanoreceptors and large proprioceptive fibres do not produce pain no matter how frequently they fire, while nociceptive fibres report tissue-threatening events.

## Tissues

Different tissues produce pain only when injured in biologically relevant ways. Skin easily signals pain, while the brain, lung, liver, and spleen may not hurt when cut or burned. Hollow viscera hurt when obstructed and stretched, and mesentery can hurt when pulled. Acute pain warns of sudden damage, while chronic pain accompanies pathological conditions such as gout, arthritis, and cancer.

## Brain and Pain Control

The brain can inhibit sensory input arriving at lower nervous-system levels. Descending pathways from medullary and brainstem nuclei can suppress noxious, warmth, skin, mechanoreceptive, or movement-related input. Stimulation of nuclei such as the nucleus ceruleus and nucleus raphe magnus can reduce pain, and similar systems may be activated by stress or by one pain masking another.

## Perception

[[Human Perception (Perception)|Perception]] lies between sensation and complex cognition. It is automatic interpretation based on prior experience; people perceive trees and words, not disconnected colours or sounds. Self-perception of body position comes from vision, vestibular receptors, and somatic receptors, passing through vestibular nuclei, thalamus, and parietal cortex.

This makes perception partly a hypothesis about the world. Reflex responses may occur before conscious awareness, but cortical perception organizes sensory input into recognizable patterns. Once speech is learned, words are heard as meaningful language rather than separate acoustic fragments; similarly, visual input is interpreted as objects with shape, movement, depth, brightness, colour, and orientation.

## Vision

Vision involves the occipital lobe plus posterior temporal and parietal regions. The visual cortex processes edges, movement direction and speed, stereoscopic depth, brightness, colour, and orientation. Retinal ganglion cells include X-cells for high-resolution vision, Y-cells for fast movement, and W-cells for directional movement, projecting through geniculate, collicular, primary, secondary, temporal, and parietal pathways.

## Hearing

Hearing begins in the cochlea, where tone frequency is mapped by location on the basilar membrane and loudness by impulse rate. Auditory input travels through brainstem nuclei, inferior colliculus, medial geniculate nucleus, and temporal auditory cortex. Cortical neurons respond to sound onset, offset, duration, repetition, intensity differences, timing differences, and frequency changes.

## Emotion

[[Human Emotion (Emotion)|Emotion]] involves the whole brain but especially frontal, temporal, limbic, thalamic, and hypothalamic regions. Leucotomy showed that disrupting frontal-thalamic connections can reduce social inhibition. Electrical stimulation of temporal and limbic regions can evoke fear, loneliness, disgust, sorrow, anxiety, ecstasy, or guilt. Hypothalamic circuits organize fight-or-flight defense responses with strong sympathetic activity.

## Mating and Visceral Behaviour

Mating behaviour is organized in the anterior hypothalamus and neighbouring septal region, near centres for urination. Hormones influence these circuits, and stimulation can produce sexual emotions or thoughts. Hypothalamic regions also organize urination, defecation, eating, and drinking; lateral areas promote feeding, while ventromedial areas inhibit it.

## Higher Cerebral Functions

Higher cerebral functions include conscious thought, memory, reasoning, speech, purposeful movement, and unconscious cortical information processing. Cognitive science models these processes abstractly, while neuropsychology connects behaviour with brain damage. Frontal damage can affect working-memory executive control, and parietal damage can impair mental imagery or spatial functions.

## Language

[[Language]] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Language)) is concentrated around the Sylvian fissure in the dominant hemisphere. Broca area in the inferior frontal lobe supports expressive speech; damage causes deliberate, telegraphic speech. Wernicke area in the posterior superior temporal lobe supports comprehension; damage causes fluent but empty speech. The arcuate fasciculus connects posterior and anterior language areas, and damage can cause conduction aphasia.

Aphasia is a disorder of [[Language|language]], not merely speech. Reading and writing often show the same kinds of errors as spoken language, while isolated disorders of writing or reading can occur when more specific cortical systems are damaged. These patterns make clinical language disturbance one of the clearest ways to connect mental function with localized brain networks.

## Memory

[[Memory]] includes working memory, long-term memory, episodic memory, and semantic memory. Long-term memory is widely distributed, but bilateral limbic lesions involving the hippocampus, thalamus, and their connections can cause amnesia. Such amnesia usually affects new episodic memories more than old ones, producing anterograde memory loss while leaving short-term memory relatively intact.

## Core Equations

Health and behavioural sciences often quantify risk, dose, change over time, and population variation:

$$C(t)=C_0e^{-kt}$$

This models concentration falling exponentially over time, often used for clearance, decay, or elimination processes.

$$z=\frac{x-\mu}{\sigma}$$

A z-score tells how many standard deviations a value lies above or below the mean.

$$\text{risk}=\frac{\text{cases}}{\text{population}}$$

This ratio turns the preceding idea into a measurable comparison, with the numerator as the quantity of interest and the denominator as the scale used for comparison.

## Key Events & Developments

- 18 days after conception: The neural plate first marks nervous-system development.
- Second prenatal month: Early reflex activity can be detected.
- 1830s-20th century: Clinical lesion studies and later neuroimaging clarified human brain function.
- 20th century: Pain, reflex, language, memory, and autonomic systems were increasingly mapped through physiology and clinical neurology.

## Interesting Facts

- The mature brain contains roughly one trillion neurons and 100 trillion synapses.
- The pineal gland responds rhythmically to daylight and functions like a biological clock.
- The cerebellum is a motor structure; damage affects coordination rather than sensation.
- Pain control can be produced by descending brain pathways that block noxious input.
