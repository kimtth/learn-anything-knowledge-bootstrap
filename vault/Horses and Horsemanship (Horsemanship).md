# Horses and Horsemanship (Horsemanship)

> [!abstract] Reference navigation
> Horsemanship is the art of riding, handling, and training horses through coordinated guidance of direction, gait, and speed with minimal unnecessary interference. Its history runs from Central Asian riding and warfare to modern clubs, equestrian sports, and training traditions.

## Introduction

![[attachments/horsemanship_001.webp|420]]

*Rider and horse at a dressage competition*

Horsemanship ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equestrianism)) is the art of riding, handling, and training horses so that a rider controls direction, gait, and speed with maximum effectiveness and minimum effort. Good riding asks the rider to interfere as little as possible while still guiding the horse clearly. Once mainly tied to cavalry, cowboys, work riders, and wealthy sport riders, horsemanship became more widely accessible through riding clubs, stables, horse shows, and popular equestrian sports.

## History

From at least the 2nd millennium BCE, central Asian nomads used horses for riding, hunting, and war. Scythians were skilled horsemen, used saddles, and may have developed an early stirrup-like aid, while Sarmatians rode bareback with control from knees and body weight. Hittites, Assyrians, Babylonians, and Hyksos used horses in war, and the Hyksos brought horses into Egypt about 1500 BCE.

In [[Greece]], riding developed quickly after horses arrived through Scythian influence in the 8th and 7th centuries BCE. Philip II of Macedon organized cavalry, and [[Alexander the Great]]'s army used separate horse units. Xenophon's On Horsemanship ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/On_Horsemanship)), written in the 4th century BCE, advised mild bits, balance, and training without force, principles that remained important. Later riding traditions grew through military, courtly, sporting, and schooling systems.

## Fundamentals

The horse's center of gravity changes with every movement, and a mounted horse carries an unstable rider of about one-fifth its own weight. The rider must therefore adapt to the horse's motion. A fitting saddle, secure seat, independent balance, and correct use of aids allow control without pain or harshness.

Good seat gives the rider stability and freedom to use hands, legs, voice, and weight. Skilled riders do not simply overrule the horse; they persuade it firmly while preserving rhythm, impulsion, and confidence. The seat changes with the task, whether ordinary riding, dressage, jumping, cross-country work, or show riding.

## Walk

The walk is a slow four-beat gait with distinct successive hoof beats. It may be a free walk, where the horse uses head and neck freely while contact remains; a collected walk, short and full of impulsion; or an extended walk with long, unhurried strides.

## Trot

The trot is a two-beat gait in which diagonal pairs of legs move nearly together. Riders may sit the trot and absorb the movement, or rise, or post, by lifting slightly from the saddle as diagonal pairs leave the ground. Posting reduces impact on both horse and rider.

## Canter

The canter, or ordinary slow gallop, is a three-beat gait with one foreleg and both hindlegs leading in sequence. The horse may canter on the near or off lead, depending on which forefoot leaves the ground last, and the rider's body moves more forward than at the trot.

## Gallop

The gallop is an accelerated canter in which the rider's weight comes sharply forward as the horse may reach about 30 miles per hour. Some authorities treat it as a four-beat gait in extended running. Other special gaits include the rack, single foot, pace, fox trot, and amble, many of which are performed by horses bred for them.

## Training and equipment

Training can include collection, extension, turns on the forehand and hindquarters, lead changes, speed changes, reining back, lateral movements, dressage refinements, jumping, and cross-country riding. Communication comes through the bit and the aids. Repetition teaches the horse a language of hand, leg, seat, and voice signals.

## Bits

Bits include the snaffle, double bridle, and Pelham. The snaffle is the simplest, with a straight or jointed mouthpiece and rings for reins, and is used in racing, cross-country riding, and early schooling. The double bridle combines a snaffle and a straight bit for advanced work, while the Pelham adds curb action and gives control with limited discomfort.

## Bridles

The bridle is a set of straps that holds the bit in the horse's mouth and allows control through the reins. Its parts include the headpiece, headband, cheek straps, noseband, and throatlatch. Driving bridles may include blinkers that limit the horse's sight to what is ahead.

## Aids

The horse's powers of observation, timidity, memory, and understanding shape the use of aids. Natural aids are the voice, hands, legs, heels, reins, bit, and rider's weight. Artificial aids include whip, spur, martingales, special nosebands, and reins. A good rider approaches quietly, maintains light contact with the mouth, uses hands and legs together, and shifts weight in harmony with the horse.

## Seats

The seat, saddle, and stirrup length must suit the riding purpose. The forward seat, used for hunting, show jumping, and cross-country riding, places the rider near the middle of the saddle with the torso forward and the hands following the horse's mouth. The dressage or show seat is deeper, more upright, and meant to show collected, graceful movement with light contact.

## Bareback

Bareback riding ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bareback_riding)) means riding without saddle or blanket, staying on mainly by balance in the hollow of the horse's back. It is uncomfortable, especially beyond the walk or slow canter, but may be used for exercise when a horse suffers from saddle galls.

## Dressage

Dressage ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dressage)) began as military training and was formalized from the 16th century. Its goal is the harmonious development of the horse's body and ability, producing calmness, suppleness, keenness, straightness, regular paces, lightness of the forehand, engagement of the hindquarters, and generous submission to the rider.

Campagne is elementary but thorough training, including work on the longeing rein and collection. Haute ecole is the most specialized form, associated especially with the Spanish Riding School of Vienna and its Lippizaners. Movements include pirouettes, piaffe, passage, levade, courvet, croupade, ballotade, and capriole, all based at least remotely on natural equine movements.

## Jumping

In jumping, the rider's hands control the forehand and the legs act on the hindquarters while the seat rises slightly from the saddle. The rider must follow the horse's head and shoulder through takeoff and landing, then avoid sitting back too soon so the hindquarters remain free. Training begins with ground poles, then gradually higher, varied, and irregularly spaced obstacles.

The jumping horse should approach quietly, energetically, and collectedly; keep its head down; choose takeoff; and move calmly to the next obstacle after landing. Competition demands thoroughly trained horse and rider, warming up before the event, and confidence over each fence before it is raised.

## Organizations and horse shows

National equestrian bodies, including the American Horse Shows Association and similar organizations in many countries, affiliate with the Federation Equestre Internationale, founded in 1921 in Brussels as the international governing body for equitation requirements. These organizations set rules, judge events, settle disputes, and support competition standards.

Horse shows developed from horse sections of agricultural fairs into organized competitions. The Royal Dublin Society held an international show in 1864, and the National Horse Show in New York began in 1883. Important British shows included the Richmond Royal Horse Show, Horse of the Year Show, and Royal International Horse Show, first held in 1907.

## Interesting Facts

- The walk has four beats, the trot has two, and the canter has three.
- The gallop can reach about 30 miles per hour.
- Posting at the trot reduces the impact on both horse and rider.
- Bareback riding ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bareback_riding)) may be used when saddle galls make a saddle painful.
- The Spanish Riding School of Vienna is especially linked with haute ecole and Lippizaner horses.
